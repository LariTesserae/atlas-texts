They are light.

That is the first thing you would notice, and the last thing they ever stop considering. Not frail — up there, on a continent with nothing underneath it but warm cloud, frailty would not last a season — but spare, narrow-boned, hollow in the way birds and reeds are hollow, built for a world that must carry them. A grown adult weighs little more than a child would, where there was once ground. Their ribs show. Their bellies are flat even in old age, even after meals. Excess weight is not vanity here; it is a sag in the floor of your neighbor's house three districts away. It is a bridge beginning to sing at the wrong pitch under your feet.

They know their mass the way other peoples know their names. Children are weighed in woven slings against bundles of cut vine. A household knows, within a handful of fiber, how much it adds to its swelling in the tower-tree. When someone grows heavy with pregnancy, someone else in the family will often take the long bridge to a thinner district for a while, or a store-gourd will be emptied, or an old, waterlogged section of wall will finally be cut away and dropped, turning, into the white below. Nothing personal in it. A courtesy. A physics.

And they are quiet. Even more than the frogs and the moths and the great chewing beetles, they are quiet.

Their voices, when they use them, are made for a few feet of green dusk: low, breathy, rounded off at the edges, consonants softened so as not to startle anything on a narrow way. But a great deal of their language never becomes sound at all. The mat carries vibration better than the wet air carries words. Footfalls are felt long before they are heard, and so they learned, over uncountable generations, to speak downward. A pressure of the heel. A dragging toe. A stillness, precisely held, that means *stop* more urgently than any shout. A child fussing in a gourd-house can be soothed by a grandparent two rooms away shifting weight in a hammock in an agreed rhythm. Lovers conduct entire arguments through the floor while their faces remain placid above it. The hunting cats, they say, can hear a voice. They have never yet learned to listen for a foot thinking.

Out of that necessity their feet have become a second pair of hands, and in some ways a more honest pair. The toes are long, splayed, independently strong, with nails thickened into shallow hooks for bark and cane-work. The soles are not callused so much as *sensate* — layered, ridged, able to read the weave beneath moss the way fingers read a face. A good Repair-walker can find rot with her feet that her eyes would miss in that light, the dull place, the give that is wrong by half a degree. Babies are carried against the chest in slings and learn balance before they learn speech; their first exploratory gesture is not a grasp but a testing press into emptiness beside the sleeping platform, waiting, delighted, to see if anything springy will rush up to meet them.

Something often does, when they are very young. Or so the old people swear.

Their hands are for persuading. Long fingers, double-flexible, the skin of the palms permanently darkened and grooved by fiber. Everyone braids — in sleep, almost, the hands continuing a rope while the mind rests — because rope is road and wall and cradle and suture and grave. They twist it on the thigh, as their ancestors did, with stone adzes at the belt and bone needles in a twist of hair, and knives of shell and river-glass older than any river anyone can point to. To call them primitive would be to miss what their hands are doing. They are not failing to invent harder tools. Heavier tools would fall, and falling, where *down* is negotiable, is a complicated sin. A dropped hammer near a great anchor trunk might slide slantwise through the air for an hour and embed itself in someone's grandfather's house-tree. So they tie everything. Tools to wrists. Cups to tables. Children, loosely, to bedframes. Themselves, at night on wide floors, by one ankle, out of habit older than fear.

That loose ankle-cord is the closest thing they have to a universal garment. Clothes otherwise are woven reed and oiled fiber that do not mind never drying: short wraps, sleeveless tunics that shed the interior rain down their seams, hoods of broad leaf-laminate for crossing exposed causeways during the Flowering. Their skin, olive-brown to deep umber, has a permanent sheen from the oil and the condensing air, and in the dim districts a faint green cast that is not paint — a friendly mold, cultivated, that lives in the outer skin and in their hair and helps them digest the ever-present damp the way the moss on the walkways digests sound. In the bright gaps it photosynthesizes, barely, tastably; old people who sit long in a shaft of sun will tell you they are less hungry afterward, and no one argues.

They see well in a world that is twilight at noon. Their eyes are large, dark, gold-flecked, with a second inner lid that sweats condensation away in a blink. They do not look directly at one another much — there is so much to watch at the edges. The moths drifting one way all at once. The frogs' throat-sacs going dark in sequence from a point outward. The fine hairs on the back of the mat, and on their own forearms, leaning. Looking straight at a person is for festivals and marriages and informing someone of a death. The rest of the time they attend to each other peripherally, which from the outside looks like dreaminess and is in fact a highly evolved form of politeness: *I am not the most important warm thing in your landscape.*

They move as if the floor were a sleeping animal they like. Single file on the causeways, though there may be room for three abreast. Each foot placed, tested, committed to. Ceremonial, to an outsider. It is not ceremony. It is reading ahead through the soles. On a bridge two days long and one person wide, with luminous green distance falling away on either side into cloud, they will pass one another — rarely, and only at the grown widenings, wart-like platforms with a rail and a bowl of black water — by turning face to face, hands linked, and revolving slowly, like dancers who have all the time there is and also none. Children find this hilarious and practice it at dangerous speeds while tethered. Someone always falls a little. The tether, and the sideways gravity near the big roots, and the reaching young steps, generally conspire to give them back.

They eat what the persuaded world offers and what it has learned to offer in the same place every year. Pollen skimmed golden from bowl-water during the nine days of Flowering, beaten into cakes that taste of honey and iron. Young shoots, always taken as part of a pruning, never as a separate greed. Fruit from the hanging gardens, and stranger, paler fruit gathered — by the few who will go — from the Underlooming, where things ripen inverted and fall up into the hand if you wait beneath, which is to say above, them. The browsers give milk thin as rainwater and, at the end of their long lives, meat that is eaten communally and quickly, the bones carved into needles on the spot so the weight does not travel. The dog-sized frogs are not eaten. No one remembers deciding this. The frogs hold the bowls, and the bowls hold the water, and the water makes the weather. You do not eat your own rain.

Their houses grow around them while they live in them, which changes the meaning of *family* slightly. A couple will hollow a gourd on the vine and plug its doorway with a carving; twenty years later their grandchildren are sleeping in alcoves that swelled where the plugs were, and the doorway has lowered, as doorways do, so that entering the house of an old family means bowing, correctly, to everyone who ever lived there. Houses remember. Cut one down in anger — it happens, in feuds that are conducted mostly by refusing to share pollen-cake in a pointed, foot-transmitted silence — and it will come back next spring in the same place, a little greener, its doorway a little lower still, and the feud is usually over, because what is there to say to that.

The old, eventually, stop leaving. Not from infirmity — their joints, adapted to constant micro-sway, often outlast their eyes — but from a recognized thickening of relationship. An elder will move into the wart-swelling halfway up the tower-tree and prune less and braid more and be consulted, increasingly, as a part of the architecture. People bring questions to the swelling and sit with their backs against warm inner bark and feel the answers through the floor. When an elder dies, which they prefer to do during the Long Sway so the whole continent is already moving in the direction of farewell, the body is not buried, there being no earth to receive it that did not begin as someone's floor. It is woven in. Cut the dead weave out, plant the new: the Repair is also the funeral rite, identical in gesture, which is why the young people who walk the lines with coils of fresh fiber are treated with a tenderness usually reserved for midwives. They are always, somewhere under their hands, replacing a great-grandparent with a bridge.

They marry for the Sway as well, and time conceptions — loosely, hopefully — so that children will learn to walk when the world is teaching balance on a continental scale, rocking them in their knees before they have words for it. To be motion-sick there is not an ailment. It is a kind of illiteracy.

Down below, in the Underlooming, live their others. Not a separate species. Cousins, exiles, the descendants of builders who went down through the root-tangle to tend the anchor trunks and stayed, generation by generation, until *up* had changed its mind around them. They are paler, their cultivated mold white as fish bellies, their eyes larger still, and they sleep hanging, comfortably, head toward the cloud-sea. They can walk on the underside of the mat for short stretches where the gravity leans, placing those long toes into the weave overhead like a ceiling, and their ankle-cords are not habit but language. Things dropped by the people above — tools, seeds, occasionally a child who slipped a tether in the Flowering light, caught in the lean of a pillar and delivered, slantwise and indignant, into an upside-down orchard — are returned by them along the anchor trunks, without comment and without any expectation of thanks that could be expressed in foot-speech upside down. The above-people leave pale fruit at the root-doors for them. The below-people leave warnings, in arrangement of fallen petals, of cats.

Because the cats do hunt both worlds, moving through the lean near the trunks as if gravity were a suggestion they had personally outgrown. And the mat-wasps guard the young bridges. And the grazers, bells stuffed with moss, must not be startled mid-step. So the whole vast, swaying, continent-wide civilization proceeds at the volume of a held breath: pruning, flowering, swaying, repairing. Making staircases by expecting them. Catching upward rain in their mouths without comment as it passes. Teaching their children that planting is encouraging most of the seeds to come back down, and that everything built is alive, and that — this is the part the children understand first, strapped loosely to their sleeping platforms a mile above the clouds, feeling distant strangers time their dreams to the same slow motion in their knees — everything alive is, in the end, carrying you.
