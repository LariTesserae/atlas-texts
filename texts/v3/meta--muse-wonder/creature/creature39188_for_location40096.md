At first you would not say that anything *lives* there, in the singular. You would say it is overgrown.

There are too many shapes for one body. A hedge. A canopy. A thicket of straight stems around the presses, a tangle of vines overhead, a mat of roots underfoot, moss, fern, algae in the sumps. From the levee it resolves into green in general, the way a crowd from a distance resolves into noise.

It is only after you have been inside a while — after your shoes are wet and your ears are full and the path you came in by has swollen shut behind you — that the repetitions begin to bother you.

The leaves on the north wall and the leaves over the pump house have the same, slightly lopsided curl. The pods ripening by the eastern presses split in the same hour as the pods on the far side of the bowl, though that side is in shade. When a valve sighs and pressure falls somewhere high in the pipes, the whole floor answers, not just the ground near it: a soft settling, everywhere at once, as if a single animal had exhaled through its feet.

Follow any trunk down far enough and it does not end in its own roots. It flares, meets another flare, grafts. Follow a root and it braids into the mat, joins, loses its name. At ground level, almost everything green in the bowl is continuous. One immense, many-trunked individual, old enough that it has forgotten — if a plant can forget — which parts of itself were planted and which volunteered.

It did not arrive all at once. You can read a clumsier history in it, if you know to look. Its lowest ten feet are square where it was once trained up scaffolding beside the engine houses, and that squareness is still in the wood, carried upward year by year like a childhood habit in an adult body. In one corner a whole panel of its leaves is still patterned, faintly, from a radiator grille it grew against fifty years ago. Whole ranks of it grow straight because there is a cable run buried beneath them and it liked the warmth in winter. It has learned the factory the way a river learns a mill race: not by intention at first, just by finding the easy channels, then by making them easier, then by being unable to imagine flowing any other way.

Which makes it hard to say where it ends and the works begin.

The pipes that run over your head are partly copper and partly itself, hollow woody stems of the same bore, with bark collars where metal flanges used to sit. Those are not imitations. They carry real pressure. The pump deep under the root-mat — the one you feel as a thump in the soles of your feet — lifts marsh water into it, and it lifts that water the rest of the way itself, by transpiration, millions of leaves pulling in the green shade they themselves made. It turns valves itself too, very slowly, stems trained around handwheels thickening in summer to close them a fraction, shrinking back in the cool to let them open. The presses that take its pulp are iron, certainly, but they stand on a plinth it has cracked into islands and re-jointed with buttress roots so that they no longer rock. It has not crushed the main shaft, all these years. It has grown three clean, polished supports around it instead, like fingers carefully not closing.

This is the bargain its body is built around. It keeps the geometry it was given. It does not overwhelm the buildings, even though it could; there are trunks as thick as oil drums held inches off a wall by nothing but their own redirected growth. In return the iron does things roots cannot. It brings water up in dry months from below the bowl. It concentrates pressure. It cracks, very precisely, the swollen seed-pods it sheds anyway, and sorts the amber pulp from the husk. The grove gets a reliable drought, survived. The factory gets a crop that replants, repairs, refuels itself. Neither side is in charge. The tenders who live in the sheds at the rim will tell you that if you ask them — outside, where you do not have to shout — and then they will shrug, as if to say the shrug *is* the management plan.

It is, by any ordinary measure, extremely slow, and yet it is not still. It is the loudest living thing you have ever stood inside.

Most of that noise is its working. Water hissing through leaf-chutes it grew for the purpose, hollow stems knocking as pressure waves travel through them, wood creaking as it takes up hydraulic load, the whole canopy shifting an inch, an inch and a half, toward a break in the cloud. That rasping, rushing, conversational roar is not something it makes the way a bird makes song, on top of its life. It *is* its life, overheard: sap going up, water coming down, pulp moving toward the presses on cane slats, excess energy shed as sound and warm vapour through the skylights. When the plume shows above the levee on a cold morning, that is it breathing out.

Its day is tidal, but the tide is pressure. In the morning it is comparatively slack. The root-mat lies lower, the black lenses of marsh water widen between the roots, the paths are where you left them yesterday. It feels — though it would not feel it in those terms — for light with the slow chemistry of leaf and stem. By afternoon, with the sun sieved green above it and all that leaf area pulling, the sap is up. The floor of the bowl genuinely rises, an inch, two inches. Pools shrink as it drinks them inward. Swollen roots close one gap and, relaxing elsewhere, open another. Pods that were green at breakfast split with a wet crack by four o'clock and offer their pulp to the conveyors. The frogs that live in the water caught in its leaf axils call then, if they call at all — one note, a pause as if listening to the machinery, another note. It does not respond. It may not distinguish them from drips.

It has not gone dormant in living memory. Winter is not a sleep for it, only a slowing, a conservation, a turning-down of the roar to a murmur. The engineers who first worked here apparently expected to shut the plant down seasonally. You cannot shut this down. You can persuade it, over a season or two of pruning and shading and withheld water, to do less. Persuasion is the right word and also entirely the wrong one, because there is no one moment when it agrees. You cut here — and cutting is the main language the tenders have — and months later a valve elsewhere begins to open a little wider. You thin a canopy panel and next summer the pressure in the south pipes is better and a buttress root has begun to frame the doorway you always duck through. It learns, not quickly, and it remembers in wood. A decision, for it, is a shape it grows into and then cannot easily grow out of.

Whether it knows it is one thing is a question that stops being useful after a day inside it. It certainly behaves as one thing. Light is budgeted across the whole bowl with an accountancy you can almost see: no gap in the canopy stays unclaimed long enough for a competitor to seed, no root zone is left unsaturated, every litre that falls as condensation is caught by a tilted leaf and delivered somewhere. That is why the bowl feels so oddly empty for all its fullness. It is not being inhospitable. It has simply taken up all the available ways of being a plant here, personally. Epiphytes can ride it. Moss can coat its tanks. Small soft-bodied insects can live inside its stems and are, perhaps, less visitors than a kind of fauna of the blood. But nothing else can get a foothold, because the foothold is already a foot.

The heron that lands on the tallest press housing in the mornings is tolerated the way you tolerate a gull on a roof. It stands above the noise, preposterously still, and the grove does not shift a leaf toward it or away. The human tenders are tolerated on somewhat more involved terms. It has grown hooks for their tools on one particular trunk. It keeps the roof of their control hut lashed, after its fashion, with living ties that tighten as they grow. Their sheds are in the few places where its roots make a reliably dry hummock. In exchange they prune, guide, clear algae from gauges, read the ticking dials it cannot read, and perform the small, quick adjustments that a hundred-year body cannot perform for itself. They live their short, fast lives in the shelter of its long one, using hand signs in the roar, and growing old listening to a neighbour who creaks, drips, lifts, settles.

It is not tame. Tame things can be left alone and stay as you left them; it will grow through a forgotten door in a single summer and politely remodel a staircase into a trunk. It is not wild either, not anymore. Take away the pump and it would survive, probably — it is too large and too rooted to die of one disappointment — but it would have to become something else over twenty or thirty years, unwinding bargains laid down in its own timber. It has preferences that have become anatomy. It likes its water lifted. It likes its pods pressed. It likes the straight warm line of the old cable run and the open panels where the light comes thickest, and it turns toward those things with the whole, grinding patience of a hillside deciding to be a slightly different hill.

If it has a centre, it is not in any one trunk. It may be in the grafted ring below the root-mat where the pump's vibration travels outward and the afternoon swelling begins. Or it may have no centre at all and be most itself at its edges — in the newest vine feeling its way around a leaking pipe joint, tasting iron and sap together, deciding, over a season, to become a seal.

You leave it the way you entered it, by pushing through. Behind you the stems close. From the levee it is gone again into a single bowl of green, vibrating almost imperceptibly, putting up its thin plume of warm breath. Nothing waves. Nothing watches you go. The floor is still rising toward evening under all that leaf, the presses are still turning somewhere muffled inside it, and it is still there — one creature, patient, hydraulic, half-built and half-grown, keeping house in the only body it has ever entirely trusted: a factory it has spent a lifetime learning how to be.
