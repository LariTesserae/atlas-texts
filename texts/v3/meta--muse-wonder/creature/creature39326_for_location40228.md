They do not look like visitors here, and not quite like lowlanders either, though that is what their grandparents were.

You first meet them standing where there is apparently no ground — a man up to his ankles in a shining lake, a woman with a punt-pole over her shoulder walking straight across open water, children running a line that vanishes under lily pads. Only when you are close do you see the causeway a hand's breadth under the surface, and the way their feet find it without looking. Their boots, if they are wearing boots at all, are already full. Many go barefoot from May onward. Their feet are broad, pale, splayed on the granite, soles thick as leather from dome-rock and submerged stone, toes quick and separate, feeling for the edge. Above the ankle, everything changes colour. Trousers, skirts, wool stockings carry a permanent tide-mark of green-brown, algae and peat worked into the weave. Above the knee they are slate grey, rust red, oiled black. Brass buttons. Brass buckles. Nothing ferrous that can be avoided, because iron up here is a brief opinion.

They are not tall people, not by lowland measure. A century and a half of thin air and short, violent work has made them deep in the chest and light in the frame, with large hands that are always a little swollen from cold water. The skin at the knuckles is cracked and healed and cracked again. Nails are short and dark. Women and men alike keep their hair cut close under wool caps or tied hard back from the face, because loose hair in that wind is another sail, and because it never properly dries anyway. It smells faintly of peat-smoke even when it has just been washed.

Their faces burn and peel in the high sun between showers, then go white again in the mist. They squint, not at distance so much as at glare — that flat, multiplied light off forty kilometres of water. The older turbine-keepers have a particular deafness, not total but carved out in the middle registers where generator and waterfall agree. They turn one ear, the better ear, to you and watch your mouth. Even the young shout out of habit on the domes near the rim, and then, absurdly, speak almost mouth-to-ear when they come in out of the noise, as if ordinary volume were a thing that had to be remembered.

They live in families, but the family is rarely just blood. It is a dome, a power house, a lock, a length of leat. A household might be two sisters running a turbine house with a brother-in-law who came up from the low country to mend flumes and never got his lungs right for going back down, three children, an apprentice from two lakes over, and a widowed reed-cutter who sleeps in the boathouse loft because her own bothy took water in the big surge three summers ago. Names attach to functions that attach to places: the Lock Bells, the North Leat people, the families at Fifty Falls. Ask where someone is from and they will not name a village. They will make a small gesture with two fingers — channel, dome, channel — the route.

Their houses are stone, low, slate-weighted against wind that comes up off the rim carrying its own rain upward. The roofs are lashed. The doors open away from the prevailing weather and directly, often, into shallow water. Inside, the floor is a single granite slab tilted just enough to drain to a brass grate, because flooding indoors is not a disaster but a season. Furniture stands on stone feet. Paper, books, bedding, flour — anything that can swell and rot lives upstairs in slate bins or in tin-lined chests warmed, incredibly, by a pipe run off the generator. Everything else accepts damp as its condition. Wool blankets steaming by an electric ring. Kettles that never leave the heat. A smell in every house of hot copper, wet wool, woodsmoke flattened by the air outside and creeping back in under the door, fish oil, crushed sedge from somebody's legs.

Children grow up fluent in a world lowland children would drown in politely. They can swim before they can read, pole a flat-bottomed punt before they can ride anything with wheels — there is almost nothing with wheels up here. They learn the bell codes as a second alphabet, and the more important one. Three slow strokes from the lock: a lake above has overtopped. Two and two from the power house: sinkholes pulling hard to the north, keep off the brown ponds. A frantic, irregular ringing that is not a code at all but a human hand frightened: surge front coming down a meadow, get to a dome. They learn to hear the quarter-hour hush when the frogs stop, and to climb without being told. They learn which floating turf islands are thick enough to bear a grebe's nest and a small boy, and which will roll. They collect stranded fish from warm puddles after a fall in level and do it fast, barehanded, laughing, before the herons finish the same work with less mercy. By ten they can read a meadow that looks like solid grass and know it is a hand of clear water over peat that will take you to the thigh.

Work is done in bursts because the water at noon numbs hands in a minute. Mend a leat joint, haul a net, set a turbine bearing, cut sedge standing waist-deep in black water — twenty minutes in, ten out with your hands wrapped around a generator housing or a mug or the warm neck of the power-house dog. The dogs are small, barrel-chested, half otter in temperament, bred up here without anyone quite deciding to breed them. They sleep on warm machinery and swim causeways as a matter of course.

Food is fish and whatever can be kept dry long enough to matter. Trout from under submerged grass, pike from the channels, coarse fish nobody below would prize and everybody here does, smoked over peat in sheds that stand in a foot of water through a perforated floor. Lily seed, reed shoots in spring, imported grain and salt and tea kept like ammunition. Gardens, where they exist, are heroic little rafts of imported soil on the south side of a dome, boxed in slate, netted against terns, growing potatoes with leaves blackened by wind and roots practically in the lake. A good garden is a form of boasting.

They export power and import almost everything else, including soil for their dead. The chapel stands on the highest dome, its graveyard a shallow terrace of earth hauled up the escarpment road sack by sack over generations, retained by granite, planted with rowan. Graves there are still damp — there is no defeating the principle — but they do not fill to the brim and reflect the sky, which is the distinction people here care about. The dead are carried to it by boat and then shoulder-high along a causeway, so a funeral in mist appears, from another dome, to be a slow procession of neighbours carrying a box across the surface of a lake. Bells give right of way. Locks stand open. Turbines are not stopped; no one would think of asking.

Romance, quarrels, debts, marriages follow the water routes. Courtship is often lock-work — staying late to help lift punts the one man's height between lakes, again and again, until the help is noticed for what it is. A wedding gift might be dry ground rights, a share in a dome, a brass bell properly tuned to carry over a particular reach without being mistaken for the next channel's bell. Lowland spouses are common and conspicuous for two summers: they cough in the thin wet air, they cannot tell one bell from the falls, they keep trying to dry their boots by a fire and making the whole house steam. Some leave on the cargo punts. Some stay and become, to the eye of anyone born here, indistinguishable except that they never quite trust a causeway they cannot see, and feel for each step with a pole to the end of their lives.

Old age is measured in knees and hearing. Pole-work and dome-climbing make prophets of rheumatism; the cold finds every joint and stays like a tenant. The old sit in power-house doorways with their feet in the sun, out of the water for once, mending nets they can mend by touch, listening to the table. They are the best forecasters. They feel a change in rim-thunder through the stone under a chair leg before the frogs know. They know by smell whether rain coming across the plateau is clean rain off the escarpment or brown surge-water full of cut grass and somebody else's upstream country.

In winter — the long sealing of it that the summer description barely hints at — they draw in to the turbine domes, where moving water keeps a black throat open in the ice around each leat. They live then more than ever by generator-heat and lamplight, skating the frozen shallows between domes, hearing the falls muffled under ice at the rim, mending everything rust has eaten since spring. Children born in winter are called leat-born and are supposed to be lucky, though whether that is true or only kind is not discussed.

At evening in summer, when the rain pauses and mist takes the lakes a few feet deep, they are at their most themselves and their least visible. Heads and shoulders above the white layer. Reed-cutters coming home with bundles high to keep them dry. A girl running a line of posts across a drowned meadow, bells answering dome to dome, nearer and stranger in the thin air. Someone poling standing up, only the pole and the torso moving steadily over nothing. They do not wave much. They call across the water — names, water-news, jokes in the shorthand of people who know the shout will be half taken by the rim.

Nothing about them is dry, solemn, or still. They live on the backs of whales of granite in a sea that was a mountain range before trees existed, read the sky less than they read a rise, warm their hands on machines that feed on waterfalls, and walk, daily and unremarkably, on water.
