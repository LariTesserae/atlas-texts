From a distance you would not pick them out. That is the first thing.

On the levee you see the plume of steam and hear the pumps through the ground, and at dusk you see floating lights behind leaves. Down inside, on Pump Lane or Dry Shed Cut at noon, people are passing constantly across the bridges and under the awnings — but they are the same colors as the place they are passing through. Skin stained at the hands and forearms with green fiber juice that does not wash out, only fades to a grey-brown. Work shirts once blue or white or khaki, softened to the color of wet canvas, darkened permanently at the collar and under the arms. Trousers rolled by reflex to mid-calf, even indoors, even in the dry season that is not dry. Grease under the fingernails from the mill, and under that again, black earth from the root-mat.

They move like the town moves, which is to say the machines hurry for them. A man hauling a bale on the rail trolley is not hurrying the trolley. A woman crossing the market square with taro in a reed basket on her hip steps around the trip-root in Second Canal without looking down, shifts the basket, keeps going. Nobody runs, except children for about twenty yards before the heat takes it out of them. The air in the lanes, trapped under two and three layers of leaf, will not support running. It is warm water to breathe. Work is done at a pace that can be kept from the first steam blast to the last, shoulders rolling, machetes and saw-teeth and belt drives doing the fast, repetitive part, the body doing the steady part. Outsiders, the dried-fish traders who come down the gravel track from the levee road, are conspicuous for the first day by how quickly they try to walk, how soon they are soaked through, how they keep slapping at mosquitoes and missing.

They are not a tall people, though that may be less blood than posture and building. Doorways are hung in fig walls that were trained for two seasons, and they are hung low. Walkways between the dormitory blocks sag and lift with roots. Everyone ducks under awnings, stoops under pipe runs carried over canals on living bamboo bridges, bends in the soaking pits and over the decorticators. After a lifetime of it they carry themselves slightly forward, knees soft, feet placed flat and a little splayed — a useful gait on planks polished with algae, on springy root floors worn smooth in the middle. Their feet are broad in front, the nails thickened. Many go barefoot inside the bowl, or in sandals of oiled leather that have to be re-oiled every few days or bloom white. Shoes, in the outside sense, are for the levee.

Generations have lived here, long enough to train the first figs on their iron frames, long enough for aerial roots to become buttresses and for the buttresses to be load-bearing. That is written into the families. A house is not owned so much as occupied in succession; you inherit a wall that is still growing, a walkway your grandfather bound, a painted subsidence mark on the pumphouse door that your mother checked before you. People marry mostly inside the Combine, or with the garden families up on the levee rim, and a wedding is marked less by paper — paper softens, swells, molds at the edges within the month — than by the starting of a new wall. Two frames are set, young figs planted and tied. In two seasons, if they take, there will be a place to hang a door. Courtships here have a literal growing period. So do feuds. You cannot move out in anger. You can only begin, slowly, to train an elsewhere.

Work is the town, and the town has two priesthoods who do not call themselves that. The pump engineers come first in any formal listing, because if the two big diesels stop during the late-summer flood pulse, the wells lift together and the lowest sheds go dark from beneath and then under. They work in the bass thump you feel in your soles within a hundred yards of the mill, they communicate a surprising amount in gesture and lip-reading, their hearing goes early in the high registers, and they keep their tools, their valve leathers, their own boots, in a state of continuous greased preservation against the wet. They check the marks, they judge by an extra inch in the canal at breakfast what the river outside is doing without needing to climb the levee.

Equal to them, though quieter, are the pruners. They are out at first light on ladders that lean against living walls, men and women both, some of them well into their sixties with forearms roped from climbing and saw work. They thin a leaf roof so a lane gets light again. They cut back the root that is lifting a bridge, and they know — and this is law, first unwritten and now very written, painted in the mill office where the paint has to be renewed — which root may never be cut through because it is holding up a dormitory. Children learn those roots by name and prohibition the way other children learn roads. The pruners decide, in practice, how big a family can become and where the new pipe run can go. Nothing is cleared. Everything is negotiated, season by season, with a plant mass that will grow through corrugated iron if it is not answered.

Around those two are the rest of the Combine in its layers: the decorticator crews who feed cane and reed into the grinding roar and come out deafened and sweet-smelling with fermented fiber; the sawyers cutting bamboo flat at fifteen feet; the trolley hands; the rope-makers stacking the damp finished bales under open sheds where they cure and give off a heat of their own; the repair shops for belt leather and machetes and pump valves; the laundry women who know that nothing put out between the blocks will ever get crisp and have developed an exact science of *dry enough* — aired after the midday bright in the central yard, beaten free of mold spores, folded with dried leaves that drink a little damp. The cookhouse and mess hall work from the one permitted hearth beside the boilers, and the woodsmoke is always with the steam. The market in the root-floored square in the late afternoon is run largely by older women, selling taro, greens, lengths of mill rope, kerosene and salt and machine oil brought in from outside, and dried fish, because the canals produce frogs and hyacinth and algae in abundance but not much that people eat.

They eat what keeps. Taro from the wet intervals, cane shoots, greens that like shade, fermented fiber-pit byproducts that smell sweet and slightly sour and cling to the hands. Fresh things spoil persuasively fast. Salt is wealth. Oil is wealth. Their teeth are kept busy; their stomachs are used to water that is boiled as a matter of unthinking routine, drawn from wells only four feet deep and brimming, never trusted clear. Illness here is rarely dramatic. It is chronic and environmental: the fungal bloom in the folds of skin if leather or cloth stays wet against it, the cough that settles in the chests of children in the stillest weeks under the awnings, the lungs — dogs do not thrive here for this reason, and neither do some people, and those families quietly trade up to the levee gardens. Mosquitoes raise welts that everyone stops noticing by age ten. Snakes found coiled on warm pipes in the morning are not a panic; they are carried on a long pole to the rim and released, because killing is less useful than relocating and because everything here, person and animal, is understood to be seeking the dry warm place.

Social knowledge is stored where paper cannot go. In painted marks and notched posts. In the names of lanes — Pump Lane, Dry Shed Cut, Second Canal — which are directions as much as addresses. In the memory of where a bridge presses down when the hyacinth mats rise with the flood, where to step in the market when the root mat is slickest, what a fig trunk does when it weeps clean water in the morning against a warm wall. Old people are the archive, and they are listened to with a practical respect that has nothing sentimental in it; if an old pruner says that wall will not take another story, the dormitory does not get another story. Children grow up fluent in a vocabulary outsiders would find excessive: a dozen states of damp, from a shirt aired to leather on the turn to floorboards gone dark from beneath, each with its consequence and its remedy. They can tell by sound at night, during the brief midnight pause when the mill shuts down, how many frogs are in the canal and therefore how high the water is.

They are not isolated by ignorance but by gradient. Twenty feet up the embankment and the air moves, clothes dry, the river is visible. Traders come down, a heron comes to the settling pond behind the mill, occasionally a new engineer or a bride is brought in and spends a month perpetually damp, perpetually green-stained, learning not to fight the pace. Some leave that way too. Most who are born in the bowl stay, not because they cannot climb the track past the banana stands, but because their competence is so specific and so total here — the knowledge in their feet, their hands, their lungs, of this one saturated grid of canal and fig and machine — that outside it feels thin and wastefully dry, loud in the wrong ways, built without negotiation. At dusk they go home across the little bridges with trousers rolled, lights coming on behind leaves, the steam blast trembling every loose leaf, the pumps taking up again underneath everything. Their windows glow inside the vegetation, not on it. They too are lit from within a denser green, held, maintained, and slowly thickening.
