The people here are quieter than the place requires, and the place already requires very little noise.

You notice it first in how they move. Nobody strides. On boardwalks a hundred meters long between one cluster of houses and the next, they walk in a loose, unhurried single file even when the walkway is wide enough for three, keeping to the tested center line out of habit, boots placed, not dropped. They pause without being asked when a section of planking changes color. A darkening from underneath, a new sheen, a faint sighing through a crack — they will stand and look down at it the way farmers in other places look at the sky. Then one of them will tap the rail twice with the back of a knuckle, testing heat as much as sound, and they will go on, or turn back, without discussion.

Touch metal with the back of your hand first. Never step off a marked path, however solid the crust looks. Shake your boots in the morning. The rules are not posted much. They are in the bodies.

They are born to thin air and thick heat, which should not coexist, and their bodies have made a negotiation neither altitude nor lowland bodies quite understand. Deep chests, slow at rest. A way of breathing through the mouth in the steam districts and through cloth in the salt districts, without seeming to think about either. They sweat constantly and drink constantly — not from taps, exactly, though there are taps, but from bowls and bottles left under condenser fins, water the machines have cleaned by cooling. In a kitchen the good water is the dripped water. It tastes faintly of iron and of nothing. Roof water is for washing. Pool water, however turquoise, however temptingly clear in its rim of beaded sinter, is never for anything human. Children learn which blues are beautiful from the boardwalk and which blues would take the skin off a hand before they learn to read.

At noon they vanish.

It is not laziness and it is not quite custom. The light comes straight down at altitude and straight up again off the salt, and the heat meets in the middle at head height. Eyelids are not enough. So midday belongs indoors, under the wide roofs, behind salt-blinded glass and lime-washed walls gone streaked within months of painting. The town then seems uninhabited except for the drip under the eaves, the tick of condensation into gutters, the subterranean turbines you feel more than hear through a floor set on stilts.

That is, in fact, when much of the social life happens. Schools hold their lessons in shaded courtyards then, children cross-legged close together because voices in saturated air carry strangely — far, but flattened, intimate and public at once. Nobody shouts across a flat. You walk up to someone if you have something to say, and you stand nearer than strangers elsewhere would stand, and speak lower. It has made them, to an outsider, either very reserved or very familiar, with nothing in between. On the open boardwalk they may pass with only a nod. Inside, at midday, an aunt will mend a shirt two inches from your face and tell you, in the same even tone used for weather, exactly which of your cousins is dying.

Work is crepuscular and nocturnal. At dawn and again in the long evening, crews are out in loose, pale clothing — long sleeves despite the heat, because sun and steam burn differently and both have to be kept off — broad hats, veils of fine mesh that go salt-white at the edges, gloves worn thin at the fingertips from testing surfaces indirectly. Brine-yard workers go out under floodlights at night when the air is only hot, tending evaporation sheds and centrifuge halls that are mostly automated and need, therefore, a particular kind of human attention: not labor so much as listening. Listening for a pump whose note has gone grainy. For a vent that should be breathing on a cycle and isn’t. For new water where there was dry crust yesterday.

The oldest families can read the basin like a slow text. Their houses sit now in shallow mineral bowls because the terraces have grown up around foundations laid by great-grandparents, sealing them in centimeter by centimeter. There are bathing steps, grandmothers will show you, that descend into dry white stone. There are pools that have migrated a street over in one lifetime, boardwalks rerouted three times around them, a power station rebuilt three times on the same pad because the boreholes wander underground like roots seeking. Nobody here talks about permanence with a straight face, and nobody talks about leaving, either. Impermanence has become a form of tenure. You maintain. You re-lime. You move the path. You let the sinter have the old steps and cut new ones where the water has decided to be kind this decade — warm rather than scalding, mineral rather than alkaline, a place to sit to the waist in the evenings while the bacterial mats downstream glow orange and green and the brine flies hang over them in silent clouds.

No green thing grows, so food comes up the switchback road in electric trucks, and that dependence is simply unremarked, like the dependence on condenser water. There are market halls smelling of dried things and imported fruit going soft too quickly in the humidity. There is protein from the cooler salt ponds, tiny red shrimp eaten crisp and salty; there are stories, disbelieved by children, that the first surveyors ate the flies. What the town exports is not food. It is power from underground steam, lithium and borax and sulfur from the brines, and drinkable water manufactured out of poison and heat. The people live, in that sense, the way the waders live: not at the bottom of the food chain but deftly along its edges, taking what the mats and minerals make possible and never mistaking the source for something tame.

They share the basin, without sentimentality, with everything else that has learned the same rules. Lizards the color of wet ash on the hot railings, moving aside for a hand without hurry. Black beetles queuing under cold pipes for condensation, each displaced drinker going patiently to the back of the line — a rotation old residents point out to newcomers with a flicker of recognition, not quite a joke. Pale moths at dusk flying low over warm ground. Thousands of pink-tinged waders lifting in the evening in one slow, soundless sheet and resettling on the cooling ponds. The humans are another shore species among them: long-legged on their stilts and boardwalks, picking their way, rarely calling out, gathering at the water margins at the tolerable hours.

Children are recognizably children here in one respect: they test edges. But their games are played on known boards, in courtyards, in the maze of pipes and trestles where a cooler drip can be caught on a tongue. They know geyser time the way other children know clocks — twenty minutes, a soft exhalation two streets over, a rain of warm droplets on a roof — and they can tell, as their parents can, the smell of a safe seep from a bad one, iron and warm bread from sulfur and rot. Boots by every door are turned upside down on pegs against beetles. Bowls by every door are right side up against the drips. Those two gestures, performed without looking at bedtime, are as close as the town gets to a creed.

In the evening the whole basin exhales and so do they. Steam columns rise from canals and vents and rooftops. Lights come on in separated clusters across the darkening salt, each district its own small constellation with long dark between. People come out onto porches raised above the sighing ground, sit close, speak low, watch the birds rearrange themselves on the shallows. Sweat hits the planks. A sled glides past on its rail almost without sound. Somewhere underground the turbines take steam from the rock, and in the brine yards the night shift is beginning, and a grandmother is telling a child that the pool at the end of their street was once, in her girlhood, at the end of another street entirely, as if that were the most natural story in the world to inherit.

It is.
