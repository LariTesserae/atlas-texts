There is nothing here that would step forward to meet you.

What there is, at first, is only tenancy at the margins. In the lee of every third or fourth wall, where the shadow lasts and the wind comes apart, the rosettes live. Ash-grey, thick-leaved, edged in white spines too dry to be called thorns, they do not so much grow as *hold on*. Their roots are not roots for water. They are wedges. They pry into the basalt, grain by grain, and worry loose a mineral damp that was locked in the stone when it cooled. They open at the false dawn without turning to any sun you could point to. They fold at dusk with a movement so slow you doubt it until the leaf that was flat is sealed. In a week they have crept a hand's breadth along a wall, leaving the stone paler behind them, as if licked clean.

The lichen lives more openly and less understandably — mustard rings on the cold faces of the houses, expanding when the temperature falls, tightening almost to nothing in the held, grey noon. The moth lives in one roofless house, or seems to; its wings are the colour and texture of the dust on the floor, opening and closing, opening and closing, with no panic and no flight. The lizard the length of a hand has a translucent throat and watches from a crack that, the next day, is no longer in quite the same wall. There are three-toed prints that cross a lane and end not in a scurry or a burrow but abruptly, at solid stone.

They are not the inhabitants. They are what the inhabitant permits.

Follow any of them long enough and they betray this. The rosettes turn together, a fraction, toward a warmth that is moving *through* a wall. The lichen rings thickest where new stone has risen, still unb leached. The prints do not end at a wall because something walked into it. They end because, in the night, a wall arrived where the walking had been. The small lives here are not living in houses. They are living off a process, the way flies live off a carcass or birds off a whale — picking at edges, gleaning cold, following heat.

The resident is the town.

Not a spirit of it. Not a builder hidden under it. The town itself, considered at the speed at which it actually moves.

It has a body, and the body is the bowl: two hundred paces of young, unsettled ground still heaving itself into existence. Its skin is porous basalt and scoria and salt-pale clay, plum-black and iron-red, dusted white. It drinks sound into that skin, which is why your boots make no crunch and a dropped pebble ends with a felted *thud*. It drinks water the same way, instantly and without trace or darkening, not because it is thirsty in any animal sense but because water, pulled down between grains to where the cold still lives, becomes part of its nightly machinery — freezing, expanding, lifting.

That lifting is its heartbeat, slowed past recognition. Every night the ground rises under the dust in sheets and teeth. Stones shoulder up from below, fresh-edged, dusty from the dark, not yet greyed by the flat light. Frost grows out of shaded walls not as dew settles but as white hair, directly, stone exhaling stone-cold vapour, then giving it back to the air by sublimation before noon, drier for having breathed at all. Under your palm a wall will thrum with thermal settling, stones easing a millimetre against each other — a shiver, a rearrangement while it cools. Warm patches linger on the walls where no sun has struck and no hand has rested. It is metabolic in the loosest, truest sense: cold in, stone up, pattern out.

For what it does with that energy is sort. Freeze and slope and a wind you barely feel move risen rubble into lines, and the lines hold, and holding, they become curbs and thresholds and spine walls and compounds inside compounds. Where heave is weak, the ground stays open, and you get a plaza. Where it runs in currents, you get lanes that no feet have worn. Slabs and dead stems and lashed mats blow in off the rim and settle across leaning tops, and you get roofs. It maintains itself with the stubborn homeostasis of any living thing: take a stone from a wall and by the next cold cycle two have risen near the gap. Build a cairn in a lane to mark your way and within days it has been elongated, given direction, incorporated. Let a house at the eastern edge slump sighing into gravel and already, nearer the centre, a new enclosure is pushing up, roofless, waiting for lichen to find it. The number holds — sixty, seventy — the way a body holds its temperature.

It migrates, too, imperceptibly eastward and downhill, shedding rimward houses like old skin and growing new ones at its leading edge. Its maps go wrong in a month. It digests what falls into it and organises what it cannot digest: a bootprint wells up full of dust from underneath and is gone; a dropped strap creeps a thumb's width overnight toward the nearest alignment; a mouthful of spilled water, vanished in an instant, marks the exact place where, the following week, a low line of stones will begin to insist on being a wall.

Inside the houses the stillness is different from the stillness outside, not emptier but held — floors swept clean by minute air currents threading doorless openings, no nests, no droppings, no hearths. These are not rooms waiting for people. They are the shapes its own circulation leaves behind, chambers and courtyards formed the way a shell forms chambers, by growth at the edge and abandonment at the centre. The beautiful basin in the plaza, fitted tight as teeth and never once wet, is not a well that failed. It is more like a mouth that has evolved past drinking.

And the lesser lives fit into it as precisely as gut-flora. The rosettes splitting basalt grain by grain are loosening tomorrow's building stone. The lichen softening north faces is preparing them to crumble back into sortable rubble. The moth and the lizard and the unseen three-toed maker feed on lichen, on spores, on each other, in such low numbers and at such long intervals that they never trouble the pattern that feeds them. They live by not drinking, by taking moisture from rock and vapour from sublimating frost, because that is the only economy the town runs.

It does not know it is a town. That is the final, uncomfortable refinement of it. The geometry is doing, without hands or intention or need, everything a settlement was invented to do — sheltering lee strips where tender things can root, raising windbreaks, clearing commons, renewing its housing stock, moving slowly away from exhausted ground. The resemblance is not a disguise. It is what happens when one restless piece of the world insists, night after cold night, on rising and sorting and rising again, inside a rim where the wind drops its loads and the light never quite arrives. Pattern, persisted in long enough, becomes indistinguishable from habitation.

So the houses stand occupied after all, each one full of the single tenant that built it by becoming it — growing, folding, flowering into walls, subsiding into lanes, and growing again in the blue granular dark, with no one to live in it, and no vacancy either.
