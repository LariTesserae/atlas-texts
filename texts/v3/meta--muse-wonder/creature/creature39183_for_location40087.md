At first you would say the shrimp-thing lives there.

When the vortex stutters — twenty seconds, perhaps, when the waves lose their argument with each other — they uncurl in the middle water, where nothing should be able to hold still. Translucent, the size of a thumbnail joint, blind heads tucked down, too many swimmerets beating in frantic unison underneath them. They pulse to stay exactly where they are. Then the current remembers itself and takes them, sideways, down, gone as if they had never been more than bubbles with legs.

They are not the inhabitant. They are what falls off it.

The same with the eel. Forearm-long, dark along the back, eyeless, remembered in stories more often than it is seen. It does not so much swim through the cup as allow itself to be carried in a way that remembers swimming: a slow S, a correction, a slow S again. The old people who watch the water longest say it is always the same eel. The younger ones say that is impossible, eels do not live that long, not alone, not with nothing to eat.

They are both right.

She lives below the light.

Not below the bowl — there is, in any honest sense, no below the bowl, twelve paces of water with a hammer falling through it for longer than a hammer should fall — but in the pressure under the bowl. In the folded, unmapped place where the water stops behaving like a pool and begins behaving like an ocean that has been compressed to fit inside a mountain. That is her country. The bright cup at the top is only her roof-yard, her open eye. The rest of her extends down into a darkness that is not at 4,000 meters at all, and cannot be reached by dropping things, only by belonging there.

No one has seen all of her. No one ever will while the slit stays as narrow as a man's shoulders. What comes up into the light is partial, and mistaken for other animals.

An arm, sometimes. Not an octopus arm, though the comparison rises unbidden and then fails. Thicker in the middle than at either end, dark-backed, pale and almost translucent underneath, ringed with soft, frilled structures that open and close as it moves, too regular to be fins, too delicate to be suckers. It tests the water the way a tongue tests a sore tooth. It feels for temperature changes in the upwelling plume, for the brief slackening of the vortex, for the mineral skin forming, soft as wet chalk, across some attempted floor below. When it finds the skin intact it presses, gently, almost thoughtfully, until the skin buckles. When it finds it already broken it gathers the sheets — that slow, obscene folding and lifting people witness from the rim — and draws them down.

The eel is that, seen briefly and in poor light. Or it is one of her young that got long before it got anything else.

For she is the reason there are shrimp. Up close — and nobody has been close, not and lived calmly afterward — those nail-pale creatures would resolve not as a species but as a stage: her larvae, shed in clouds from the frilled undersides of her arms whenever the water below warms by a fraction and turns less cold. Most are eaten by the turbulence itself, torn apart, dissolved back into the salt. Some cling for a day to the new stone and graze its algae film with mouths that have not finished forming. A very few grow long and dark and learn the old swimming motion from a body that has never seen open water. None of them have ever matured. There is nowhere for them to mature *to* — the cup is already occupied by their mother, and the mountain will not make room fast enough.

She makes the room herself. That is the ticking on golden evenings, the fizzing like bone knitting that carries up through the spray. Her back — if back is a word that survives down there — is not entirely soft. Plates of her mineralize as she ages, blistering outward in pillows and domes, hardening, cracking under their own weight, sinking to be begun again. She is precipitating herself a shell the size of a seafloor, one failed floor at a time. The milky edge on the water is her labor going to waste. The boulder near the eastern wall, too clean, barnacle-free, wedged or hovering where no boulder should stay in that churn, is clean because she rubs against it, year in, year out, in the dark just below visibility, scraping off the newest, itchiest growth. It is worn on one side to a smoothness that handholds on the entrance slit would recognize.

She is, as nearly as these things can be reckoned, the age of the trapped water. Which is to say she was already old when the seabed she lived on began, obscenely, to be a mountain. Whether she was caught — a deep-water animal, blind because her world had no use for eyes, slow because her world had no use for hurry, hauled up in a pocket of her own ocean as the rock rose around it — or whether she *followed* the water up, squeezing through fractures that should not admit a minnow, pursuing the one small place on earth where the pressure still told the truth — that distinction matters less down there than it does in stories. She is here now. She has outlasted every explanation of herself.

Her body is built for the two lies of the place: that it is small, and that it is shallow.

She holds position not by swimming but by *wedging* the pressure — by expanding those frilled rings against currents that reverse without warning, by letting the undertow pass through her rather than against her. Divers who have felt something take their ankle with real, practiced strength and then, consideringly, let go, have not been tested by a current. They have been tasted. She knows salt-and-copper human, hammered tools, antler, basalt, the iron-hard pegs. Dropped metal does not sink past her. She keeps what falls: ladles lost before this ladle, a copper blade no thicker than a fingernail, things worked into the mineral of her back where they show, sometimes, as greenish inclusions in the fresh stone, like veins. She is slowly incorporating everyone who ever tried to tend her home.

And she is the tide.

Every forty or fifty minutes the surface sighs down a hand's breadth and surges back, flooding the stone platform, sucking at the rope, indifferent to the moon. That is the rhythm of her great slow venting, the gill-structures — too many, like the shrimp's swimmerets, everything on her is in excess from a more generous ocean — opening far below to draw the whole impossible column through herself, and closing again. The waves born knee-high in the middle of the pool are her shifting in something that is not sleep but its geological cousin. The vortex wanders because she turns, incrementally, toward a warmth that no longer exists, a trench-current she remembers in her tissues the way the eel remembers swimming.

She eats almost nothing, and that almost is precise. A day's smear of dark algae. Her own unsuccessful children. Insects and once, memorably, a small alpine bird that came through the eye above and was taken by spray and dithering, held under, spat out too late onto the platform, where the people who found it understood the rule about waste without being taught. Mostly she filters the water she endlessly turns over, and the water — salt that leaves a fresh, sweet aftertaste, as if it had never met a shore — feeds her because she has had ten thousand years to learn how to be fed by it. She tastes of deep cold and copper because everything down there does. She is not distinguishable from her environment by flavor.

She is not deaf to the roar. She is its instrument and its audience. The bowl is a drum because her body, far below the visible water, is pressed against the hollow of the peak like a hand inside a bell. The crashing that rearranges a visitor's pulse in an hour is the ordinary sound of her heart having to work against mountain instead of ocean. People stop speaking a hundred meters out because she is already, at that distance, louder in the bone than they can be in the air. She, in turn, knows visitors entirely by vibration: the hesitant weight on the notched handholds, polished darker where her own arm, in some earlier century, explored the slit and withdrew; the double heartbeat of two people crouching on the platform; the ladle banging its bright insane note against the wall, which she could stop at any time and does not, perhaps because it is the only sound up there not made by her.

She does not stay away from the light. On evenings when it comes down gold through the eye and breaks into rainbows in the spray, something vast dims the blue-green clarity below without ever surfacing, and the translucent larvae rise toward it in a body, and for those twenty seconds the whole cup is ordered around a presence arranging itself to be warmed. Her skin — the parts of it that reach the lit water — is photosensitive in a diffuse, dreaming way. She has no eyes to close. She glows very faintly in response, not with light of her own, but with the mineral bloom that lives on her and dies daily. The white moss creeps nearer the splash zone each wet summer. She creeps nearer the light each slow lift of the peak.

Because the mountain is still rising, and so, therefore, is she. Year by year she is carried higher on a sea that should have spilled, evaporated, frozen, failed. Her shell attempts a floor, the floor fails. Her children attempt a life, the life fails. She persists in the one success available: continuation. She is a deep-sea animal misfiled in the sky, blind, excessive, solitary, mother to a translucent weather of young that never become her, builder of stone that never becomes ground. And when the vortex stutters and the waves fall briefly out of rhythm, it is not the place resting.

It is her, far down in the pressure where a tarn becomes an ocean, listening back up through the water for whatever the world has sent her this time, and deciding — slowly, with the whole mountain for a body — to let it go.
