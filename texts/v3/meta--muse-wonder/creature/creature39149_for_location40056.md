On the Table, everything that lives has learned the same bargain: spend almost nothing, and you may go on almost forever.

The bargain is written first in the ums.

From the rim they look like vegetation, then like a second ground laid a meter above the stone. Up close they stop resolving into a herd at all. A herd implies edges, movement, animals arriving and leaving. The ums have only density. Here they thin enough that you can pass between shoulders. There, fifty kilometers on, they stand so pressed together that their felt has grown from flank to flank in grey bridges, and to cross you would have to crawl under bellies through a warm, lightless undergrowth of legs, listening for a heartbeat that may not come again in your lifetime.

An um is waist-high, barrel-bodied, built low against a wind that rarely remembers to blow. Six legs when you count carefully, five when you don't, because one is often folded up into the belly — not lame, just resting for a century or two. The legs end in broad, splayed hooves, soft-edged with age, spreading very slowly under the weight above them until hoof and stone have negotiated a single shape. Lichen crosses that negotiation without noticing. It grows over the hoof, over the fetlock, down the crack in the slab, tying animal to continent in a pale green seam. To move, an um must first tear itself free. Most decide the step is not worth it, and postpone it indefinitely.

Their heads hang at the end of necks thick as your thigh, the face wide and blunt and mostly hidden. Two horns curve down, not for fighting — no one has seen a fight; the old skulls show horn worn smooth by rubbing against other horns, against stone, against the same patient spot for ten thousand years. The mouth below is small, lipless, almost prim, designed for closeness: for licking a groove fractionally deeper, for cropping lichen back to the colored skin of it, for taking in mouthfuls of mineral dust and working them with a tongue blackened by pigment.

The eyes are the hardest part to find and the hardest to forget. Pale, amber-grey, wet-looking, set deep in the felt on either side of the skull. They do not track you. If you stand long enough, one may begin to close against your presence. Botanists who spent three summers beside a single warm female recorded a blink that began in June and completed itself the following spring, the lower lid rising like a slow tide over an eye that had presumably finished looking at whatever it was looking at. What the ums see, on that schedule, no one knows. Their shadows — black, sharp, entirely ordinary shadows — graze and lie down and turn their heads with a freedom their bodies have surrendered, as if the animal's intentions now live a little outside it, getting ahead, practicing a life no longer being led at full speed.

The felt is not a coat so much as a country.

It grows continuously and is never shed cleanly, layer upon layer of fine grey-brown hair matted with rock flour, salt, shed skin, and time, until an old um may carry a hand's thickness of itself on every surface. Rain, if rain remembered the Table, would never reach the skin. Sound does not reach it either. Lay your ear to a flank and you hear your own blood first, and then, much later, perhaps, the deep interior weather of the animal: a gut shifting, a breath gathering somewhere behind the ribs like weather forming in another country.

Inside the felt lives everything else.

Ticks swell to polished chestnuts in the warm seams behind the horns, their mouthparts buried for decades, feeding at a rate that would starve anything impatient. Moths live out whole genealogies in the dark above the shoulders, laying eggs that hatch into caterpillars that eat felt and are eaten, very slowly, by the tiny white spiders whose webs span from hair to hair. The spiders are patient in the ums' own image: a fly blundering into a web may take a season to cross the distance to the spider's mouth, and the spider, watching it arrive like a falling star, has time across generations of visitors to consider the meal. Mites carve visible trails. Beetles make slow parishes under the belly. When an um dies — if it dies — the felt keeps its tenants for a while, cooled now, thinning, the moths emerging for one last dusk that takes years to finish falling.

For most of the herd there is no reliable difference between living and dead, and the ums themselves do not seem to insist on one. The air is too dry for rot, too thin for the big scavengers, too cold at night for decay to get a proper hold. A dead animal stands, held up by its neighbors, its felt still the color of the ground, its eyes dried to milky pebbles that nevertheless catch the light at evening exactly as living eyes do. Lichens climb higher on the dead. Their legs are colder. That is nearly all. Over centuries the bones inside may soften and settle, so that a corpse subsides a few centimeters into a slightly deeper stance, as if finally relaxing. Some of the oldest presences on the Table may be less animals than shapes that other animals are holding up, remembered in felt.

The living identify themselves rarely, and by heat.

Most ums are ambient — cool as shade, cool as the slab under them. But thread the lanes long enough and you will brush a flank that is warm. Not feverish. Deeply, startlingly mammalian, as if you have put your hand on a sleeper under many blankets. The smell changes: skin, salt, milk, old wool, something green from the mouth. The flies around that animal, motionless in the air a moment before, now seem to have always been exactly where flies gathering would be. Something in the animal is, by local standards, awake.

An awake um may take your entire visit to turn its head toward you. It may take your lifetime. Breathing speeds to a sigh every few minutes. The blood comes down into the legs. One folded hoof may begin, over weeks, to unfold toward the ground. These awakenings are not rare so much as un-schedulable; across forty kilometers of herd, at any given moment, some hundreds of animals are presumably in the midst of living at something like normal speed, eating seriously, mating, giving birth, dying — but distributed so sparsely in time that the plain as a whole never appears to change.

Birth, when the shepherds witnessed it, was a matter of leaning.

Two warm ums stand flank to flank for a season. Felt grows between them. Something smaller is eventually present in the shelter underneath, indistinguishable at first from a thickening of the mother's legs: a calf already coated, already standing, already old-looking in the face, nursing in bouts separated by months from a teat hidden in the belly wool. It may take thirty years to emerge fully from between the adults. By then it is the size of a dog and has never once lain down. Some calves grow up still partially embedded in the herd's continuous mat, fed by mouths that pass chewed felt and lichen pulp along a line of heads turned, each a fraction, toward the young. It is possible the herd does not reproduce so much as extrude new members at the rate the stone wears down.

They eat the Table itself, in the end.

The long parallel grooves in the rock are tongue-work. An um lowers its head — a project in itself — and licks the same line in the stone that its mother licked, and hers before her, back beyond any change in the species that the fossils bother to record. The tongue takes up salt, iron, the black and ochre skin of lichen, microscopic plants living inside the translucent upper layer of the rock. Crescent pits cluster where teeth have scraped. Generations have polished whole districts to a dark glass. What passes through an um comes out dry, fibrous, almost incombustible dung that accumulates in corky piles around the hind legs and becomes housing for beetles, then soil for cushion plants, then, centuries later, food again, chewed by a neighbor who has finally arrived at it. Nothing is wasted because nothing is ever finished being used.

Above them, the other citizens keep the same faith at different scales.

The kestrel-birds hang over the plain facing into winds that finished days ago, wings set in a stoop that proceeds at the rate of erosion. They feed, presumably, on the slow flies, on moths rising from the felt in multi-year dusks, on each other eventually — there are birds locked together mid-sky in old descriptions, talons closed, descending together across decades like a single falling emblem. Their shadows cross the herd in an afternoon and wake nothing.

On the ground, among the hooves, long low predators lie with their teeth in the throats of prey that have not yet completed dying. They are not statues. If you sleep beside one and wake for enough mornings, you can measure the jaw closing by the width of a hair. Their bellies rise. Their eyes, when the light is right, have moved. The ums do not object. Violence on the Table is just another form of standing still, prosecuted so slowly that victim and killer may both be mummified into the posture, still technically engaged centuries after any human witness gave up waiting for the outcome.

And people — people tried.

The hut-builders, whoever they were, were not conquerors of the herd but late arrivals to its metabolism. They slept in the lee of dead ums, cut felt with bone knives, strung looms with hair that took a generation to become cloth. They kept tallies they could not finish. The markers they left — one white stone, one black — stand beside animals that have moved, by those measurements, less than a body's length since the markers were placed, though the placed stones themselves have sunk noticeably into the rock. It may be that the last shepherds did not leave. It may be that some of the warm flanks you pass belong to shapes that began, very long ago, with fewer legs, and that the herd, which accepts corpses and calves and predators into its general standing, made room, and slowed them, and felted them over, until the difference stopped being important to anyone present.

That is the true population of the Table: not a species but a rate of living, shared by everything that stays. Lichen spreading in maps. A thumbnail cushion plant holding one waxy flower open through your whole expedition, pollinated by a fly whose approach is a rumor among the petals. Birds falling out of the sky at the speed of geology. Animals grazing a stone down grain by grain, drinking condensation from under each other's chins, hearing one another in notes so low and so widely spaced that a conversation might require the rise and fall of a small civilization to complete.

Stand among them long enough and they include you, politely, without moving. Your shadow goes on ahead, turning its head, lying down in the grooves. Your steps begin to arrive after you have decided them. In the lanes between the ums, brushing warm and cool felt, hearing nothing, you understand that they are not waiting for anything. Waiting implies an end. The ums have simply reduced living to the part that can continue — breath hoarded, hunger forgotten and remembered, a blink shared across a season — and then stood, horizon to horizon, being that, while the ordinary sun crosses an ordinary sky too quickly above them to matter.
