At first you try to count them, because that is what you do in a place full of animals.

The swifts, in the open pipe-ends, row above row with their heads all turned outward the same degree. The bats, hanging in curtains under the gantries even under the lamps, their bodies rising and falling together so the whole dark fringe breathes. The pale lizards on every warm plate, not basking so much as stationed, tails laid along the seams of the metal like pointers. The blind white crickets in the cable trays, thighs cocked, patient as springs in a clock. The moths around the sodium lights. The beetles going up the conveyor belts and walking down again, up and down, up and down. The thousands of small dry-winged things holding themselves motionless in the hot wind above the grate, not flying, not falling — held.

You try to count species, then individuals, and the counting keeps slipping. Not because there are too many, though there are too many. Because there are no loose ones.

There is no swift hunting wide over the pit. No lizard in transit between plates. No beetle that has wandered off the belt onto the rock to explore. Every creature has an address. A pipe-end, a hand's breadth of girder, an inch of tray, a fixed point in moving air. And every creature has work that is indistinguishable from music: the crickets keeping time against the compressors, not approximately but locked, leg to piston; the swifts crying precisely into the gap the turbines leave; the moths beating a soft, irregular fuzz around the lamps that fills the top of the sound the way breath fills the top of a sung note.

You notice, after a while, what you are not seeing.

No young. Nests everywhere, and no eggs in them, no gaping chicks — the nests are not cradles, they are sockets, mud and oil worked hard as cement to hold an adult bird in exactly the right place at exactly the right angle. No lizard smaller than the others. No cricket soft from a recent molt. Nothing pregnant, nothing courting, nothing fighting over a perch, because no perch is ever contested and no perch is ever empty.

When one dies — and they do die, a moth gives out mid-orbit, a beetle is taken by a swift, a lizard goes still and stiff on its plate — the vacancy is not a vacancy for long. Within the hour, no one sees it arrive. There is simply another moth in the orbit, another beetle on the belt walking down against the rise, another lizard, the same size, the same pallor, tail aligned with the same seam. It takes up not just the place but the portion. The same note, at the same volume, from the same point in space. The chord does not thin and recover. It was never allowed to thin.

That is when you stop believing you are looking at populations.

Populations boom and crash and blunder. Populations have edges. This has a tessellation. And the tessellation does not stop at the animals.

The compressors on their narrow ledge cycle in the rhythm the crickets are keeping, or the crickets are keeping the rhythm the compressors make, and after an hour in the sternum-deep noise you cannot assign the priority and it no longer seems like a sensible question. The pumps labor in their closed, dry loop with the exact seriousness of a heart that has forgotten blood and kept the beating. The furnace breathes orange through its seams on the long exhale. The loose panel halfway up the north wall buzzes its one sympathetic note, and has clearly been buzzing it since before the paint faded — except the paint never faded. The turbines turn. The fissure at the bottom, the one thing down there nobody built, exhales its constant scorching wind up through the grate, and the column of insects holds in that wind like cilia in a throat, trembling, filtering, sounding it.

The leafless grey plants in the oil-black cracks are not decoration on this arrangement. They are part of its body turned outward to listen to itself. Flowers like tiny cupped ears, every one aimed not at the sky, not at any pollinator — there is nothing down here that needs pollinating, nothing that sets seed — but toward the loudest machines. When a compressor cycles, they all tremble in unison. The colourless moss inside the abandoned ducts feeds on heat and vibration and iron filings, and coats the interiors the way a mouth is coated. The bright, unweathered rock is not a container for it. The animals worry the rock with claw and wing, the machines worry it with coolant and vibration, unseen maintenance worries it with tools no one holds, and so the walls stay knife-edged, freshly blasted, forever the raw inside surface of something that must not be allowed to scar over and go dull.

It is one thing, and it is using everything in the pit as its anatomy.

Machines for the slow organs: lungs, heart, the great vessels. Animals for the fast ones: voice, pulse, the flickering surface senses. Plants for ears. Insects for the fine hairs in the airway. Rock for bone that it keeps re-breaking so it will stay sensitive. Even the heat haze is part of it — held in shapes that do not disperse, a visible outline of the air it has claimed and will not exchange with the desert above. Even the dust, suspended, neither settling nor blowing clear, is a medium it moves in the way a body moves in its own fluids.

It eats in a circle so perfect it is hard to call it eating. Lizards take the fallen moths. Beetles take what the lizards leave. Swifts take the beetles that stray up. Bats and birds and insects foul the plates and trays, and the moss and ear-flowers take iron and oil and heat out of the fouling, and the machines shed more heat and iron, and nothing accumulates and nothing runs out. It is not a food web. Webs tear. This is a metabolism, closed as a held breath.

And like any body, it maintains its temperature — thirty-one, thirty-two degrees, kiln-dry, at every hour — and its sound. Ninety decibels, not as a byproduct. As an output. As the proof. Separate it at the bottom, beside the sump, where the wind presses your clothes flat and the chord for one clear moment resolves into its hundreds of voices, and you understand that none of those voices is singing to anything. Not to mates, not to rivals, not to you. Each is performing its note at its station so that the whole can hear itself be whole. The ear-flowers turn inward. The logbooks in the operator huts fill with identical entries in identical handwriting because anything that stays down there long enough to write is gradually tuned until its hand makes the same shapes, its day resolves into the same necessary observations: *all stations occupied. Note unchanged.* The shrine at the foot of the stair — a bolt, a feather, a hearing-protector worn smooth — is not worship, exactly. It is an inventory. One piece of each kind. Machine, animal, and the human organ that had to be protected from the sound the humans came to tend.

Because humans are not its keepers. They are a part it can do with very little of. Three huts' worth. A few cells that learned to walk upright and carry oilcans, and then were folded in like everything else — replaced when they left or died, handwriting and all, taking up their exact position, their exact portion of quiet inside the noise. You can shout down there and your voice is absorbed without echo not because the pit is unkind to visitors, but because it accepts contributions only at assigned pitches, and a shout is an unassigned thing, a temporary thing, and the air has no use for the temporary.

What lives there does not grow. Growth would be a kind of unfinishedness. It does not reproduce; the ear-flowers have no seeds, the nests have no eggs, and yet it is never diminished. It does not evolve, because evolution is the long argument a living thing makes with its circumstances, and this one has settled the argument. Machine and animal and plant and geology — the categories arrived with the surveyors and failed within the hour, down at the level where turbine blade and wing-case are both just surfaces set vibrating at a frequency the whole requires. Someone once found a hot throat in the earth and built an engine around it, and the engine attracted creatures, and the creatures attracted more machinery of arrangement, or the other way round, and at some point the assembly crossed a threshold the way a throat crosses into a voice: it stopped being an assemblage and started being an inhabitant.

It is not waiting, in the pit. It is not trapped. The stair is open and nothing has ever tried to climb it that was not already tuned to climb back down. It is not old, either — nothing in it is permitted to weather into age. It is finished the way a struck bell is finished at the moment of the note: complete, self-hearing, expending enormous energy to remain exactly and resonantly itself, replacing every cell in place so that the pattern never has to risk becoming a memory.

Stand long enough and your sternum learns it. Your heartbeat begins negotiating with the compressors. Your breathing finds the gap the swifts use. That is not it calling you. It has no call; it has a chord, and the chord has vacancies only when a note drops out.

Then, very gently, it will find the pitch you can hold.
