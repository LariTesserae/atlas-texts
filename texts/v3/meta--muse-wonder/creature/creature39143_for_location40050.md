From the rim, through the cloud, they look at first like any people who live close on water.

They pole their boats standing up, one foot braced against the gunwale. They carry baskets on their heads across the causeways when the thermal tide is down. They call — softly — from house to house in the mornings, mend roofs, beat wet reed flat with stones, hang the glowing jars out at dusk. Nothing about the shape of them is strange.

It is only after you have stayed a while, and your own eyes have stopped expecting glare, that the differences begin to accumulate, the way everything in the caldera accumulates: storey on storey, without anything old ever quite being removed.

They are a low-voiced people, not by temperament exactly, but by physics and by training. Sound carries too well over warm water at night and not at all under it, and half their lives are spent at the threshold between the two. So they speak near the ear, or across a canal in sentences left unfinished for hours. A conversation is not an exchange so much as a shared thread that two neighbours keep hold of while they work. They touch when they speak. They tap — on a post, on a boat hull, on the rim of a bowl — to say *I am here, I heard, wait, come down*. Children learn the tapping patterns before they learn all their words. Underwater, tapping is the language, and hand signs opened slowly in the green light, because a hurried hand makes a cloud of its own.

Their eyes are used to a world without edges. Born into silver-grey noon and bottle-brown depth, they squint painfully, helplessly, if they are ever taken up above the rim into true sun — very few are — and they can find a face in the submerged temple by algae-light when a visitor sees only gloom. They do not look for distance. There is no distance to look for: the rim is eight kilometres across and usually lost in cloud. They look down, and near. Their attention is vertical.

Everyone, to some degree, is a diver. Children swim before they are steady on a walkway, slipping from the reed houses into water that has never once been cold to them, learning to open their eyes in it, to follow a mooring rope down hand over hand into the dimmer storeys of their own house. Grandmothers descend by ladder through the floor to sleep in the heat of the afternoon with the internal waterline lapping at the hammock cords, their hair drifting. Men and women who work the lower town go down daily weighted with nets of stone, breathing through reed tubes tended at the surface by a child on a float — always a particular child, bonded for that work, who learns to read the tug on the tube the way you would read a pulse. There is no hurry in a diver. A hurried movement wastes the breath somebody else is keeping for you.

Their skin is almost never completely dry. It prunes and smooths and prunes again. Their feet are broad from pumice causeways and boat poles, their hands permanently supple, fingers strong from weaving reed while it is wet. Hair is worn cropped or bound tight with fibre, because loose it mats with moss spores and lake algae in a week. Clothes are little more than woven aprons and shoulder wraps, clay-smeared against the constant damp, and they smell — the people, the houses, the clothes are all one smell — of sulphur and crushed leaves and iron and warm bodies.

A family is not spread out across the town. It is spread down.

The same tower may hold five generations at once, if you count correctly. At the top, in the newest room, with the roof garden and the fruit barrel and the most air, are the youngest children and whoever has most recently given birth. Below them, floor by floor as the house rises with the lake, are parents, aunts, the makers and gardeners and stone-carriers in their working prime. Below them again, at the waterline, where the floorboards go dark and the tide comes in over the threshold twice a day, are the grandparents, who like it there and will not be persuaded up. And below them, in rooms kept flooded on purpose — reached by ducking under a clay-rimmed doorway with a fibre curtain, lit by a jar set in a wall niche, furnished with stone benches slick with copper kelp — are the ancestors.

The people do not say the lower town drowned. They say it is still inhabited, only more slowly. They go down to the temple to pray at chest-depth inside the great clay air bells because that is the level their dead were at when the water took their houses, and to pray above it would be to talk down to them. They carry fresh air down in bladders and vine-pods every morning, a chore as ordinary as feeding chickens, and breathe air that has been in other lungs, in the bell, in the flooded room, for a very long time. Family dead are not buried away from the house. They are settled into its lower storeys, weighted, seated, tended, their hair grown through with algae until a visiting grandchild must learn to recognise a face by touch and by the tapping pattern knocked, respectfully, on the wall beside it. New babies are sometimes first brought down, just for a moment, just to the waterline, and held where the warm lake takes half their weight.

So no one ever quite leaves a house, and no house is ever demolished. This makes the town incomprehensible to map and perfectly legible to them. A woman can tell you where she was born by putting her palm flat against her own tower at the depth where the water stood that year; the towers are ringed inside with generations of those marks, names and tide-lines cut together. An elder poling through the southern bay can point to a green island, solid enough now to bear a fruit tree, and tell you it was his uncle's garden raft, untended since the uncle went downstairs for good. People and places keep converting into one another at a pace too slow to grieve properly.

Their work is mostly maintenance against two opposite certainties: the water will rise a hand's breadth, and then another, and one day, in a few hundred years, the new black cone in the centre of the lake will break the surface and begin to take it all away again. They measure that cone every new moon with knotted cords, divers paying out line over glassy stone that cuts their fingers and ripples with white bacterial cloth. They record the measurement. They go on weaving the next room anyway. Building here is not optimism. It is habit at the scale of a species. Add a floor. Tie a raft alongside for a married son. If the lake takes it, it will become something else down there: a cistern, a fish trap, a cool room, a place for a grandfather.

Almost nothing they eat has ever been dry. Taro from submerged beds, beans that climb straight out of the water up the house posts, fruit from roots dangling through pumice into the lake. The one pale blind fish is not food; it is luck, a fellow resident, nearly as rare as the grey bird that comes down from the rim at dusk, at whose arrival whole canals fall silent. That scarcity of animal life does not seem to them like emptiness. The plants occupy that attention instead. They pole drifting mats back into place as you would herd something wilful. They untangle swimmers from kelp with great patience. They talk about the lily roots coming in through a wall, or a mooring post sprouting, in the tones other people use for cats.

At night, when the algae jars hang green along the canals and the steam rises through their light, the distinction gets harder to hold. The living town floats at the surface, lit. Directly beneath it, aligned street for street, is the dark town, warm, full of slow currents and sleeping divers' bubbles and ancestors at their proper depth. Between them, rising from a mountain that hasn't finished being born, comes the patient thread of air from below.

The people in the middle of it move, all their lives, up and down that thread a few metres at a time — born at the top of a tower, working at its waist, sleeping at its waterline, and settling at last into its roots — never leaving, only changing levels, as the water changes around them.
