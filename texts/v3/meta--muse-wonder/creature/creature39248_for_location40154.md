You hear the inhabitants before you learn to see them, because up here everything that lives has had to solve the noise first.

The people solve it by not quite living here. That is their arrangement. They come up the gravel grade in pairs and small crews, for two weeks at a time, and they stay in the row of low cabins by the power station, half-buried against the wind, their windows fogged every morning, their drying room thick with the steam of socks and boots that will never properly dry before the next shift. Then they go back down a thousand meters to farms and towns and trees, and another crew comes up to take their place in the beds. The station is inhabited the way a lighthouse is inhabited — continuously, by no one in particular.

So they develop a temporary species of human, adapted in the shoulders and the hands. Ear defenders worn around the neck like a second collar. Dark glasses on even under cloud, because the cloud is also bright. A way of talking that gives up on sentences and becomes pointing, miming, a palm held flat for *wait*, a thumb jerked toward the intake, whole conversations conducted on a whiteboard in a hut while the turbines go on shaking the pleasant ache up through everyone's knees. Outside they shout once, close to an ear, and if that fails, they laugh and don't bother. Their boots are wet from the knees down no matter what the boots promised. Their jackets are galvanized colors, international orange, hi-vis yellow, already freckled at the hems with green. They walk only on gravel and boardwalk, with the careful, almost guilty tread of guests who have been told that a footprint here is not a footprint but a ten-year injury.

Because they are guests. They know it in their bodies faster than the visitors on the bridge do — the ones behind the sign that says EAR PROTECTION, SLIPPERY, NO STOPPING, clutching the railing while the spray makes rainbows off their sleeves. The workers fill their bottles from the tap, not the pretty stream. They expect the river to rise by four with the day's melt. They do not kneel in the moss to listen to the water trickling underneath it. Nobody who stays more than a day kneels.

The true resident will not kneel for them either, or rise, or make room. The true resident covers everything.

It is a single, continuous skin of life, a few centimeters to half a meter thick, and it has no edge up here except where new black lava has been too recently poured or the power company has laid fresh gravel and is temporarily winning. Woolly moss in deep emerald drifts, sedge yellowing in the hollows, arctic willow pressed flat by centuries of wind into ankle-high mats, cushion plants stitched tight as upholstery and beaded, in June, with white flowers too small to see until you are almost crushing them. Cotton grass over the bog pools. Moss on the rocks. Moss climbing the concrete weirs wherever the spray reaches. Moss beginning, patiently, at the corners of the corrugated roof and around the feet of the pylons and along the damp rail of the bridge, undoing manufacture by millimeters a year.

It grows that slowly, and it never stops being wet. Step into it — which you must not — and it receives your weight like a soaked sponge, squeezes up clear brown water around your boot, and holds the shape of you for next summer, and the summer after that. It is fed from below and above at once: springs weeping out from under lava fronts, seep lines, rain blowing sideways two or three times a day even in sunlight, meltwater wandering because this land is too young to have decided on its rivers yet. When you do put an ear close, at boardwalk level, you can hear the resident drinking and draining in a small intimate trickle inside the great roar. The whole plateau is that sound doubled: rivers braiding and re-braiding over hammered tin flats, the fall throwing itself eastward, the tailrace throwing up its permanent white plume, and underneath, through moss, a thousand private movements of water going nowhere in a hurry.

Nothing up here hurries except the turbines, and they only turn at the exact speed they were set to.

The other animals have largely read the terms and declined them. There is food and shelter here only if you are very small, very flat, or very transient. A pair of ravens has taken the power station as a territory and works the roofline, shouting hoarsely down into the turbine roar as if delighted to finally meet a noise louder than their own. Ptarmigan in mottled summer brown flatten themselves into the sedge and trust entirely to stillness; you will not hear them leave, even if you almost step on them, because hearing has been abolished here as a useful sense. For an hour on a warm afternoon, in one sheltered hollow, midges find exposed skin and remind you that insects also signed the lease, on a seasonal clause. 

The rest are apparitions at the correct distance. Whooper swans standing white and improbable in far bog pools, too far away for their legendary voices to matter, silent as cutouts against the green. Sheep, bewilderingly, a thousand meters above their farms, small wet flecks on the horizon that seem to have walked up by clerical error. An arctic fox, seen last August, already passing into the tense of rumor — *was* seen, *might still be*, a story the two-week crews hand to each other along with the cabin keys. No animal builds a town here. No animal, except the moss, seems to have mistaken this for home.

And home, up here, would mean enduring what never changes and what never varies in brightness: 8 degrees at noon in summer with the cold still on your ears, light coming up off a thousand pools as hard as light coming down, midnight in June thinned only to a bluer noon, wind worrying the wires of the pylons until they sing, water rising predictably at afternoon and falling at dawn while the land itself thickens by less than a fingernail in a year. To live here permanently you would have to be content to be a surface — low, wet, slow, brilliant, holding water and releasing it, colonizing orange doors and concrete by not caring how long it takes.

That is who lives here. A skin of green over young rock, alive from edge to edge in a layer you could lift in your hand, entertaining on strict rotation a handful of deafened, dazzled, damp-kneed humans, two ravens, a few hidden birds, and otherwise — under the icecaps at either horizon, between the spill and the mist, in all that deafening, soaking light — magnificently continuing without anyone else at all.
