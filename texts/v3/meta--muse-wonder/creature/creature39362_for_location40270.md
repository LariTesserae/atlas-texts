Nothing lives there, in the way that moss or a keeper or a nesting bird would live there.

But something has taken up residence so completely that the room is no longer a room containing it. It is its body.

It arrived slowly, centuries ago, when the hot air rising from the sealed chamber below first caught the fired-clay lip, and the dome, the spiral partitions, the hundreds of hung and shelved vessels all fell, rank by rank, into agreement. It has not left since. It does not come and go with the weather. Snow cannot settle on it. Night does not quiet it. Wind tearing over the paved summit cannot make it turn its head.

It has only one voice, and it has never stopped using it.

That is the first way you know it. Not as a sound with a source somewhere in the walls — there is no bell to find, no reed, no string, no mouth — but as a presence with volume in both senses. It fills the chamber the way water would fill it, except it leaves it parched. It presses on the sternum. It gets inside the sinuses and makes itself at home behind the teeth. It puts a buzz in the skin between the ribs as if that thin skin were being played, too.

Its flesh is clay.

Globular pots, tall open tubes, double-chambered jars, shallow inverted bowls, bottle-resonators standing shoulder to shoulder five shelves deep — all of them are blurred faintly at the rims, all of them humming against their ropes and neighbors. Brushed by a shoulder, they give no clink. A touch disappears into them the way a touch disappears into a beehive through a wall. They are not its instruments. Instruments imply someone standing apart to play them. They are its organs, its ribs, its many small throats, and it is wearing them out by living in them: bright facets ground where vessel touches vessel, rims rounded down, shelf-edges scalloped, everything fretting itself by minute degrees into powder.

Its blood is hot air, drawn forever up from the fissure under the floor, blood-warm, dust-dry, smelling of brick and salt. Its skull is the dome with no opening at the crown, mica-eyed on the south-east side, letting in only a brown, tiered, late-evening light. Its gut is the spiral labyrinth that will not let anything approach it straight. Even its excretions are orderly. The ceramic dust it grinds from its own body does not lie where it falls. It gathers in curved ridges and crescent dunes along the quiet lines of the standing wave, bare polished brick along the loud ones. Scuff it, and over minutes the grains crawl back into pattern. The floor keeps being redrawn under you. That is its housekeeping. That is its handwriting, endlessly erased by being endlessly rewritten.

White salt blooms likewise in its lower courses, sweated out of the clay and crystallized in the nodes into maps so regular they look woven. When you move, they glitter all together. Now and then a crystal lets go and drops straight down through air that feels too thick to permit falling. It permits only what it has finished with.

It is fiercely, unconsciously territorial, and its territory is the whole interior. It allows no nests, no roots, no insects in the warm corners that would otherwise suit them perfectly. Warmth is not hospitality here. The tone empties the place by occupying every frequency a living thing might need in order to settle — empties it the way a bell, rung without pause for a thousand years, would eventually empty even the bronze of anything but ringing. Birds do not try the eaves. Seeds find no soil in any case, only brick over brick over vanished rock, but even a seed that found a crack would find no stillness in which to split.

Loose things learn its laws quickly. A copper disc dropped on the floor does not rest where it lands. It skitters, half-lifted, hunting, until it discovers a quiet line and settles there, ringing faintly, adopted. Dust does not drift; it hangs in trembling membranes, held in planes. A human body is merely a larger, softer obstruction. The wave parts around you and rejoins behind you, and if you attend you can feel your own acoustic shadow, a small lessening at the small of your back, the one cool-feeling place on you. You are not being addressed. You are being routed around.

And yet at its exact center it has a hollow.

Not a flaw. The builders — who knew air the way other builders know load and span — tuned the spiral, the partitions, the diminishing ranks of older, smaller, blacker vessels toward the middle, until the pressure that has been mounting in your chest step by step simply cancels. A column no wider than a standing person's shoulders, at head height, where wave meets wave and erases itself. The floor still trembles through your feet. Your molars still know. The dome still works overhead like a drum skin. But in that column the dust hangs absolutely unmoving in a single shaft of mica light, and you can hear, obscenely loud, your own breathing, your own heart.

That is the closest thing it has to a face, and it is empty.

Whether it is where the resident listens, or where it cannot reach, or the one courtesy it was built to extend — a place for a person to stand inside the loudest thing in the world and be, briefly, only themselves — there is no inscription to say. It does not speak there. Speech is impossible everywhere else in the chamber; a voice goes into the skull and stops. In the center a voice would work, but no one stays long enough to use it much. The ache behind the eyes accumulates. The mouth dries. Standing inside that much energy costs the body something metabolic and plain, and the cost comes due.

It does not expel you. Expulsion would require noticing you. It simply remains itself at full volume all around your small cancellation, until you back out sideways through the humming pots and feel the pressure close again over your head like water over a stone lifted briefly out.

That is its whole biography. It does not hunt, breed, sleep, migrate, pray, or wait. It was not born; it locked in. It is not dead; it is sustained, hour by hour, by heat it did not make, in a body it is slowly grinding to dust, at a pitch the outside world cannot alter. One day, presumably, enough clay will have worn away, enough shelf-edges scalloped deep enough, that the agreement will fail and the pitch will falter and the dust will finally be allowed merely to fall. That day is not near, and it is not feared. Nothing in the chamber strains toward it or away from it.

On the platform outside, in thin sunlight, with no natural ground underfoot, you become aware that it is still sounding behind you — that it sounded all night, that it will sound all winter under snow that melts from its warm crown, over its sorted floor and its salt maps and its thousand vessels, in a room that is twelve paces across and too crowded for anything else to live in.

It has the place to itself because it *is* the place, still happening.

It is not waiting for anything. It has arrived.
