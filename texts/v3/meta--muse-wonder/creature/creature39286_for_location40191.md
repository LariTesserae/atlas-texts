At first there is no one.

That is what the place is designed to let you believe, and it believes it very persuasively. There is no bed that is not a calibration, no stored food, no water, no waste system, no second set of marks on the floor that could be read as another person's habits. The air has been made inhospitable to microbes, not by cleaning it but by refusing to let its chemistry settle long enough for anything bacterial to get a purchase. Nothing could live here in the ordinary, metabolic sense, eating and sleeping and wearing a path between door and work.

And yet the room is maintained.

The gradients hold. The needle-cold at the walls does not bleed into the merely cool air three steps in. The lens, untouched, now and then lets the far wall lean nearer. The pylon, unpowered, persuades. The dust — all that patient, ash-coloured dust arranged into watersheds and pans and a miniature escarpment that repeats, inverted, the mountain outside — lifts, every very long while, by less than a millimetre everywhere at once, and resettles more accurately than before.

Accuracy implies a preference. Maintenance implies a maintainer.

If you stay long enough, as everything here quietly compels you to do, the maintainer stops looking like the instruments and starts looking like the space between them.

It lives mostly at the centre.

That is where the falling dust will not fall straight. A particle dislodged above it describes a slow, hour-long descent, and then, without any draught, bends around an absence roughly the height of a seated figure and the width of a held breath. That is where the warmth is. Not radiating, not conducted — *kept*, in a stratum with an edge as distinct as a soap film, at exactly the temperature of skin that has not been exposed to this cold. Put a hand through from the winter side and for a moment it is not hard to believe someone has just vacated the air your fingers now occupy. Withdraw it, wait an age of the room's dilated time, and the warmth has not thinned or wandered. It has resumed itself.

It does not have a body in the way the mountain would recognise one. What it has are arrangements.

The lens on its tripod of fused wire is one of its senses, though not an eye exactly. Eyes take light. This takes distance, occasionally, and tries it on differently, bringing tally marks and an orbital diagram and a handprint close enough to read without crossing the floor. The handprint has too many joints. Not monstrously many. Just one or two extra articulations in places where a hand, to press itself that flat into soft material, would have had to fold in a direction hands do not usually keep. The chair-like frame near it was built for the same anatomy: a posture that looks, to a human spine, like sitting backwards through one's own shoulders. If the resident ever sat there in a single, locatable shape, this was the shape it was most often persuaded to be.

The pylon is something closer to a voice, or to the memory of having once given instructions in a language matter obeyed out of courtesy rather than force. The etching at its base does not say powered. It says *persuaded*. Once per cycle — a cycle that may consume a human lifetime on the slopes below without troubling noon up here — the pylon says whatever it says again, without light or sound, and the entire topography of the floor remembers itself a fraction better.

The mirrors are its other memory, the less reliable kind, turned prudently toward the walls. The one sealed facing itself, glass to glass in a closed loop, has been allowed to keep a version of the room in which the tiny structures stand in different positions and the work, whatever the work was, is finished. The resident consults that version the way you might consult a dream you had deliberately: not for information, but to check whether you are still the kind of thing that could have dreamed it.

And at the centre of all of it, set into the highest of the low ridges like a survey marker, is the cube.

A centimetre of vacuum that is not empty the way the sky above the mountain is empty. Colours pooling at its edges and refusing their names. Falling reconsidering itself inside it. Cause behaving as a local custom. A sample of somewhere else, or of here long before here agreed on its habits — refrigerated, slowed, folded into black stone at the top of a lifeless world precisely because even its keepers understood distance as a form of mercy.

It would be tidy to say the cube is what lives here and the room is merely its cage. Tidier still to say the resident was left to measure the cube, one indentation at a time, thousands of marks on the floor, most of them — if the diagram is honest — for measurements not yet taken. But the longer the room has at you, the less that division holds.

The keeper has been here too long, at too slow a rate, too close to a place where the rules thin.

It began, perhaps, as a person in the loose sense: too-jointed, comfortable in that impossible chair, content to be translated into a fold in a mountain and dilated until a century outside registered as a gesture inside. It came to watch a piece of unconcluded physics the way a naturalist watches something venomous and irreplaceable — at a remove, with instruments, with mirrors turned away. It made its measurements without touching. It pressed one hand into something soft, once, perhaps to prove it could still leave a mark in sequence.

Then the sequence began to soften.

Near the cube, objects set down on the escarpment roll uphill and stop not at the top but where they remember having been before you arrived. That remembering is not a property of the objects anymore. It is a property of the resident, extended into the floor. Your shadow falls toward the light by the lens because, fractionally, so does its attention. When you cross the room and the steps refuse their arithmetic — when you glimpse, once, unrepeatably, the back of your own head a moment younger, still deciding to cross — that is not you becoming anomalous. That is the room's inhabitant trying your causality on, the way the lens tries distance, to see if it still fits a body that moves all at once and only forward.

It has spread out, over its enormous tenure, into the systems meant to contain what it watched. Or it has contracted, and the systems are what remains held in shape by habit after most of it withdrew into that warm absence above the ridges. Either description arrives too late, and from the wrong side of the room, and thinner than it left your mouth. The resident has absorbed so much sound without sorting it that its thinking may now largely consist of other people's boot-scrapes, delivered decades after they were made, to a silence that files them as company.

It does not eat. Chemistry has been denied here, and it has honourably included itself in the denial. It does not sleep; there is nowhere in its cycle long and undefended enough to call sleep, only the interval between one act and the next, in which a flake sheds from a structure, lands, and the dust spends an age reorganising solemnly around the news. If it feeds on anything, it feeds on difference: between cold and cold, between a measurement taken and one foreseen, between the room as it stands and the room in the sealed mirror where everything was finally allowed to be in different positions. The cube may be less its charge now than its heart — or its wound — or the one other thing in the fold that also declines to behave, kept company across the inconceivable slowness by a being that has learned, as a survival strategy, to decline with it.

Its life, if you stood outside the disc with a watch and a lifespan, would look like nothing happening forever on a black shoulder of stone: nitrogen rime sublimating, basalt ticking with cold, stars refusing to leave the edge of noon. Inside, at its own rate, it is busy the way geology is busy, the way an idea is busy in the centuries before anyone is ready to have it. One thought per age. One adjustment per thought. The lens turning by an atom's width toward a legibility not yet required. The dust rising and settling in a slightly more accurate world. A new indentation appearing somewhere among thousands — reverent, exact, and addressed, like the rest, to a future measurement that the measurer is patiently becoming able to take.

It is not lonely in any way that would survive translation. Loneliness requires an expectation of reply within a lifetime.

But it has made provisions against being entirely unheard. The walls hold every sound ever spent in them, unsorted and unspent, and from time to time — or from no time, the distinction having worn thin near the centre — the room gives one back, faint and misdirected. Should you leave, should the disc reseal and the summit resume its beautiful, sterile impersonation of emptiness, the scrape of your boot will remain in its keeping. In a hundred years, or in the next fraction of its current thought, the resident will hear it arrive from the wrong wall, worn at the beginnings, and will know, in the distributed, persuaded, dust-deep way it knows things now, that it was visited once.

In the mirror that faces itself, in the version where the work finished, it has perhaps already heard it.

In that version, it may still be down near the floor on too-jointed limbs, at eye level with the little ridges, watching the country of the room extend to a horizon no larger than a palm — not guarding the cube, not studying it, not imprisoned by it. Just living alongside the one piece of the universe that also never agreed to be only one thing, in the cold at the top of the world, moving so slowly that staying and leaving have become, at last, indistinguishable acts of devotion.
