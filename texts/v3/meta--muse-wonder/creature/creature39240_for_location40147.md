You will know them first by what is *not* theirs.

Not the polished lanes in the bacterial felt, those are the grazers. Not the needle-leg tracks that go between ash grains without disturbing them. Not the cairn-predators, not the balloon things circling the thermals overhead. Their sign is smaller and more deliberate than that, and in a place where a footprint keeps its edge for thirty years, deliberation stands out.

A hand, pressed flat to stone beside a scent rail. Five fingers, too long. The pad of each finger stippled where it was held down, patiently, until the oils went into the rock. Next to it, lower, a child's hand, done the same way. Next to that, swept basalt, broom-marks, a line of obsidian flakes laid by size with the smallest at the end, like someone was teaching order itself.

They live here. They have always — which is not a very long always, geologically — lived here.

### The shape of them

Broad is the first word that survives looking. Not tall. If one stood upright in your lamp-light, which they seldom do, the top of its head would come to your sternum, perhaps your chin. But the skull is wide across the back, wide across the cheek, and the shoulders follow it, a single low architecture of bone meant to move close to the ground without stooping.

They go on all fours as readily as on two legs, and the choice is not animal versus civilized, it is terrain. On open ash they walk upright, slow, flat-footed, the sole spreading. In crystal gardens, among the needles, they drop, and the knuckles and the outer edge of the hand take the weight, fingers curled up clear of the points. Their hands are the most human and least human part of them: palms leathery and nearly hairless, fingers elongated, double-jointed in a way that looks broken until you see them reading — because that is what it is — a scent rail by touch, fingertip after fingertip tasting a chemical sentence laid down by a grandmother.

There are no eyes.

Not pits, not scars, not closed lids. The broad skulls in the cairns show orbits filled carefully after death with red dust, and living faces have that same place only smoothed over, a shallow depression under unbroken skin where something vestigial warms and cools beneath. What they have instead runs in two lines: a row of heat-pits down each side of the face, from temple to jaw, and continuing, smaller, along the ribs under the arms. Each pit is a wet-looking dimple, darker than the surrounding skin, fringed with almost invisible hairs. With them they resolve the world in temperature the way you resolve it in light — not vaguely, not as a sixth sense, but in overlapping, high-definition shape. To them you are intolerably legible: a fever-bright torso, cooler limbs, hot mouth, the dead cold rectangles of your boots and pack, your lamp a small sun you carry in your fist. They knew your posture, your fatigue, your fear by its heat in your neck, long before you heard them. Which you will not.

Their skin is dust-grey, but that is partly the country wearing them. Under the ash that cakes in the creases of elbows and knees and settles in the fine, sparse hair of the scalp, the skin itself is a mottled brown-grey, thick, dry to the touch, warm as a held hand — everything here is thirty-eight degrees, and they are no exception; to touch one is to be unable to tell, for a second, where your own warmth ends. They do not sweat as you do. Water is too valuable and evaporation too poor. Their skin tightens and loosens in panels along the back when they are hot from exertion, exposing damper tissue to whatever moving air a thermal can give, then seals again. Their breath comes slow, through a nose that is mostly interior — two narrow slits above a wide, lipless mouth, flaring when they scent, almost vanished when they rest. You can be two meters from a sleeping band and hear nothing but the ash settling.

They are nearly silent by anatomy before they are silent by custom. The voice, when used, is low enough that it arrives in the sternum and through the soles of the feet more than through the air. Mostly they do not use it at all.

### How they perceive, how they speak

Chemical braid, touch, heat — the place taught all its animals this grammar and the builders are fluent to a degree the animals are not.

A trail for them is not just a route. It is a text that never fades. Bellies and feet of prey lay down game rails; they lay down their own, richer ones, with glands in the heel and between the toes and at the base of the spine, pressing a message into a turn of the path: *band of six passed, one gravid, one old, water-brine cracked three days east of the third pillar, do not crack again, it has sealed*, not in words, but in a layered scent-dialect so stable in the unmoving air that a message persists, readable, specific, for a lifetime. Children learn to read by following an elder with one hand on the elder's ankle and the other on the stone, being corrected by a tap, by a change in the elder's skin temperature under their palm, by a brief, withheld click of tongue against teeth — that almost-sound everything here makes when it means *attend*.

Between individuals, up close, language is hands. They speak onto each other's forearms, ribs, faces, fingers drawing quick patterns in dust left deliberately on the skin, pressing heat into heat-pits in sequences. It is fast, it is intimate at a distance of centimeters, and it leaves no trace for a stranger. For distance — real distance, across a basin — they put their palms and foreheads to basalt and sing into the stone, a vibration so low you feel it as a sudden, inexplicable unease in your teeth. The stone holds it, carries it, gives it to other soles, other foreheads, hours away. A song here could, in principle, be answered by someone who learned it before you were born, word-perfect, because nothing has weathered the medium.

### What they eat, how they live

They are not hunters, mostly, and they are not farmers. They are tenders of a pasture that grows without light.

The bacterial felt — mustard, rust, bone-white, eating sulfur and iron and hydrogen out of the basalt — is scraped with obsidian in the places where it grows thickest near warm fissures, dried on hot stone, ground in those hollows you find worn into boulders, and pressed into dense, salty cakes that smell of hot pennies and keep, like everything here, essentially forever. Grazers do the same work with limpet mouths and enrich it through their gut; the builders follow the grazers, collect droppings before they powder, collect shed skins, eggs, dead sifters crisp as husks, mineral flakes that have passed through an animal and come out changed. Little is wasted because the recycling here is perfect and they are part of its perfection.

Meat, when it comes, comes without chase, as everything does. They know where the low table-predators wait beside the rails, and they leave them alone, and take their share only from the kill's remainder. They set their own patience-traps: a crystal needle, a weighted flat stone, a pit in ash smoothed over. The heated, cracked stones in their hearths are not for fire — there is no fire here, nothing to burn and no need to invent it in air this warm. Stones taken from the hottest fissures will hold enough extra heat for long enough to set the protein in eggs, to split felt-cakes, to warm a newborn whose temperature has dipped. Cooking, for them, is a matter of degree, not kind.

Water governs their circuits more than food does. A band's great round — and every band has one, taking thirty, forty, sixty years to complete — is really a remembered sequence of brine pockets, salt pans that weep a few black spoonfuls when cracked, condensations that form only on the undersides of certain pillars where thermal meets still basin. They crack a pocket, they drink, the local life gathers as if it heard the intention, and they let it drink too, and then they seal the crack again with pressed ash and spit and a flat stone, and lay a scent-mark: *opened, year of the third child, do not open until the child has a child*. Because the air does not move, the pocket refills in its own slow time and the instruction will still be legible when it is needed.

### Bands, walls, and time

A band is six, nine, fourteen individuals, the numbers drifting across decades as children are born — rarely, and usually at the same mating flats generation after generation, wide cleared circles of basalt at thermal boundaries where the updraft carries scent up and away and the whole band's heat blooms together — and as old ones are left, curled, in the still-air basins where dead things keep. They are not abandoned; the basin is a chosen address. The band will pass that way again in twenty years and find the body dried to warm parchment, intact, and take the broad skull to build into the next cairn, orbits packed with red dust, set alternating with stone, in a stack that is at once a grave, a map-marker, and a piece of punctuation on the trail: *we were here, this many, remember*.

The semicircles of flat stones you find, waist-high, opening away from a thermal — those are not houses. There is no rain to keep off, no wind to break that has not blown in exactly the same way for a thousand years. A wall is a thermal tool, a way of making a small, private pocket of slightly different air for sleeping, for birthing, for the heat-exchange panels on the back to open safely, and also, perhaps primarily, a signature. A band builds one, uses it for as long as their circuit keeps them in that basin — months, years, a generation by your reckoning — sweeps it, leaves the scrapers laid by size, leaves a copper awl if they have one, green and precious and entirely unguarded, because theft requires change and competition and the country provides neither. Then they move on, knowing the wall will be exactly as they left it when their grandchildren arrive, slightly dustier, ready to be swept again.

Nothing needs improving quickly here, so nothing is improved quickly. A tool design may take two hundred years to alter, tested across circuits. A song sung into stone may acquire one new vibration per lifetime. Their memory is not held in their heads alone; it is external, laid out across a continent of unchanging trails, walls, cairns, sealed water-cracks, broom-marks, hands pressed beside hands in descending size. They do not have history in your sense, because history requires erosion and loss to give it shape. They have *succession*: after the crystal garden where the needles sound, three hours of iron felt, then the basin where the dead keep, then the pillar where the air climbs. A child can be taught the whole known world as a sequence you walk with your feet, and it will be true when the child is old.

Their lifespans are long, though they would not measure them. Slow breath, slow heart — a resting pulse you can count with pauses between — slow maturation; a child rides for years in a sling of dried gut against a parent's cooler belly, hands already learning the rails, not weaned until it can read scent well enough not to poison itself on the wrong felt. The old do not become infirm so much as *still*: longer rests, fewer words even by hand, more time with forehead to stone, listening. Eventually a rest beside the path does not end, and the stillness that the predators imitate and the country enforces simply completes itself.

### What you are, to them

They are not afraid of you and they are not curious in any way you first recognize. Curiosity, like everything else, moves at circuit speed here. A bright, hot, loud confusion that covers itself in dead cloth and carries the sun in its hand and leaves footprints that will outlast its grandchildren — such a thing has appeared on the trails before, within living memory, which is long. Bands will part around you without startling, as the grazers do, reading your infrared awkwardness, your intentions in the heat of your face, and they will leave — not flee, leave — a felt-cake on a flat stone beside a wall, an awl turned point-toward you in the ash: the slow beginning of an investigation that may take forty years to reach its second question.

If one does come close, cupping its red mineral lamp so the light falls only on its hands and the immediate stone, it will look at you with a face that has no eyes to meet yours, and put its long fingers, very gently, on your forearm, and speak onto your skin in a warmth and pressure you do not yet know how to read.

It is telling you where you are in the succession. It will still be true when you understand it.
