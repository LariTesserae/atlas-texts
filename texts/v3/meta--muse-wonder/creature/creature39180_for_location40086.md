Before you see them, you touch them. 

You can't help it. There is no way to move in the Basin without touching, and they have made a whole etiquette, a whole physics, out of it. A shoulder that yields a fraction of a second before contact. A hip that turns, liquid, in a doorway that is already occupied. A hand flat, brief, unmistakable, on the small of your back — not pushing you, telling you where the current intends you to go next. They pass each other the way water passes water. An outsider stands still and becomes a rock in the river, bruised in an hour. A Basin-born body never really stands still at all.

They are smaller than you expect, and harder. Not stunted — re-proportioned. After three hundred years of heat that never fully leaves the air, of food from vats, of living five to nine layers up and as many down, the Basin has selected for compactness. Five feet, five and a half, lean to the point of stringiness, with long forearms. The forearms are the giveaway. They are muscled like a climber's, almost overdeveloped, because that is where the language lives.

They speak, but you will never hear them speak.

A child is born, as the midwives say, at full volume. The birth rooms are the only padded, deadened places in the city — you would think for the mother's sake, but it is for the infant's. For the first year of life a Basin child screams, sings, babbles, experiments with the outrageous luxury of sound, because its ears are still its own. Then the gate grows in.

It's not inserted. It's coaxed, a living valve of engineered tissue in the inner ear, seeded in infancy, tended by swarm-doses and sound exercises, that closes at about age two against the world. After that the 110 decibels at street level become a brown, felt pressure, a weather you sense in your sternum and teeth but do not precisely *hear*. Voices arrive only if they are meant for you, subvocalized by the speaker — mouth making small, tight, almost invisible shapes — and carried by bone conduction through a touch: fingers to a jaw, a wrist pressed to a wrist in a crowd, a forehead leaned for a half-second against another forehead between market stalls. Intimacy and intelligibility are the same gesture, here.

So they sign. Constantly. With both arms, from shoulder to fingertip, a rolling, expressive semaphore that can be read twenty meters off across a plaza or in peripheral vision in a transit throat. Old people sign with a beautiful economy. Teenagers sign so fast and so large it is a kind of dance, showing off, taking up room that does not exist with grammar. Lovers sign small, inside the space of a single embrace. Someone arguing in a canal barge will be silent to you and violent at a distance — whole torso involved, coat flapping wetly.

If you get close enough to look at a face — and closeness is not rude, rudeness is something else entirely — the adaptation is everywhere, none of it dramatic, all of it cumulative. Skin that has never once been completely dry. It is pruned at the fingertips as a permanent condition, slick and toughened on the palms and soles, prone to a pale, harmless bloom of symbiotic mold in the creases that the cleaner-swarms keep trimmed to a faint, pearly line. They smell, all of them, faintly of yeast and hot iron and other people. Individual scent vanished as useful information a century ago. Hair is kept shaved or cropped to a wet cap, or grown long only as a status display by people whose work guarantees them swarm-grooming all day and who can afford the risk of rot. Ears are small, sealed-looking. Eyes are deep-set under a heavy brow of lash — real, bred, and prosthetic lashes mixed without distinction — to shed the daily rain. Some of the down-level families have a translucent second lid they will deny is anything but a squint.

They are never dry, so they do not dress for dryness. Clothes are a second, negotiable skin: close weaves that hold a layer of water against the body for cooling, that can be wrung out and will be wet again in five minutes anyway, that change color with temperature and contamination and advertise, crawling, what building the wearer is currently part of, what shift, what address-trajectory. Pockets are sealed bladders. Nothing is made to be kept. A Basin dweller owns almost nothing that cannot be carried against the skin, because their room will not be their room tomorrow and they know it in their bones in a way you cannot learn as an adult.

Ask one where they live and they will not point. They will sign a curve in the air, tap a wrist implant that most of them also treat as jewelry, and subvocalize, through your borrowed contact, a probability: a district, a flow, a building that is currently moving canal-ward at three meters a day and is expected to trade its third floor with a clinic on Thursday. Their mail finds them. Their lovers find them. Their children, raised largely by district and by shift-creches that migrate in convoy through the dormitory warrens, find them often enough that the concept of *lost* means something temporary and technical, like a dropped signal.

They navigate the way the old women predict markets: by current. A Basin dweller reads a street the way you read a face. The smell shifts — ozone means fabrication is condensing upwind, sweet rot means you're drifting down-level toward the mole pens, clean steam means the Sump outflow is near. The underfoot texture of groomer-mites changes by neighborhood, bred to different roughnesses. The sound, even gated, arrives through the feet as a map of pump-load and crowd density. They know, without looking, which bridge will still be there after the rain, which roof tolerates foot traffic, which hollowed building-street is about to decide to be solid again. They plan their whole lives — work, sleep, courtship, mourning — around the two o'clock drowning rain, the nightly reversal of the canals, the four percent of the world that will be unmade while they sleep. And they sleep, most of them, in pieces: two hours in a hammock slung over a transit lane, waking with their ears cleaned by swarms and their skin checked for lesions; an hour in a family pile in a room that is warm with other breaths; twenty minutes upright in a transit throat, gate dimmed, lulled by the bass through their bones. Unbroken eight-hour solitude would be, to most of them, a form of sensory deprivation torture.

They are not unhappy about this, which outsiders find the hardest part.

They are proud, obscenely proud, of the number. Ninety billion, more or less — the *more or less* is argued in signing so broad it stops traffic. Proud of the density, of the churn, of the fact that there are no plants and yet everything is alive, that pigeons would go deaf and they did not. Their food is vat-meat that has never breathed, algae paste from sealed farms, eel from the canals, protein skimmed from the guts of filter-kites — and they season it fiercely, competitively, with engineered yeasts, because flavor is one of the few portable luxuries. Their pets are wall-runners trained to sleep in a particular sleeve. Their holy place is the Sump, where the noise becomes so total the gates cancel it to an artificial silence, and they go there — whole floating families, mole-handlers on pilgrimage with their great blind beasts muzzled and garlanded, teenagers trying not to show they are frightened — to sit in the steam and experience, for once, something like quiet inside their own skulls. Some weep. Some pray. Some simply think a thought from beginning to end for the first time that week.

Privacy, for them, is not being alone. It is a dampening: gate tightened, swarms politely redirected by pheromone signal, neighbors signing with averted forearms. You can be naked, grieving, giving birth, dying, in a room with eleven other people, and be accorded a perfect, untouched privacy if the courtesy is invoked. You can also be fully clothed in a crowd and be, deliberately, shatteringly public. They find the outsider idea — a door, a lock, an empty room held empty for hours — obscene in its waste and a little terrifying. What would you *do* in all that unoccupied volume? What would rush in to fill it?

Because something would. Something always does. Death is handled with the same unsentimental tenderness as everything else: report it quickly, because a body is volume and volume left unclaimed for fifty-eight minutes belongs to the substrate. The swarms will begin without malice; the moles will find what is not reported. So they have wake-keepers, children and grandparents mostly, whose honored job is to sit with a corpse and keep touching it, keep it *addressed*, for the hour it takes the family-trajectory to converge. Then it goes to the reclaimers, to the vats, to the building of somebody else's tomorrow clinic. They sign a name in the air over it and let it be unmade. Grief, like everything else, is practiced in motion.

Take one out — it happens, rim-ship crews adopt stowaways, the quarantine stacks occasionally produce an emigrant — and the Basin leaves with them in ways no implant removal can fix. They panic, quietly and completely, in silence. In a meadow — and a meadow, with its uncontrolled pollen, its insects that groom nothing, its appalling green — they will often have an allergic and existential reaction in the same breath. They cannot sleep without pressure on at least two sides. They sign to people who are not looking. They shout, suddenly, in ordinary conversation, startled by how far a voice can carry when there is nothing in the way, and then clap both forearms over their mouths, laughing, ashamed. They miss the rain arriving on time. They keep checking the floor for motes. They feel, in open country, the specific vertigo of a volume that has been empty for longer than an hour and has not been filled: a wrongness in the gut, like a step missing in a familiar stair.

And almost always, if they can, they go back. Down from the rim road, twelve kilometers out, the pressure building in the sternum, the layers of the roar resolving as they descend: pumps, construction, cooling water, home. Smaller than you, wetter than you, arms already moving in the big, legible language, mouth making its small precise shapes against the noise.

Not adapted to the city despite its hostility. Adapted as a fish is adapted — not enduring the water, but made of the fact of it. Settled, the way the Basin is settled: by never, for one waking minute, being still.
