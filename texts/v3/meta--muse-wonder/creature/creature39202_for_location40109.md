They do not look inhabited, at first.

That is the first mistake every visitor makes on the Table, and they make it for a long time — weeks, sometimes a whole slow season of fog-harvest — walking the fitted basalt streets between the longhouses, sleeping in guest-chambers with woven fern-trunk roofs, drinking from the condensation cisterns, and assuming the citizens must be *somewhere else*. In the next district. Down in the bath-houses. Out walking the roads. Because surely the trees are just trees.

The fern-trees help the confusion. There are ordinary fern-trees everywhere, wild ones, black-trunked and mindless, uncurling their umbrellas in the cold. And then there are the other ones, standing in deliberate rows along the avenues, standing in family clusters around the plazas, standing — this is the detail that finally gives it away — *in the middle of the houses*.

Not beside them. Not shading them. Being them, from the inside out.

A rooted citizen of the Table has grown a trunk the way a lower-world person might eventually, over a lifetime, acquire a house: deliberately, communally, and with strong local opinions. The trunk is broad and low by tree standards, because the Table persuades everything flat, and hollow in a way no wild tree is hollow. Inside are chambers. Smooth-barked rooms that smell of sap and cold moss. A sleeping alcove where the wood is warmest. A storage hollow that keeps grain at an even chill all year. A doorway — a real doorway, grown with a lintel, sometimes incorporating an older doorway from a previous rooting the way a long-used coat incorporates patches. When the wind moves through the canopy at night you can hear the whole house adjust, very slightly, around the people sleeping inside it, who are also itself.

The canopy is the face, if you insist on finding one. It grows flat and wide and densely layered, an insulative roof of dark fronds that fold tight against the cold and open, creaking, to the pale morning sun. Citizens recognize each other by canopy the way you would recognize a silhouette: this one's fronds are split deep and frost pale at the edges, that family all grow a characteristic rust-red new leaf in spring, that elder's canopy has been grafted asymmetrically for three hundred years because of the marriage and you can still see the seam. In conversation — later, much later, you realize it is conversation — the canopies move. Not waving. *Inflecting*. A slow tilt toward whoever is being addressed. A shiver that runs frond to frond like a sentence finishing itself in another room.

Below ground they are even more themselves. A citizen's roots are opinionated in a way that is difficult to overstate. They tap the fog-channels without piercing them. They skirt the old foundations, or, if the citizen built those foundations in a previous century, they go *through* them with proprietary ease, following the compressed black strata of their own earlier houses down and down. They find the warm pockets above plate-lichens and settle there like hands around a cup. They graft, underground and out of sight, with neighbors they are close to, and avoid — pointedly, over decades, rerouting through solid granite if necessary — the root systems of neighbors they are not. A neighborhood dispute on the Table is not an argument. It is a pattern in the soil that everyone can read by the time it has finished happening, fifty years later, and by then it has usually been resolved by the roots simply having made a decision together that the canopies are still politely discussing.

They have no voices, which is to say they have several, all slow.

There is the creak-language of trunk and branch, most of which happens at night when the continent-city ticks and contracts in the cold and every rooted citizen is, audibly, thinking. There is scent: leaves that can be taught to mark a new channel for fog can also be taught, with much less effort, to say *come this way*, *danger*, *the festival has begun*, *a child is being debated*. And there is the golden weather — the pollination fronts that roll visible across the districts on still afternoons, spore and pollen travelling the remembered damp currents from grove to grove. A citizen does not send a letter. A citizen waits for the season, opens, and releases a thought into the wind with his whole body, and three districts away and two years later an entire family shivers gold in the late light and knows, all at once, in their leaves, that someone they have not touched in three centuries is well, and thinking of them, and slightly larger than before.

Nobody is in a hurry about any of this. They cannot afford to be. It is not a virtue. It is a physiology.

A life begins as a cutting, and it begins in public.

When a grove decides — by canopy-vote, by root-graft, by the slow accumulation of scent in a plaza over a generation — to make a child, the cutting is taken with a bronze saw and carried in a moss-lined bronze pot, and for a while, sometimes for years, the child is the most mobile citizen in the district. It is carried to arguments. Because the planting of a child is the gravest municipal act there is: wherever that small green person goes into the ground, a street will eventually have to reckon with them. They will grow into infrastructure. Their roots will vote on the drainage. Their canopy will change the shade pattern of a neighborhood for four hundred years, and shade, on the Table, is architecture.

So the neighborhood debates, at root-speed, in scent and creak and shade, while the child in its pot puts out leaves and listens with its whole unplanted self. Children are raised by being carried through the city they will one day be. They learn the fog channels by being held over them. They learn the pattern of the avenues by growing, seasonally, toward the leans in the light where the shadows fall toward home. By the time a planting-place is agreed — a process that has, more than once, outlived the original proposal by a century and been inherited, pot and all, by the next generation of debaters — the child is already a local. They are put into the ground like a foundation stone and a christening at once, and the district re-routes a path, very slightly, to include them.

Then the long rooted middle of life begins, and it is not a waiting. It is the career.

A rooted citizen grows their house and is their house and hosts in their house, all three, simultaneously, in a way that makes lower-world grammar give up. They take in travellers — in chambers grown for the purpose, warmed by the trunk's slow circulation, where you sleep inside a person who is politely pretending this is normal. They take in stores. They take in grandchildren's cuttings in their pots along the sun-edge of the plaza-self. They serve on the council by growing, over decades, toward or away from a proposal, so that legislation on the Table is a literal landscape: stand in an old district and you can see, in the lean of a hundred canopies over a plaza, exactly what the neighborhood thought about the new aqueduct, and when, and how the opinion softened. Their memories are rings. To remember something precisely, a citizen has to do it in winter, when the year's growth is available to be felt. Very old citizens remember in strata — house beneath house beneath house — and their recollections come with floor plans.

Marriage is a graft, and it is exactly as permanent as it sounds. Two canopies, grown together frond by frond over a century, sharing light, sharing weather, eventually sharing a root system so thoroughly that disentangling them would be surgery on a city block. Married groves are landmarks. Fast walkers navigate by them. Children are cut from the joined canopy and carry both parents in every leaf, and the marriage itself becomes, in time, load-bearing: there are rope-bridges on the Table anchored to couples.

And when, after centuries of rootedness, a citizen decides to move — the decision itself can take a generation to finish arriving — the whole district turns out with bronze saws and levers and water-weight cranes, and spends a season excavating them in a festival of careful creaking that is mostly chanting and mostly engineering and entirely both. Uprooting is the great social event of the Table. The roots come up trailing soil like a bridal train, like a comet tail, like the entire street plan of a life being lifted gently out of the ground. The chambers empty. The house becomes a house — for a while, ordinary tenants live in the vacated trunk, and speak quietly, the way you do in a place someone has just left after three hundred years.

Then the citizen walks.

The walking phase is the part visitors actually see and fail to understand, because a walking citizen does not look like a tree doing an unusual thing. They look like landscape, relocating. Trunk held low and forward, root-mass gathered and trailing behind in a long dragging train that tastes the ground as it goes, canopy folded close against the cold of travel, branch-limbs working the measuring chains and the municipal bells. They move perhaps forty kilometers in a decade. They take employment with the city while they do it, because walkers are precious: they are the only citizens who can insist on distances. A walker drags a chain between two plazas and rings a wooden bell once a day so the road can hear itself, and the road, attended, remembers being three days long. Walkers carry the cuttings. Walkers carry messages into yesterday evening. Walkers are the clergy of a religion whose only doctrine is that the way to the next district is the length it was last year.

They are also, though nobody says it this way, the young. Rootedness is maturity. To be planted in middle age, enormous, chambered, hosting travellers inside your own grown rooms, participating in legislation by leaning — that is adulthood on the Table. The walkers are the restless ones between lives, hauling their roots, seeing the continent-city at the dizzying speed of a kilometer a season, falling in love with views. Most citizens walk only once, maybe twice. A few — the road-keepers, the bell-ringers, the ones who plant themselves mid-route and become milestone-ancestors around whom the road politely shortens — make a vocation of the between.

Death, when it finally comes, is indistinguishable from promotion. A citizen who stops growing does not leave a corpse; they leave a building with unusually good bones. The trunk chambers are cleaned, the canopy preserved as roofing until it composts into the next layer, the root system becomes part of the district's water-commons forever, still channeling fog along the routes it preferred in life — and the fog, which prefers remembered routes anyway, keeps coming. Ancestors are not buried on the Table. They are *occupied*. They are compressed, gently, flatly, era by era, into the black strata under the new longhouses: the same house, again and again, all the way down, each version a person who decided, once, that this was a good place to stand for a while.

They are not wise in the way visitors want them to be wise. They are not serene. They have feuds that take two hundred years and are conducted entirely by shade. They have romances conducted by pollen that arrive, embarrassingly, at groves that have been rooted out of earshot for decades and cannot reply until the weather turns. They have festivals that begin before the guests leave and are still going, at growth-speed, when the guests' grandchildren return up the stair. They have civic scandals about drainage. They find walkers slightly frivolous and cannot do without them. They gossip through their roots.

What they are is *continuous*. A citizen of the Table does not live in the city, or build the city, or remember the city. A citizen is a temporary, ambulant insistence that this flat, unfinished, continent-sized place is one place — that the district sixty kilometers of moss away, with the same street names continuing the numbering, is *here* — and they hold it together by standing in it, growing through it, walking it back to length when it drifts, and being, in the end, stood upon by whoever comes next.

Nothing here is finished. They have made a civilization out of that, a person at a time, flat enough — for now — to live on.
