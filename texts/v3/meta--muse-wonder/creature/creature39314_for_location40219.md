You would not find them by looking for where they live. There is nothing to find: no village, no winter camp that lasts the winter, no graveyard, no field. Only, sometimes, a place where the ground has been warmed in the wrong pattern.

A shallow oval pressed into a heat-mat, still holding body heat hours after it should have cooled. A ring of stones sorted — not built, sorted — iron-heavy ones to the east whether or not east means anything that day. Stalks from a mineral pan cut cleanly and laid to drain into a skin. A trail of footprints in crawler tailings so fresh the crushed rock still steams, going barefoot straight across ground hot enough to make a stranger hop.

Then no sign for three days. Then another oval.

They travel the way everything else on the plateau travels: not across it, after warmth.

There are families of them, though *family* is a matter of route more than blood. A band is whoever is walking the same crawler-company that season, sharing the same good flank, the same drill-holes for fish, the same stretch of tailings where the mats have bloomed after a passing. Twenty people. Sixty. Sometimes, when two companies fall into parallel and their following storms merge, several hundred moving together for a few weeks in a silence so complete it feels ceremonial, before the routes shear apart again for another twenty years.

They are human, at a distance. Up close the plateau has been editing them for a long time.

They are low and broad through the chest, from a life of breathing warm, wet, slightly mineral air that sits in the lungs like bathwater. Their skin is dark — not from sun, there is none, but from heat-mats and iron dust and crawler grease worked in over generations until even children who have never left a lee camp carry a rust-colored cast. Most keep no hair. It holds damp, it holds charge, it tells a hunter where the head is by temperature. What hair remains is worn shaved close or bound down flat against the skull.

Their eyes are the least useful part of them and it shows. Small, deep-set, grey-irised from generations under cloud, pupils slow to bother changing. Many of the older walkers are effectively blind by sixty and entirely unembarrassed by it; they navigate better than their grandchildren. They feel the country through the soles of their feet. Barefoot is not poverty here, it is literacy. Callus so thick it can stand fresh tailings still steaming can still read, through that callus, a degree's difference in the ground — a mat rooting well, a buried warm seam bending left, old tailings going cold and safe to cross, the tremor of a herd coming through basalt a mile off, the infrasonic working rhythm of a crawler changing cycle somewhere beyond pressure in the teeth.

Children learn that before they learn words. They are carried the first years in slings against a parent's belly or back, sharing heat, learning the other language first: a tap against ribs meaning stop, a hand flattened meaning *vent soon*, fingers walked down a forearm meaning crawler, herd, hunter. Their spoken language comes later and stays small. Sound dies too quickly on the plateau to waste on distance, and makes too clear a claim on a place. They speak close, mouth near ear, voices pitched low enough to be felt in the sternum as much as heard. For anything farther they use feet on stone, knuckles on a hull, stones clicked under water in a crater pool. A whole conversation — *the pan on the overhang is dripping fast, the mats are up, don't bring the children that way, a cat is reading us* — can pass through the ground under a camp while everyone appears to be asleep.

They dress in what the crawlers leave and the mats provide: leathery heat-mat fiber beaten soft, black and oxblood, layered loose so damp can move through it instead of pooling; grazer hide at the shoulders, where rain finds them; moth-wing membranes, improbable as it sounds, sewn as inner linings because they shed charge and keep the skin from prickling raw when a company is working hard. Nothing is ever dry, so nothing is designed to be. Clothes are judged by how they smell after a week (sweet, iron, faintly rotten means good; sour means the fiber is dying and the mats won't take you), and boots are for crawler work only, thick-soled, because the decks inside a working hull get hot in places that shift without warning.

Some of them do that work. Every band has its inside-kin.

Not everyone who lives by a crawler ever goes in one. Those who do start young, when they are small enough for the baffles and patient enough for the heat, and they come out changed in ways the walkers discuss with great politeness and private unease. Inside a crawler it is not dark the way the plateau is dark. It is orange-dark, seam-lit, the light of the belly coming through service gaps and cooling grates, everything humming through the bone. Inside-kin learn the machines the way herd-children learn animals: not from manuals — if there were ever manuals they rotted before anyone's great-grandparents were born — but from apprenticeship of the hand. This valve runs idiosyncratically warm. This centrifuge sings a half-beat slow before it needs its filters combed. This old one, the one listing always slightly to its good flank, must never be fed from that side in cold weather. It is maintenance and it is husbandry and it is religion, and they are no longer able to tell the plateau which is which, and consider the question rude.

They eat what they clean from the intakes, first pick of mat and parasite and trapped small life. They trade down at ventings — parts knowledge, warning, ingots sometimes, tiny dense dark bars that walkers value not as wealth but as perfect heat sinks and tool weights, still warm from the hold. In exchange the walkers bring pool fish dried in tailing heat, grazer meat, news from other routes carried body to body across months of walking. Marriages are arranged then, quickly and cheerily, in the lee while the crawler exhales and nobody can hear themselves think. Children of those unions may choose which side of the hull to grow up on. Some change their minds at twelve and are mourned and celebrated at the same meal, because to go inside is to mostly leave the sky, such as it is, and to come outside is to be half-blind and footsore for a year.

The bands that never go inside keep herds instead — or rather, they keep company with herds. They have no fences and would find the idea funny in a bleak way. The big grazers follow the warm seams; the people follow the grazers following the crawlers following seams no one has mapped except in foot-memory and story. It makes them pastoralists of a manufactured pasture. After a crawler passes, there is a brief, rich week when the tailings bloom and everything with a mouth arrives in order of size. The people time their lives by it: births planned for the bloom, old people making one last effort to reach it, courtships, settlements of debts in meat and fiber. They milk the grazers only when the herds allow it, cut meat mostly from animals the predators have already read and marked, and take a religious care to leave the smaller herd-followers — the long-eared scavengers, the hand-moths — entirely alone. Those are fellow passengers. Eating them would be eating the warmth itself.

Time, for them, is not the grey brightening that passes for midday. It is crawler cycles and condensation cycles. Short time: pan dripping, mat opening, vent coming. Long time: the route. The oldest stories are itineraries remembered in the feet — go three companies west until the standing stones hum in a hand's-breadth of water, then follow the red iron country until you smell a crater pool that no longer exists. People are named for where the route was when they were born: Tailings, Third Vent, Lee-Rain, The Place Where The Old One Turned. To ask someone their age is to ask which crawler they are keeping up with and whether it has reached the mesas yet. Some routes take longer than a life. Grandchildren are told, you will finish this hill your grandfather began walking beside. They accept that the way lowlanders accept a harvest.

They are not a peaceful people so much as a quiet one, which is different. They fight — over inside-places, over a herd cut off by a venting, over an ingot traded twice. They fight close and mostly with hands, because noise draws cats, and because a body thrown on warm ground announces itself for a long time afterward. Murder is rare and exile is the punishment: not being driven out, exactly, just no one flattening a hand when you approach. No shared lee. No warned vent. The plateau does the rest, kindly, over about a week.

The dead they give to the tailings. A body laid in a fresh warm scar will be taken up by mats within a season, fronds unrolling black over the ribs, the whole brief bloom richer there, grazers pausing over it for years without knowing why. Inside-kin do it differently; they are compacted, formally, very deep, with the ingots that are never emptied. Neither group can hear the other's custom described without shivering.

And at what passes for midnight, when the cloud thins and a few stars stand hard in it, they are all awake — walkers breaking camp before a cycle turns and the sheltering lee becomes a vent side, herd-keepers moving barefoot among the grazers with palms flat on hot hides to read their calm, children wide-eyed and silent, watching the fish rise in a black pool to take the insects falling out of the steam, inside-kin deep in the orange dark laying a palm to a hull-plate and feeling, through iron and time, a hillside being planed away somewhere ahead. None of them think of the crawlers as theirs. If pressed they would say, with a patience that suggests they have answered visitors before, that it runs the other way.

They were not born here. Their ancestors came up out of the cloud sea below for reasons that have become three contradictory stories and a song. But their grandchildren were born here, and their grandchildren's feet can read a warm seam through thick callus in the dark, and no one now alive could breathe the cold thin air of the lowlands and live.

The plateau is finishing the job it does on everything: rounding off the fresh break, until the inhabitants hold the shape they intend to keep.
