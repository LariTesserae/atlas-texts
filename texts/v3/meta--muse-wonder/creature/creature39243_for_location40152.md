The first resident is not human at all.

It was there when the mountains were still deciding, in geological terms, to give up. It is the Mat — one organism, clonal and continental in its patience, simplifying a range down to a table it could lie across in the sun. Leaf, root, lichen, hollow trunk, spongy hollow and dry rise: all of it one body, drinking dew through a million surfaces, knitting over every cut, groaning very slowly in its great roots under Ternwick. It does not hunt. It incorporates. It holds lost noises in its stems and returns them a day late, held at the wrong temperature. Lately it has taken to practicing the city — raising quarter-scale tenements in woven green out on the empty ground, a train that will never move, a chimney that whistles domestically in the wind. Whether it is learning Ternwick or remembering it forward is an argument for surveyors late at night. Either way, it is the landlord.

The humans are the infestation on top of it. Loud, brief, well-lit, and entirely aware of the arrangement.

You can tell a Ternwicker down in the valley, before they even open their mouths — and then you can certainly tell them after. They are built, over generations, for thin air and brightness. Deep chests that look slightly too large for otherwise spare, wiry frames. Slow, economical movements at altitude that turn out, at sea level, to be restlessness. Eyes habitually narrowed against a light that makes everything look freshly washed, skin weathered to a permanent, wind-burned ruddiness and then stained, around the hands and ankles and the hems of everything they own, a faint indelible green. Sap gets into the pores. Root-cloth gets into the skin. Children grow up smelling faintly of crushed leaves and hot copper.

And they are loud, not as a temperament but as a trade.

A newborn in Ternwick is first presented, as a matter of custom, to noise. The looms clacking in the Loom Quarter, the fog-fans turning on the rim, the shriek of the sawmills, a bell rung close — not to startle it, but to place it. Silence up here is not peace. Silence is the condition under which the Mat grows fastest, under which a path goes ankle-deep between Monday and Friday, a sleeping hare comes up netted in tendrils, a causeway disappears. So Ternwickers talk while they walk. They sing tuneless working songs on the raised root-fiber causeways between districts, they carry rattles and clappers the way valley people carry umbrellas, they appoint their children — grave, bored, immensely responsible boys and girls of nine or ten — to stand in pairs at street ends with a hammer and a length of iron pipe and keep a rhythm going through the quiet hours. To be a bell-keeper is usually a first job and often a last one. Old men who can no longer work the Squeeze Works sit all day in a chair that has to be cut free of bindweed every morning, and strike, and strike, and nap between strikes without ever quite letting the interval grow long enough for the street to forget itself.

It shapes their voices. They speak from the diaphragm, pitched to carry across low ground where sound behaves badly anyway. They are not shouting, exactly, when they hold a conversation at what a visitor thinks is an unconscionable volume across a breakfast table; they are simply refusing to let the room go undefended. Their houses help. Five-storey tenements pinned — not founded, never founded — with long iron augurs into the root-crowns, walls of woven living hedge over iron frames, roofs of turf thick enough to need cutting. A Ternwick house is never quite still. It settles in the night with a tiny creak as something grows an inch under the floor. It puts out shoots through the floorboards in the Market Crown, where no one is entirely sure which beams are dead timber and which are still alive. Families prune their own sitting rooms. They lash new growth into the wall or cut it back, depending on the season and their patience, and they learn as children to sleep through the faint, vast ticking and groaning that comes up through the soles of the boards: the body underneath, drinking, deciding.

Their whole civic life is a negotiation with movement. Because the Mat swells perhaps an inch a year here, three there, unevenly, a district rides like a very slow ship. Streets bend. An address is meaningless without its date — *14 Condenser Row, as of March* — and surveyors are consequently not back-room functionaries but a kind of priesthood of theodolite and listening rod, men and women who go out and put an ear actually to the ground to catch the low note of a main root under strain weeks before a subsidence. Ternwickers defer to them the way port towns defer to harbor pilots. They also follow them, anxiously and superstitiously, out beyond the pruning line to look at the imitations, and they do not cut the imitations down.

Work, up here, is mostly a set of ways of arguing with vegetation. The saw crews who ride in an open car ahead of the morning train, throwing back armfuls of pale fiber already twitching toward the rails. The stokers who paint chimney bases with tar and lime every Monday and find exploratory leaves at the rim by Friday, and pluck them almost tenderly, the way you would correct a child. The loom-hands who weave root-fiber into cloth tough enough for roofs and hulls and winter coats, and go home with their forearms cross-hatched with fine pale scars. The condenser crews in their long glass terraces, harvesting water where there is no river — from fog dragged through cold mesh by the great rim fans, from dew, from sap pressed greenish and faintly sweet in the Squeeze Works and stored in perpetually sweating tanks. Nobody in Ternwick drinks anything that did not recently belong to the Mat. This does not bother them. It would bother them more to pretend otherwise.

Even their animals are employees. Rooks, tolerated because they make a racket that fits, nesting black and argumentative in the chimneys. Moths by the thousand in the glass houses of the pollen works, the only reliable pollinators for the seed-crops, tended like machinery and counted like currency by quiet, soft-handed people who are the nearest thing the city has to jewelers — and even they hum while they work, under their breath, constant and unconscious. A hare seen at the plateau edge, fur green all summer from sleeping in the Mat, makes the newspaper not because hares are useful but because they are evidence: something with blood still occasionally visits, and leaves unincorporated.

They have their dangers catalogued and priced in. Do not sleep out on open ground unprotected, however tired, however benign the afternoon. Do not walk off the lit causeways in spore season, when the gold dust off the high trunks makes distances lie and a man can walk all night toward lights that never approach and arrive at dawn having lived, by his own report, most of a disappointing decade in the interval. Do not trust a sound the first time you hear it, and do not entirely distrust it the second time, tomorrow, when the stems give it back. Do not, above all, let the noise stop at night. The fans slow but never stop. The bells keep their uneven human tempos over empty streets. Couples talk in bed longer than they strictly want to. Someone in every tenement is always, by rota, awake and making some small deliberate clatter — mending a pot, playing cards on a tin tray, reading aloud to nobody — while through the floor comes the other sound, enormous and patient, practicing perhaps the shape of a chimney in the dark.

What is strangest to a visitor is not their fear. It is their ease.

They prune their city off a living thing and wear it and drink it and stake their houses into it, and they sleep well. Children play elaborate counting games on the causeways whose rules exist mainly to keep them chanting in unison. Old women argue cheerfully with the bindweed on their balconies. When a building burns, they put it out, rebuild it staked a little differently, and then go out three weeks later to stand around the Mat’s green quarter-scale copy of it — doorways, roof-pitch, lopsided chimney and all — making approving, uneasy noises, and leave it standing. Some of that is superstition. Some of it is the newer, colder comfort of the surveyors’ theory: that the organism has lived with looms and bells and fog-fans long enough to learn Ternwick as a seasonal shape, like flowering. That if every human left on tomorrow’s train, the plateau would not go quiet and empty. It would, in time, grow the districts again. Hollow. Green. Making, when the wind went through them, the small domestic sounds of houses.

The Ternwickers maintain the noise anyway. Especially those who believe the theory most. It is, depending on who you ask at the Market Crown after the evening whistle — the real one, or its echo arriving punctually the next morning — a defense, a habit, a prayer, or simply good manners.

They are, they will tell you without any particular gloom, a loud and temporary way the place is currently happening. Underneath them, immense, sunlit, millions of years old, the actual citizen of the plateau lies across its flattened mountains and learns them, politely, by heart.
