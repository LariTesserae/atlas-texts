The people of Karal Sump are, for the first few years of any newcomer's life, almost impossible to tell apart.

Not because they look alike. Because your eyes have not learned how to see in the green dusk yet, and because down there no one ever presents themselves all at once. A hand comes out of the mist to take a pipe-rail. A shoulder glistens past a lamp. A voice finishes a sentence that began two terraces above. By the time you learn that a face is not something seen, but something assembled slowly from warmth and breath and the particular way a person wrings a wall-cloth in the morning, you have already begun to become one of them.

They are surface stock, originally. The grandparents remember this. You can tell them, even now, not by their age but by the way they move when the light shaft is at its brightest — they flinch, very slightly, upward, an animal remembering sunburn. Their skin has kept a memory of dry country in it. It cracks in long, pale lines at the knuckles during the Dry Days and never quite learns to sweat efficiently; they pour rather than glisten, and suffer worse than everyone else when the Condenser is gathering.

Their grandchildren have never been up.

They are born sixty meters down into air like a held breath, and opened their eyes for the first time to lamplight blurred by mist. They are paler, not white but a rinsed, underwater gold and olive, the color skin goes when it has never needed to defend itself from direct light and has instead learned to live perpetually wet. In the amber lamps they look burnished. In the green shaft-light they look faintly verdigris, as if the copper-green moss that lines the leaking pipes had a cousinship with them and was not shy about claiming it.

Their pupils stay wide. Their eyes keep the Sump's habit: adjusting, and then adjusting again. A child of Karal can read the rust-red lichen maps on the Condenser's fins at a distance where a surface man sees only shadow, and can tell by a thinning in the mist whether a shutter three rings down has been opened. Take that same child to the rim at noon — it has been tried, out of sentiment, by surface-born parents — and she will weep without noise, hands pressed flat to the sides of her head, overwhelmed less by pain than by sheer *distance*. Everything up there is too far apart to be real.

They are smaller than their grandparents, though no one planned it. Not stunted. Condensed. Ceilings sank as wood swelled. Doorways were cut for people who had already learned to duck under pipe-streets. Stairs were built steep to save space. So a generation grew with a permanent, graceful stoop in the upper back, strong calves from a life lived entirely on ladders and switchbacks, and a way of turning sideways in alleys that is not politeness but choreography. Two Sumpers passing in a lane do not squeeze by. They perform a slow, practiced rotation, chest to chest for a heartbeat, each smelling the other's cooking and soap and sleep, and then they are past. Nobody apologizes. There is nothing to apologize for.

Their hands are their truest faces. Living by the law that a wall belongs to whoever keeps it dry makes everyone, from the age they can hold a cloth, a wall-keeper. Palms are softened and ridged at once, fingertips perpetually a little pruned, nails kept brutally short against the damp rot that loves the quick. You can read a life there faster than in a face. The black crescents under a smith's thumbnails. The copper stain on a fin-scraper's fingers that no washing removes — nor would she want it removed; it is her drip-right made visible. The fern-cutters go with forearms crosshatched in fine, pale scars where fronds and knives have each mistaken the other over the years. The root-masons, who coax living root and swollen beam into holding each other up, have palms so callused by bark that they can take a hot rivet from a boy's tongs without changing expression.

They go mostly barefoot. Boots rot, and leather stays wet, and the iron pipes and stone terraces are warm at all hours anyway. Their soles are thick, silent. A Sump child learns the town through her feet before her eyes: which boards give sweetly and are rotting, which pipe-bridge will be slick with algae after the Drip, which threshold stone belongs to a house where the old woman will leave melon rind out for whoever passes, wordlessly, as rent for the sound of someone alive going by.

They speak low, and rarely begin anything. Speech costs breath in that packed heat, and breath is communal property whether you like it or not. So a sentence will be started by a woman hanging laundry on the third ring, taken up — not interrupted, *taken up*, the way you take up a load someone is tiring under — by her brother mending a cistern below her, and finished, sometimes hours later, by a neighbor who heard the middle part through a wall thin with damp and has been considering his contribution while he worked. Strangers mistake this for telepathy, or for an intimacy so total it frightens them. It is mostly economy. It is also, unavoidably, intimacy. In Karal you know whose baby is teething by the change in the night-breathing two houses over. You know a marriage is thawing before the couple does because the grievance they left in a damp place to soften has started, audibly, to be carried more lightly up the stairs.

Naturally they marry drip-rights, and naturally everyone pretends this is not the whole of it. A good marriage brings a north-facing wall that sweats drinkably even in the Dry Days, or an inherited roof where the Condenser's sideways rain pools an extra hour, or a grandmother whose hands know which seep-hole in the east wall opens after surface rains no one below will ever see. A bad marriage is two families finding their cups set out under the same pipe for thirty years. Love in the Sump is not blind; it is hydrologically precise, and all the more tender for it. Courtship is a matter of small, witnessed competences. To wring someone's morning cloth for them without being asked. To cut the vines back from their flue before your own. To stand under the cold pipes on the first night of the Drip, mouth open like a child, and not be embarrassed.

They time their lives by the Condenser's nine-day breath rather than by any calendar lowered from above. They are Dry-Day people or Drip people and believe this explains temperament. In the Dry Days, when the fins go cold and dark and the machine is drinking moisture out of their very lungs, the whole town is a degree more irritable, a degree more inward; lips crack in unison, tempers are banked like coals, ferns and old resentments curl alike. When the Drip begins — sideways, upward, impossible, the water remembering the sky — nobody announces it, because everybody's skin knew an hour before the first bead fell. Householders set out their cups. Voices drop below even their usual register so as not to startle the broadleaf plants into folding. Children get wet with a solemnity that surface children reserve for snow.

Work is slow because everything that could be hurried has already killed someone. The fin-harvesters climb to the crown at the cool top of the pit where the rust-red lichen grows in maps, and scrape a season's dye with wooden blades, moving like divers. The flywheel-tenders cut back the wet-handed vines in their daily ceremonial armistice, never quite winning, never intending to. The elevator takes its hour to descend and stops for goats that died twenty years ago, and the operator — always an elder, always half-asleep in a way that is actually attention — would sooner quit than revise the schedule, because somewhere in that hour a dozen kitchens time their rice by its creaking passage and three babies are rocked to sleep by its stopping.

At night they are, in the mist, mostly disembodied kindnesses. A lamp carried past becomes a drowned star; its bearer is inferred. They sleep thinly, in the open, behind shutters that swell shut, and the great ferns turn toward their warmth through the walls and uncurl against the plaster with a sound like a page turning, and nobody finds this lonely. To be grown toward in your sleep by green things that want your heat is, in Karal, a form of being known.

The dead are not buried — there is no ground that is not also a floor, a garden, a cistern. They are composted, formally and unhurriedly, into the roof-beds, and the tubers that come up in ash and sump-mud the following season are eaten without ceremony and with complete awareness, both. Hence the oldest Sump saying, which is not morbid to them at all: *the root is now holding up the beam.* Grandmother is in the melon vine that brushes your head in the alley. You do not point this out to guests. You duck a little lower, out of respect.

They are not a finished people. Forty years is not evolution; it is only habit hardened into the body, and the body still arguing with it. They cough, all of them, a soft, damp, communal cough that is less an illness than a dialect. Their joints ache early. Their clothes rot off them in sweet-smelling seasons and are rewoven from loom-thread and fern fibre in patterns that indicate a ring, a trade, a marriage, a debt. A blind white moth blundering in the lamp-glow can stop a market stair for ten minutes while it is ceremonially witnessed, named, and misremembered later as larger. The black lizard on the hottest pipe has not been counted in living memory, and a child who tries to count it aloud will be hushed with a hand that smells of wall-water — not from superstition, exactly, but from a people's hard-won suspicion that some things stay only so long as you do not make them into facts.

Surface folk who descend — blinking, overdressed, loud with the clean loudness of dry places — are quiet within a week. The heat does it first. Then the dark. Then the realization that privacy was a climate they once lived in, not a right. Those who stay shed their boots, then their outer names, then their urgency, in that order. Those who climb back up after a year away say, invariably, that the waste looks empty and the sky looks too big to trust.

Ask a Sump-born woman what she is, and she will not say a settler, and she will not quite say a towns-person either. She will put her palm flat to the nearest wall, feel it sweat against her skin, and consider, finishing some sentence her grandmother began the week before.

"The machine dreams it's a town," she might say, if the Drip is starting and she is feeling generous toward explanations. "Most days we're what it's dreaming with."
