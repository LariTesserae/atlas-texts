At first you will think no one does.

There are animals, but so few, and so unwilling, that they read as accidents — the ash-grey wader testing each step for whether it belongs to the same day as the last, the eel-thing whose back and tail do not quite join up in the same pool. And there are the turtles, if they are turtles: slow, black, moss-humped shapes that hold still in the sedge for hours at a time while water beads and runs off them.

Stay longer. Stay until your clothes are wet through and will not dry, until you have stopped trusting distances and started walking where the reeds lean away. Then one of the turtles will sigh — not through its mouth, through the peat under it, a small displacement of the general roar — and lift a head you had taken for a knot of alder root.

They are low, the way everything true to the basin is low.

Not short, exactly. When one of them unfolds to stand in a place where the gravity is running true, there is a surprising length to her — long in the leg, long in the forearm, long in the neck that has been folded down into her shoulders against the damp. But they rarely unfold. Standing presents too much to the mist, and the mist carries instructions that are not meant for skin. So they go crouched, bellies near the sedge fans, knees bent outward, moving with a deliberate, rocking slowness that keeps their weight spread and revisable. At any moment they can settle. At any moment they can become landscape again.

Their feet are the first giveaway that they are not visitors. Broad, splay-toed, webbed not between the toes but behind them, in a frilled membrane that lies flat on the peat and will not let them sink. Each toe ends not in a nail but in a pale, horny rootlet that curls when it touches ground. They do not step; they plant. They press down, wait a breath while the rootlets taste for drift — for the shallow, invisible pooling of weight that slides through the valley through the day — and only then commit. Their hands are the same, though finer. When they wade they hold them up out of the water, fingers knotted and interlaced lightly, the way the alders hold their roots. It is not a gesture of surrender. It is how they keep their hands in the present while their feet are still negotiating with a moment ago.

Their skin holds the valley in it.

Dry — there is no dry here, but comparatively, between one wetting and the next — it is a dusk color, iron-grey going to olive at the joints, pale almost to translucency on the underside of the arm, the throat, the belly. They have learned, like the sedges, to live by reflected light, and they are brightest underneath, where the pools shine up. Tiny black flecks move just under the surface of that paleness, not quite in the blood. When a pool flashes, they flash in answer, a brief, scattered brightening that travels across a shoulder, down a spine, and is gone. If you startle one at close range the whole flank will open for an instant like an eye, and then politely close.

That suspension is in them the way it is in the fungal mats under the peat, the way it is in the water itself. They may once have eaten the lattice, mouthful by mouthful over generations, the way the fungus ate it. They may have been made by it in the first place — a maintenance fauna, a soft interface designed to go where fields and membranes could not be pointed. Their grandmothers' grandmothers could not have told you. Down at that scale, and over that length of sunk time, descent and infection and design are all the same word, and they have forgotten the word anyway.

Their heads are turned mostly down.

It shocks you at first, how little they look at the sky. The cloud deck is a constant pearl-grey false ceiling to them; nothing arrives from there except weather, and weather they can feel earlier and better through the soles of their feet. Their eyes, large and dark and set wide, have a second lid that sweeps sideways against mist, and the reflective layer behind the retina is tuned not up but down. They navigate by pool-light. They read the bright patches that drift across black water where the dissolved machinery is thinking a little harder. They read reflections the way you would read a face — with allowances. When an inhabitant wants to know what she is about to do, she will sometimes look, quite seriously, at her own reflection finishing a gesture before she has begun it, and nod, and comply. When she wants to remember something, she goes and stands beside a still pool for an afternoon until what she has lost surfaces in it, slightly altered and without mist.

Their ears are small, sealed crescents. They cannot afford ears, not with the roar. Everyone here shouts at arm's length; they do not shout at all. Up close they speak in a low, felt language barely above the ground-hum, mouth near mouth, so that most of the meaning arrives through the jawbone of the listener. At a distance they speak through the ground itself. A heel pressed and released in a certain peat hollow, a palm laid flat where fungal threads run thick, sends a pattern out under the marsh — five beats, a pause, five beats, a variation. The reeds take it up at their own pitches. The mats pulse faintly in sympathy at night. A conversation across two kilometers may take an hour, may arrive smudged by a passing gravity basin into a slightly different sentence, and this is considered normal, even useful. Some things, they seem to feel, should be allowed to revise themselves in transit.

You will never find where they live because they do not keep a where.

Nothing built stays in the same shape here, and eggs would not, so they carry everything. They carry their tools — a few shaped stones, a reed knife, a cord of braided sedge — hung close to the body where their own warmth, such as it is, stabilizes them. They carry their food as they find it: bitter sedge hearts, the soft interior of certain roots, small blind fish they coax up not with bait but by lying still in a channel until their own reflected light draws a school into the bowl of their cupped hands. They carry their children inside, and then outside, for a very long time.

A child is born — the word is too abrupt; a child is gradually extruded, over a wet season, from a thickened fold low on the parent's back, where the skin is darkest and the flecking densest. For months it is unclear whether you are looking at one inhabitant or two. The infant's rootlet toes grip the parent's spine. Its eyes open downward onto her back and learn light from her flashing before they ever see a pool. When it finally plants its own feet, it does so a step behind her, a step in her settling footprints, testing whether that patch of world has agreed, overnight, to stay roughly where she left it. Adolescence is simply the slow lengthening of the distance — one pool behind, then three, then out of earshot and into ground-speech, then gone for a year into the northern reed beds where the roar changes key.

They sleep in company with the water.

At nightfall — slate replacing pearl overhead, the booming sinking half an octave — they go out into the shallows and let themselves down until only the crowns of their heads and the backs of their sealed eyes break the surface. Moss will take anyone who holds still for a season, and it has taken them gladly; old individuals carry a living thatch across the shoulders and skull, liverwort and pale thread and a miniature geography of sedge seedlings, which moves faintly when they breathe. Turtles, an outsider persists in calling them, and the mistake is forgivable, and by the time a person is old enough to be that patient, that encrusted, that unwilling to be hurried by a folding of the air, the difference has largely stopped mattering. They dream there with their ears under, listening to the lattice do its loud, purposeless arithmetic. Sometimes their dreaming joins up. The pools pulse east to west across the basin, the fungal fans brighten in sequence, and for a few minutes every sleeper in twenty kilometers twitches in the same direction, troubled by the same displaced pressure crossing the valley very fast and very large, over and over, in a place none of them can point to.

They do work, if work is the right name for it.

No stranger's work, though. You will not see them mend anything, because there is nothing left with edges to be mended. What you will see, if you watch a whole day and do not get impatient with reversals, is tending by body. An inhabitant feels a channel beginning to run too purposefully between two pools that should not yet be reconciled, and she lies down across its invisible sluice, not damming it, just lending it her confusion of weight and warmth until it loses conviction and spreads. Another finds a place going silent — that terrible, concentrated silence that means the medium under the peat is doing sums too intense to spare any for sound — and he squats at its margin and hums, badly, deliberately off-pitch, tapping an uneven rhythm with his rootlet toes, introducing small loyal errors until the place remembers how to be ordinary loud ground again. They move fallen alder limbs a hand's breadth. They clear a fan of sedge from a pool's mouth with their teeth. They stand in up-raining water and let it rain through them, droplets detaching from their hair and accelerating upward, because it seems to relieve a pressure they can feel in their teeth.

Whether the basin needs this, none of them could say. It may be maintenance. It may be worship that has decayed, very thoroughly, into habit. It may be that the lattice, having eaten its instructions down to a few persistent urges, now expresses those urges through the nearest warm bodies available, and the people of the basin experience being briefly borrowed as a kind of weather in the joints, and do not mind.

They mind very little, by any standard you bring in with you.

They are not afraid of predators. They are not afraid of drowning, or cold, or the dark that is only a darker grey. What makes a whole company of them freeze in the sedge, rootlets locked, reflections holding their breath a second too long, is misplacement. One of them will set a foot down and withdraw it sharply, not in pain, in apology. A child will begin to cry — a thin, reed-pitched sound — because the voice that came back from the mist ahead was hers but altered, kinder and older. They will wait then, all of them within ground-speech, perfectly still while the air unfolds itself and distance agrees, provisionally, to mean one thing at a time. Kindness here is largely a matter of not insisting.

They die the way banks slump.

An old one, moss-deep, slow past the possibility of insulting comparison to turtles, chooses a loud place — loud being honest, loud being alive — and settles a final time into the peat with her pale underside turned toward the nearest pool. The settling takes weeks. The black water takes the flecking out of her skin cell by cell and spreads it wider, thinner, more usefully. The fungal mat comes up to meet her from below, pale threads finding the familiar paths of nerve and vessel, occupying them with a respect that looks a great deal like continuation. For a season the pool beside her shows reflections particularly early, particularly clear. For a season messages tapped into that stretch of ground arrive with a distinct, unhurried flavor that the others recognize and answer in kind. Then the peat sighs a hand's breadth, a new black pool opens already complete, and what was a person is distributed at last into the general bookkeeping.

That is what lives in the basin.

Not rulers of it, not victims of it, not a remnant crew still waiting by an instrument panel that dissolved before their species finished arriving. A people who sank with the land and let the land's long accident get into their blood and their backs and their grammar, until there was no clean line left between the engineers, the engineered, and the wet, roaring wilderness that resulted. If the valley is a mind dreaming loudly of a purpose it completed and forgot, they are the part of the dream that gets up, most mornings, plants its feet with great care in a world that will not hold still, and goes and dreams the next stretch by hand.
