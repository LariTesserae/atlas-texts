They are, at first sight, unremarkably human. That is the disguise that lasts longest.

Coal-damp mill people, market people, chapel people, people with rope-burned hands and a tradesman's stoop — if you met one at the seam, coming sideways out of the pod with a basket of white asparagus on her back, you might mistake her for a settler from any hard new country. Pale, yes. Quiet, yes. Wet to the elbow. But the new countries usually explain a person. This one has been at work on them for generations, and it shows in smaller ways the longer you stay.

They do not look up. There is no up to look at. They look *across*, and *around*, and with a particular unfocused attention at the living surface beside them, the way sailors listen to weather in the rigging. Their pupils are large, but not dramatically so — they are simply people who have never had reason to contract them for long. Sunlight, when a thin place in the skin admits it, makes them flinch and laugh at themselves for flinching. Children are taught not to stare into the seam. Old people sometimes do anyway, briefly, the way other old people smoke one forbidden cigarette a year.

Their skin keeps the damp. Hair is worn cropped or braided tight and oiled, because loose hair collects condensation and, worse, spores. In the poorer districts collars bloom faintly green by evening and are sponged clean at night, apologetically, as if the body were a rented room one hoped to get the deposit back on. Their clothes are layered, close, muffled — moss-felt, braided fiber, oiled canvas at the factory quarters — and they wear soft shoes almost universally, not from poverty but from courtesy. In a town you walk on, a hard heel is an interruption. You can tell a district's wealth not by ornament but by dryness: the fine houses on the upper curves have learned, through generations of grafted drainage veins, to keep a floor merely cool instead of slick. The poor sweep standing water toward their landlord's heart-fruit and call it rent.

They move beautifully and without thinking about it, which makes newcomers — who move like dropped cutlery — feel obscurely shamed. A citizen walking home may pass, in ten minutes, from a boardwalk street to a wall-street to what was, from your platform, the ceiling, and the body makes the whole adjustment without ceremony: a hand trailing along a root-rail at the curve, a slight lean, a step that becomes the first step *down* in a new direction. There is no dizziness after childhood. Children go through a delighted phase at about four or five of running at walls and shrieking, silently, mouths wide open, at the moment the world politely re-decides beneath their feet. Adults pretend not to find this funny. Cats trail the children, entirely unsurprised, waiting for dropped food to fall in interesting directions.

Speech among them is low, unhurried, and strangely uninflected — not flat with stupidity, flat the way you carry a full bowl. Strong feeling is a kind of spillage here. It brightens the street-fungus, swells doors in their frames, and sets the plant listening in a way nobody has ever been able to define but everybody recognizes, as a change in the pressure behind the eyes. So they have become masters of the understatement and the postponed sentence. A proposal of marriage may take three weeks of remarks about drainage. A declaration of war, historically, took one raised voice in the stock exchange and a whole quarter of foxfire flaring white at once; the building was closed for a month to recover, and nobody involved has raised their voice since. They argue furiously about whether you should sing to a house-seed, and they conduct the fury in near-whispers, leaning close, red-eared, while the lamps around them pulse like embarrassed hearts.

They hear with their whole bodies in a way land-people do not. The town's engines are felt in the molars, the bridges in the tendons of the ankle. A machinist stands with one bare palm not quite touching a drive-belt and reads the factory through the hairs on his wrist. A good midwife can tell, from the contraction that moves through the pod every seven minutes, whether a birth is going with the town or against it, and times the final effort to the squeeze, so that hundreds of citizens have exactly the same pulse-interval woven into the first thing their bodies ever did. Thieves learn the rhythm too. So do lovers. So do the people who set the charges in the foundry, who count under their breath — six minutes of ordinary time, then the tightening, the gain, the moment when every tool in town is briefly stronger than the hand that holds it.

They are gardeners before they are anything else, whatever their trade. A loom-house family still knows how to persuade cane-wood to split without a wound. A clerk keeps pollination brushes in his waistcoat and can sex a budding room at a glance. Building is a marital art here: you do not force the plant, you court it, splint it, feed it ash and silt and ground shell, defend its soft young walls at night from the glass snails and the blind beetles with your own sleeping body curled in the doorway. As a result their patience has a quality outsiders find either saintly or unnerving. They will wait an afternoon for a wall to advance a finger's width and discuss the advance afterward with the seriousness of a stock report. They will also, when persuasion fails, simply live around the failure for thirty years. There are kitchens in this town that a staircase rerouted itself through in 1911. The family sets an extra plate. The neighbors use the pantry door. Nobody cuts.

This has made them inveterate interpreters of accidental shapes, because in a place where the walls grow, nothing is entirely metaphor. The translucent fish in the cisterns really do seem to trace the street plan. Houses really do sometimes know a visitor's name, in the sense that a doorway preserved from a great-grandmother's bedroom will sigh it, approximately, through the grain when the contraction passes. The dead are placed in the overgrowth hollows by nearly universal request, and after a few days the architecture keeps their suggestions: a curve here, a warmth there, a pillar in the market that old women touch in passing without looking, the way you would touch a sleeve. So the citizens live surrounded by relatives in a much more literal sense than they discuss. They speak to empty rooms in the same tone they'd use for someone hard of hearing in the next one. They leave the lights low for the walls.

It is not a morbid culture. It is, if anything, indecently domestic. The festivals are held in darkness, the music is played through the floor, and the whole population dances by pulse, so that for one night of the year the bridges swing gently with people who are all, briefly, keeping the same time as the thing that contains them. They eat fungus and fern and blind white asparagus and small eyeless fish, and they eat them gratefully and with elaborate sauces, because a cuisine is how you prove to yourself that scarcity is a choice of attention. They trade in the stock exchange for futures in neighborhoods the plant has not grown yet, and lose real money, occasionally ruinously, on streets that bud wrong, or bud as organs, or refuse to bud at all. They keep silent pigeons. They baptize children twice, once with water and once by carrying them across the void on a bridge at night, so the child learns before language that the empty middle of the world is also a way to get home.

Below the factory quarter, the tunnels find earlier streets and less ripe citizens, translucent and industrious and unsurprised. The people of the town proper have reached a working arrangement with this fact, which is to say they do not reach one at all. Officially the reports are exaggerated. Unofficially, supplies are left at certain tunnel mouths: iron filings, sugar, grief — whatever the lamps eat. Children born with unusually clear nails, or an extra sensitivity to the contraction, are watched with a tenderness that has an apology in it. It is possible the town understands, in the part of itself that speaks quietly, what it is for. A population that pollinates by hand, that returns its bodies to the walls, that warms the tissue with engines and feeds fungus on feeling and times its births to a seven-minute squeeze, is not merely living inside the pod. It is doing work for it. They may be the plant's hands. They may be its memory, organized on purpose into streets so it can be carried through the opening. They may be the soft, necessary ruin a chrysalis requires.

They know the pod will open someday. They talk about it the way they talk about everything consequential: obliquely, while doing something with their hands. Houses are nonetheless maintained. Debts are recorded. Seeds are planted in districts that, when gravity chooses one direction and keeps it, will be hanging in air. This is not denial, exactly. It may be the deepest habit the place has taught them: that you tend, with total seriousness, a thing whose final shape you will not be consulted about.

At the plant's night they retract to their platforms and their tiered streets, and the bridges draw back, and each neighborhood becomes a scatter of foxfire across the green-black sphere. Then you can see what the citizens most essentially are — not a crowd, but a constellation that has learned to live at distances, joined through tissue, humming faintly to one another below the threshold of speech. A woman stands outside her ripening house with condensation in her cropped hair, one foot bare on the living floor to feel the next contraction coming, and calls across forty meters of dark — not loudly, never loudly — to a doorway she cannot see. Somewhere around the curve of the world, a lamp brightens, just slightly, in answer.
