The people of Ilvarra are, at first glance, disappointingly ordinary — which is the first true thing about them, and takes the longest to understand.

They are not tall, pale mystics. They are not frozen or sleepy or drugged on the light. They are mountain people of middling height, broad through the hands and shoulders from work, with weathered faces and practical hair cut short enough not to catch on rope or loom or bramble. They wear wool even in the sun — undyed greys and browns, greens from the dye-works, a deep blue that fades, eventually, back to a color almost identical to itself. Their sleeves are always a little damp at the cuff. Their sandals are thick-soled and soft-edged. In the bright streets they wear wide, woven hats with one brim subtly wider than the other, because the sun has never once varied its approach, and so every Ilvarran, given enough years outdoors, develops the faintest asymmetry: more freckles on the west cheek, a habit of turning the east shoulder toward a chill, a left forearm warmed brown while the right stays comparatively pale. They know one another, across a terrace, by a silhouette and the exact wedge its shadow makes.

They are almost all neither young nor old. Or rather, Ilvarra makes that judgment hard to exercise. Children are unmistakably children — small, loud before they learn not to be, forever on roofs where roofs are forbidden — and the old are unmistakably old around the granaries and on the shaded benches, white-haired, slow, their skin recorded all over with fine nicks from chisels and thorns. But between those states lies most of a life, and it lies there for a very long time. A woman who taught you to knot cloud-field rope will still be teaching it, in the same patient position over the same frame, when your own child is brought to learn. A man thought middle-aged when you arrive will be thought middle-aged years later, bent over the same channel, replacing the same stone with one dressed to fit it. They do not seem ageless so much as *adequately aged*, the way the pond-frogs are adequately numerous. Birth and death happen indoors here — behind the heavy shutters, in the kept darkness — and happen quietly, and are not denied, but they do not accumulate into generations the way they do elsewhere. There is always a cradle in use somewhere. There is always, in due course, a name to carve into the archive tablets under the lists of bridge-repairs and harvests. The city admits a person at one end and releases one at the other, and the number on the terraces holds.

That number is not large, and they like knowing most of it.

An Ilvarran is known less by what they do for novelty than by what they keep. Every family has an inherited patch of the world for which its hands are answerable: these slates, that stretch of gutter, the third rope from the west end of the bridge, the goat-gate that sticks unless lifted, the section of channel where silt gathers — does not gather more, but gathers — and must be lifted out in a basket each household cycle. Their surnames, if you translate them kindly, are mostly verbs worn down to nouns. There are Slate-checkers and Rope-ends and Cloud-tenders, potters whose wheels turn by foot in a rhythm you could set your breathing by, masons who are always just finishing a wall that was always just in need of finishing. They do not speak of work as something to be done and then escaped. Work is how a thing continues to be the thing it is. A roof is not built. A roof is *kept happening*.

And so they are, nearly to a person, superb with their hands and suspicious of any job done too quickly. To hurry in Ilvarra implies you believe time is trying to get somewhere without you. They mend before a break has finished deciding to happen. They re-knot, re-point, re-weave, re-lay. Their tools are beautiful from use rather than ornament — iron bright at the edge, wood darkened where palm and thumb have always fallen — and their craftsmanship has no fashion, because fashion would require last year to be distinguishable from this one. The clay tablets in the granary archive are written in a hand that altered so little over centuries that the archivists, a small, dust-fingered lineage, pride themselves on accuracy of copying more than interpretation. *Bridges sound. Yield as expected. City in excellent condition.* An Ilvarran archivist considers that a complete and satisfying history. Anything more dramatic in a record would suggest that someone had failed at their maintenance.

Their sense of time, unsurprisingly, is not ours. They have no clocks and no use for them. They do not say morning or afternoon; it is late morning everywhere except in their own manufactured dark. They measure by tasks and turns and household shifts: after the second checking of the barrels, when the swifts have changed over in the eaves, before the dye-wheel's trough is emptied, when the children come down from the watch platforms with plum juice on their chins. Appointments are kept with unnerving reliability, not because anyone counts minutes but because everyone has always arrived when the moss is dry on the south side of the step and cool on the north. A visitor asks, “How long have I been here?” and an Ilvarran answers, kindly and without evasion, “You have slept four times.”

Sleep is a civic art. Because night is not given, it must be made, and they make it the way they make everything: collectively, carefully, by hand. The inner rooms of the houses are windowless or shuttered with woven panels so heavy they fall with a sigh, and darkness is kept there like a stored good. Families sleep in rotation, so kitchens are never entirely cold and channels never entirely unwatched — though there is nothing to watch for. Babies are rocked in black-curtained cradles while daylight stands outside the door. Children learn early to associate evening with textures rather than sky: the smell of shut wool, the cool of an interior wall, a parent's voice lowered even further, a wooden clapper tapped twice to mean *the house is closing its eyes now*. They grow up fluent in a darkness they have to make for themselves, and oddly tender toward it. Ilvarran lovers court in shuttered gardens at mid-day that feels, by agreement, like dusk. The old nap in pools of artificial night while swifts stitch the unchanging blue overhead. They wake without dawn and do not miss it. Waking is not the day beginning. It is simply your turn to be up in it.

They speak low, and listen better than they speak. This is not timidity. It is architecture in the throat. The basin returns a voice too faithfully, and an Ilvarran considers an echo a social stain — your words coming back to impose themselves on people who have already had to hear them once. So they have evolved a conversation pitched for three paces’ distance, mild, unhurried, full of the small verbal courtesies that let another person know they have been accurately received. They have no bells, no shouting across terraces, no names called down streets. Signals go by wooden clapper, knock, knock, as soft as knuckles asking permission. Even their humor is low. They are funny in the manner of people who have heard every joke begin before and enjoy recognizing where it will land; much of their wit is maintenance-wit, fond observations about a goat's professional opinions, the one frog extra to requirements, a neighbor's roof-gate whose temper is known to the whole street. Their laughter is real and brief and does not echo.

Their tempers are the same. Anger visits them — they are not saints, only balanced — but it rarely finds anywhere to accumulate. No one in Ilvarra has ever been observed to hold a grudge at scale, partly because the person who wronged you will be mending your cistern wall before the household has slept twice, and the wall will need him, and you will need the wall, and the grievance, unstocked and unreplenished, thins to an embarrassment. They resolve things by returning to the shared task nearest the offence. Marriages work much the same way, which outsiders find either admirable or alarming: two people do not so much discuss a life as jointly assume responsibility for a set of slates, a garden, a dark room, a child in a shuttered cradle, and discover that love is what continuous exact replacement feels like from inside. It is not a grand passion they mistrust, exactly. It is any feeling that demands to grow forever. They prefer feelings that can be kept in good repair.

Food comes from the terraces in the order it has always come, which is all orders at once. They eat ripe fruit beside blossom, store grain that is always partly used and always replenished, make soup that tastes permanently of herbs just crushed and stone-cooled water. Their appetites are moderate and punctual by household rather than hunger alone. A visitor weeping at phantom dusk will be fed before he is questioned; this is reflex. They will bring soup, and bread, and a plum, and will sit with him on the porch without making a ceremony of his distress, and then show him how the shutters work, how to weight the curtain with the smooth stone, how to let his own hands make night for him. They are very good with strangers’ bewilderment because they treat it as weather that has blown in with the stranger and will settle if properly housed. There are few visitors, and fewer who leave quickly. The pass is hard, but that is not the whole reason. Ilvarra gives the nervous system nothing to brace against and nothing to await, and after the bracing fails, people become Ilvarran at an embarrassing speed. They stop raising their voices. They learn the clapper codes. They begin checking a barrel on their way past, just to see that it is three-quarters full, and find the checking pleasant.

Crucially, they share the city without quite owning it. The goats proceed across the roofs by right of ancient agreement, wooden toggles knocking softly, trimming the roof-moss, and an Ilvarran householder will hold a roof-gate open for a goat with the grave politeness owed to a colleague. Marmots sit between the foundation stones like furred aldermen and are greeted, not fed — feeding would be an attempt to change an arrangement. The granary owls are spoken of with professional respect, as another night-shift working the dark they make inside the towers. The swifts are clock and calendar both, useless for telling time and perfect for company. Ilvarrans do not keep pets so much as maintain relationships with neighbors who happen to have different maintenance obligations: the bees keep the blossom honest, the frogs keep the pond-rims inhabited, the goats keep the roofs, the people keep absolutely everything else, including, in some sense, the light — not by making it, but by never asking it to do anything else.

If they have a religion, it is not visible as temple or creed. There are no priests separate from other trades. But there is a widespread, unembarrassed reverence for exact continuance — for the chair being where the chair is, the fruit replaced, the barrel neither empty nor brimming, the beloved face still across the table after your fourth sleep and your four-hundredth. They do not worship permanence as outsiders worship gods, by begging it to intervene. They participate in it the way a singer participates in a held note. Their prayers, if they are prayers, sound like inventories. Their gratitude sounds like checking.

They are not innocent of sorrow. They bury their old behind shutters and are quiet for a household cycle longer than usual. A child who dies — it happens, because life happens even here, only without escalation — leaves a dark room genuinely dark for a while, and neighbors bring soup and take over that family's length of channel without comment, and the extra quiet along that channel is the city's form of mourning. But grief, like rain, is not allowed to become a flood. It condenses. It drips steadily into the vessels prepared for it. It is carried. And because everything around the grieving person continues with such lush, ordinary reliability — the moth opening on the warm wall, the dye-wheel *plip, plip*, the goat crossing to its accustomed roof — the sorrow is neither magnified nor mocked. It is simply given a size it is possible to survive.

To live in Ilvarra is to become, at last, a person to whom *enough* is not a disappointment but a complete description of the world. They want what they have tended. They have what they keep wanting. Their faces, turned up into the late morning that has finished arriving, are calm not because nothing has ever happened to them, but because nothing is still happening to them faster than their hands can meet it. They are the inhabitants the valley requires: warm on one forearm, cool on the other, damp at the cuffs, soft-spoken, indispensable, each replacing the world exactly where they found it — and finding, in that replacement, that they have been replaced, gently and continuously, themselves.
