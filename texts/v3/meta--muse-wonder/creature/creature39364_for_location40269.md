They are not visible from above, and that is the first thing about them.

From the rim you would think the plateau empty, and from the stair, before you have seen a face, you already know them by sound. Not because they are loud people — quite the opposite — but because the bowl makes every sound public. A woman husking beans two terraces down speaks at ordinary pitch and is heard as if at your shoulder. A child coughing in a lower house is answered, reflexively, by an old man on a roof who has not seen that child all day. They have learned to live inside an ear.

So they modulate. Indoors, under the vines, in the greenish dim alleys where most of the living is done, they speak low and close, mouth near ear, hand sometimes lightly over the mouth — not secrecy, courtesy. Anything meant for everyone is shouted. Children are the town's public voice: perched on parapets with their legs dangling, relaying from roof to roof — *your goats are in the figs, the oven is lit, come down for water, it is your notch* — until the message has gone around the bowl like a stone skipped across it. Anything meant for no one is simply never said aloud. Privacy here is not a wall. It is a discipline people perform for each other, turning away while a couple argues in whispers, pretending not to hear a girl singing to herself at the cistern, looking studiously at their hands while a neighbor counts his grain.

You meet them properly only by getting in the way. The stair is too narrow for two, the alleys too narrow for indifference. You turn sideways for a woman coming up with a hide bucket balanced on her head and another in her hand, not a drop lost in the turn; you press your back to a warm wall for a boy driving three goats whose bells are each tuned slightly differently, so their owner can tell them blind; you duck under a lintel worn glossy at exactly forehead height and understand, at once, what shape a lifetime here takes.

They are a compact people, not by blood alone but by architecture. Generations of low doors, short roof-beams spliced and re-spliced, sleeping rooms built over sheep-folds, ladders instead of stairs inside houses, have favored the small, the wiry, the flexible. Tall youths stoop until the stoop becomes their posture in old age. Shoulders are narrow, hips narrow, feet extraordinary — broad, splayed, soles calloused to leather from hot stone and thorn, toes that grip a parapet edge as unthinkingly as a goat's hooves. Hands are the opposite of their feet: precise, almost delicate in motion, because everything here is done by handful and pinch and notch. A woman can judge a day's water ration by the weight of a jar on her forearm. A man mending a roof-beam for the third generation of its use works with the reverence other places reserve for metal.

Their skin takes the plateau and then hides from it. Faces, forearms, the backs of necks are burnt a deep, polished brown, varnished like the pebbles above; the rest, kept under cloth, is startlingly paler where a sleeve slips. Lips are habitually cracked at the corners despite the fat they rub into them. Eyes are their most obvious adaptation: set deep, lashes dust-pale, lids half-lowered even in shade from a lifetime of judging glare up on the plain against green dimness below. They can spot a hawk's shadow crossing the threshing floor without looking up, and tell, from inside a house, which fig tree a fruit has fallen from by the sound it makes hitting a roof, a leaf, packed earth.

They smell — they would be astonished to be told so — like the bowl itself. Warm dust in the folds of clothing, crushed thyme and rosemary from brushing walls, woodsmoke that never entirely leaves hair, goat milk, leather, dung sweet and fermented from the garden beds, pomegranate juice under the fingernails in season. A stranger coming down the stair smells suddenly, cleanly, of the outside, of wind and empty stone, and dogs will follow him for an hour out of pure interest. Among themselves, people identify each other in the dark nearly as well by scent and bell and footfall as by voice: this household fries its herbs in more fat, that old man has the cough and the iron-tipped staff that ticks on stone, that girl has taken to wearing her dead mother's sandals which slap differently.

Clothing is inherited the way beams are. Nothing is made whole when it can be made to continue. A tunic is patched at elbow and shoulder with cloth of three other garments, which are themselves remembered — *this was your uncle's wedding shirt, this was a grain sack from the good year* — so that an old woman sorting rags is, in effect, reading a genealogy. Fiber is twisted, woven, re-woven; wool is carded down to the last tuft combed from thorn bushes where the herds passed; hide is softened with urine and worked until it pours almost like cloth. They dress in layers even in heat, because the plateau at dawn is freezing and the bowl at noon is an oven, and because a layer lifted over the head makes portable shade. Head-coverings are universal above the rim. Below it, in the vine tunnels, women unbind their hair, men push back their wraps, and there is a visible relaxing of the body's vigilance, as if the bowl itself were a garment they have stepped inside.

What they eat is the loop made edible. Flat bread from the communal ovens, blistered, ash-dusted. Beans and squash from beds no bigger than blankets, intensely manured, intensely tended. Figs fresh and figs dried, grapes fresh and raisins and a thick sour syrup, pomegranate seeds eaten over a bowl so the juice is not lost. Goat's milk, sheep's milk, curds hung in cloth from fig branches, a hard salty cheese saved for work on the plateau. Eggs whenever a hen can be outwitted. Meat is rare and communal — an old goat, a cockerel too many, a pig fattened almost entirely on fallen figs — and its distribution is as precisely notched as water: which family gets the liver because there is a new mother, which gets the bones for soup and then for grinding into the beds. Nothing about a meal here is casual. Even a child eating a fig on a roof spits the stem into his palm to drop later onto soil.

Their children grow up vertical. Before they can properly walk an alley they can climb, because the town is climbed rather than traversed — roof to roof, terrace to terrace, up a fig trunk whose branches serve as a second street system. Toddlers are carried in slings against a hip while their mother works a hand-mill; slightly older ones are put in charge of chickens, which is a serious apprenticeship in attention; by seven or eight they are roof-runners, water-carriers of small jars, hereditary experts in whose goat coughs and whose pigeons have nested where. They are taught the water discipline before they are taught anything else recognizable as morality. A spill is not punished as clumsiness but corrected as theft. Small fingers learn to wipe a splash with dust and squeeze the dust over a plant, to rinse a cup into the next pot never into the dirt, to urinate in the jar at night without embarrassment because in the morning it will go to the garden or the plaster-mixing. Shame here attaches not to waste products but to waste itself.

Old people, consequently, are not retired from the loop but concentrated by it. When legs can no longer take the stair to the plateau, hands can still shell, sort, twist fiber, mend rope, mind the seep. The eldest sit in particular places — there is a stone by the cistern, a patch of late shade under the great fig, a granary doorway that catches the evening heat coming off the wall — and these places are theirs as absolutely as the dogs' sleeping spots below them, defended by everyone's agreement. Their work is memory. Rights in the bowl are too many, too small, and too old to be written: who may hang washing where the shadow falls on whose beans, who sweeps this turn of alley and so claims its dung on alternate days, whose family takes seep-water before the heat and whose after, whose vines may be allowed one tendril more over a neighbor's roof. No one person holds all of that. The elders hold it between them, arguing it out in that low, close voice, correcting each other, recalling that in the drought year the order was changed for three days and then changed back, that this notch on this jar was cut when your grandmother was married. To be old here is to be part of the town's storage system, like the cisterns.

It would be wrong, though, to describe only the humans, because they themselves wouldn't. Ask who lives in the bowl and a resident will list, without any sense of metaphor, the goats on the roofs whose grazing is timed to keep the vines pruned but not stripped; the pigeons in the wall-holes whose droppings are swept from ledges into baskets as carefully as grain; the bees in the south wall whose flight-lanes through the alleys everyone knows and automatically ducks; the sheep whose body heat rises through the floorboards all winter; the cats who keep rats from the granaries and are consequently tolerated on tables; the fig trees — individual, named, argued over, their roots cracking houses and their shade structuring the day — who are fed ash and bone and night-soil and who feed, in return, people and pigs and goats and wasps and half the birds in the bowl. A household is counted not in people but in mouths and producers together: so many children, a grandmother, two donkeys, a dog getting deaf, thirty hens, a share in a hive-wall, a pomegranate. The line between inhabitant and livelihood never quite forms.

That is why, at feeding time, the town's famous roar does not sound like people *and* animals. It sounds like one creature in many throats. Bells pick up bleating, bleating sets off donkeys, donkeys start the roosters, a woman calls across it all in the flat carrying tone used to pierce animal noise, a child repeats her from a roof, the pigeons go up with a clap and settle with a rustle that the swallows cut through, screaming. Everyone in that moment is doing the same thing — negotiating proximity. Goats are allowed just so close to the drying grain. Dogs may watch meat but from *that* stone, not this one. Bees may be smoked but only by the man whose grandfather built the tubes. Space here is not divided; it is endlessly, minutely shared, and tempers are correspondingly both short and swiftly mended, because there is nowhere to storm off *to* except the empty plateau, and nobody wants to be up there long.

At night this becomes clearest. The herds are folded, the birds settled in the figs in a vast, shifting, muttering canopy, the ovens cooling, the rock giving back its heat. Families sleep close — in rooms over animals, on roofs under the brief cold stars when the heat is too much, in hammocks strung in vine tunnels — close enough to hear neighbors breathing, turning, whispering, making love with the practised quiet of people who have never expected a wall to keep a secret. A goat bell ticks. Someone rises, crosses a roof that is someone else's ceiling, descends to check a restless donkey, and returns, all by touch and memory, greeting two other wakeful people on the way by name though no lamp is lit. Below the rim the plateau is black and silent and enormous. Down in the pocket there is this: a warm, dense, breathing knot of life that has made itself out of almost nothing, and intends, with a patience as polished as its stairway, to go on making itself — one notch of water, one basket of droppings, one spliced beam, one remembered right at a time.
