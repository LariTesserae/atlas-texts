It is not the foxes. Foxes den under the shelters because the digging is easy there, and go up to the plain to hunt. It is not the ravens, who have a warm wall at Station Three and a whole silent country to commute over. The crews thought so at first — fox, raven, a reindeer gone wrong on the ramp — because the signs would not hold still long enough to be anything.

A depression in the moss beside a boardwalk, round as a dinner plate, already springing back by the time you looked again. A beard of hoarfrost knocked clean off one railing and none of the others. Condensation licked from the underside of a pipe in a long, intermittent line. And in the red zones, where no one sensible tries to speak, the loose ice dancing off the lagging would sometimes dance wrong: pause, shiver sideways, settle, several metres from any boot.

Whatever lives in the Cut lives first by not being heard. In a place that has only one sound, that is the same as not existing.

It comes up out of the mat, not onto it.

That is the first hard thing to understand. The mat is not its floor. The mat is its roof, its sea, its sky turned upside-down. Twenty centimetres down the roar is muffled to a pressure in the teeth. Half a metre down there is dark and wet root and fungus and cold moving at insect pace. That is where it spends most of its life, travelling horizontally through peat and sphagnum that may be metres deep, never digging down to soil — there is no soil to stand on — never quite breaking the surface either. It makes low, shifting tunnels that close behind it like water behind a slow swimmer. Researchers find them in their plots and mark them as subsidence. In July a tunnel can re-knit in a month. In winter it stays, a faint sunken script under the snow, legible only if you already know how to read.

When it does surface, it surfaces flat.

Low, long, otter-broad and badger-low, but lighter than either looks, because most of its apparent bulk is not body. Its back carries a living thatch. Sphagnum has seeded in the coarse guard hairs; feather moss threads the undercoat; in the oldest animals there are neat pale patches of reindeer lichen growing between the shoulders, and crowberry sprigs rooted in nothing but shed skin and damp. In summer a surfacing animal is bottle-green and rust and oxblood, dappled mustard where the lichen dries. In winter the whole garden freezes iron-grey and the snow stays on it. It can lie beside a pipeline sleeper for an entire shift-shift while a crew works twenty metres away, and be nothing but another hummock that is breathing very, very slowly.

Its feet explain why the mat tolerates it. They are absurdly wide, splayed, fringed with stiff hair, the hind pair nearly as broad as soup ladles. It does not so much walk as distribute itself, letting its weight sigh out across the sponge. It prefers the undersides of the boardwalks, where the mat is compressed already and sheltered from what light there is, and runs those long wooden shadows from shelter to station like roads. Step for step it does less damage than a booted human. A track lasts minutes. A fox track lasts until thaw.

It has no ears to ruin.

There are no external ears at all, only smooth, furred swellings where the skull thickens, and canals sealed shut with cartilage shortly after birth. It is not deaf — the Cut would not permit that waste — but it hears the way the ground does. Through the jaw. Through the breastbone laid against peat. Through the soles. The compressor stations are not loud to it; they are geography. Each one shakes the valley at its own worn frequency, Station Two a fraction rougher than Station One, Station Three with a double beat where its housing meets the bench rock, and the animal navigates its whole eighteen-kilometre world by gradients of vibration, the way a fish knows a current. Near the red lines, where the roar comes up through boot leather and makes tea tremble in a cup, it will press its chin down and go still with an expression the crews read, hopelessly, as contentment. Its heart, which in the tunnels beats some twenty times a minute, quickens there and syncopates. It is the only warm thing it knows that never pauses.

So it builds where the shaking is.

Its nests — if nest is not too airy a word for a felted hollow in the peat, lined with cotton-grass down and its own shed garden — are always in the penumbra of a station or a valve housing, never in the worst of the noise and never out of it. The constant tremor keeps ice from knitting solid in the bedding. Waste heat does the rest. In the willow thickets where a flange leaks warmth and the twigs grow waist-high and black with rime, whole extended families winter in a stacked, communal torpor, adults on the outside turning grey with frost, young in the damp centre, waking in rotation to lick condensation off the pipework and eat whatever has been foolish enough to overwinter near them.

It eats the underside of the Cut. Fungal threads and sedge rhizomes and crowberry fruit kept cold-preserved in the mat since August. Insects slowed to a manageable honesty by the temperature. Lichen, which it crops with flat worn teeth and does not digest so much as farm in a complicated gut, emerging altered. Occasionally a raven drops something by Station Three. Occasionally a crew, eating lunch with their backs to the wind that isn't there, loses something off a boardwalk, and learns not to go back for it. It is not a hunter in any hurried sense. It is a grazer of processes so slow that only the noise is faster, and it matches them: grows slowly, breeds slower, one blind pup every third or fourth year, raised first in the dark tunnels and then on the boardwalk undersides, taught stillness before it is taught food.

Communication, such as it is, goes through the ground.

A forefoot drums once against a sleeper and the mat takes the message a hundred metres, a low felt syllable. Two beats, pause, one: a station boundary. A rapid trembling made by shivering deliberately against frozen sphagnum: young nearby, keep weight off. The crews, who talk down here in fluent hand-signals, swear the animals have answered them — a lifted forepaw, held, lowered again beside a transformer fence. It is probably only threat, or curiosity, or coincidence repeated until it has the dignity of habit. But the older hands knock their frost-mallets twice on a railing before they start work on a section, and wait a breath, and would not be able to tell you why they would feel rude not to.

Its eyes are its concession to the surface, and they are poor ones. Small, black, set under a translucent nictitating lid that never fully opens in what passes for daylight. It sees orange work-light better than sun, movement better than shape, the blue-grey dimness of the floor as a varied and sufficient world. On the plain it would be blind and lost. That is not speculation; twice, animals flushed by bench-work have gone up the ramp ahead of the noise of chisels, and both times they stopped well below the rim, disoriented not by cold — cold is home — but by the thinning of the roar and the widening of the light, and turned back down into shadow as if into water. Above the rim the wind would strip its damp coat in an hour. Silence would leave it deaf in the only sense that matters: unable to tell where anything was.

Because here, the roar is also cover. Under it, a raven cannot hear it move. A fox cannot hear it breathe. It never vocalises; the pups squeak once, wetly, at birth, and are licked quiet, and that may be the only unmediated sound it ever makes. Everything else is vibration, scent laid down in tunnel walls, the slow chemistry of a body that smells mostly of peat and iron and cold aluminium. Predation is a matter of being seen, which is a matter of impatience, which it has bred almost entirely out of itself. It can wait out a fox beside a pool that is skinning with ice at noon in July. It can wait out a human rotation. It can, in the nest-hollows where old garden-backed animals die and are not removed, simply become infrastructure: bones as light and hollow as pipe insulation, the moss closing over them, the lichen on the back outliving the back by a decade.

They are long-lived the way the benches are long-lived — by not being weathered very fast. Forty years, perhaps, for an animal that knew the Cut when its drill-marks were new. The population is probably not large and cannot be. Eighteen kilometres long, a few hundred metres wide, one mat thick: a country shaped like a corridor, divided by shaking into a chain of small felt territories, each centred on a station's orange light and fading toward the quieter, colder dark between. In midwinter the vapour from the stacks flattens and travels sideways and never clears the walls, and down in that trapped breath the animals move more, following the heat-shadows, drinking frost-melt where it beads, their grey backs indistinguishable from the grey floor.

From the rim at noon you will not see them. You will see the snow lying unbroken, the black smudges of willow where the heat leaks out, the little steady lights, and the vapour going nowhere. The roar comes up out of all of it, continuous, as if the young valley had been given exactly one thing to do at speed.

Underneath it, at ankle-depth, at jaw-depth, the valley's true tenants are listening with their whole bodies — patient, earless, garden-backed, at home in a noise that drove everything else away — and answering, now and then, with a single soft beat that no one up on the silent plain will ever hear.
