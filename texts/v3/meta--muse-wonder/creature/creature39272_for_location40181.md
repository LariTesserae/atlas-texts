They are hard to find at first, even when you are standing in their country.

Not because they hide. Because everything here is vast and they are not, and because their camps leave almost nothing that looks like habitation from a distance — no smoke, no stone, no cleared ground, no straight line. A platform of woven fiber lashed between two vascular columns a hundred feet up, where the folding is slow that season. A line of hammocks slung underneath a floor-leaf, each one tied at both ends, each sleeper tied in. A rack, a net, three resin pots beaded with frost. Below, the glassy moss is unscarred. If the wind-gate opens, the whole arrangement sways very slightly and goes still again, and the people in it do not look up. They are already holding on.

They are pruners. That is the closest word.

They are human, recognizably, stubbornly so — two arms, upright, faces that fog in the cold — but the Hollow has been editing them for a long time. They are tall in the way of the grazers, long in the leg and narrow through the chest, built for distances that go on for months and for climbing when the floor becomes a wall without asking permission. Their skin is the color of the underside of a leaf, pale to the point of translucence in the hard light, and in a bright district you can see the veins in their wrists and throats, blue-green, slow. Some of the older cutters have a faint green cast all over that never washes out, from years of sap under the fingernails, in the cuts, in the food. Their hair is usually white early, or kept cropped close under fiber caps, because loose hair collects rime and drift-seed and breaks off in the cold like grass. Their eyes are large and very dark and they squint constantly, not from dimness — never from dimness — but from brightness that comes from everywhere and nowhere. Out in the open chambers, between columns miles apart, many of them wear slitted masks of polished seed-husk, or a smear of dark resin under each eye. Children go bare-faced and learn the squint before they learn to read a floor.

They do not get warm. This is not stoicism; there is simply no warm to get. The light falls at full noon and casts a hard shadow and puts no heat into their shoulders at all, and so their whole physiology has arranged itself around conservation rather than comfort. They move slowly, deliberately, conserving gesture the way surface people conserve water. They speak low and close, because the plant drinks sound and a voice dies within a few yards anyway — conversation is a thing done face to face, breath fogging between mouths, or hand to hand. What needs to carry farther goes through the plant itself: a knuckle or a bone staff rapped against a living column, a faint deep note traveling through tissue, answered minutes later from a camp you will never see. Their languages are half tap-code. A stranger learns quickly that silence among them does not mean absence. It means listening through the soles of the feet.

They wear the Hollow. Layers of beaten fiber, soft as felt where it has been worn and gone stiff with frost at the edges, bound with cord and sealed at the seams with resin against the damp. Everything crinkles faintly when they move and sheds small crystals of ice. They go barefoot more often than an outsider expects — thick-soled, calloused feet that can feel what a staff would miss, the difference between a floor that is a leaf and a floor that is a lid — but on knife-grass country they bind on soles of seed-husk, and after a week the husk is scored to ribbons and discarded, and the grass behind them is still razor-keen and unblunted, and closes over their footprints like water.

They eat what drifts and what can be persuaded. Drift-seed, caught in nets strung across the updrafts along the sunlit veins, split with a thumbnail and eaten raw, tasting of cold green and iron. Young growth-tips, cut before they harden, before the plant decides what they were going to be. Frost-flowers, which must be gathered while they are still facing the light or they go bitter. Clear pond water never, if they can help it — it leaves resin on the teeth and worse afterward. Instead they tap a vein: one small careful cut, a cup tied into place, a mouthful that is sweet and faintly medicinal and thick with the plant's own antifreeze, which is likely why their blood can stand this cold at all. They are precise about sap the way sailors are precise about weather. Slow amber is food. The clear fast-running kind is the plant moving its blood somewhere urgently, and they will not touch it, and will cut a stranger's cup down without apology. There is almost no cooking. Fire is a tool of last resort and a kind of obscenity — the resin feeds it too eagerly, the damp resists it, and a wound answered by frantic growth is a terrible way to die, sealed inside new translucent tissue with the smoke. What needs softening is fermented in sealed pots in the less-cold air beside a vein, or dried in the wind of a gate until it is hard enough to crack a tooth on. Meals are unspectacular, frequent, and eaten walking.

Because they are always, eventually, walking.

No one builds against a wall. No one sleeps untied on ground that may buckle, tilt, or be revealed by morning to have been the top of something. A camp is a hypothesis about the next few weeks, and everyone old enough to tie a knot knows how to abandon it. They read the light the way surface farmers read a sky they have actually seen — and these people have never seen a sky. Amber light means a vein upstream is thickening, and this country will dim within the month, and dim means slow, and slow means the corridors will start to close. Blue-white and hard means something has opened above, light is flooding in undelayed, and growth will come fast and violent: new reed-forests a man's height in a week, lung-mosses inflating and sighing out spore-clouds, fans of knife-grass pushing through the peat in glittering arcs. Paradise and death trap in the same season. They move toward the amber and away from the blue with the unhurried urgency of people who know that both will kill you if you admire them too long.

Their work is keeping the world passable. With seed-husk knives and bound-fiber spades and bone awls they cut back the seams that would seal a corridor, coppice the reed-forests before they topple, clear the cilia of a stoma-gate the way you'd clear a throat. It is not farming. They do not plant. The Hollow plants itself, frantically, at geological tempo, everywhere and all the time; the human task is subtraction. A good cutter can look at a glossy seam in a wall — wet, sharp-edged, entirely unworn — and tell you whether it wants to be a crack or a scar, and whether to help it or stop it, and will be right often enough that children are apprenticed to her for years. They believe, with evidence, that the long-legged grazers with their translucent antlers do the same work at the wild edges, cropping growth-tips in districts no camp has reached, and they leave those animals strictly alone. To kill a grazer would be to let a road close. Moths the size of two hands they tolerate in their hair and their nets, carrying golden puffs of pollen from flower to improbable flower, because improbable flowers are sometimes the only thing marking where a wall used to be.

They are scattered because the world is scattered. A camp might be thirty people, might be nine, might not meet another camp for two seasons if the folding goes against them. Marriages, trades, arguments, and funerals all wait on geography. When two camps do find their districts connected by a corridor that was not there last year, the meeting is treated with a kind of practical astonishment — news exchanged, tap-codes updated, crystal-sap lenses and good husk traded, children stared at. Their shrines are only this: arrangements of cut lenses at the feet of the great veins, focusing the cold light for a moment onto a plate of dense old peat polished dark by generations of hands. No outsider stays long enough, or returns by a route that still exists, to see the same ceremony twice in the same place. Their dead are wrapped in fiber and carried, sometimes for days, to a folding place, and given over without malice and without particular hope. A season later that ground is unrecognizable, and very green in the particular way of fed things, and the family, if they pass that way again, which they usually do not, does not stop.

What they fear is specific. A floor that sounds dull through the staff. A new crack on its first day, breathing old wrong air. A district gone suddenly quiet — quieter than the usual quiet, the dripping stopped, the tread-moss holding still underfoot, refusing to bloom behind your step — because a gate is about to move and the entire atmosphere of a province is about to lean. Then they hold onto something that is not the ground, and they hold onto each other, and they wait for the wind and the soft, low thump that arrives through the soles of the feet minutes after the visible event, like something remembered. Children learn that grip before they learn the tap-codes. Everyone here has a story, told close and low, about a camp found later by travelers passing through a newly split corridor: preserved whole in translucent new growth, platforms and hammocks and a rack of knives, the plant closing its fist very slowly around people who are still, if you look long enough, recognizably holding on.

At the edges of the known Hollow, though "edge" is a word they use politely for outsiders, the cutters go alone or in pairs and come back changed in their squint. They say the light there comes in colors for which there are no camps and no words. They say the columns grow in spirals. They bring back maps scratched on fiber and then, after a night's sleep, discarded — not because the traveler lied, but because by the time he returned the way had folded shut behind him and opened somewhere else entirely, and an honest map of a country that no longer exists is just a decoration. These returned ones are listened to carefully and believed completely and followed never, which the people of the Hollow consider the only sane response to true information.

They have no word, exactly, for the plant. Or they have too many, most of them verbs. It is not a god; gods can be elsewhere. It is not a home; homes stay. It is the condition under which light arrives without heat, distance arrives without horizon, and silence arrives before sound. A child born on a lashed platform in full underground noon will open her eyes to a brightness with no source she will ever see, feel frost feather the rim of her hammock, hear nothing but dripping and the tick of ice forming and her mother's breathing a hand's breadth away — and learn, in order, to squint, to hold on, to listen through her feet, and to cut carefully and never deeply, in a world that is growing over her even as she grows up in it, bright and cold and mostly empty, and entirely, patiently alive.
