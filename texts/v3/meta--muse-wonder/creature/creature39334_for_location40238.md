The one who lives there is never, at any point, only one.

From a summit you see her first as part of the valley traffic — another dark, house-sized bulk in a river of bulks, flank to flank, moving so slowly that only the steam makes the motion visible. A vent sighs along her spine. Another answers, a little ahead. Another behind. Her piston-heart thuds, and after a pause the herd takes it up out of phase, so that the sound never quite stops, it just travels through meat and bone from body to body like weather.

Come closer — down out of the head-height cold, down into the knee-high fog where the mat is warm — and she separates herself.

She is six-legged, but not evenly. The front four legs are pillars, broad-footed, splayed to carry the furnace; the rear two are longer, jointed higher, and drag a little, steering. Her hide is not hide all the way through. It is keratin laid over deposited silica, plate over plate, each plate darkened by smoke from within and rimed white on its outer edge with frost that never melts. Between the plates, at the warm seams, furnace-moss grows directly out of her. It glows faintly at night. Small blind things live in it and groom it, and eat the lichen spores that settle there, and are in turn eaten by the spark-moths that gather in the updraft over her back. She carries a lee-side garden wherever she goes: glass grass in the shelter of her belly, a fringe of copper feather-lichen along the windward curve of her shoulder where her own exhaled moisture condenses and freezes and thaws again.

Nothing on her is wasted, because nothing here can afford to be decoration.

Her head is low and mostly mouth. Millstone molars, four sets deep, turn in a four-hour cycle — take in, crush, hold, crush again. She does not bite so much as quarry. Feather-lichen, iron-rich scree, black basalt grit, the occasional obsidian shard dulled first in her cheek pouches: all of it goes in together. The lichen is fuel. The rock is food of another kind. In the long arched retort of her gut, bacterial cultures hold a dull, permanent red, and while the molars grind above, below there is smelting — slow chemistry at temperatures that would cook any ordinary animal from the inside. She is not burned by it because she is plumbed around it. Meltwater circulates in channels under her skin, picks up furnace heat, carries it out to the frostbitten edges of her feet and ears, comes back cooler, goes around again. Her blood does not so much flow as get worked, pumped in timed surges by the great piston in her chest: *thud* — and her whole frame shivers down to the soles — then a long, building pause while pressures mount, a hiss from the valves in her neck, a release of white steam that becomes snow before it reaches the mat, and then *thud* again.

You could set your watch by her if your watch ran very, very slow.

She was born beside an ingot, as all of them are. Not laid, not exactly — extruded, in a wet mineral sac, into the warm hollow left where her mother had voided copper the week before. For her first season she did not have a furnace of her own. She had a starter culture, fed mouth-to-mouth as regurgitated bacterial slurry from three different elders, none of whom were precisely her parent, and she kept warm by pressing her soft, unplated side against the ingot and against her siblings, in a cluster that rotated through the night so no one stayed on the cold outer edge too long. The ingot cooled over months. By the time it was only pleasantly warm to a human hand, her own gut had caught. She remembers — if memory is the right word for a route retained in the feet and in the gut — the first dull ache of heat in her center that was hers, that she had made, that she would now have to keep making every hour for the rest of her life.

Because that is the contract: stop, and freeze beautifully intact.

She does not sleep. She idles. In the months-long night her chewing slows, her vents close down to blue-lit slits, the herd presses in so tight that the spaces between their bodies become a second, shared climate, warmer than any one of them could manage alone. Arcs crawl over their backs from lichen to lichen. She listens — through her feet, through the shared thudding in the ground, through the ticking of a thousand gape-mouth fans on the ridge above — for the stresses in the plateau letting go in the dark, for new cracks sighing out their one hot breath. The herd will turn toward that breath, eventually. It may take them a season to arrive. They are in no hurry that you would recognize, and in a desperate hurry in the only way that matters: they never fully stop.

By day she grazes the lichen forests down to a bright nub and leaves behind her a trail that is immediately alive — flattened mat steaming in her footprints, dung full of half-refined ore and bacterial culture, seed-spiders dropping from her warm underside to colonize the print before it cools. Every few weeks something in her lower chambers finishes, cools, separates, and with a muscular shudder she voids an ingot from her flank. It falls ringing into the mat and stands there steaming, pure copper or nickel, almost machined in its regularity, and the plants turn toward it in the space of an afternoon. If she is old enough, and she is, other young will be brought to that ingot. If she is lucky, one of them is carrying her culture.

She perceives the world the way the plateau permits: light without warmth, so she does not trust brightness; vibration without distance, so she trusts the herd; heat only where it has been made, so she trusts her own seams, her neighbors' flanks, the brief geothermal vents, the sun trapped in the bladders of the mat-pines she crushes as she passes. Her eyes, set low and shielded under silica brows, are less for seeing than for measuring glare — how much light there is to store, how long until the long night, whether the sky is thick with the sail-kites whose shadows mean moths, whose moths mean a protein fall she can inhale through her secondary nostrils. She knows individuals not by faces but by rhythms. That one's piston misses every ninth beat. That one vents left before right. That one, her sister of the same ingot, walks with her furnace pitched a note lower, and when they press together — which they do for days at a time, sharing a moving wall of heat — their hearts gradually synchronize, and the grinding of their teeth falls into a shared four-hour round, and for a while the boundary between one animal and the next is mostly administrative.

The predators know that. The glass-toothed skaters come at the seams, at the outer edge of the herd in the dark, striking where plates separate when a leg lifts. She has a long white score along her third leg where one got in and was cooked for its trouble, sealed in as the wound mineralized. The pack that hunted her that night also hunted huddled, rotating their own warm core, outer members frost-feathered and half-blind — even murder here is a collective heating strategy. She does not hate them. Hatred would be a waste of temperature. She simply keeps to the middle when she is shedding plates, and keeps the young to the middle always.

She will die the way she was born, as a source of heat others gather around. First the chewing will slow past the point where the culture can hold its red. The piston will lengthen its pauses. The herd will not leave her — leaving her would open a cold gap — but smaller things will begin, politely, while she is still alive, to mine her: boring into a cooling flank plate for the nickel laid down in the bone, prying out spent ingots she no longer has the strength to void, eating the moss gardens off her back as the warmth under them fails. Her last exhalations will condense on her neighbors. When the furnace finally goes out she will stand for a long time, perfectly preserved, a hollow foundry cooling in the bright air, until seedlings root in her eye sockets and the lichen takes the plates, and something — a grazer, a new crack in the rising earth, the slow crush of the herd still moving — brings her down into the mat she fed on.

And the valley does not quiet. The thud just loses one voice. Steam still rises. Ingots still ring as they fall. Ridge after ridge to the horizon, the same arrangement repeats: a life that eats rock and makes metal and manufactures summer inside itself, that can never be alone and never be still and never be warm for free, moving at the pace of ice through the killing light — not a machine built to survive the White Foundry, but the Foundry itself, briefly up on six legs, looking around.
