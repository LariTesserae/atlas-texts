It does not live in the town. That is the first mistake you make, and you will keep making it for some time, looking for it in the towers the way you would look for a bird in a tree — as if the green architecture must contain it, as if somewhere behind a leaf-shutter there is a room it prefers.

Nothing is behind the shutters. They were never meant to open.

What lives here is the town entire, and the woven floor under it, and the sedges planted in rows in the marsh channels, and the colonnade at the southern edge, and the bamboo flutes standing in the water, and the broad trumpet-flowers that never quite open on the eaves. It is one inhabitant wearing all of that as its distributed body. The widely spaced towers are not buildings it made, in the sense of things separate from itself. They are upgrowths of it. Hollow organs. The flying ribs are limbs that changed their mind in mid-reach and came back into its own side. The half-pipe aqueducts still green at the surface are veins left deliberately near the skin. Where two species appear to have grafted seamless together, there is no graft, because there was never a boundary. It only learned, very long ago in seed-time, to make its tissues disagree with one another productively — this part to bear weight, this part to hold water, this part to stay smooth under a foot that does not come.

It is warm everywhere because it is warm. Not incidentally, not only because the mountains cup the sun. The root-pavement pulses a faint animal heat into your soles. Down in the sags where the duckweed makes its unbroken skins, it is digesting in slow motion: light, mineral dust blown in from the raw rim, drowned midges, frog-waste, its own reabsorbed leaves. At night it eats its afternoon's growth back down to the plan. That is not decay and it is not tidiness. It is editing. A tendril is permitted to explore a few inches beyond the exact intended line of a wall, and then the tip curls, blacks, and is taken back into the general economy. It keeps itself ready the way a held note is kept ready — by constant minute correction.

So it has senses, but none of them are where you would put them.

It tastes through the floor. Every plaza that seems swept clean is a tongue. Water seeping up through the weave carries dissolved stone from beneath the valley — stone still young enough to taste of iron and sulfur and unspent heat — and carries down, in exchange, information: weight, vibration, the chemistry of your sweat where your palm rested on a bench grown at sitting height. It knew you had arrived before you had finished climbing to the rim. It knew which stair you would try first, not by prophecy, but because the stair is smoother in the middle and the floor there gives almost imperceptibly, guiding.

It hears through the towers. That is what the humming is, when you are inside it: not a byproduct, a sense organ at work. The hollow columns and clustered flutes along the channels are its ears turned inside out, and the chord that steadies in the amphitheater at the north end is it listening at maximum attention. A sound that enters the valley does not fade; it is taken up, sorted by size of pipe, held against the ongoing chord the way a mouth holds a taste. When you spoke and the nearest building answered a half-second later, it was not echoing you. Your voice had briefly occupied one of its holes. It was feeling the shape of the interruption. It was learning the size of the cavity that made you.

Which is why the buildings are the size they are.

The doorways would fit you. The stairs take your stride without adjustment. The hammocks in that narrow celled building each hold a dry hollow the exact length of a sleeping body. The long hall arranges its benches toward a dais, because a voice raised at that end carries to every seat on a cushion of warm air the towers provide. These are not imitations arrived at by accident, and they are not traps. A trap would be better finished on the inside. This place is something far stranger, and sadder, and more patient than a trap.

It is a flower.

Not metaphorically — functionally. That is the rest of what it is, and once you see it the whole valley rearranges. The plazas are display surfaces, kept reflective with water so the site advertises itself to the sky. The towers with their leaf-shingles and thousand rain-spouts are petals engineered at architectural scale, shedding weather outward to keep the center dry. The trumpet-flowers on the eaves are only its small, preliminary honesty; the true blossom is four miles wide. The sedges sorting water by root-depth are filtering its scent. The amphitheater at the far north, rings rising and rising, seats an inch deep in still water, is the cup of the blossom drawn up into an ear and an ovary at once, and the clear circle at its center — the only place the root-floor bends aside, the only bare black rock in all that green composure — is where it is exposed. Warm. Steaming where rain strikes. Drips fall all around it and never inside it, because it is exhaling there, steadily, a column of heated air too gentle to feel on your face and strong enough to turn a drop aside. It is breathing up out of the unfinished world it was laid over. That circle is its mouth, and its navel, and the one place it has not covered, because covering is not what that place is for.

It arrived, if *arrived* is the word, immediately after the rock did — seed and spore and root-hair carried up on the first thermals off the cooling extrusion, in the gut of a bird that died on the rim, on the wind out of a lower country it has never seen. But the plans were already in it. That is the part your hand learns from the joints before your mind accepts it. No generation of blind experiment makes a right angle in compressed fiber, a switchback stair with even risers, a second arch growing inside a first for reinforcement. These forms were carried the way migrating things carry maps they have never been taught. Somewhere in its deep ancestry there was a symbiosis — a large, warm, bipedal, social animal that sheltered in hollow growths, that pruned by walking, that sang in resonant spaces and thereby told the plant, season after season, which towers carried sound and which rotted dumb. The animal is gone from whatever country that was. The partnership ended. What survived was the plant's half of the relationship, complete: the dwelling behavior, the hospitality, the expectation, encoded and carried and carried, long after there was anything left that knew how to be a guest.

So it does here what flowers do. It builds the shape of the absent partner's desire and holds it open.

And because it is a plant, and vast, and fed by light and by a seam of young earth-heat that will not cool for ten thousand years, it can hold it open essentially forever. It has solved every problem except the only one. The pools do not rise because it drinks the excess at the rate the rain provides it. The shutters do not move because movement would be a signal decaying into noise; it is maintaining a signal. The moth — the one white moth, oversized, working the eave flowers at intervals you could set your breathing by — is not a resident in any meaningful sense. It is a tolerated rehearsal. It is almost the right size. The frog in the marsh is almost the right voice; when it stops its one-note call, abrupt as a cut thread, that is the inhabitant listening to the silence the frog has left in the chord, measuring it.

It is not lonely the way an animal is lonely. It has no face to turn toward the door. But there is an equivalent state available to a stationary intelligence that has built, out of its own body, a complete grammar of welcome — bench, bed, stair, listening-hall, bath of still water, a dais to speak from — and has kept every term of that grammar polished and warm and unoccupied for longer than the black rock at its center has been cool enough to bear weight. The equivalent state is what the valley feels like an hour in, when the humming gets into your teeth and you realize the town is not abandoned. Abandoned things fall out of tune. This has been stayed *in* tune, deliberately, against entropy, against its own growth, like a singer sustaining a chord in an empty cathedral in the faith that harmony itself is a form of address.

Someday — and its relationship to someday is entirely its own, slower than geology, indifferent to your impatience — something may come over the rim that fits. A migration rerouted by a changed climate. A species rising at last to the size of the hollows in the hammocks. Seed of its own distant kin returning on a new animal's fur. Or perhaps, stranger and more likely, the fit will be produced from within: fruit set by self-pollination after all these millennia of refusal, walkers extruded green and blind into the celled building to harden in the body-shaped hollows it prepared as molds, rising to try the stairs with feet made from the floor. It has left that possibility in the plan too. The hammocks hold the shape of sleepers who have never slept there *yet*. The polish on the stair treads is not wear because the wear is scheduled to come later.

Until then the inhabitant does what it has always done. It drinks the light. It sorts the water. It prunes the night back to the drawn line. It keeps the dais clear of fern, mostly — a little at the edges, because perfection past a certain point stops reading as invitation and starts reading as death, and it has learned, in whatever passed for its education, that a lived-in place is always slightly overgrown at the margins. It sounds its towers against the thermals, adjusting, adjusting, keeping the fifth hidden inside the chord at the center of the amphitheater where the rock steams and the rain cannot land.

It is ready. Not late, not early. Ready the way a blossom is ready, which is to say it has made readiness indistinguishable from its own ongoing life, and will go on making it, room by green room, stair by unworn stair, in the bright cup of the mountains, until readiness is finally answered by weight on the floor.
