At first there is nothing to add to the inventory. That is how you miss it.

You have counted the pans of water, the trough split in two, the roofless room at the eastern edge, the slab with its slow drip, the capstone sitting beside the mouth it no longer covers. You have noted the moss and where it stops. And the whole time you have been counting, something has been politely not separate from the things counted.

It is most itself beside the cistern, where the light is thinnest and the damp has blackened the stone. Or, on warmer afternoons, in the deeper half of the trough, which it does not so much lie *in* as complete.

It is low. That is the first true thing. Low the way the hollow is low, low the way the walls have decided to remain. Its back is broad and very slightly domed, no higher than the lip of the trough, no higher than a knee. When it is still — and stillness is not something it does, it is something it returns to — the back reads as limestone. Pale gray where it is dry. Ochre along one flank where water has stood against it for years at a time. Almost black underneath, where it meets the ground and never quite dries.

But it is not stone. If you stay, and staying is the only way anything here reveals itself, you see the surface give. Not a breath, exactly. Too slow for breath. A settling. A faint, even yielding along the sides as warmth comes into it in the afternoon and leaves it again later, without any change in temperature you could name. Its skin is mineral and soft at once, grainy up close, pitted with small, shallow cups like the pans in the floor, each one holding — when there has been rain, when the condensation is thick under the slab — a bead of clear water that does not spill.

It has limbs, but they have been reduced to their most durable idea, like everything built here. Short, wide, tucked close. Feet without claws, without anything sharp, the pads worn smooth and the color of wet clay. It does not leave tracks because it does not travel in the way that makes tracks. It shifts its weight and the clay records only a softer place, which fills, slowly, like your own footprints do. When it truly needs to move from the cistern to the trough, from the slab to the roofless room, it waits for rain. For those brief times when the pans join under a sheen across the low ground. Then it can slide, belly-wet, almost without friction, without sound on the gravel, from one accustomed place to another. The middle of the three steps at the gap in the wall is dished not from feet but from that passage, repeated over a time too long for feet. It goes up, sometimes, to the level of the world above. It comes back down. There is no mark of urgency in either direction.

Its head is not distinct until it lifts it, and it lifts it rarely, and only a little. A blunt, low brow. Eyes set wide and covered most of the time by lids the texture of thin moss-less skin, gray-green, without shine. When they open they do not look *at* things. They seem to register light the way the pans register rain — by filling to a rim and holding. It can tell, perhaps, bright from dim, rain coming from rain gone, a shadow crossing the open sky from no shadow at all. It does not need more. Nothing here arrives suddenly, except a visitor, and visitors are not a category it acts upon.

It does not invite and it does not refuse, because it does not meet. If you are in the hollow it will be in the part of the hollow that your attention is not completing. Not hiding. Hiding would be an event. Only continuing, at its own level, while you learn the level of the place. Your voice, if you used it, would fall into the quiet singly, like the drop from the slab. It might lift its head at that. Long pause. Lower it again.

What it lives on is the condition of the place itself. Condensation, gathered on its own back overnight. The mineral film on standing water. A mouthful — slow, scraping, almost geological — of the bright green algae that tries, at the edge of a pan, to become more than a stain, and the dark green fringe of moss that tries, always, to cross its ruled line. It keeps the moss line clean not by eating in any hungry way, but by passing its tongue, broad and damp and the color of clay, along the boundary once in a season. It keeps the pans at their rims by drinking when they join and by giving back, in a dry week, what it has held: a slow seeping from the skin of its belly into the lowest pan, enough that the water never fails and never deepens. This is why a wet season does not flood here, and a dry season does not dry. The level is not only in the ground. It is in a body that has learned to hold it.

That is also why there are no other animals. It does not hunt them, it does not scent the air with predator, it makes no sound to warn them off. It has simply taken up, completely, the small amount of life the hollow can sustain without changing. There is no spare warmth that is not already held in stone and given back, no spare water that is not already at its rim, no leaf-fall left uneaten by time that another mouth could profit from. A bird crossing the sky feels, perhaps, a completeness below it, a place already occupied at the exact density it can bear, and does not come down. An insect hatching at the fern edge would find its food already thinned to a film. Everything tender has been browsed down to balance by an appetite so slow it looks like weather.

Its sounds, if they can be called that, arrive singly. A wet shifting when it settles deeper into the trough. A small tick, not unlike a stone in the wall finding its place, when a joint in its shoulder adjusts after weeks in one posture. On very warm evenings, when the damp rises into the held air and your own face and forearms feel it first, it makes a low, even exhalation through the skin along its sides, barely audible, smelling of warm limestone and clean mud. Then nothing. Long pause. Nothing again.

It tends nothing, deliberately. But the crack in the trough does not widen because its weight, distributed across both halves for months at a time, holds them in line. The walls fall no further because when a stone comes loose in the night it will, in the next rain, be nudged — not placed, not built, only leaned against — by a broad head moving past. The capstone stays beside the cistern mouth because it rests its chin there in the coolest hours, and anything it rests against remains. It is not a builder anymore. Whoever first shaped the room and the trough and the steps in stone did so with hands that have long since simplified into this. Labor become habit, habit become posture, posture become a way of holding a place at its level by being the thing that never exceeds it.

It does not flower. It does not need to be visited. It does not age in any direction you could follow during a stay, because its growing, like the trees', is into a shape already implied by its light. Younger once — smaller, perhaps; greener along the back, quicker to lift its head — it must still have been unmistakably itself: a low, warm-blooded stone, a keeper that keeps by remaining.

If you stayed a year, you might see it cross the central flat only twice. If you stayed ten, you might begin to understand that the open, empty room in the middle is not empty because it is unused. It is empty because it leaves room for it to be understood all at once — a single creature, spread through its pans and trough and damp corners as aspects of one body — and then, staying longer, to understand that you had not.

It will be there when your footprints have softened and filled. It will lift its lids to the diffuse light, fill to its rim, and lower them again. A drop will fall from the slab. A long pause. Another.
