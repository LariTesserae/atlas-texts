The technician is not the one who lives here.

He is legible in the huts — the sealed suit hung against a wall, the magnetised cups, the bed made straight and then left long enough for a film of green dust to settle evenly over the blanket — but he is only an interval. Weeks pass between one entry in the logbook and the next, in the same careful hand. He comes up through the thin air, tends what needs tending, and goes down again.

The lizard is not resident either, though it pretends better. Flat on the warm side of a cable conduit, the colour of bleached bone, it will stay so still that the eye edits it out. The moth, dark, working silently from flower to flower, is closer. But they are both visitors to a system that was already complete without them.

The resident is what you have been mistaking for the valley floor.

At first it looks like one of the cushion-plants. Perhaps a little too regular, a little too large — a low dome a metre and a half across, packed tight against the base of a condensation vane where the leakage keeps a permanent damp halo in the moss. Its surface is divided into pads, each pad blue-green and slightly waxy, furred white at the margins. The pads overlap like leaves. Some are scarred. Some carry a neat, identical fringe of moss in a collar on the shaded side, just as the pylons do. If you mark its position against a rock in the morning and come back at noon, you will doubt your mark. It appears to have turned, only a fraction, following no sun you can track.

That slowness is not camouflage. It is tempo.

It moves the way the mirrors move, the way the flywheels in their open pits move: too gradually to be witnessed, fast enough to have arrived by the time you look away. A rotation begun at dawn is completed after dark. In a day it can cross perhaps the length of its own body, travelling not over the moss but through it, its underside unpeeling and re-adhering so softly that it makes less impression than a hand brushed over wet ground. That is why the tracks in the valley are always old. They are not footprints at all, but the long, shallow troughs where one has rested for a season or a year, edges already softened and healed over with new growth, filling each evening with clear water.

Underneath, it is not rooted, exactly, and not quite animal. The belly is pale and tender as the underside of a leaf, dense with fine, root-like hairs that find purchase in grit, in the vesicular hollows of the young rock, in the seams between pipe and pylon. Through those hairs it drinks, and through them it listens — if listening is the word for a sense that arrives entirely as pressure. Sound will not carry here, and it has no use for it. It has no voice, no ears to speak of, no call. What it knows of the world comes up through its whole ventral surface: the faint charge in a cable felt as a tingling change in moisture, the tread of a service crawler minutes before the crawler rounds the slope, the technician's boots coming station to station through rock, the flywheels spending their stored noon in the dark in one long, even tremor.

Its eyes, when it chooses to open them, are set low around the rim of the dome, half-lidded slits under thickened pads, protected from the hard white glare by a nictitating film the colour of bottle glass. It does not see sharply. It sees light and the refusal of light: the advancing edge of a shadow across stones, the particular brightness that means frost, the rigid columns of heated air over the furnace chambers. The rest of its perception is chemical and patient. A tongue — broad, dark, almost indistinguishable from a wet leaf — emerges in the dusk hour and moves from pad to pad, from vane to rock point, tasting the day's work: mineral condensate, ozone, spores, the hot-metal tang where the furnaces are cooling.

For most of the day it does nothing that would separate it from vegetation, because for most of the day there is nothing to do. The brilliance up here has no warmth in it; its blood, if the slow fluid moving through its pads counts as blood, stays close to the temperature of shade. It does not hunt in any active sense. It lets the valley come. A moth settling to drink from the cups of its pads may find the cup closing, not with a snap but with the unhurried persistence of a hand shutting in sleep. Fern spores drifting from the mirror fields dust its back and are gradually worked into the fur at its margins. It combs fog the way the plants taught it to — or perhaps it is the other way around. The blue-green pads hold symbionts in their tissues, small green lives that take the merciless light and make sugar from it, and pay for their lodging in the surplus. It is fed from above by light it cannot look at, and from below by water that never becomes a stream.

That is why it keeps to the machines. A wild gully might give it an evening of wetness; a condensation vane gives it a climate. It positions its dome where dripping is guaranteed, one flank in permanent shadow so its underside stays cold and damp, the other offered to the vane's slow pulse. Vines have learned the warm side of the conduits; it has learned a more precise geography. It rests where moss collars keep the metal from freezing outright, where a root mat it does not have to maintain is already holding last week's dew. In return — though return implies intention, and intention at this speed is hard to judge — its body shades the base of the vane and lowers the temperature there, and its hairs knit the loose scree together so the pipes are not buried. The installation leaks water for the plants to steal, and it is one of the things that has learned to steal it best.

You can see it drink inside the transparent pipes, if you watch long enough. A droplet on an inclined section hesitates. It gathers itself. It climbs a few centimetres against its own weight, toward the coldest metal — toward the point nearest its tongue pressed to the pipe's outer wall, cooling it further, drawing the water through by a gradient too subtle for the pumps to register. The production records, if anyone kept them in those terms, would show a loss distributed so evenly across so many years that it looks like evaporation.

It has no season so much as a cycle borrowed from the machinery. When the storage bladders swell through the afternoon and breathe out at night, it swells almost imperceptibly too, pads firming with taken water. When the furnace chambers open on their days-long cycle, it orients itself toward the valley centre for the duration, rim-eyes open, as if attending to something. The pale residue the crawler-haulers leave in the collection trays — mineral salts, spore dust, a finespun grey ash — it will cross a whole mirror field to reach, moving a few centimetres an hour for days, and spend a night with its tongue in the tray.

There may be more than one. That is the difficulty. The domes pressed to the vanes on the eastern slope share a pattern of scarring, a particular way the pads divide, that the ones near the furnace array do not. Between the domes, under the layered ferns and the moss three and four deep, something continuous moves water and sugar and signals in pulses too slow for a human pulse to imitate — a mat, a network, a second set of pipes grown rather than laid. It is possible that each dome is an individual that has lived here since the arrays were young, growing at the rate of stone. It is also possible that they are organs of attention belonging to one body whose true extent is the valley's root system, surfacing where the machines make living easiest, the way fingertips surface at the end of an arm buried to the shoulder.

Its age shows in the way frost treats it. At night, when the temperature drops below dew point all at once and the whole bowl briefly, secretly sweats, the domes are the last surfaces to go still. They take in the wetness with an audible — almost audible — series of tiny ticks, droplets striking and being absorbed. Then the cold seals everything. Frost feathers over its back pad by pad, following the divisions between them like a map being redrawn in white. It does not shiver it off. It lets itself be sealed in, metabolism sinking toward the temperature of the rock, the green symbionts in its tissues darkening to near black. If you put your palm to it then, cautiously, you would feel neither plant nor animal warmth, only a cold that is differently textured from stone, and beneath it, far down, something that might after a long minute be a heartbeat, or might be a flywheel in a pit across the valley spending its energy through the ground.

It endures the technician the way it endures weather. When the suit comes creaking softly through the fern, the rim-eyes close in sequence, from the side nearest the disturbance, a slow wave of shutting. The pads draw marginally tighter. To a man checking vane seals and wiper arms, recording spore yield and noting that the moss seems thicker again this year around Array Four, it is simply part of what he has been trained not to clear away, because clearing it reduces condensate efficiency by some stubborn, unexplained percentage.

He makes his entry. He leaves. The suit hangs empty in the hut for an hour like something shed, and then it is gone downhill.

The dome remains at the base of the vane. By evening it will have turned the fraction of a degree it had been intending since dawn. By next week it will be against a different fin, where the drip falls cleaner. In a year it may have crossed to the shaded side entirely. In the hour before the light goes — not dimming, but thickening in the blue and then switching off the sharp peaks all at once — its tongue comes out again into the valley's one permitted sound, the near, private dripping in the undergrowth, and it tastes the water that the air has given up, and the machines have caught, and the plants have held, and no river has ever carried away.
