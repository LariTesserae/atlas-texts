If you ask who lives in the pit, the first honest answer is: everything that has ever fallen in.

Mites live in the felt on the underside of leaves and lay eggs that dry like dust and wait. Ants live in the seams of bark, in root-seams, in the seams of any visitor who stands still too long. Beetles the size of a hand live at the rim, harvesting what the wind brings. Lizards live exactly where the light makes coins on the branches. Snakes live as moving vine-shadows. Birds live at every height, arguing over branches that will be dead by the end of the week. Bats live backward, in the cliff cracks, waiting for their turn at the sky.

But most of those are tenants of a season, or an hour. They hatch, feed, shout, and are eaten. The valley's real inhabitants never come up at all.

You can hear them before anything else, underneath everything else.

*Boom. Boom-boom. Boom.*

Not a call. A sentence sent through roots.

They live in the dark between the root-mat and the fermenting earth below it — in that hot, hollow, half-collapsed world where fallen trunks rot without ever getting wet, where white larvae seethe in black mulch and the air is thick enough to make your eyes water. Up top, visitors bounce across the mat like insects on a drumhead. Below, the makers of the drum are at work.

They are about the weight of a pig, and built low and wide for pushing. Their shoulders do most of the living. Their forefeet are not feet so much as spades — broad, horn-nailed, webbed with callus — for tearing root bundles and packing them again behind them. Their hindquarters are smaller, almost an afterthought. They move by shoving the forest aside and letting it close over them.

They have almost no eyes. What remains are two amber pits set deep under bone, sensitive perhaps to the difference between canopy shade and a fresh tree-fall far above, nothing more. They have no external ears at all. Their heads are smooth, sealed wedges of skull and leather. Sound would be useless to them as air anyway. The whole valley is already screaming.

Instead, they hear with their feet, their jawbones, their bellies pressed to the mat.

Place a palm against a root down there and you will feel their country talking: quick triple taps for rich, thin mat. A slow, rolling thump for danger. Families keeping contact while they forage out of sight of each other. Young answering their mothers, half a beat late. Predators have learned to lie among the roots and counterfeit the pattern — *here, here, the mat is thin, the mat is rich* — and so no message is ever repeated the same way twice. Every colony has an accent. Every adult adds a private irregularity, a drag or a double-strike, the way a person signs a name.

Their skin is their water bottle. It is grey-brown, hairless except for stiff sensory bristles along the flanks, and it sweats nothing. It folds in loose, oily pleats at the neck and shoulders, slippery with a resinous secretion that makes them smell like the trunks they rub against and discourages most biting things. Their nostrils close to slits. Their mouths close and seal. They breathe in short, economical sips through filters of bristle, and even their breath is recycled in convoluted passages inside the skull, moisture caught before it can leave.

They never drink, because there is nothing to drink.

They eat the valley from the inside and extract its locked water the slow way: ferment-mulch, larvae, beetle grubs fat on rot, the pith of fallen gel-trunks, roots swollen with stored syrup. Their teeth never stop growing — flat crushers at the back for fiber, two curved chisels in front for opening a trunk just enough to take the gel and no more, so the tree skins over and lives. Their urine comes out as a paste that dries to yellow crystals. Their droppings are dry pellets, already half soil. Very little leaves them that the valley cannot immediately reuse.

They do not build homes. A home would be a death trap in a place where the floor invents itself daily. They build hollows the way a swimmer makes a wake — temporarily. A female about to give birth packs a chamber tight under a thick root braid, lines it with chewed husk, and stays only until the young can grip her back with their spade-feet. After that the family drifts, a loose procession through the under-mat, collapsing old tunnels, opening new ones, sleeping in shifts with their heads all turned toward different vibrations.

Sleeping, they contract. In the cooling night their great shoulder muscles tick and creak, and they press together to share what little metabolic water their skins give off as vapor, licking it from each other's pleats before it can escape.

Their young are born blind even by their parents' standards, pink, sealed, silent. For the first days they do not thump. They knead. Tiny vibrations against the mother's belly that gradually become rhythm, then pattern, then — sometime in the second week, when their skull plates harden enough to carry it — their first true boom, felt by the whole local mat. Other adults answer. It is not celebration. It is registration. Another weight has joined the floor.

And they *are* the floor. This is the thing visitors, stamping messages clumsily through the roots from above, only half understand. The boomers keep the valley from choking on its own speed. They thin the mat where it grows too thick to bear. They collapse the ferment-pits before gases can build to a killing pocket. They drag fallen fruit and carcasses down into the dark where larvae and fungus can unlock them. The huge bald-headed birds that wait all day with such terrible patience up top are waiting, specifically, for the boomers to make a mistake — to open the wrong chamber, expose the wrong tunnel, roll a dead elder too close to the surface.

Because they are also, inevitably, soil.

One that dies in the open underlayer is gone in days: first the flies, then the larvae, then roots questing in white thousands into the carcass, seedlings already splitting its ribs when it is found. One that dies deep may be eaten by its own family, not from hunger so much as thrift — water that expensive is not buried. Either way, nothing is wasted. A boomer walking through the ferment dark is walking, quite literally, through its grandparents.

They have no fire, no tools, no platforms, no shrines, no reason to climb toward a sky they would experience only as heat on their sealed skulls and a sudden terrifying thinness in the vibrations. The rope ladder means nothing to them. The salt desert above the rim might as well be the surface of the moon.

They know the valley not as green, not as loud, not as beautiful. Up on the surface those words still belong to eyes and ears. Down below, the world is pressure and tempo: the tick of growing cellulose, the snap of a root under tension, the distant crash-language of a falling tree sending its shock through everything, the patter of a hundred seedlings racing for the light-hole it left.

To them, the forest is one long, continuous announcement that food is arriving, ground is changing, family is near, death is useful, move on, pack this, open that.

*Here,* they say with their shoulders against the roots, all day and all night, in a code that never quite repeats.

*Here. Here. The mat is thin. The mat is rich. We are underneath it, making it.*

And the whole roaring pit, green wall to green wall, answers by growing on top of them.
