If you meet one of them out on the open mat, you will hear them long before you see them, and you will probably hear them wrong the first time.

The Plant will have their voice ready. It keeps boot-steps in its hollow Trunks and coughs in its root-wells and gives them back days later, perfect and directionless. So they do not shout across the emptiness the way you would expect people in an empty country to shout. They tap.

A short pattern through the soles of the feet, felt in the knees. A low hum with the mouth closed, that makes the mat under you answer sympathetically. A song-stanza sung straight down, into a cut root, addressed to whoever is traveling underneath. When a real person replies, they will do it three times — once to announce, once to let the Plant steal it, and a third time, slightly changed, so you know there is still a throat behind the echo.

Then, out of the star-dark, a shape.

They are not tall, despite the long-legged animals they hunt. The thin air and the spring of the ground have made them broad rather than high: broad chests that move slowly even at a run, broad feet in soft, many-layered boots of cured leaf and grazer-hide, splayed to read the mat. Their center is low. They travel in a kind of deliberate crouch over uncertain ground, one hand often trailing, palm down, fingertips spread on the warm felt to feel for bog-breath, tunnel collapse, the first lift of a growth pulse beginning under them.

Their faces, when the hood comes back, are startling the first time because of the eyes.

Not monstrous — just tuned past the point you are used to. Huge, dark, wet-looking eyes that catch the aurora and the faint ground-phosphor and the green smudge of a bog a mile off and make something of it. In the one grey hour of summer sun they squint to slits and wear slatted masks of bone; most of the year they go bare-eyed and blink very seldom, and a thin, clear inner lid flicks across when wind or pollen or Trunk-ash is blowing. Pupils so dilated that the iris is only a thread of color — grey, brown, a startling moss green — around a pool.

They protect that sensitivity like a tool. No one looks directly at a fire. Firelight is for the backs of the hands, for warming tallow, for seeing the edge of a knife. Children are taught to find their mother in a crowded tunnel — crowded meaning six people — by smell and by the particular tick of her breathing through her teeth, before they are taught to find her by sight.

What they are rich in, besides eyes, is hearing and patience.

Outer ears are small and can be folded flat by a muscle against the skull when the Trunks really start to sing, because the bass can make an unprotected ear bleed. But the inner hearing is exquisite in a way that is almost social. They can separate the true runner-herd drumming up through the mat from the Plant’s memory of a herd last week. They know the age of a call by the fraying on its echo. A good listener, an old one, sits with her cheek pressed to a Trunk wall in a hollow chamber and reads the plateau — weather coming two valleys away, a skin-sheet tearing loose somewhere above the cloud, seed-pods warming toward split — the way you might read weather in the ache of a joint, except she will name places and distances.

They smell of the place compounded: sweet mat-smoke in the hair, sour tallow worked into every seam, iron sap on the hands, animal musk, the faintly rotten green stink of the tunnels. Their skin is pale where it is covered, not from any delicacy but from a lifetime without sun, and weather-burned to a dark, wind-polished brown where it isn’t. Frost has taken most of them somewhere by adulthood — a white notch in an ear, a fingertip smooth as a pebble, a toe gone cleanly and cauterized with frozen sap. They decorate the losses without sentiment: a bead on a cord for a finger, a carved tooth on a string where a tooth was lost to chewing frozen pod-pulp too young, braided Trunk-fiber rings around scars where the mat tried to grow over a sleeper who forgot, or was too sick, to tether.

Because they all tether. That is the first thing a child learns and the last thing an old person gives up. A cord at night, always — to a stone footing, to a Trunk rib, to another person. Not thick rope. Rope is wealth and they waste none of it. A wrist-loop of braided fiber, a waist-line while traveling in file across the open, children clipped between adults like beads. In sleep they turn and sigh and the line goes taut and slack and taut, and you can lie awake in a hut listening to the whole family breathing at slightly different distances from the wall.

There are, loosely, three ways to be a person here, and they marry and quarrel and trade across all three.

The mat-folk live mostly *in* the Plant. They maintain the hatch-openings with their woven doors, they enlarge grazer tunnels, they sleep in fermentation warmth while the frost on the surface would kill them in an hour. Down there it is never quite silent and never quite still: roots tick, blind beetles the size of dogs pass in their slow trains cropping the fungal gardens off the Plant’s shed skins, shaggy under-grazers shove past with their leaf-ears folded back, eating the world and leaving architecture behind them. Mat children are born down there into dark that is total and warm, and some of them keep the habit as adults of feeling their way with the backs of their hands, because the delicate skin there wasn’t callused by surface wind. They are the best tunnel navigators, the keepers of bog-lore, the ones who know which marsh steams clean and which one breathes out a sweetness after a quake that will lay a whole sleeping burrow down forever. They harvest fungus and beetle-eggs and root-honey, and they are slightly despised and completely depended upon by everyone else, as people who live in the warm gut of the world often are.

The Trunk-folk live vertically. They cut doorways into living Trunks — not deep, never greedy, a chamber and then another chamber when the first begins to sigh and narrow — and move in for ten years, twenty, a generation of soot-darkened, resonant domesticity. A Trunk hut is loud inside. Every footstep is reported back by the well above and below. Babies learn early not to cry at certain pitches that set the whole column ringing. Cooking, lovemaking, quarrels, deaths — the Trunk keeps it all, imperfectly, and will play fragments back to the grandchildren. Trunk families hang their tools and dried meat and sleeping furs from pegs driven into flesh that slowly grows around the peg; moving out means leaving half your household embedded, and they leave a bead necklace or a carved mark in the closing scar the way you might leave flowers, or an apology. They harvest ice from the fluted ribs, catch the dawn weep in leaf cups, drop lines into the hollows for the feathered frog-bats that nest there, and listen upward for the canopy things.

No one has a good description of the canopy things. Everyone has a recording of one, in wood. Their calls fall down the Trunk wells days late, looped and deepened, predator and prey hopelessly confused. Sometimes a feather comes down that is not a feather — a long, iridescent scale with a barbed edge, turning for twenty minutes through still air. Sometimes a shadow, on the clearest star night, crosses a patch of sky in the wrong place, too low for cloud, too slow for falling leaf. The Trunk-folk, sensibly, keep their hatch-ropes strong and do not leave meat uncovered on a roof.

And then there are the surface runners, the ones you are most likely to meet — if *meet* is the word for a distant tap answered in your boot-soles. They follow the herds of long-legged, black-coated runners that travel between Trunks in their thousands, breath making low fog, feet drumming the hollow ground into a map you can navigate by. A hunting family will travel days inside the vibration of a herd, never quite seeing it, eating when the animals eat, sleeping tethered in a line downwind during the growth pulses that send new green swelling across the plateau. They know pod-fall by concussion, skin-fall by the sudden silence where a sheet has cut the wind. Their huts, when they bother, are a single cured leaf weighted with obsidian rubble, a windbreak, a fire that smokes more than it flames. They own almost nothing that is not rope, tallow, a stone saw, a bone knife, a story. Their songs are the maps and the maps change every singing, because last season’s Trunk-bud is this season’s tower and last winter’s bog walked half a valley west.

All of them eat mostly what the Plant gives up unwillingly: amber root-honey chipped from cuts, steamed pod-pulp when a fall pounds a square mile flat and fertile, fungal sheets from the tunnels, fat under-grazers trapped in their own burrows, runner meat in glut and then nothing for days. Nothing is wasted because thin air makes hunger a kind of clarity. A joint is cracked for marrow in silence, and the bone afterward is a tool, a toggle, a flute that will play one true note in a Trunk mouth and call — or seem to call — something down.

They are scattered because the place enforces it. A settlement is a hut, maybe two. A crowd is thirty, for the pollen noon. When the sun finally shows its sliver at high summer and the whole plateau steams and the Canopy miles above opens something unseen, they come in from tunnels and Trunks and herd-lines and stand coated in falling luminous dust, mouths open despite the taste of cold metal and herbs, children held up on shoulders into the brief light. It gets into the lungs and the seams and the stored water. They believe — practical, therefore sacred — that it is what lets them hear the third call at all, the real one under the copy. Then the Trunks inhale, every loose fiber on the plateau streams spookily upward, ears pop in their rows, and the dark closes again and they scatter before the growth starts.

The animals they live among are not quite separate in their minds from weather, or from the Plant, or from each other. The under-grazers are fellow tenants whose tunnels become roads. The dog-beetles are slow, blind livestock that no one quite tamed but everyone steers by planting fungus where they want a passage kept open. The moth-storms off the bogs are a season and a food and a navigation hazard. The frog-bats drop at long dusk in squeaking choruses to hunt them, and are in turn hunted, half-seen, by things in the Trunk wells gliding on membranes that make no sound of their own — the Plant provides their sound later, elsewhere.

It is, in the end, hard to say who here is the inhabitant and who is the place. A single organism makes the floor, the towers, the climate, the echoes, half the food, most of the danger. Mountains keep shrugging up underneath it and being stitched down. Animals make tunnels that people enlarge, people cut chambers that bats roost in, herds follow growth that follows pod-fall that follows a flowering no one has ever seen with their own eyes.

And the people go on in the middle of it, tethered line to line, enormous-eyed, listening for the third voice, renewing their songs because a stale stanza becomes a lie, waking early to check whether the ground has grown over anyone in the night — and if it has, cutting them out gently, apologizing to both the sleeper and the Plant, and moving, as everyone and everything here eventually moves, a little further across the body of the world.
