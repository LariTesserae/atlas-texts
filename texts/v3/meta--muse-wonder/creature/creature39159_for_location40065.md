First you would mistake them for people enduring something.

They are not. They are people *using* something, the way other people use a river.

They are low-voiced, even when they can afford to be loud. That is the first thing you notice at dusk, when the wind drops off the rim and the bowl at last goes quiet. They emerge from the deep rooms — blinking, unstoppering their ears, working their jaws side to side with the palms of their hands — and they do not shout for joy at being able to hear again. They whisper. Then they murmur. Then, after a while, they talk, but always as if sound were water in that place: measured out, not spilled.

Nobody there wastes a noise.

Their ears are ordinary to look at, perhaps a little scarred at the rim from a lifetime of plugs being pushed in and twisted out. The plugs themselves are the true local garment. Before sandals, before a name is quite settled, a child is fitted for them: warm beeswax kneaded with resin and a little seed oil, pressed into the small hot ear and allowed to set, then pierced with a thread so they can hang around the neck when not in use. Adults wear theirs on cords, on bracelets, in little leather pouches at the belt, the way other towns carry keys. A good pair lasts years. A bad fit is not merely uncomfortable; it is dangerous. At noon, in the uncovered streets, an unprotected ear can ache for a day. Near the center, it can bleed.

And yet they are not deafened people. Strangely, the opposite. Living inside a tuned instrument has made them listeners of impossible refinement. A grandmother mending cord in a shuttered inner room, her plugs in, the whole house rattling softly around her, will pause and tilt her head and sign to her daughter: *third stone, east rim, going flat.* She will be right. They hear through their teeth, through the soles of their feet on the terrace boards, through the sympathetic buzzing of the jars in the pantry. They can distinguish, blindfolded, between the bowl's chord on a clean windy day and the same chord when sand has drifted into one of the rim-flutes, or when a retaining wall two terraces below has bulged half an inch into a new shape. For them pitch is not music first. It is information. It is the weather report, the structural survey, the town crier, delivered continuously whether anyone wants it or not.

Their spoken language reflects that. At dusk they speak in a low, almost sung register, vowels held very pure, consonants softened so as not to waste effort — after a day of pressure in the chest, shouting feels obscene — and every child grows up knowing, in the body, what a fifth is, what a true octave feels like in the sternum. Their singing, when the council singers perform it from the ledge at dark, is not decorative. It is calibration. Long notes passed from voice to voice, adjusted by a finger's width, until the human chord agrees with the memory of the day's stone chord. Babies fall asleep to it. Old people die having heard some version of it almost every night of their lives.

But for most of the day they do not speak at all.

The sign language is not an emergency substitute. It is, if anything, their first language, the one dreams apparently come in. It uses the whole upper body. Hands shape the nouns; shoulders and eyebrows carry grammar; a tapped elbow means a question about the past, a stamp on the hollow threshold-board — felt, not heard, through the floor — means *danger*, *come here*, *the baby is awake*, depending on rhythm. Lovers conduct long arguments across a terrace without either of them unplugging an ear. Craftsmen working in the rim workshops, where the wind hits first and conversation is hopeless from breakfast onward, hold entire technical disputes in gestures, tools still in hand. Joke-telling loses nothing. If anything it gains a certain ruthless timing, because a punchline delivered by suddenly going completely, theatrically still in the middle of all that clattering doorway-junk and shivering air reads even from two rings away.

Their bodies are adapted in smaller, less obvious ways. They walk the pan with their chins tucked and eyes narrowed, not from submission but from glare, and so among themselves they have the habit of raising the face fully when addressing someone — a local sign of honesty, of giving you their whole attention, since indoors and at evening it costs them something to do it. Their skin is darkened and then whitened again by salt dust worked into every crease; they anoint it with rendered fat and plant oil, so that at dusk they shine faintly. Calluses form not just on palms and feet but on the fingertips, from feeling for vibration in walls, in jar-rims, in the stems of plants to tell whether pollination has taken. The best tuners have fingertips so sensitive they are excused from rough work in winter and treated, mildly, like aristocrats.

There are aristocracies, of a sort, though small ones. The rim-families live highest, nearest the flutes, and inherit the rasps, the mallets, the wet-clay recipes, and the dangerous honor of climbing out at dawn — the only safe hour — to retune a stone that the night's cold has cracked. The granary ring keeps the sound-milled grain and knows, to the handful, what the city can eat. Lowest of all, in the cool under the houses, sit the water-keepers, who guard the sealed jars brought in by mule train twice a year and dole out drinking water by the cup under law so old no one argues with it except teenagers, briefly. Between these, the rest — potters, dyers, weavers, flakers of stone, menders of roofs — live where their grandparents lived, in houses whose doors are painted and repainted in ochre, black resin, powdered blue, each color part pigment and part address, part ward, part family signature legible even at noon through a slit eye.

The town feels half-empty because in a sense it always is. The population that the stored water can support is hard and known, and it has not grown in living memory. Salt must be gathered, and salt must be traded, and so for months of the year a portion of every family is out on the pan with the mules — gone to the distant wells, to the markets where a skin of water can be bought with a sack of white plates scraped from their own horizon. Their houses are sealed and kept. A neighbor sweeps the step. A cousin checks the roof mat after storms. Children are taught whose sealed door is whose, and that you do not speak of an absent household as abandoned. They are simply at the other end of the city's life, the part that happens silently, out under the glare, where there is no bowl to sing for them and they must do all their milling and cracking and threshing the slow way, by hand, and dream — they will tell you this themselves, laughing, when they return — of noise.

Which means the plants are in some ways the truest permanent residents.

The people come and go. The vines stay. There are stems along the council ledge thick as a man's wrist, black-barked, trained along the same wire for five generations, and the family that lives beneath one will introduce it, quite naturally, as the older occupant of the terrace. Cut a leaf — they rarely do — and it does not bleed sap like a green thing should. It exudes a bitter, translucent gel, or a sour pulp, or a resinous oil that smells of hot stone and seals the wound almost as you watch. Nothing thin, nothing impatient grows here. A leaf takes a season to unfold. A hanging trumpet flower may be a decade returning to full size after damage. The shrubs keep their foliage folded tight as fists all day against the light and only at night, when the moths come out, loosen into their true softness.

They are never watered. To pour drinking water on a plant would be a kind of civic madness. Instead their roots abandon the downward search entirely and go sideways, horizontally, like the town itself, into the shaded backs of walls, into mortar seams, into the cool joint where terrace meets bedrock, spreading mats of fine pale hairs that harvest dew before dawn and whatever condensation the night cooling draws out of the stone. The people help them without water: they position awnings to funnel the dawn damp, they leave certain walls unplastered so the root-hairs can breathe, they carry night-soil and ash and cracked seed-husk from the central circle and pack it around the root crowns. In return the vines shade the houses, cool the terraces, hold the mud-brick in a living net, and feed them — the broad leaves cut and cooked down, the fruits that set only because noon shakes the pollen loose, the seeds pressed for the oil that lights their lamps and softens their earplugs and keeps their skin from splitting.

No bees do this work. The bowl's other animals are too few and too specialized. There are pallid lizards in the wall-cracks, fast as thrown string, who live on the black salt-beetles that live in turn on spilled grain in the lower stores; the beetles thrive on vibration, which shakes a steady rain of dust and husk down to them, and are tolerated almost as livestock. A single hawk circles at evening, looks into the bowl, and declines it. And there are the moths — five, six, perhaps seven, no one is entirely sure and no one would ever catch one to count — large, pale, clumsy night creatures that emerge when the trumpet flowers open upward and blunder between them, dusted gold, treated with a tenderness usually reserved for sacred objects. They are useless. The plants do not need them; the noise has seen to that. That may be exactly why they are cherished. They are the one living thing in the bowl that is merely there.

Children are raised deepest in the hillside, in the sand-walled sleeping alcoves, until they are old enough to be trusted with plugs and with the geography of safe hours. Their games are silent by design and grow vocal by slow permission as the afternoon fails — tag played entirely by shadow, elaborate counting games on fingers, then whispering, then, oh, the luxury of ordinary talk as the first cooking smoke rises. They learn the tunings before they learn the trade histories. They learn which terrace boards are drum-boards and which are just floors. They learn never to cross the center at noon — not because of a god, exactly, though the shrine in the east wall suggests older arrangements, but because the body itself teaches the lesson once, thoroughly, to anyone who tries: the shivering at the edge of vision, the ache in the chest, the small stones dancing, and an adult's hands hauling you back by the collar into the shade, signing *stupid, beloved, never again* all at once.

At night, when the singing is done and the food eaten and the mending folded, they sit out on their terraces in the one true quiet and look down at the empty middle of their city. They do not find it eerie. It is theirs the way a hearth is theirs, or an altar, or a held breath. Tomorrow it will crack their nuts and thresh their grain and shake the fruit onto their vines and deafen them into their fluent, funny, eloquent silence, and they will put their plugs in and get on with living inside the loudest tool ever made out of a hole in the ground.

They are not a people who survive the bowl in spite of what it does.

In spite of nothing. They are what it does, given hands.
