It does not live in the yard the way a rat lives in a wall. The rat is only one of the ways it moves.

At first you try to count it. That is natural down there, where there is nothing empty to look at and your eyes keep needing somewhere to rest. You count the water-skaters where a pool goes still, the black beetles turning in their tight circles, the mosquito larvae hanging by their tails from the warm surface by the pump. You count snails until counting fails — thumbnail snails in herds on the rust, fist-sized snails with white, pitted shells, all of them grazing the same red mat in the same direction, leaving clean dark lanes behind them as if a single mouth had passed, very slowly, many times.

Then you notice they are not separate problems to solve.

Nothing down there wakes up all at once. It wakes in its circulation.

When the hum under the floor is low, it rests. The newts lie three and four deep on the warm motor casings, slate-grey, faintly orange-spotted, their gills fanning in the wet air, taking heat through their bellies. The frogs sit sealed in the gauge housings with one pale eye to the fogged glass and do not blink. The eels hang slack in the deeper channels, almost invisible until a shadow makes them muscular. The white shrimp — eyeless, translucent, picking blindly through rust flakes and shed skins — move like thoughts that have not yet decided to matter.

Then the pitch rises half a tone.

You would miss it if the animals didn't teach you to hear it. The skaters clear to the margins. The rats, wet silver things with neat feet, climb exactly one pipe higher on their highways. The eels come together before a sluice that has not yet moved, weaving themselves into a single dark rope in the current, patient with a patience that is not individual. Thirty seconds later a solenoid clunks somewhere inside a wall, a gate sighs open a quarter inch, one pool sinks two inches and another brims over a lip worn smooth for precisely that spill.

That is it breathing. That is it thinking, if thinking can be water finding its next level.

It has made organs out of what was left. The pumps are hearts, not metaphorically but functionally: low, electric, felt through the soles before they are heard, keeping a warmth moving through cold mineral water so its blood never quite stills. The flywheel turning at walking pace in the pit is a slower heart underneath them, driven by return flow, clicking once per revolution on its loose tooth — a pulse with a slight irregularity it has learned to live around.

The pipes are not its skeleton. They are its gut and its roads and its high dry ground all at once. Things travel inside them unseen, and things travel on top of them whiskered and washing their faces on junction boxes, and things travel under them where the condensation gathers and falls in the north corner in a rain that never stops and never spreads. In that rain it feeds best. The heron that drops at dusk through the impossible gap between cables is not part of it, exactly, but it is tolerated like a visiting beak — because the rain drives small lives to the surface, and what the heron leaves behind goes back into the sediment, and the shrimp go through the sediment again.

Its skin is coatings. No plants grow there, so it does not green. It reds and whites and blacks: bacterial mats rippling almost imperceptibly in the outflow, fungal fans fine as frost in the brick joints, black slime on submerged iron, a pale phosphorescent crust where lime leaches, dries, leaches again. Its skin eats iron. Its skin eats rust outward in furry threads and binds burst pipe to concrete to brick until torn metal rounds like river stone. What looks soft on it is stone beginning. What looks like fur is it digesting its house.

Its eyes are distributed and mostly borrowed. A frog’s eye behind a dial that no longer measures pressure. A rat’s eye at the bend of a conduit, reflecting amber. A thousand snail eyes on stalks turning together when a causeway becomes a weir. Indicator lamps at ankle height, burning dim and doubled in black water — not eyes, but it has learned the hours by them, greyer above in the morning, browner at evening when the lamps take over and reflections become more solid than the iron that made them.

It has no dry place. That is the point of it. It lives where water is not contained but continuous — standing in pools at impossible different heights, sweating through concrete, beading inside cracked glass, gathering in the upturned bowls of valve wheels, falling only over one corner of its own small world. Its weather is internal: stillness, rising, spill, rest, three or four times an hour. Its tides are between tanks. Its migrations are twelve feet long and absolutely seasonal to it — snails timing their grazing to a freshly exposed mat, eels pouring in one coordinated pulse through a gate to the next level down, newts sliding one by one off a cooling casing without a splash, crabs sidling out from under the grating in homes they have stolen: a bottle cap over soft flesh, a valve seal, a hollow ammeter trailing its wires like whiskers, a length of copper pipe dragged for reasons that will make sense in another generation.

Because it does reproduce, constantly and in layers. Eggs in neat spirals on submerged warning signs. Larvae in the one warm cistern that steams. Skins and shells and husks and small bones tucked into every bracket and conduit bend until the walls bulge slightly with remembered bodies. It does not discard its dead. It archives them in its joints and grows over them, the way the yard grew over the gantry that fell across two pools and became, without anyone deciding, both bridge and reef.

It smells close, in the air just above the warm water: iron, brine, ozone from old electrics, and a low animal musk. Not rot. Occupancy. It is intimate as a burrow and impersonal as an engine.

It is not fast and it is not still. It does not hurry when the pumps rest and it does not panic when they quicken — the whole of it simply redistributes. Up a pipe. Off a casing. Through a sluice. To the edge of a film. It expects obstruction, expects gates to sigh shut on their counterweights behind it, expects the path back to be flooded differently than the path in. Straight lines were a human habit above it. Down here everything that survives has learned to let the levels decide.

If you stand there long enough it begins, absently, to include you. Not to eat you. To dampen you. Your clothes go wet through with no rain touching your shoulders. The cold finds your wrists and ankles first. Your notebook curls like something trying to return to pulp. You become another warm vertical surface for condensation, another temporary causeway animals gauge and go around, another big slow animal breathing too loudly in the dim green-grey. It does not look up at you. It has almost no up. The town exists to it only as muffled pressure above the walls, a weight that feeds it water and grit and the fine black dust that sifts endlessly into its shrimp-picked sediment.

It will not follow you up the iron stair. The stair is worn in troughs by feet and by flow following the same line, but its life thins with light and dryness. By halfway up you are already leaving its weather. Behind you a gate will settle. Something small will drop into a pool with a sound disproportionate to its size. The hum will steady, one pool will find its level against another, and it will resume — snail, eel, newt, pump, fungus, frog in the broken gauge — being one long, low, crowded thing that was not built and was not born, but adopted a dead machine pool by pool until the distinction wore as smooth as everything else it touches.
