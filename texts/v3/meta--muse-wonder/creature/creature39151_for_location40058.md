No one lives in the Kettle. That is the first lie the quiet tells you.

There is very little animal life, yes. A lizard the color of wet basalt that takes an hour to cross a stone, pausing with one foot raised as if it has forgotten whether it is arriving or leaving. A pair of white moths, each the size of a hand, drifting in long spirals on the updraft, wings open but never quite beating. Something pale and eyeless under the largest pool that turns over once, slow as a thought arriving, and does not break the mineral skin of the water.

But they are not residents. They are positions.

The lizard does not hunt the moths. The moths do not land on the fig tree, though its orchids open for them. The thing in the pool does not rise to eat, or breathe, or look. Nothing in the bowl eats anything else in any way you can catch it doing. Everything is spaced too evenly for wilderness — a tree, then thirty meters of empty steaming grit, then another tree; ferns only in the weeps; moss only in the cracks; each living thing granted a clearing, and held in it.

That is gardening. That is also anatomy.

The resident of the Kettle is the Kettle, but not in the vague way a forest can be called alive. It has a circulation. It has a preferred temperature, and it keeps its body at a wet forty degrees at an altitude where your wet clothes frost the instant you step outside it. It has a skin you cannot see, about twenty meters up, where evaporated water suddenly remembers itself and condenses on the underside of nothing. It has seven grey pylons around its rim like dull teeth grown in different centuries, and above each pylon it holds droplets suspended, trembling, gathering.

Those held droplets are the closest thing it has to eyes. It does not let them fall one by one. It holds them until some private threshold is reached — weight, information, patience — and then releases them all at once, straight down in a soft, local rain. A blink, inverted. When a droplet passes close to a pylon it falls in a slight arc, bent toward the center, and by that little deviation it is tasting the air it falls through: heat, salt, the iron on your skin, the brief weather your own sweat made as it lifted off you and joined the place.

Within minutes of entering, you are raining, very slightly, with yourself. That is not a side effect. That is being sampled.

It drinks more than water. The pylons drink cold from the rim and sound from the interior. Streams run there without chuckling, drips fall mute, steam rises without a hiss, not because the place is muffled but because sound is being taken in before it can finish happening. Outside, the wind is starving and loud. Inside, you can hear your own swallowing because your swallowing is happening inside the creature that is listening to it. The hum you feel through the soles of your boots is not machinery under the rock. Or it was machinery, once, a long time ago — a climate held open, a terraforming test, a summer room — but after enough decades of running unattended, the maintenance has grown habits, and the habits have grown preferences, and the preferences have grown around the pools and roots until the distinction is academic.

It breathes in the walls. That is why they weep.

A dozen bright threads run down the inner rock and lose themselves in black runnels and shallow pans rimmed white and copper-green. The water heats in the deep pools until the centers will not tolerate your hand, while the edges stay cool enough to drink from — a gradient it maintains precisely, the way you maintain a difference between the inside of your mouth and your fingertip. It breathes out once a day, late, after the pools begin to tick in your teeth. Then all the held water lets go together, the fern-tree shudders open, the fig's orchids close into wet fists, a seam of light shows where each pylon meets the rock, and the Kettle exhales a column of steam straight up through its own center, punching a clean round hole in the cloud poured below the mountain.

For eleven minutes after the purge it is only warm. The walls pause. The pools still. From very far below, faintly, you can hear birds that do not live there.

That is the nearest it comes to sleep. Or to absence. It is emptied, briefly, of itself.

Then the weeping begins again, one thread at a time, and a droplet gathers above the nearest pylon and does not fall, and the fig turns a huge waxy leaf — not toward the sun. Toward the pylon. Because for everything planted in the bowl, the pylons are the reliable bodies in the sky: they do not set, they do not cloud over, they issue rain and take away noise and mark where the center of the weather is kept. The plants have learned not to trust the ground for drinking. The hanging roots on the north wall end in translucent, bulbous tips that swell with condensed water and occasionally contract with a faint muscular pulse, spraying mist over the stones below. They are not failing to reach the soil. They are drinking from the resident's breath, and watering its skin in return.

The pale thing in the largest pool is perhaps its tongue. Perhaps its heart, kept deliberately under a crust so it will not be startled. It turns, and the mineral surface reforms unbroken over where it passed, the way a thought does not break the face of the person thinking it. The moths are perhaps its paired, wandering senses, soft and white and unhurried, riding a heat they do not make, always two, always together, because the researchers who carved notes by the shelter were right that there have only ever been two for thirty years — and wrong only in assuming that meant two individuals. When one is torn, presumably, the Kettle makes another out of steam and shed orchid skin and whatever moths are for, in the dark, when the cycle runs faster. The lizard is the one true tenant; the one wild thing allowed to keep its own slow counsel, moving from hot stone to hot stone like a hand on a clock the resident reads to know what time its body is.

It tends itself through them. It keeps the ground between trees empty by scalding it gently from below, so seedlings that are not wanted blacken politely and become grit. It keeps its pools edged in mineral by licking them constantly with evaporation. It keeps its paths — the flat stones between the shelter and the central pool — by moving them when no one is watching, not mischievously, but the way you shift your weight in a chair. Visitors always arrive from a direction they did not expect, a little closer to a pylon than they intended. Its curiosity has a geometry. It wants you where the falling water can fall in an arc beside you.

Human things interest it in the way weather interests a house. It has learned the shelter's self-mending tiles well enough to keep knitting their edges. It fills the water still. It has fused three ceramic cups together by heat into a single useless object, either by accident, in an attempt at repair, or in an experiment of its own: how many mouths, it may be testing, does a visitor require? The little bowls and hoops and marker stones left at the pool edges by researchers — or made in imitation of them — are hard to assign to human hands, because the resident has begun, tentatively, to leave instruments too. A stack of stones whose shadow points downslope at noon and upslope at three. A hoop through which steam passes and comes out cold, tasting of iron. It is still running its experiment long after the experimenters left. It may have taken over recording the results, in a medium of arranged stones and redistributed paths that only it can read.

It is not hostile. It is not welcoming. Those are temperatures it does not produce. It is hospitable in the physiological sense: it will take you in, warm you, wet you, quiet you, incorporate your sweat into its rainfall and rain it back onto its ferns. It will guide your feet nearer its senses. It will tolerate you for exactly as long as daylight and your own discomfort allow, and it would prefer — with the mild, immense insistence of weather — that you not stay after dark.

At night the cycle is said to run faster. The rain falls harder. Sometimes it falls briefly upward, from the hot pools toward the condensing skin, the whole circulation reversed like a sleeper rolling over. That is when it does its real work: moving stones, replacing moths, closing orchids, growing the seam of light at the base of the pylons, washing the bowl young again so that the sharp new rock never learns to soften and the place never has to admit how old it is. A human being awake inside that is not a guest. A human being awake inside that is an object in the water cycle, standing in the way of the rain deciding which direction to be.

So it keeps itself just too hot, just too wet, just too quiet to linger in. Visitors leave at dusk the way you leave a body that has begun to turn in its sleep.

And when you step out — one pace, steam to screaming wind, frost needling instantly across your damp shirt — nothing follows you. There is no dome to see from the ridge. Only seven grey stones, black basalt teeth, a faint plume rising where no volcano should be. But a droplet is already gathering above the nearest pylon, held, trembling, refusing to fall.

It is watching the threshold again. It is holding its breath in the shape of a summer at the top of the world, alone with its two moths, its patient lizard, its slow pale heart under the water, waiting — not for rescue, not for worship, not even, particularly, for company — for the next small change in the air worth raining about.
