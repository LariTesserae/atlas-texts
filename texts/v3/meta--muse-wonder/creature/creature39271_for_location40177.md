They are not the first thing you notice. The valley is too full for that, and they do not separate themselves from it.

At first you take them only for workers moving between the other movements — a figure in wet-weather clothes stepping aside for a tractor, another pair pushing a harvest cart under a conveyor, someone high on a catwalk opening a valve by a quarter-turn and moving on before the water has finished responding. They wear the place. Dark green and grey jackets, patched at the elbows, trousers tucked into rubber boots, hoods up or hoods down according to a judgment about the rain that you cannot yet make, because to you it is all one dampness and to them there are differences. Their cuffs are wet. Their cuffs are always wet.

It takes a while to understand that they are not commuting through the machine. They live inside it, and have done for generations.

You see it in how they move. Nobody hurries, and nobody stops. Hurrying would be dangerous on slick rails and narrow lanes, and stopping, in the wrong place, is a kind of accident. They walk at the pace of the racks and the sluice pulses, a steady, unshowy pace they keep up all day, stepping into a planted margin or a painted recess to let a cart pass without breaking stride or conversation. Children learn it before they learn anything formal: keep to your side, never stand in a gateway, listen for the sigh of hydraulics behind you. Old people keep it into their eighties, hand on a rail, body angled by habit even when the lane is empty.

They read the valley the way other people read weather.

There are no sirens here, and they do not need clocks the way you do. The banks of lights along the terrace edges change, the mid-level sluices lift, a particular pump house deepens its hum through the soles of the feet, and a crew knows, without looking up from the kale, that the harvest carts are to go in now and the wash station will want them in twenty minutes. A newcomer hears only trickling and muted clunks. They hear sequence. They can tell from indoors — from the pattern of drips on a walkway roof, from a change in pressure in a tap — which tank is filling, whether the upper intakes are taking in cloud-water fast, whether rain is coming or whether this is just the cloud dropping for the night.

Their bodies have taken the climate.

They are pale, not in an unhealthy way, but with the even pallor of people who live in diffuse light. The sun is never a disc here; it does not mark faces. When they go down below the cloud line, on the rare trips to the forest towns, they squint and burn and come home glad of the grey. Their eyes are good in dimness. They spot a furring vent, a slick patch of algae on a rail, a bean tendril reaching for a fan housing across a shadowed shed where you would see only green blur.

Their hands are the most weathered part of them. Short nails, because long ones split and catch. Skin ingrained at the creases with compost and mineral scale, knuckles enlarged a little from cold and work, palms callused from cart handles and crate edges and the endless turning of small, stiff wheels. Half of them have a permanent pale line where a wedding ring or a work ring sits, and a darker stained line on the forefinger from testing soil, tea, nutrient solution — everything here is tasted, pinched, rubbed and smelled. Knowledge lives low down, in the fingers.

They are not a uniform people. The valley makes its own distinctions, fine as water levels.

There are the water-keepers, lean and solitary by temperament, who walk the canals and the under-deck reservoirs with lamps and wrenches, who know every valve's reluctance in winter and speak, when they speak, in terms of head and flow. There are the glasshouse families, working in the one real warmth the place affords, sleeves pushed up in the steamy rows, foreheads damp for once with sweat instead of mist, smelling of tomato leaf and basil when they come out into the cold corridor. There are the rack-movers and rail-hands, broader, quieter, good at waiting for a three-storey greenhouse to sigh two centimeters to the left. There are the smelter and mine people down at the lower narrows and up at the headwall, fewer than there once were, with hearing a fraction dulled despite the enclosed machines, still carrying in their clothes a faint hot-metal and wet-stone smell that no wash quite removes. And everywhere, outranking all specialties when necessary, the scrapers: crews of teenagers and grandmothers and off-shift electricians with brushes and flat blades, because the moss does not respect job descriptions and a month of politeness toward it would close the vents.

In practice everybody is at least two of these things. Shifts rotate. A woman who spends the morning pruning tea hedges on a south wall spends the afternoon repairing a conveyor seal. A boy apprenticed to the pump house is expected to know why the chard beds are limed and when. This is not versatility for its own sake. In a place where a blockage anywhere backs up everywhere within the hour, ignorance is a physical obstruction.

They live in the housing blocks set into the terraces, but “indoors” is a relative term. The flats are small, low-ceilinged, built against retaining walls that weep if not constantly tended. You enter through a boot room. There is always a boot room, and a rail of jackets in ascending order of dampness, and a mat that never dries. The rooms beyond are sheltered rather than warm, lit with the same hooded work lamps that burn under the eaves outside, and furnished with things mended as faithfully as the buildings: a chair re-caned three times, a kettle whose handle has been rewrapped, a table made from a decommissioned growing tray, its aluminum edges still stamped with a terrace number.

Nothing is unassigned in public; at home, that becomes a kind of tenderness. Every hook has an initial. Every shelf has a person and a purpose. Families are close not only by affection but by square meter, and they have developed the social equivalent of the painted lanes — ways of stepping aside inside a small room, of being private without leaving, of lowering the voice at exactly the point where the wall becomes thin. Arguments here are quiet and, perforce, short. Sound carries oddly in fog and not at all through concrete, but resentment, like water, will find a blocked channel and back up. They have learned to clear it early.

Their children are born into motion and damp.

Babies are carried in slings under open jackets, heads covered, taken along the lanes while a parent checks gauges, asleep to a lullaby that is mostly pump-hum felt through a chest. Toddlers are put in the warm houses in winter to play between the lettuce tiers where the air will not bite them, and are retrieved smelling green. By six they can name the crops, the tanks, the safe recesses; by ten they are useful, carrying cuttings in buckets on the cable line, scraping liverwort from paving joints with the seriousness of people doing necessary work — which it is. School is in the long block on the middle terrace, its windows fogged, its curriculum half botany, half hydraulics, its playground a roof terrace railed and planted round the edges with cold-hardy flowers. The children are hardy in the unremarkable way of fish in a stream. They cough in spring, they stop coughing. They think eleven degrees is weather and a clear view to the lower rail yard is a fine day.

Some leave. The cable car goes down as well as up, and every few years a young person takes it with a dry bag — the only truly dry bag they will ever own packed for them — and does not come back except as a voice on the antenna line and parcels at the lower station. The valley does not treat this as betrayal. It cannot feed infinite people, any more than it can permit an unassigned corner. Births, departures, marriages between terraces, an occasional incomer who marries in or is taken on for a skill and then spends five years learning not to stand in gateways: the population replaces itself piece by piece, like the steelwork, like the concrete, without ever stopping to be rebuilt.

Old age is visible and employed. There is no separate country for the retired. A former water-keeper with stiff hands sits at the wash station grading herbs because her eye for rot is still the best on the terrace. A widower tends the engine-house cats, keeps the storeroom moused and immaculate, and maintains, in defiance of all regulation and with universal connivance, three pots of useless, beautiful alpine gentians on a valve housing. The very old move to the lower, slightly warmer terraces, closer to the canteen and the clinic in the old smelter building, where the brick chimneys that no longer smoke carry the antennas that bring them news of their grandchildren below the cloud.

They eat what the place excels at, and they eat it without romanticism. Enormous cabbages, kale, potatoes from the stacked troughs, chard with red stems bleeding into the pan, beans in season until everyone is quietly tired of beans and then preserved beans after that. Tea from the hedges on the warm walls, drunk constantly, from enamel cups whose rims are chipped in patterns as familiar as family handwriting. Protein comes up the rail line in scheduled loads and down from the tanks in smaller measure; meals are timed to sluices and shifts, served in the canteen under whose eaves the sparrows live boldly, the only creatures besides the ravens and the storeroom cats that have negotiated a permanent tenancy. The people feed the sparrows crumbs, officially by accident.

They are not solemn. The fog flattens voices, not humor. There is a dry, brief joking that moves along a work crew like a pulse down the canals — about the deer that got in last autumn and was treated as a visiting dignitary and a pestilence for a week, about which terrace grows the worst kale and will defend it to the death, about newcomers who try to keep a notebook and watch the pages curl and the ink run. Courtship is conducted in the warm houses and on maintenance stairs, in borrowed dry gloves, in the offering of the one unrationed luxury: a truly waterproof pair of socks, saved and given. Weddings are held when the lights change on a rest-shift, in the canteen, with flowers that were always going to be saleable anyway picked an hour earlier and put back into water afterward.

What they are, finally, is tenders of a negotiation.

Between growth and machinery, water and concrete, warmth for the crops and cold for the people, density and breath. They scrape the moss and plant the gabions. They cut the beans back from the fans and train them up the pipes again the next week. They replace a pump seal with one machined from the housing of the pump it replaced. They keep the made soil made — compost, ash, crushed rock, spent medium sieved and returned — and if you ask, one of them, embarrassed, may tell you that the ash of their dead goes, after the formalities below, into the high beds at the top of the valley, where the alpine flowers are. Not many words are wasted on it. It is simply the assigned place that makes sense.

Stand still — in a proper recess, out of the lane — at dusk, when the cloud drops and the upper terraces vanish and the lit greenhouses become rectangles of faint mist, and you will see them pass through the murk singly and in pairs, lamp-light traveling with them, voices at ordinary volume for three sentences and then gone behind tomato vines. They do not look like inhabitants of a town, or staff of a factory, or farmers of a very vertical farm, though they are all three. They look like the human part of a single long-lived organism that has learned, over a hundred years, how to keep itself fed, watered, repaired, and green at the altitude where clouds are made — and to do it quietly, in wet cuffs, without ever needing to raise its voice.
