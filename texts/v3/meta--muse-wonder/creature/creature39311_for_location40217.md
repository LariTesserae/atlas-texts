You would not see it as separate from the place, at first. That is not camouflage, exactly. It is just there in the same way everything else in the bowl is there: upright in the shallow water, shoulder to shoulder with the columns, wet to the waist, unmoving for longer than you would think anything with breath could comfortably be unmoving.

One of the pillars will seem warmer when you brace your hand against it. Then, after a while, it will have a pulse under the mineral skin.

It is shaped roughly like a person because a person is also a narrow thing that can turn sideways. But years in the gaps have refined the resemblance. Its shoulders have become slight and very smooth, its hips no wider than the space between two fused tufa shafts, its ribs rounded out with pale crust until from behind there is little difference between spine and stone. Cream and ochre have settled on it in waterlines. Rust-brown stains its shins. Where it stands most often in the deepest pools, a thin mineral rind has formed over the backs of its calves, down the outsides of its feet, along the forearms where water drips and dries and drips again.

It does not move quickly enough to disturb the account of the place. It wades. It turns. It puts a long palm flat against a column and leans its weight through, feeling for the submerged ledges that have been worn glassy. Its feet know them without looking. Looking is not much use anyway; its eyes are small, grey-green, filmed as permanently as glass left in steam. It finds most of what it needs within fifteen feet — which, down there, is the whole visible world.

Its hair is dark and sodden and hangs in ropes that are stiffening at the ends, going iron-hard with deposit. Its skin never unwrinkles. Fingertips, heels, the soft parts between the toes, all stay puckered from a bath that has lasted decades, but the minerals seal the skin almost as fast as the warmth loosens it. Small cuts whiten and close under a crust. Its nails are thick, yellowish plates. When it flexes its fingers, the joints make no sound, only a faint resistance, as if each knuckle were learning again how not to be stone.

It breathes the air as it is — low thirties, close, smelling faintly of limestone and warm iron and nothing rotting. It does not cough. Its lungs have made their bargain with the mist. To speak, which it rarely does, it puts its mouth very near stone or water; sound down here is a private thing. A call would be wasted. Instead it taps: a fingertip against a wet pillar, a soft *tick* answered by dripping, a pressure sent through column and pool to be felt in the soles of the feet. It listens with its cheek laid to tufa, hearing movement as a thickening in the water.

It eats what the bowl tolerates. The pale film in the warmest pools, gathered patiently with the cupped hand and with the thin copper ladle where the scum collects in unbroken sheets. A snail, now and then, taken from below its silver track and eaten whole, shell softened by the water. Weeds never come. Fruit never falls intact through the shaft of light. Once in a great while an insect from the plain blunders down through the strip of sky, strikes the mist, and drowns in the central pool, and that is a meal of a size worth crossing the bowl for. The tiny pale fish it leaves alone, whether from indifference or some older knowledge. They flicker in the deepest basin, fingernail-small, and vanish. It watches their motion the way it watches drips fall — not as food, but as proof that something else can still move in cracks it cannot enter.

It drinks only from the east wall, where the chipped cups descend beside the steps, each brimming into the next. It keeps those cups clear with a thumb, feeling for grit, for the first roughness of new deposit that might change the overflow. It drinks before the sweet water has a chance to mix with the bitter pools. The rest of the water is for standing in, for sleeping in, for letting take the body's temperature and keep it, steady, in the low warmth that neither night nor noon can alter.

Sleep, for it, is not lying down. The low platform between the columns under the overhang — dry by a hand's breadth, blackened in one corner from fires it no longer bothers to light — is where it rests when the legs finally ache past mineral patience. Most nights, if night is still the word, it sleeps upright in the waist-deep basin near the center, wedged gently between two pillars, chin tipped forward, hair trailing in the current-that-is-not-a-current. The perpetual slow rain from the vault over the pool strikes its head and shoulders all night long. It wakes already wet, already warm, with no clear edge to waking.

What it does, across a day that barely lightens and a night that barely darkens, is maintenance so small it almost is not work. A palm smoothing new scum away from a channel mouth so a runnel can keep to its old course. A finger enlarging the dimple where a drip lands, no deeper than the drip itself intends. A shoulder eased against a leaning column — not to move it, never to move it, only to know that it still leans the same way. Sometimes it feels along the throat beneath the southern wall where the water leaves, clearing by touch what cannot be seen: a leaf that has somehow survived the descent, a soft clot of mineral, a dead snail shell. It removes nothing large. It adds nothing large. It understands the place's settled arithmetic in its hands.

It is not young. The encrustation has begun to win in places it cannot reach — between the shoulder blades, behind the knees. Its turning is slower than it was. The lashed wooden poles across the narrow climb are more necessary now, and its grip on them leaves a lighter polish. There will come a time, not soon by the measure of anyone above the rim, when the turning stops altogether in some good spot: water to the hips, a column at its back, the light, such as it is, falling nowhere in particular. The deposit will continue. The film will go on gathering along the forearms. The mist will complete the shape.

After that, a visitor coming down the worn steps will put a hand against it for balance, and find it warm for longer than stone should be warm, and then not notice it at all among the hundreds of other pillars — shoulder to shoulder, elbow to elbow, rising out of the shallow water in their finished, balanced stillness.

Until then, it remains the one exception the bowl makes for motion. Rare as the heron, slower than the snail, paler than the fish. It parts the mist when it passes, and the mist closes behind it without resentment, and the drip it passed under goes on falling into its accustomed dimple, exactly as before.
