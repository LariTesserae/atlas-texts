At first you mistake them for the ground, and that is not a metaphor. It is their whole way of being.

A grazer does not sit *on* the plateau so much as continue it, a little higher. The shell is a low, broad dome, almost a disc — dinner-plate wide in the common adults, cartwheel wide in the grandmothers, table wide in the few ancients who may not have moved a body's length in a decade. Seen from above it is indistinguishable from the pavement around it: red-brown, black, iron-varnished, pitted and polished, ringed in concentric growth lines like a tree stump or a target worn down by weather. Lichen takes hold on the oldest backs in black lace and sulfur freckles. Dust settles in the grooves. A small stone resting on a large shell may stay there for years, carried inches at a time.

Underneath, if one deigns to lift itself for you, everything is soft and close and secret. Six stub legs, jointed low, each ending in a broad, damp pad that can taste water through the sole. A blunt head without apparent eyes, more a muscular lip than a face, that extends and retracts like something tasting the air. The mouth is on the underside, a rasping plate. They do not bite. They abrade. At the dew hour, just after dawn, you can hear them at it if you lie with your ear near the stone — ten thousand faint scrapings, sand on sand — as they harvest the night's green film: algae, bacterial crust, salt bloom, lichen spore, and in the end the mineral skin of the rock itself, ground down molecule by patient molecule. They are eating the plateau. Over sufficient time, which is the only kind of time they recognize, they may be one of the reasons it is so flat.

They move by following moisture downhill into the past tense. Not downhill in elevation — there is almost none — but down the gradient of damp: from a sunlit pan edge where the dew has already vanished, into a crack where it lingers, to the shadowed underside of a cairn where it will survive until noon. Inches in a morning is good going. A migration between pans after rain, a distance you could cross in forty paces, is a communal undertaking of days, shell nudging shell, the young drafting in the humid wake of the large. Nothing about them can hurry. Even startled — and almost nothing startles them — the response is to settle. Legs fold. Head tucks. The rim of the shell meets the stone with a slight, muscular sigh and seals. Sealed, a grazer is a small sealed climate of its own: the day's warmth banked above, the night's water trapped below, worms stirring in the dark margin, young breathing damp air.

Because they are born live, and they are born underneath. There is no nest, no egg exposed to that killing sun. The young emerge soft-shelled and pale as pan-mud directly into the shelter of the mother, and they stay there at first, stacked and overlapping in the humid dark, rasping at what filters down. As they harden they climb — first to the rim of her, riding the lee edge out of the wind, then onto the top. It is common to see a great old female moving, if moving is the word, as a sort of living cairn: herself at the base, three or four plate-sized children clamped to her back, juveniles on them in turn, and on the uppermost, no bigger than a coin, a dusting of rust-colored mites arranging themselves in slow, herding spirals. The oldest ride has been a landscape for her passengers for so long that cushion plants have rooted in the dust of her grooves. She is pasture, vehicle, mountain, and grandmother simultaneously.

This layering is the real population of the plateau, and once your eyes learn it, the emptiness never recovers. The grazers themselves, shell to shell in the good country, so close that a path shouldered through them closes behind you inside the hour. The mites on their backs, tended and cropped by the silent, long-legged birds who step from shell to shell like a man crossing a river on stones, never troubling to touch the actual ground. The hair-thin worms who live in the damp eclipse beneath every shell and pour down into the cracks at sunrise, leaving castings fine as flour. The nameless burrowers below them all, whom you know only by that flour, renewed daily. And after rain, in the pans themselves, the translucent swimmers who hatch, breed, and finish in an afternoon while the grazers crowd the shoreline shoulder to shoulder, drinking, literally, through their feet. It is a crowd that occupies not just the surface of the world but several stacked surfaces above and below it.

They communicate, if they do, almost entirely by touch and timing. The ticking that rises an hour after dawn is not a call; it is ten thousand shells expanding a fraction in the sun, a physical sound, and yet the synchrony of it is uncanny — tick, tick, from horizon to horizon, too soft to locate, like the plateau clearing its throat. Vibrations carry through packed gravel better than sound carries through thin air, and a sealing shell, a shifting foot, a male inching his rim deliberately against a female's during the brief wet season, all of it must register in those broad, tasting feet as a kind of conversation too slow for you to overhear. Courtship is a matter of adjacency maintained over days. A male who finds himself beside the right female when the pans fill simply remains. That is the whole ceremony. Remaining, up here, is the most elaborate gesture available.

Predation, accordingly, is not pursuit. The birds pick. The burrowers wait below for what weakens and sinks. Mostly the grazers are killed by age, by a crack in the shell that lets the sealed dark dry out, by a season of dew too thin to repay the night's sealing. A death is an event with a year's duration. The birds take the soft parts first, through the widening gap. The worms rise from beneath. The small grazers rasp the inside clean with a diligence that looks almost ceremonial. And then — this is the thing you do not expect from an eyeless disc that eats stone — the others move the shell.

Not far. Not fast. Rim to rim, nudged a mouthful at a time over weeks, the empty domes are butted into arcs, sometimes two layers high, always opening away from the prevailing wind. Inside such an arc the wind drops, dust stays instead of migrating, dew survives an extra precious hour, and a tuft of grass can risk itself where none would dare in the open. The stacked young shelter there. The birds fold into them at dusk. A grazer will spend the last strength of several seasons adding its grandmother to a windbreak, and the plateau is dotted with these arcs by the hundreds, each one a small, inherited refusal of flatness, a piece of architecture made entirely of ancestors.

They are heavy in the hand if you try to shift one — surprisingly, warmly heavy, like lifting a filled pan — and they brace against you with a patience that outlasts your embarrassment. Let go and it does not flee. It resettles, with a tiny adjustment of those six feet, into the exact contour of the stone, and within minutes you would struggle to say which of the surrounding discs was the one you touched.

By late afternoon they all do this. Legs withdraw. Heads vanish. Shells come down and seal against the cooling rock, hoarding the day beneath them, and the plateau performs its oldest trick in reverse: a nation folds itself back into geology. The ticking stops. The rasping stops. What remains is pavement, gravel, pans going white at the edges, light lying ordinary and gold across thirty kilometers of apparent nothing.

But you have lain at dawn with your ear to that nothing and heard it grazing. You know now that the flatness is not empty and never was. It is inhabited at the speed of dew — a slow, sealed, stacking people, wearing the colors of stone, building with their dead, carrying their children and their gardens and their parasites on their backs, and eating, century by patient century, the surface of the world from under themselves.
