They do not stand up, at first. That is the wrong way to look for them.

In that light, at that distance, an upright thing would show — even a reed-bed shows twice, once torn away and once not yet there. But they have learned not to give the pan a vertical line to repeat. When you do see one, days into the walking, it is because the water beside you has begun to move against the wind, in a broad, patient displacement, as if something wide were pushing its way just under the sheet-water, and then a back breaks the surface. Not rises. Breaks it.

Wide. That is the first true thing.

They are low the way the land became low, by having everything that could be taken off them taken off. A shoulder is the highest point. A head, when it lifts, lifts only enough to put the eyes an inch above the water, and then it settles again, chin in the warm film, so that the skull is half in air and half in the better-conducting wet. From a distance they look like moving berms. From nearer, like people who have decided, over many generations, to be furniture for the weather: broad-backed, short-limbed, splayed.

Their feet are obscene and beautiful and entirely sensible. Toes long, webbed to the second joint, spreading with each step over an area the size of dinner plates, so they do not punch through the crust so much as persuade it. The soles are not calloused in the way a walker's soles are calloused. They are *tuned*. Thick in the center ridge where a deep note needs deadening, almost translucent at the edges, freckled with pits that fill with mud and empty again. They read with them constantly and involuntarily — iron-salt stripes hundreds of miles wide coming up through the clay as a faint difference in texture, brine sighing up below, the approach of a storm three days out as a tightening in the drumhead underfoot. A child who loses feeling in a foot is considered blinded.

They do not have ears you would look for. No flaps to be torn by shear, no open holes for the roar to occupy. Where ears should be there are smooth, slightly bulging plates of bone under wet-leather skin, sealed over since before birth. The whole skull is the ear, and a bad one on purpose. It takes only the lowest, slowest pressures, the enormous syllables coming up from the caverns underneath the crust, and lets the surface scream go past mostly unregistered. Mostly. Their sinuses are vast, resonating, perpetually aching. They snort mud-water through them to clear them. Their eyes, set wide and a little high on the flattened head, are horizontal slits with a milky third lid that never quite opens; they see down more reliably than out, watching the reflection to tell which reed-bed is real, and even then they trust the foot's opinion over the eye's.

If they were ever tall, the pan edited them. The wind shear and standing waves punish anything slender. Those who live longest here go on all fours without shame and without thinking of it as animal — palms down beside feet, forehead sometimes down too when listening hard, the body making a low table the pressure can slide over. Their spines have widened rather than lengthened. Their ribs are flared hoops. When they rest they do not sit; they settle, distributing themselves, becoming a slight rise the water goes around. Algae films them while they sleep. They wake green and rust-red and peel it off in sheets and eat the best of it.

Eating is mostly that: what blooms and dies in an afternoon. They farm nothing so much as delay. Inside the curve of a berm, where the sound drops enough that acres of algae do not all die at once from a passing boom, they tend shallow gardens demarcated by fingertip ridges in the mud. Films of shocking blue, films the color of old meat, cultured in rotation according to the taste the clay is giving off that week. They skim flatworms from below the crust with reed hooks, blind canoe-long things they kill by pressing, never by striking — a sharp impact would ring their bones for hours. They have no fire that matters. Warmth is ambient. Food is warm. They chew slowly with flattened molars, mouths built as much for carrying and shaping mud as for eating; you will see one moving along a berm-line with its lips and cheeks packed with good clay, laying it down in wet mouthfuls, patting it with its tongue, building by body.

And they almost never speak into the air.

A voice let loose up there is a donation to the continent. It will come back weathered and wrong, it will still be wandering next season, it may answer someone else. So speech, real speech, goes down. Palm flat. Forehead to the wet ground. The other palm of the listener flat a yard away, or ten yards away, fingers spread. What passes is not words as you would transcribe them; it is a grammar of pressure — tap, sustained lean, a drumming made with the heel of the hand, a whole sentence sent as a shiver through the shared skin of the world, punctuated by pauses where both parties listen to the deep water underneath argue back. Their throats have not vanished, but they have narrowed to other work: humming against a partner's sternum in a berm's quiet pocket, a private vibration that never becomes sound in the air at all; clicking softly to find the thickness of crust; weeping.

In the shadow of a well-made spiral — and their berms are theirs, rebuilt continuously with mouth and hand and dragged reed, spiral always curling the same way against the prevailing pressure — the roar falls to the inside of a seashell, and there, in air that feels terrifyingly empty, they do their loudest living. That is where children are born, underwater in the lee, into hands that keep them submerged a moment too long by your reckoning so their sealed ears and flooded sinuses learn the deep register first. That is where the old are taken to do what, for them, passes for talking plainly: pressing their faces down and telling the ground things they do not want returned. That is where mating happens, rib to resonant rib, and councils, if they can be called that, in which a dozen flattened bodies arrange themselves in a ring, feet toward the center, and hold an argument entirely in tremors through a disc of mud they are slowly, collectively flattening thinner, until it carries perfectly.

Quiet frightens them the way sudden silence before a mud-burst should. A child raised too long behind too good a berm will panic at being carried out onto the open pan less from the noise than from the loss of a ceiling the noise had become. Their lullabies are not songs. An adult lies beside the infant and breathes in a pattern the crust takes up, a reassuring, repeating thump, felt in the teeth. To be soothed is to be accompanied in the bone.

They have names, but the names are not stable property. A name sent through the ground stays local and dies with the pressure that carried it — good. A name ever shouted, in extremity, in birth or terror or warning, escapes. It goes around the basin. It comes back years later flattened and unfamiliar from a thousand miles of water, and by then it may belong to the storm that carried it. They are careful, therefore, with the distinction between persons who are presently embodied and persons who are presently *returning*. A voice from an empty berm is received politely, listened to down through the feet, and answered through the ground if it seems lonely, without any strong commitment to the idea that its owner is dead, or absent, or hasn't arrived yet. Their etiquette accommodates answers that arrive before questions. If your companion replies, puzzled, differently the second time, you do not call the first reply false. You note the direction it came from, as you would note weather.

That has made them, over time, something other than a population. Twice, at least, they are counted: once in bodies, low and broad and mud-slicked, migrating not toward places but toward pressure-knots where the interference eases for a season, where algae will not die all at once, where the deep water's slow booming is grammatical that year; and once in accompaniments.

Walk with one long enough — and no one walks the pan alone without acquiring this, but *they* cultivate it — and its footsteps, phase-shifted by the crust, organize. A quadruped ripple trots beside it, made of standing water and its own returned noise, holding its pace, learning, after weeks, to pause half a beat before it pauses, the way a dog anticipates a familiar road. They feed these things, in a sense, by walking in rhythm. They discipline them with sudden stillness. They mourn them when a storm-system crosses and scrambles them back into spray. Some of their berm-spirals are not sound-shadows for bodies at all but corrals, tuned hollows where a particularly coherent echo — a grandmother's stride, a drowned child's splashing pattern, a composite thing hammered out of a whole family's footfall over a generation — can be kept cohering a little longer, given a hollow that turns like a head, a ridge that holds like a spine, allowed to persist in the mist as a presence that does not need to be alive to be *kept*.

Outsiders have argued about whether the fleshy ones made the echo ones as tools, or pets, or gods, or whether it went the other way: that sufficiently organized noise, given a continent-sized mirror and unlimited time, eventually hammered mud and spray into shapes that trotted, then lingered, then wanted lower and slower shapes still, and the bodies are what the accompaniments built to listen downward with. The inhabitants do not find the question urgent. Sequence is a local opinion. Cause arrives when it can. A person is a pattern that the pan currently agrees to repeat — sometimes in meat, with tuned soles and sealed bone-ears and a mouth full of building-clay; sometimes in a standing disc of water holding its shape a heartbeat too long beside you on the flat.

Their dead make the matter plain, inasmuch as anything is plain. They do not bury, which would be a lie about where down goes, and they do not raise mounds higher than four inches, which would be a boast the weather would correct. A body is settled on the surface in the lee of the spiral, weighted gently with reed bundles, and left for the mud that is already almost liquid to take back at its own obscene, patient pace. For days the settling corpse sends small, irregular pressures into the crust — gas, collapse, the slow give of the rib-hoops — and the family keeps a foot on the ground nearby, receiving it, the way you would hold a hand. Later, predictably and never on schedule, its tread comes back across the basin on some storm's leading edge, unmistakable, accompanying whoever happens to be walking that stretch. Nobody screams. Someone, perhaps, adjusts their pace to match it for a while, so it will not travel alone.

They are not numerous. They are not rare, either, in the way a thing spread ankle-deep over a continent can be everywhere by being almost nowhere. They refuse the heights — there are none — and they refuse the surface, which belongs to the birds who cross screaming at insane altitude and never land, and they refuse true depth, which belongs to the blind lengths moving under the crust speaking pressure to each other in sentences that may take a year. They occupy the interface: skull half in air, sole half in mud, body giving the water a shape to remember briefly.

Look again, then. Not for a figure against the horizon; there is no horizon that will hold one. Look for the water going around something that was not a rise a moment ago. Look for a spiral of mud a little fresher than its surroundings, reed bundles rotting in an intentional curve, and behind it — if your own ears can stand the dropping-away of the world — a pocket of relative quiet where several wide, low shapes lie with their foreheads pressed reverently to the wet earth, saying nothing audible at all, while beside them, just outside the berm, made of spray and returned thunder and a rhythm older than its body, something with no feet keeps perfect, faithful time with feet that have stopped moving, waiting for them to start again.
