You meet them first by sound, or by the lack of it.

A wooden sole clicks on limestone, stops at a boardwalk, goes silent. A voice ahead of you in a lane too narrow for two wet coats, lowered without thinking as you approach, not in deference but so it will not travel. In the morning, before any bell, a hundred sluice-boards clatter up in sequence, terrace by terrace, as the night's water is let go — and that clatter is how they tell time, and where they are in the city. Each district has its own lag. You learn to hear it.

They are not tall people. The terraces and the low lintels, blackened and sagging a finger's width lower every generation, have seen to that, but it is more the way they carry themselves: close, economical, shoulders a little forward against the perpetual slight chill, chins tucked into collars. They climb all day without noticing they are climbing. A woman carrying a bucket up forty worn steps will not be out of breath at the top, only annoyed if she has spilled. Their calves are thick, their ankles strong, their feet broad in their wooden-soled shoes and splayed a little from a lifetime of wet pavement, boardwalk, and steps hollowed into bowls where every foot for three hundred years has turned in exactly the same place.

Their hands are the other tell. Broad, short-fingered, the knuckles enlarged early. Masons, sluice-keepers, fullers, dyers, stone-sorters, reed-cutters — in practice almost everyone is several of these by season — and the water does its work on skin kept damp from dawn. In winter the cracks open at the joints and are rubbed with sheep fat. In summer the nails go soft. Old people's hands curl gently inward, not quite fists, like hands that have spent sixty years gripping rope slings wet with canal water, lifting counterweighted boards, turning stones over to find the mossy face and set it inward. When they talk, which is not often at length, they gesture with those hands low and close to the body. A raised hand on a cistern means *this much, no more*. A palm turned down over a channel means *slow it*. Children learn the hand-signs before they learn to read, because a shout across water on a still morning is an obscenity.

They are pale in a particular way — not sickly, but weather-washed. The sun up here, when it comes, is hard and clean and burns fast on wet skin, so they cover: layered undyed wool, grey and brown and a deep peat-black, leather aprons for the wet trades that shine with use, hoods that are up or down according to a calculus of wind off the moor that a stranger cannot parse. Everything smells faintly, always, of the city itself: damp wool drying, woodsmoke, algae, limestone after rain. They change their socks the way other people wash their faces. A household is judged, quietly and without appeal, by whether it can keep bedding dry. The evening fire in high summer is not for warmth exactly; it is for boots in a row, coats on pegs steaming, lofts aired. To offer a guest the dry pair by the hearth is the first hospitality. To offer a dry bed is a declaration.

They speak the same language at the marsh-gates and at the northern quarries, technically. In practice it thins and turns as the water does. Where the channels begin to run the other way, on a ridge you could step over without noticing, vowels shift, a word for sluice becomes a word for bridge, people stop understanding a joke. No one finds this strange. They do not, for the most part, believe they live in one city. They belong to a terrace, a watershed, a run of canal whose moods they know the way you know a face — when it runs clear, when it carries the brown peat-stain down from the moor after a storm, when a certain green thickening means the reeds at the bend need cutting before the autumn. A woman who has lived sixty years on one stepped street can tell, by the sound against the culvert under her plinth at night, whether it will rain *here* tomorrow, and would not presume to guess for her married daughter twenty minutes' walk uphill. Locals do not ask if it is raining. Their whole conversation about weather is locative.

That localness is total. Ten years here and you need never have seen the far side; most haven't, and feel no lack. Marriage is usually within a few terraces. News travels at the speed of water and of women spreading washed wool on the upper roofs at noon to catch the brief sun, calling down, roof-yard to roof-yard, in voices pitched to carry one house and no further. Markets are murmur and gesture. Disputes — over a diverted hand's-width channel, over a wall whose stones were not properly sorted when it fell — can last a generation and be conducted at a volume an outsider must strain to hear, and that restraint is not politeness laid over anger. It is the anger's actual form. To raise your voice is to admit you do not belong to the place that eats sound. To waste water, or let your stretch of channel silt while your neighbors turn out with shovels, is worse.

They are maintainers, not builders. Nothing here is new-looking because nothing is permitted to stay new; a fresh stone is an embarrassment until weather takes it. They inherit gradients the way other peoples inherit swords: an immense, unspoken, bodily knowledge of which way water wants to go across a slope you or I would swear was flat, passed hand to hand at the sluice at dawn. A boy poling a floating market-boat one street farther uphill after the afternoon rain than he could in the morning is not playing — he is apprenticing. A girl combing algae from a slow canal with a wooden rake learns the week, the season, the health of the fish ponds at the city's edge, all at once. By the time they are grown they can kneel, put an ear near a plinth, and hear a blockage forming three houses down.

They eat accordingly: small cold-water fish from the raised ponds, sheep from the high grazing, kale and onions from walled courtyard gardens drained with fanatical care, and a great deal of what can be pickled, stored in lofts on stone feet, kept above the seep. Meals are hot, plain, and eaten early. There is fish on stone, bread kept dry in wall-niches, broth that steams the shutters. They drink the water, of course, but from the right cistern, above the right line, in the right month, and they think people from lower, drier countries are careless to the point of madness about this.

There are almost no dogs, and they find the barking in other towns hard to forgive. Their cats are grey, long-haired, and reserved. The swallows, when they come for their frantic few months above the canals, are the one lively, noisy thing they openly delight in — children go quiet to watch them, which tells you everything. At night the city closes its oiled-cloth shutters and goes completely dark, and they move through that darkness without lamps, by step-count and the sound of water in the channel beside the lane, unhurried. Their hearing, by middle age, is extraordinary. Their eyes, adapted to hard noon glare off white walls and to months of soft grey, squint reflexively at any strong color. Orange lichen on a sunward wall is about as much ornament as most streets allow themselves.

They grow old stooped over channels and are not, as a rule, long-lived in the way dry-country people count it — the damp gets into chests, into joints, a soft ticking cough in winter lanes — but they age in place with a completeness that looks, from outside, like contentment. The oldest are given the sitting jobs: sorting fallen stone, mending rope, watching a cistern's water-line and rapping a board with a stick when it reaches the carved hand. The dead are carried out, not down; there are no cellars here, and nothing is buried in ground that sighs and fills. They are taken up to the moor terraces where the city thins into pools and grass, under low stones that will themselves, in a season or two, be half-sunk and reflecting the sky.

If you stay a year you will stop asking how big the city is. You will know your terrace's water in peat-stain and ice-tick, you will turn sideways in the lane without thinking, you will lower your voice on a still morning and feel everyone turn if a stranger doesn't. Your coat will never quite dry. And on an evening when the boards go down and the cisterns fill and the pools in the courtyards hold the last light after the streets have gone grey, you will understand that the inhabitants are not living *on* this wet plateau of stone. They are its weathering — slow, close, continuous, moss turned inward, re-set terrace by terrace, making a place that has no edge habitable by never, ever trying to finish it.
