They are slow people, not by temperament — by necessity. 

No one hurries on the Anvil. Hurrying costs air, and air is too thin here, and too hot, to waste. A man climbing three rooftop streets to work stops on the landings without embarrassment, rests his forearms on the rail until his breath comes back, and resumes. A woman carrying a sealed pressure-pot across town sets it down, shakes out her hands, waits, picks it up again. Even the children learn it early: the shouting, tumbling run of lowland children would leave them grey-lipped and vomiting in this altitude, so their games are played sitting down, or standing, or moving one deliberate square at a time across shaded courtyards. From above, at noon, the whole town can look almost still. Then a beam engine nods. A valve sighs. Someone shifts a spout an inch. It is enough.

They are barrel-chested, most of them, the second and third generations especially. Born at almost six thousand meters, they grew ribs like hoops, big quiet lungs, hearts that do unhurried, powerful work. Their blood is thick with it. Visitors from the cloud-level arrive scarlet-faced, headache-sick, weeping at the nose; Anvil-born nurses set them in the shade, give them salt water a sip at a time, and do not sympathize much. Descending is almost as bad for the natives. Their ears ache for days in the thick lower air. Their joints feel swollen, their sleep goes dream-heavy and stupid, the damp closes on their skin like a hand. Many have never been down. They have seen the cloud ocean from the roof streets all their lives, and the black peaks breaking through it, and that, to them, is what *below* looks like — not ground. Not home.

Home is measured.

Everyone on the Anvil can read a jack-line at a glance. The white paint marks on the building legs are their true calendar: three centimeters up since spring, a finger's width tilt to the southeast, this corner due for Thursday. Small boys go in the dew-hour with slates under their arms, calling numbers in clear, unchildish voices. Old men who can no longer climb squat in doorways and correct them by sound alone. Addresses are given in storeys and risings rather than streets — *second roof above the glassworks, house lifted twice since the wet year* — because a printed map is an historical document here.

So they are a people of maintenance. There is no finished thing in their lives, and they do not expect one. A house is not built, it is begun, and then cranked, shimmed, re-stepped, re-roofed, surrendered floor by floor to the heat underneath. The bottom rooms go first to storage, then to curing brick and chemical cake, then to a small iron door sealed with an offering of salt while the family cuts a new entrance above. Grandmothers live at the bright tin tops of houses whose ground floors they were born in, now dark and ticking warm behind sealed masonry thirty years below them. They take a fierce, unsentimental pride in this. To ride well — to keep level while the mountain decides — is the local definition of dignity.

Their hands show the place. Short, broad, calloused across the palm from ropes and crank-handles, nails often split from cold night metal and hot day metal handled in the same shift. Soles too: even through asbestos-soled boots, even through nailed sandals, the heat comes up, so they walk with a particular flat-footed patience, testing, as if perpetually crossing a griddle politely. In full sun their skin goes copper-dark and peels in fine white flakes; in the brine and sulfur districts their cuffs and boot-tops carry a permanent pale crust. They smell, most of them, faintly and cleanly of hot iron, salt, cork lagging, and the iron-egg reek of the bacterial mats. Perfume is unknown. Soap is a luxury made from imported fat, and used sparingly, on Sundays, with stored water.

They dress for steam and blister: loose cotton that can dry on the body after the climb through cloud, wide hats of woven fiber with copper brims that can be hung by a hook at night lest they warp, gloves hung at every belt beside a water flask and a small brass level. No one leaves metal tools in the sun. No one leans bare-elbowed on a noon railing twice.

Water makes them communal in a way no sermon could.

Every Anviller, from the refinery master to the smallest roof-child, has a wet-hour job, learned before letters. When the bells go — not urgent, never urgent — hatches swing, spouts turn, nets unroll across condenser fins, old women set basins in the precise sag of tarpaulins, men in pipe-duck shirts climb to scrape the night-radiators before the sun steals back the dew. Then the cloud spills over the rim for twenty minutes, the streets steam knee-high, the cisterns tick under the floors, and the work stops. They drink that morning's catch with the mineral taste still in it. Their tea is weak because water boils lukewarm at this height and takes forever to sing; their food comes sighing and clamped from pressure vessels, soft stews and dense cakes cooked all day by ground heat, seasoned largely with salt they make themselves and with patience. A good cook on the Anvil is not inventive. A good cook is punctual.

In place of gardens, they tend the mineral country that tends itself. They can be found in the off-shift evening, these supposedly purely industrial people, crouched by an exhaust channel arguing gently over a night's growth of salt needles — an inch high, branched like frost, gone by the next rain and never grown the same way twice. Brine-workers name especially fine thickets after dead relatives. Sulfur flowers around the vents are left untouched until they flake. The slimy tapestries in the warm runoff — rust, cream, poisonous green — are recorded in chalk on the channel wall, their creep of centimeters marked month by month. It is their landscape painting, their scripture of slowness: the first thing here, they will tell you, that is neither stone nor built.

That tenderness extends, oddly and fiercely, to the rare animal. Wall lizards, descended from stowaways in packing crates a generation ago, are protected by something stronger than law. Children set bottle-caps of precious water for them in the sunny districts and squat breathless, ribs heaving, to watch a throat pulse. White moths from the pipe-ducts are brushed, not swatted, out of kitchens at dusk. When a condor works up from beneath the roofs, huge against the cloud sea, work genuinely stops — cranks rest, men shade their eyes — until it finds the dome's updraft and rises past the chimneys. In a place with no fields, no trees, no weeds in the gutters, anything that lives of its own accord is news, and a kind of proof.

They are hushed people, partly because the lagged machines drink the noise, partly because thin air punishes loudness. Conversation happens close, at a level voice, often with a hand resting lightly on a wall to feel what the building is doing. At night they can hear the dome through the legs of their beds — a faint settling, a tick, a groan so low it lives in the teeth — and they sleep through it the way sailors sleep through a hull. They baptize children with cistern water touched to the forehead, a drop only. They marry on rooftop streets at evening, when the heat comes up through the shoe-soles and the windows light dense as a circuit and the stars start too suddenly overhead. They bury their dead sideways, in the cold chambers of the dead north spur, because down is not rest here. Down is fire arriving slowly.

And eastward, where the town stops at the bell-fence and the raw dome steams and shoulders itself up in the dark, they keep timber already cut, pipes already threaded, a street already named. That is perhaps the truest thing to know about them. They do not curse the mountain for being unfinished. They do not pretend it will ever finish. They set their jacks, they read their lines, they raise one corner and leave another to tilt until Thursday, and they live, breath by measured breath, on top of a world that is still being made — as if being made along with it were the most natural condition there is.
