At first you would say that no one lives here.

That is what the place seems designed to make you say. The bowls are set out, but no mouths come to them. The sandals wait by the doors in pairs, the sleeping platforms are smoothed and empty, a broom leans still warm, somehow, from a hand. In an oven there is a fire that does not smoke or eat its wood. In a basket, reeds are half-woven and still green at the cut ends. Everything has the particular neatness of a room you have entered a second too late.

But the lateness never ends. Walk the walkways at noon — walk them at what would be noon again if noon could repeat — and you will always have just missed them. A figure bends far across a flooded paddy. You cross toward it, and the paddy is only buffalo and white cranes again. Around a corner, a courtyard is empty, but the water in a jar is still rocking gently against its sides. A mat has been newly rolled. A fish has been cleaned on a board, its scales in a bright, wet pile, its flesh laid in strips. No flies come.

Stay longer, and absence begins to develop habits.

The footprints, for instance. They are not common. This is not a crowded people; the spareness of the place, one tree to a courtyard meant for fifty, one pavilion at the end of a long terrace, is theirs. They believe — or their bodies believe, which may be the same thing — in distance. One keeper to a district of ponds. One family, if family is the word, to a cluster of garden-houses. Sometimes two together, sometimes three, never more that you can prove. Like the butterflies, they go in twos and threes when they go openly at all.

The prints they leave are bare, broad across the ball of the foot, the toes splayed from a life in mud and shallow water. The heel hardly marks. They walk as if weight were something to apologize for. You find the prints most often on lime paths after — after what? There is no after rain. You find them leading away from you around the sides of storehouses, down into the cool mouths of granaries, toward the water stairs. They do not fade while you watch them. If you look away, they are gone, the path swept or wetted clean, and somehow you cannot be angry. You have the sense that you were being politely avoided, not deceived.

They are shy the way wild animals are shy, and for a related reason. To be looked at here is to be fixed.

Everything in this valley holds its shape because it is held in mind as that shape: the canal full to its lip, the dome of the flowering tree, the blue paint unfaded, the ripe fruit at exactly the ripeness of fruit. The inhabitants are subject to their own law. Under a direct gaze they conform to themselves so intensely that they almost stop. The distant worker holds the bend of working. The heron — no, not the herons, the herons are herons — but a tall woman standing in taro at the edge of vision will hold the pose of standing, one foot raised, neck inclined, until looking becomes a kind of unkindness and you turn away and release her.

So they have learned to live in the unattended parts of the world. In the undersides and interiors. In the hollow hills. Below the meadows that are roofs. Down the cisterns where gardens grow upside-down from old pavements and black fish turn in lit water. Their true houses may not be the open garden-houses at all, with their seed jars and ovens and sleeping platforms; those might be only their noon rooms, their public faces. Their real dwellings could be in the layered dark between floors, in spaces a hand's breadth turned, over generations, into corridors: cool, green-smelling, loud with dripping that arrives seconds late. Places where your shadow remembers evening. There, perhaps, they can move at ordinary speed.

If you become still enough — not watching, not waiting in the predatory way, but sitting on a bench until your own breathing conforms to the place — they will come nearer than sight.

First it is a pressure in the ribs, below hearing. The cranes do that when they stretch their necks. So do the frogs with their silent throats. It is the local form of speech. The air here will not carry voices properly; sound goes damp and astray and lands in the wrong direction. So they speak, if they speak, through the breastbone. A greeting feels like a single deep note touched inside the body and withdrawn. A warning is sharper, behind the sternum. Laughter, which you may feel once and mistake for your own sudden sadness easing, ripples.

Then a smell: clay, crushed green stems, wet rope, skin kept clean in pond water. Then the sense of work continuing around you without hurry. A basket set down without a sound. A leaf trimmed. A hand, perhaps, steadying the bench you sit on so it will not creak and startle you.

They are not tall. Carrying will do that over enough lifetimes — baskets of soil, baskets of silt, baskets of ash and dung mixed in exact proportions, carried up onto roofs that were themselves carried onto roofs. Their shoulders are broad and rounded, their necks short and strong, their arms long in a way that is only noticeable when they reach. Their hands are the clearest thing about them. Everyone here, old and young, seems to have large, exact hands, the nails trimmed to crescents, the palms polished like their wooden mallets, the fingers stained green at the creases and red with ochre and dark with charcoal. When they die — but we will come to dying.

Their skin is brown deepened by sun that never leaves, and kept wet. Not sweating, exactly. They shine as the buffalo shine in the paddies. Many go bareheaded with their hair cut close or bound up in fiber, but out in the open plazas they wear hats woven from reed and taro leaf, broad, low-domed, shaped so cleverly that the small dense shadow of the brim joins the small dense shadow at their feet. Their eyes are narrowed, not from suspicion but from light, and when you catch one — rarely, and then only in reflection, in a lotus pond, where looking is indirect enough to be forgiven — the eye is dark and wet and unsurprised by you.

They wear what the place grows: skirts and wraps and aprons of beaten fiber and split reed, dyed charcoal and ochre and that shell-blue, always a little damp, always smelling faintly of the pond they last waded through. Woven charms hang at their wrists as they hang on the horns of buffalo. Amulets against what? Perhaps against crowding. Perhaps against being seen too clearly.

They eat lightly and continuously, the way hunger works here: a gourd opened, a few grains from a painted jar, fish eaten raw or warmed beside the smokeless fire, fruit taken at the moment of ripeness and replaced, later, when nobody is attending to the branch. They drink water that tastes of clay and stems. Their teeth, when a pond reflection gives you a smile, are worn flat from grit in the ground grain. Their digestion is probably slow. Everything about them has adjusted to a world without urgency, where time does not pass so much as accumulate in layers.

Children are almost never seen, which makes sense if children are the least able to hold a shape under scrutiny. You will find small footprints among the broad ones, but only in the soft margins, and small tools laid beside large ones, and a sleeping platform with a second, narrower mat rolled at its foot. Sometimes, feeling with your breastbone, you catch a quick double-pressure, shy and mirthful, from behind a seed jar, gone before you can turn your head. Their games may be the reason paths around pavilions deliver you inside them. Their games may be why butterflies always travel in company. It would be wrong to chase the feeling. Everything here raises its young by not insisting on them.

Old age is also a matter of layers. A keeper does not so much retire as descend. As the back bends further, as the hands grow more exact and less quick, they take charge of lower gardens, then of root cellars and covered reservoirs, then of the upside-down beds in the cisterns where the light comes from below the water. Below that, rumor — not spoken, only felt in the ribs on very still noons — says there is work among the pavements of the ancestors, maintaining the maintenance.

For nothing here is allowed to rot at the proper rate, not even people. When one of them dies, and they must, because new footprints appear beside the paths, the dead are carried — in baskets, in twos and threes, at unwatched moments — down through the planting soil and the clay and the crushed shell, and laid into the making of a new floor. A broad tile pressed over them while the clay still holds the print of the hands that set it. Soil sifted over that. A garden cut in a spiral above.

That is why you can dig anywhere and find no earth that belongs to itself. The valley is not built on its people. It is built, tenderly and without waste, *of* them.

They are not ghosts. That is important. Ghosts would explain the silence too cheaply. They breathe. They leave warmth in ovens. Their cut reeds smell cut. They mend the tile you broke and leave you with the worse, unbroken tile and your memory of violence. They refill what you pick. They keep the lotus ponds as mirrors and the canals at the lip and the weeds spaced like ornaments, not because the world cannot change, but because somebody, in every district, at every level, is continually remembering it correctly.

They are the reason the fauna is silent, perhaps. The frogs watched throats swell for generations and learned discretion. Or the people learned from the frogs. By now teacher and pupil are indistinguishable, and the whole valley keeps its voice in its chest.

If you stay too long, they will begin, cautiously, to include you in the spareness. A bowl will be set where you customarily sit, though you have no customary place. Sandals not quite your size will migrate toward your door. A hat will be left on a peg in the noon room. Your shadow will stretch long in the alleys between storehouses, where evening is remembered, and people-shaped pressures will pass through your ribs in greeting as you go.

And one day, bending far across a paddy to your work — whatever work your hands find, carrying, sifting, lashing, trimming — you will understand that to a new arrival you are only a figure in the distance, already gone when approached, leaving behind a swept path, a warm oven, a basket half-woven, and the strong impression that someone has just, politely, stepped away.
