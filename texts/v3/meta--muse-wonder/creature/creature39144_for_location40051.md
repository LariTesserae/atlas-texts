It does not live on the bottom. The bottom is only where it goes to feed.

It is born in the ceiling.

In spring, when the light that never reaches fifty metres down has, for a few weeks, become just strong enough to brown the underside of the ice with algae, the eggs rise to it. They are not laid there. Laid down on the cobble fields, among the black and green and rust-red stones, they come loose from whatever stalk or jelly held them and begin, very slowly, to float.

Nothing floats cleanly here. The water is already too cold. As an egg rises through the slurry, frazil finds it — platelet after platelet of new crystal striking its soft outer coat and sticking, so it goes up not smooth but furred, spiky, turning. Most are stolen sideways by the tide before they have risen ten metres and are ground against a keel or eaten or carried out over the sill to brighter, warmer water where they cannot live. Those that make it lodge in the platelet layer, that soft second roof hanging under the sea ice, metres thick and spongy and lit, on bright days, from very far above, like cloud.

It hatches there, blind.

Not blind the way the Greenland shark looks blind, with clouded eyes that still turn toward movement. Its eyes close before it is a finger long. They stay under the skin as two darker places, unopening. Light has never told it anything true. Up, for a while, is simply where the food is. It crawls upside down through the platelet ice, belly to the ceiling, fins broadened and stiffened at the edge into something between a paddle and a hand, the tips hooked, able to grip and pull its body through slush that would swallow anything built for open water. Its mouth is underneath — which is to say, facing down into the black — no, facing toward the ice. It grazes.

The algae is sparse and tastes, insofar as anything here tastes, of cold and iron. More important are the animals that graze it before it can: small crustaceans, translucent, quick by the standards of this water, picking through the crystals. It eats them by waiting. Stillness is its first skill. In the platelet layer it hangs motionless while frazil settles on its back and makes it part of the ceiling, and when something moves close enough to be felt as a disturbance against its face and along the long lateral seam of its body, it opens its mouth and takes the water in, animal and crystal together. The crystal does not kill it. Its blood is loaded with the same quiet chemistry that keeps the shark unfrozen — proteins that find the face of a forming ice grain and bind to it, stop it growing, hold it small until it can be passed or melted by the tiny, hoarded warmth of digestion. Everything it swallows comes in frozen. Everything in it has to be taught, chemically, how not to freeze further.

It grows a millimetre at a time. A season in the ceiling might add the length of a thumbnail. Its skin is not scaled — scales would trap frazil, give the ice places to take hold. It is smooth and loose, grey-white below and mud-grey above, and it secretes a clear mucus, constantly, at a cost it can barely afford, so that crystals slide instead of anchoring. When it sleeps — it sleeps in the sense that even this metabolism can go lower — it has to wake at intervals and shiver itself free, a slow ripple from head to tail, shedding a soft fall of accumulated ice that sinks a little, melts a little in relatively warmer water, rises again.

Eventually it gets too heavy for the ceiling. That is not a metaphor. Platelet ice will hold a juvenile. It will not hold an adult. One tide, usually on the outward pull, when the whole basin is draining back over the sill and every eddy is unwinding and re-forming the other way, it lets go.

The fall takes hours.

It does not swim down. It sinks, fins spread, turning slowly to keep its belly toward the mud it cannot see and can already feel as a change in the return of silt against its skin. Frazil streams past it sideways. The dimness is gone long before it is halfway. There is a point — it crosses it every generation and never registers it as an event — where even the faint blue-grey that showed there was a ceiling ceases, and below that everything it will ever know is touch, pressure, the tick of crystals against its face, the hiss of sediment being dragged over sediment, and, transmitted strangely clear through the water from the ice far above, the groan of the pack shifting.

On the mud it becomes a different shape without changing. It flattens. Those hooked fins that pulled it through slush now pull it over the plain, or dig in at the edges and hold it when the tide is at full strength and the current is lifting clouds off the floor. Pressed flat as a skate, nearly the length of a person when it is old — and it can be old, fifty years, a hundred, nobody has ever counted its rings and lived in its time — it can let water strong enough to carry a diver pass over its back and only stir the mucus on its skin. It keeps its head slightly raised. Its mouth, turned now toward the mud, works differently. It no longer grazes. It filters, and it scavenges.

There is very little to do either with. Marine snow falls toward it and is mostly stolen before it arrives. What reaches the bottom is an accident of eddies: a dead amphipod, a fleck of the sunken kelp that lies in olive ribbons across the mud rotting over years, a feather star's lost arm, shit from the ceiling, a fish that died up in the world and sank through everything to this last flat place. It finds food by smell, except smell here is not like smell in moving air. A scent does not arrive. It is carried away, and carried back twelve hours later when the tide turns, so that a dead fish a kilometre off announces itself twice, coming and going, and the animal may spend three days working a zigzag up a signal that reverses twice a day, patient as the basin itself. When it arrives, the isopods are usually already there, hand-sized woodlice picking at the carcass, and the amphipods swarming in their brief plenty. It does not drive them off. It settles alongside and feeds for as long as there is feeding — weeks, on a shark; months, once in its life, on something larger that came in over the sill already dying and never left. Its teeth are small and blunt and numerous. It is built to rasp, to hold, to take in cold flesh and crystal and grit and sort them somewhere inside itself in the dark.

It is not warm. Held in a hand — and hunters have held the young ones, drawn up by accident on bone hooks through the platelet slush, and mostly let them go back down the hole because there is almost no meat on them and what there is makes the mouth numb — it would feel colder than the water it came from, an animal at the temperature of its own freezing prevented only by chemistry. Its heart beats so slowly that the tide is a faster clock. Between beats, silt can settle on its back. In time, if it rests too long in one place, the basin begins to do to it what it does to the lost knife and the stone sinker: coat it, half-bury it, take it in. Adults learn, if learning is the word for a behaviour worn into them, to shift their weight and ripple their fins in their sleep, so the mud never quite closes.

It has no voice. It can hear, after its fashion — the whole underside of its head is sensitive to vibration, and the groaning of keels grinding overhead tells it to move off the shallow whalebacks of buried bedrock and into the flat, because ice that is groaning is ice that may be driving a keel down twenty metres and rearranging the ceiling. But it makes no sound of its own except accidental ones: the faint tick when its fin-hooks strike a cobble, a soft expulsion of silt when it settles. Others of its kind find each other, when they do, by chemistry and by patience, converging over days on the same fallen thing. Mating, observed by no one, must happen there among the scavengers, unhurried and largely still, eggs released to the stones with a coat that hardens in the cold and then, at some signal of season even the adults down in the permanent black can feel only as a change in what rains from above, loosens and begins the long spiky rise back to the roof.

It has never seen the surface. It has never breathed air. Brought up through a fishing hole it would die not dramatically but by a cascade of small failures: the light first, enormous and useless to sealed eyes; then warmth, a few degrees of it, unbinding the careful balance of its blood; then air on skin made only for water under ice. It belongs so completely to the in-between that the two boundaries of its world — ice above, mud below — are not boundaries to it at all. One is the nursery it fell from. The other is the table it feeds at. Between them is not emptiness. Between them is the whole medium of its life, thick with crystal going in every direction at once, reversing twice a day, too cold to be liquid and too restless to be solid.

The very old ones carry evidence of the brief human presence up above: a scar at the lip where an iron hook furred with rust finally rotted out, a groove from a braided line that sawed and healed, once — carried for years, worked loose at last on a cobble field — a stone sinker knotted into the loose skin of a fin. They do not fear the lines. The lines mostly come back empty, and so does the animal, mostly, from its own slow hunting: days of filtering silt for a mouthful, weeks pressed flat in a mud cloud of its own making, head up, eyeless, tasting the water reverse and reverse again, feeling through its chest a turbulence too large and too silent to be heard.

Twice a day the basin changes its mind. It changes with it. That is the whole of its calendar — tide, and the slightly longer tide of the ice groaning, and the longest tide of all, which is its own body growing too heavy for the ceiling it was born in, and its eggs, light enough to rise back to it.
