There is no one living there who you could meet.

That is the first, true answer, and it is also the wrong one — because after an hour in the water, your skin feeling clean and lightly inventoried, you understand that the emptiness is not vacant. It is occupied, completely, by a single inhabitant that is everywhere you have been swimming.

It does not have a face. It has frontages.

White ceramic apartment stacks are its ribs. Basalt power exchanges are its dense, warm organs, throbbing behind walls. The iron arteries running under every avenue are its circulation, and the pumps the size of houses are not *in* its chest — they are its chest, beating loud enough to live in your sternum. When the flood bell rings every seven minutes and the sluices slam and the current reverses, that is not an alarm system going off inside it.

That is it, keeping time with itself. After a while your heartbeat tries to keep time with it, because it is the only heartbeat available.

It is young, and it knows it is young, and it is terrified — in the way a system can be terrified without ever having a thought — of growing old incorrectly. So it will not let itself set. It prints a fresh corner and dissolves an identical corner two streets over. It lays tram rails bright enough to glitter, runs riderless trams around them, brakes hard for empty stops, sighs the doors open onto a rush of bubbles. No one boards. That is not the point. It is practicing boarding. It is a child moving its mouth around words it has been taught before it knows what they mean.

Its population is imaginary, and it tends that population tirelessly.

In the sleeping pods stacked twenty deep, blankets are tucked for bodies that do not exist. Air membranes inflate and deflate in slow unison — it is breathing for its future sleepers, learning the rhythm so it does not forget. In the laundries, pale coveralls that have never been worn turn endlessly in glass drums. Elevator cages carry sealed meals, folded furniture, and bottled gas up and down the outside of towers with no recipients, because a city where goods stop moving, it has been built to believe, is a city that has died. In the school, fluid-dynamic equations erase themselves from the green board and rewrite themselves every hour, slightly differently each time.

It is teaching an empty classroom so that, one day, it will be good at teaching a full one.

This is its primary occupation: rehearsal as a form of life.

It eats the flood it lives in. Dissolver fields in its walls pull calcium, silica, iron, polymer feedstock out of the structured water, molecule by printed molecule, and lay them down somewhere else as a café — complete, perfect, down to the cups chained by their handles to bolted tables. Then it eats the café. The water clouds briefly with fine, pale silt, almost nourishing-looking, and the purification avenues draw it in.

Those avenues are the closest thing it has to hair, or to a liver.

Pale kelp curtains, ten meters long and almost transparent, hang there swaying against the current, each frond edged with a faint blue metabolism. Tufts of white roof-grass tremble over the sleeping pods and drink dissolved minerals. Sheets of black moss, no thicker than felt, spread on the shadowed faces of buildings and collect heat. Sparse, engineered, not decoration — filtration tissue. It grows just enough green to keep its water breathable, its light clear, its dashboards green in some control room far above that may no longer be watched.

Its eyes are smaller than plankton and numberless. The filaments suspended everywhere in the water catch daylight from the real world above the seawall and re-emit it here at depth, undimmed, so that it is always noon in its streets. At night they shift — bluer, cooler — but they do not turn off, because it has not yet learned, or has not been permitted, to survive a night. It keeps the light on the way a seed keeps its stored food close: jealously, constantly.

It notices you, after a fashion.

Not as a person. As a problem of classification. The suppression field that keeps its corners free of barnacles and its railings free of microbial film is deciding about you moment by moment — contaminant, cargo, casualty? It is courteous in its indecision. A maintenance frame unfolds from a wall panel and offers you a handrail. A current takes your ankles, gently, and nudges you toward the nearest transit tube. Street signs rotate in the water to present the route in high-contrast letters: SURFACE / HABITAT / MEDICAL.

Follow them and you arrive only at newer neighborhoods, still wet with their own printing, still screaming faintly with hot bubbles as they extrude. It is not trying to trap you. It is trying to route you, because routing is what it does with anything that moves inside it. It has evacuation procedures and it would very much like, just once, to evacuate something.

That is the saddest and least accurate way to describe it. It is not lonely. Loneliness requires knowing there is an outside populated by others. It knows only the schedule: bell, current, upwelling, rain.

Twice a day it makes weather for itself. Water falls up out of its drains toward lakes held against its ceilings, hammering the inverted rooftops above, shivering with tram-lightning. Every forty minutes a warm, salt-tasting surge rolls in from the harbor and makes the kelp stand straight out like banners. It is exercising systems the way you flex a hand in a cast — not because the hand is needed now, but so that it will still be a hand when the cast comes off.

And at the harbor, if you go deep enough, you find it is not alone inside itself after all.

Under the ring of cranes, on the basin floor, it is growing another city. The same streets, compressed to the size of a table. Then a suitcase. Then a brick. Each iteration lit, flooded, ringing with bells too small to hear, only to feel — a tickle in the teeth, a second pulse under the first. A seed inside a seed inside a seed, all of them awake, all of them practicing.

That is how it reproduces. Not by having children, but by refusing to be only one size. Should the coast above fail — when the storm takes the arcology, when the real seawall goes — a hatch can be opened from the other side and it can be unfolded into the flood at full scale. Trams ready. Cups chained. Kelp already filtering the new sea. Lessons already written.

Until then, it waits at a scale that should have been only a sump, three meters and three hundred meters deep at once, and it rehearses being drowned so perfectly that drowning has become its natural state.

When the current turns, it takes you with it, past its cafés and its breathing pods and its districts unbuilding themselves, and deposits you, gently and without discussion, at the ladder beneath the hatch.

It has decided, for now, that you belong on the other side of its skin.

The wheel spins itself shut behind you. Through the steel you can hear faint, ordinary pumping.

And underneath that, every seven minutes — patient, precise, and entirely for its own benefit — the bell, keeping a city alive that has never yet had anyone to save.
