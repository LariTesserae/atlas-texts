No one is assigned to live there.

The log in the plastic sleeve says so, in the tidy way of logbooks everywhere: inspection, fifteen minutes, initials, out. Green, green, amber. Pressure noted. Leak noted — *monitor*. The door pulled hard to its seal from the outside.

But the room is not empty between inspections. You can tell if you know how not to look for something standing up.

First there is only the fern.

Three pale fronds in the drain corner, rooted in nothing, shivering in the wall's tremor. And the algae under it, black-green on the steel. If you came back often enough — up the switchbacks, ears still plugged from the climb, hand finding the wet handle — you would notice the algae is not growing the way algae grows when it is left alone. It does not felt over. It is cropped. Neat crescent bites taken out of it at the edge where the spray keeps it youngest. It is grazed down to a lawn.

And the drain grate is never quite seated. One corner lifts a fraction. And the quarter-inch sheet of water crossing the diamond-plate does not always run clean to the corner. Sometimes it is troubled in a line, low down, as if something has dragged a soft belly through it and left a wake that the next minute's condensation has not yet erased — from the sump, to the warm plinth of the pump motor, to the great elbow of the pipe, and back.

It came down from the reservoir, most likely. Not on purpose. Nothing comes down a penstock on purpose.

Something small and river-born, an egg or a larva or a half-grown coldwater thing, taken in at the intake high above when the screens were being changed, shot down through the dark inside the iron at a pressure that should have killed it, and spat — miraculously, brokenly — through the one bad flange, in that fine needling spray, on the day the room was first charged. It landed in the fog by the motor. It stayed.

It has been making itself belong ever since.

By now it is the size and weight of a wet work boot, but lower, and wider, and built entirely for holding on. Four feet, if feet is the word, splayed and suctioned and webbed clear out to the toes, each toe tipped with a blunt, horny pad that finds the raised diamonds in the floor plate and locks. It does not walk so much as it persuades itself forward, one anchored foot at a time, because every six or seven minutes the solenoid clacks, the pipe takes the hammer blow in its elbow, and the whole room tries to shake loose whatever is in it.

That is why there are no spiders. Their work falls down. Its work is its body.

Its skin is the color the room has taught it: institutional green gone grey in the dimness, spangled palely across the back like new galvanizing, slick even when it is momentarily out of the water. Along its sides the skin thins until it is almost translucent, and you can see a slow, cloudy movement underneath, not quite blood, not quite breath. Its head is broad and flat and has no ears to ruin. Where ears would be there are only two tight, cartilaginous seals, puckered shut, gleaming. It does not hear the roar the way you would hear it, with that pressure-ache building down into the jaw.

It hears with its sternum.

That is its great occupation, and its great comfort. In the long roaring intervals between cycles it hauls itself, foot over deliberate foot, across the steel plain, through the cold sheet and the faint rainbow of oil, to the pipe where it enters its brutal turn down into the floor. The pipe is waist-high to a man; to it, the pipe is a cliff, a green iron wall curving up into the brown obscurity near the ceiling. At the base of the elbow it presses itself flat — chest, throat, the soft underside of its jaw — against the painted iron.

The river goes by inside. Its river. Water out of the high reservoir, still smelling, through the iron, of snowmelt and stone and deep, sunless cold. It cannot get back in. The inspection hatch is bolted against a pressure no hands could open, let alone its hands. But it can lie against the turn where the water strikes and shudders and sings in the metal, and let the sound come up through its bones in a language too low for the air. A continuous thunder in the breast. A river in flood saying the same thing over and over so fast it becomes a kind of silence to live inside.

When the town far below wakes and begins to draw, it knows before the gauge does. The groan changes pitch in its ribs. When the town sleeps and the pressure climbs, the pipe tightens against its cheek like a held breath. It keeps its whole calendar this way. Six minutes of flood. The clack. The hammer through the floor. The sump float rising under the grate, clicking, the pump jolting in its knees. The float falling. Six minutes again. A day is not light and dark in there — the amber work lamp never goes out, only dims a fraction when the motor kicks — a day is a hundred and fifty small shakings, and then the terrible interruption of the door.

It fears the door the way mountain animals fear a road.

First the seal sighs. Then dry air, thin and bright with altitude, cuts the fog. Then the sun, however briefly, finds the wet floor in a white blade, and the world outside delivers its unbearable quiet — wind, a truck far off on the pass, birdsong spaced out in all that emptiness. Sound with gaps in it. Sound you could fall into. It wedges itself then into the one true shelter the room affords: the narrow, warm shadow between the motor housing and its concrete plinth, where the leak-spray turns to perpetual mist and the heat makes a pocket almost like a summer bank. It seals its ears tighter. It lowers the pale, opaque lids over its eyes — eyes the color of the fern's new fronds, made for amber gloom and almost blinded by day — and waits for the boots to finish being careful on the slick plate.

It has watched boots long enough to learn the liturgy. Look at the panel. Green, green, amber. Tap the gauge that never sits still. Shout nothing to no one, because shouting is useless. Write. Go.

It has begun, in its own way, to keep the room.

This is what makes it more than a survivor. Between cycles it tends its corner. It clears the drain mouth with patient strokes of its forefeet so the sheet water keeps moving and the fern is not drowned. It licks mineral weep from the concrete seams and grazes the algae back from the fern's root — that hairline of damp dust caught in a bolt recess, the only soil in its world — so the fronds get what thin light the caged lamp throws that far. When the sump float sticks, as it sometimes does, gummed with a thread of its own shed skin, it nudges up through the grate from below and worries it loose before the pit can rise too far. It is rewarded, if reward is the word, by the jolt going through its body when the pump catches.

It drinks the ceiling rain where it falls, mouth upturned to the big irregular drops. It licks condensation off the sweating pipe in crooked lines, iron and cold paint on its tongue. Now and then a worker leaves something behind despite the rules — a washer, a bright bolt, a cable tie still smelling of the sunlit world — and these it collects, slowly, over weeks of dragging, and arranges in the lee of the chained red valve wheel, where the floor is a fraction drier. Not a nest. It sleeps pressed to the pipe, not in anything. More like an altar, or a map of things that fell out of the enormous elsewhere and survived being wet.

It is young, because the room is young. The paint has not chipped for it, the concrete has not learned to crumble; its whole life fits inside the brief history of this lining over the mountain. It may still be growing. Its back has begun to ridge where it braces against the hammer blow, and the pads of its feet have thickened into something that clicks, faintly, on the diamond-plate when the pump rests — the only sound it makes that is wholly its own. Perhaps in ten years, when the galvanizing has dulled and the fern has made a fourth and fifth frond out of pure persistence, it will be heavier, slower, greener, more completely the shape of the place. A creature whose pulse has entrained to a solenoid. Whose bones remember a reservoir it can hear but never re-enter. Whose meadow is three fronds wide and whose river runs forever through a wall.

And when the door has sealed again and the fifteen permitted minutes are over, and the inspector is already descending into ordinary quiet with ringing ears, it comes out of the warm fog by the motor. It crosses the steel plain, foot locking to foot, through the thin shining water.

It lays its breast against the elbow of the pipe, where the iron river hits its turn and does not stop, day or night.

It shivers, constantly — not from the cold.

From the tremor in the only world it has, holding it, the way the wall holds the fern.
