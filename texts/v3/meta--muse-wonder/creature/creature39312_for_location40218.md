At first you will mistake them for miners.

They come up in the elevator sometimes — to check the trunk shutters, to service the pumps that push cold dry air down the shaft — and at a distance they have a miner's economy of movement, a miner's tolerance for being enclosed, a miner's clothes patched at the elbows and knees. Ochre canvas. Soft boots. Tools that do not clink.

Come closer and the mistake dissolves. Miners look at rock as something to be removed. These people look at everything, including each other, as something that is still deciding what shape to take.

They are not especially tall, not especially strange to look at. Eighty years is not enough to remake a species. It is enough to remake a posture, a skin, a set of reflexes. What the tree-city has made, first of all, is quiet people who listen through their bodies.

An older woman on a bench in the Pollen Commons will seem to be doing nothing at all. Her shoes are off — thin-soled slippers of leaf-cloth, folded neatly beside her — and her bare feet are flat on the polished root-wood. She is receiving the evening report before it is read aloud. She can feel a sawmill cycling down two districts away as a change in the grain under her heels. She can feel her grandson coming home before she sees him, not by his footsteps exactly, but by the particular negotiation between his weight and a branch that has always liked him and lifts a fraction to meet him. When she wants to answer someone across the plaza, she does not raise her voice. She stamps once, lightly, in code, and taps the bench beside her with an open palm.

Everyone learns this before they learn to read. Children are taught letters, of course — moths carry them in wax tubes for oil — but they are taught vibration first. Press, listen, wait for the pulse. One beat a minute in a limb that is thinking its way somewhere. Faster in a house-wall that wants pruning. A dull, spreading ache in the wood when rot has started somewhere uphill. To shout below is not just rude. It is illiterate. It muddies the medium everyone else is trying to think in.

So they speak low, and many speak with their hands as much as their mouths. During Bloom Hours, when the hand-sized moths are out and a misplaced vibration can pull a whole spiral of pollinators off course, the factories and terraces switch almost entirely to sign. The dialect that has grown up from that necessity is beautiful and total: whole sentences delivered by people still holding tools, still carrying children, their fingers flicking in the greenish shade. Teenagers, inevitably, prefer it even after hours. There are jokes that can only be told silently. There are flirtations conducted solely through a shared root while looking studiously in opposite directions.

Their skin gives them away next. No one down there is ever washed, and no one is dirty. Water is poison, contraband, a thing carried only in sealed skins under permit from the condensation altars in the shaft. So skin is oiled instead — lamp oil, nut oil, a finer milky oil the tree itself weeps in certain seasons — worked in every morning until the person takes on a faint, sealed sheen. It smells faintly of cedar and mint and hot copper. Hair is oiled and braided tight or shaved close. Lips are oiled. The insides of nostrils are oiled, with a little wooden applicator everyone carries, because the air, for all its coolness, will split an unprotected membrane by afternoon.

What sweat they produce, they produce reluctantly. It is partly training, partly diet, partly the simple selection of who could stand to stay after the first mining years. The ones who stayed stopped craving a drink the way surface people understand craving. Thirst down there is a schedule and a flavor, met with a cheekful of cool, dense fruit pulp, a spoon of salted oil, a piece of leaf bread so rich it coats the mouth. Newcomers talk obsessively about water for a month — about rain, about baths, about swimming — in the wistful way exiles talk about a country that tried to kill them. The city-born listen politely and do not really believe them. A child born in the Root Flats will have heard of rain before she sees it. She will imagine it, usually, as a kind of industrial accident.

Their hands are broad-palmed from listening. Their soles are thick and astonishingly articulate; a pruner can read a street with her feet the way you might read a line of print, stumbling — literally stumbling — over a false note in the wood. Their eyes have adjusted to a daylight that never comes from any one direction. They do not squint upward. They have a habit, disconcerting at first, of looking slightly past you while they talk, tracking the moths, the slow rearrangement of leaves the size of warehouse roofs, the subtle brightening of a light-fiber that means weather has changed around the trunk thirty kilometers above and three hundred meters up, in a world most of them visit twice a year.

They work, almost all of them, at adjacency.

That is the word they would use if you pressed them, though mostly they just name trades: pruner, listener, surveyor, beetle-handler, fiber-tender, bud-midwife. A factory engineer rendering fallen limbs into timber is also checking, palm down every hour, that the limb she is feeding to the friction-saws is truly finished growing and not merely resting. A surveyor does not so much draw next year's streets as argue for them, rerouting a dotted line of copper staples around a green knuckle that has declared other intentions. A woman who plants a house — and a house takes four or five years of feeding with current and resin and low electric song before it is even weather-tight — is making a commitment more binding than most marriages. The house will outgrow the plan she had for a nursery. The house may, in its second decade, decide that the kitchen wants to be round. She will not hammer it flat. She will call the district pruner, make resin-milk tea, and have the long, serious, faintly comic conversation that all domestic life down there eventually becomes: what is this room trying to do, and can we live with it if it does?

Even the beetle-handlers, who come closest to the old surface image of labor — leading their dog-sized beetles along the terraces at dawn, mandibles blunted, chitin oiled against the dryness — are not clearing a road so much as maintaining a truce about where the road, for now, is.

This makes them patient in a way that can look, to a visitor, like passivity. It is not. It is a population that has learned that force is extremely expensive underground. Cut wrong and you do not get a stump, you get a black sweet rot that runs along a living root overnight and takes a terrace with it. Flood a root, grab an ankle with pale new growth, try to hold a street exactly where last spring put it, and you learn. They negotiate with light, with current, with beetles and owls and the tree's own slow pulse. They are relentless negotiators. They simply do it at the speed growth understands.

Among themselves, that patience becomes an ethic. You do not interrupt a person who has a palm flat on something. You do not finish a house, a sentence, or a child's education too firmly. Licenses matter enormously — to plant a house-seed, to cut, to open a bud-yard, to carry unsealed water — because one person's improvisation becomes everyone's architecture by autumn. But within a license, improvisation is honored. The loveliest apartment towers near the trunk were once miners' huts. Everyone knows which families talked their huts into becoming that.

They are born, mostly, to people who were born here now. They marry late, often when a bud-yard forecast suggests there will be room. They keep their families small and their households elastic: a widowed fiber-tender may be absorbed, over a season, into the widening wall-space of a cousin's house. The dead are dried, oiled, and given to the deep undersides of branches where the root-moles farm their fungus. No one calls this burial. They say the person has been *returned to negotiation*. In a year there will be mushrooms. People eat them without sentimentality and with a certain attention, the way you might listen for a familiar rhythm in the wood.

At night they lie in rooms that are still extending themselves, an inch of new cork, a beam sighing as it cools. Their papers lift in the tree's exhale. Their curtains stir. A baby, lulled not by rocking but by that slow pneumatic pulse coming through the floor, sleeps with one foot unconsciously pressed to the wall.

If you ask one of them — the woman on the bench, say, when the growth report is done and the moths are rising in their spirals — whether she would like to live on the surface, she will consider the question seriously. Politeness, too, is a form of listening.

She will say she has been. She will say the sky was very interesting but it did not answer when she put her hand down. She will say the light up there falls on everything whether it wants it or not. Then her grandson arrives across the Commons, not quite where the path was this morning, and she feels him coming a moment before she turns — and she turns, exactly on time, to meet him.
