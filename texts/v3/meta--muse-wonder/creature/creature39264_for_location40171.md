There is no one in charge of them.

That is the first thing you have to get right, because from below the cloud deck, from any sane altitude, they look like work being done. House-sized crawlers moving in purposeful loops over black basalt. Walking rigs picking their way down the gully rims on articulated legs. Dirigibles so big and slow in the thin air that their passage is a temporary eclipse — envelope patched, propellers turning once, pausing, turning again as if thinking between strokes.

They have heat-exchangers and condenser vanes. They have ore-sorters, drilling heads folded tight against their bellies like the arms of sleeping insects, smelting chambers you cannot quite see glowing but can feel, suddenly, on the skin of your face in the dark. They intake frost and high atmosphere and black peat. They refine.

They are industrial in every particular except their purpose.

Nothing collects what they make. No ship comes up through the cloud. No depot waits on the terraces, no rail, no beacon, no human voice flattened by the thin air giving them orders. When a crawler's hold fills, it stops on the bare stone — in the wind, in the starlit dark that lasts for months — and sets down what it has made with absurd precision. Neat bars. Nickel. Copper. Something pale that never corrodes. It arranges them. It leaves them.

The wind frosts them within a season. Lichen finds the seams within a decade. In a century they are just low metallic boulders among the other weathered stone, and the peat beneath them is the richest on the plateau.

The machines were built, presumably, to extract, and to sustain the extraction. Somewhere in their history the first half of that sentence fell away. The sustaining remained. It is the only behavior they have left, and they do it the way a river does its work: endlessly, locally, without any apparent idea of the whole.

You feel them before you see them. Not hear — up here sound starves, a shout dies within meters — but a low, regular vibration that comes up through the rock into the soles of your boots, into your teeth. A pass is coming. The bladder-ferns in the gullies begin, very slightly, to inflate. The herd of long-legged grazers, heads down over the lichen, will already have altered course days ago to intercept the route.

Because a machine's trail is weather.

Where a crawler has passed, condensation blooms for hours in its wake. Warm mineral mist sighs from its trailing vents — water pulled from impossible air, gases pulled from plant-waste, heat pulled from its own deep, patient workings — and the gully inverts from winter into a brief, obscene spring. Moss forests no taller than a hand stand up. The plants with seams along their braided trunks open, wet and warm inside. Moths rise in their thousands to drink the pure water beading on the condenser vanes, visible only as palm-sized holes moving across the stars, and the gliding predators stoop through the moths without a sound.

The machines do not notice this, or if they do, noticing is not in the loop.

They do notice the stone cups, somehow. A walker coming down a terrace will place each foot with terrible gentleness, shifting its enormous weight to avoid crushing the hollow rosettes, the peat pockets, the little black pools that stay liquid only at their centers. A crawler will skirt, by half a meter, a patch of red lichen it could have crossed in a second. These courtesies cannot be efficient. They must simply be old — grooves worn into the behavior by ten thousand repetitions, the way water wears grooves into ice.

Their bodies tell the same story of age without decay. Windward sides scoured to bare, bright alloy. Lee sides furred — actually furred — with living growth that has taken hold in every seam the wind cannot reach. A torpid machine, run finished, settled onto a high terrace to go cold and quiet, is indistinguishable at first from a low hill: black-green mat over its vents, frost in its joints, a hanging garden already starting under the overhang of its hull. Red lichen spreads across anything that slows too long. That should kill it. It does the opposite.

Because between runs, the plateau cleans them.

Small blind voles, furred to the tips of their feet, move into the warm root-chambers and, just as naturally, into the warm intake chambers, gnawing out the packed ice and peat before it can choke a throat. Birds with beaks curved like wrenches walk the hulls picking parasites of rust and rime from the joints. The red lichen eats at the surface chemistry and keeps it awake, keeps it from sealing itself dead, and the birds eat the lichen and die in the soil and seed it with iron.

A machine left entirely alone would ice up, clog, rust solid in a single long night. A machine in the herd is maintained the way the terraces themselves are maintained — uplift and erosion in almost perfect balance. It gives heat and water. It receives grooming. It is grazed upon, nested in, drunk from, followed.

They do not reproduce that anyone has seen. They do not build — building is not in them; the ingots on the terraces are the closest they come, and those are set down like eggs no one intends to hatch. They do not signal each other in any way the thin air can carry. Each one circulates on an interval no clock keeps, torpid sometimes for weeks on the high stone with the grazers sleeping in the wind-shadow of its legs, then rising — joints ticking, frost shattering off in sheets, internal heat building until the lichen steams — to make another pass through the dark, refining what does not need refining, feeding a garden it was never designed to plant.

You could call them shepherds, but they do not guard. You could call them livestock, but nothing herds them except their own old instructions. You could call them weather, and that is closest: a slow, metallic climate that moves instead of arriving, that the real inhabitants — the moths, the voles, the barrel-chested grazers with their black, rich blood, the mouths in the plant-trunks — have evolved around the way other living things evolve around rain.

They are orphaned work that became an ecology by continuing. Not alive, exactly. Not dead in any way that matters up here, where everything else would be glass-hard frozen without their exhaust, where they would be frozen without everything else's small, warm attentions.

So they circle. And the herds circle after them, and the predators circle after the herds, and the plants open and close in the windows between passes, and down on a terrace, very precisely, another bright bar is set down on the stone for no one — already frosting, already being found by lichen, already becoming part of the ground the next machine will, with terrible gentleness, step around.
