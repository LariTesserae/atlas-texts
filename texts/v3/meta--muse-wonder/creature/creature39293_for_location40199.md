Nothing lives in the streets.

That is the first mistake of looking. You keep expecting the life to be *in* the city the way animals are in a forest — something smaller than the place, moving between its parts, leaving it when it dies. Birds in the canopy. Hands on the rails. Mouths for the fruit. But the polish on the rails stopped increasing because there is no longer any difference between the hand and the rail.

They are still here. They have only stopped being separate from their houses.

Look longer at a doorway, one of those arches where two trees leaned together in youth and fused. It is not quite symmetrical. No grown thing is. There is a thickening at shoulder height on one side, a shallow concave polish where, for centuries, someone entering ducked and brushed the same place. The trees did not grow that way by accident and then get used. The using and the growing were the same gesture, slowed down until you cannot tell them apart.

That is the resident.

Or rather, those are its outer edges. Once there may have been many of them — upright, mobile, animal in the ordinary way, with hunger and voices pitched for conversation. The city remembers that shape everywhere: in stairs bowed to a human stride, in sleeping platforms, in benches set around fountains at a social distance, in market stalls sized to arms reaching across. It was built for bodies that walked.

But nothing here was built with tools because, generation by generation, they stopped picking the tools up and instead *became* the building. Not as punishment. Not as metamorphosis in a flash. As an answer.

An answer to the heat that never lifts, so skin thickened to bark on the sunward side and stayed soft and wet only where it joins the stone. An answer to the noon that never ends, so eyes — useless under unmoving light, under canopy that throws the same shadow forever — sank first into the head, then into the trunk, then closed altogether under plates of lignified tissue. They do not see the city. They feel the exact pressure of its light through their leaves, all the time, at full strength.

An answer to the water, which is everywhere and needs no carrying. Feet put down roots not metaphorically but in earnest, splitting first into toes that lengthened into the cracks of the pavement, then into true rootlets, drinking straight from the sweating basalt. Why walk to a cistern when the whole mountain weeps drinkable water through its pores? Why have a gut, a mouth, a season of eating, when your crown is a hundred broad leaves held open in permanent photosynthesis and your skin is green along the ribs where you face the light?

And an answer, above all, to the sound.

No animal with ears could stay sane here. Conversation is impossible. Music is impossible. Even thought, in a skull built to think in the pauses between sounds, is impossible. So they gave up the pauses. The roar that fills your teeth is not, to them, noise laid over the world. It is the medium they finally learned to think *in*.

It runs through root and limestone and canal water without loss, the way it runs through your sternum when you submerge. They hear with the whole fused body of the city. Moss is the crucial tissue — that dense, black-green mortar holding blocks that never quite touch. It is not decoration and it is not merely structural. It is a continuous nervous felt, damp, light-sensitive, carrying vibration from district to district along every wall. A footfall — yours, now — registers on the rim and in the central basin at the same intensity, not as a small sound beneath the roar, but as a tiny local thickening of it. They know where you are the way you know where an itch is. Without interest, mostly. Without surprise. Surprise requires a before and an after, and they have largely let go of both.

That is why you never see a leaf fall. Why the fruit hangs uneaten and does not rot, why the seedlings hold at seedling height in the cracks, why the fallen leaves in the shaded courts lie in a fixed scatter with your footprint already in it.

Those are not omens. Those are relatives.

The fruit are theirs, still produced out of an old, bodily habit, swollen and scentless now because there is no one left to summon with scent and no season to ripen toward. They hold them the way you hold a thought you no longer need to finish. The seedlings in the pavement are children who began the same settling and stalled, deliberately, at the size where root and leaf first balance — waiting, not in hope, but in the perfect patience of a lung that has forgotten exhaling is an option. The great canopy trees whose trunks contain apartments are elders who incorporated their own dwellings as they widened. The temple whose pillars flower does. Its congregation is its pillars.

In the dense center, where the streets go vertical and five storeys of roof-garden over market over cistern over house over canal are stacked in wet green layers, the individuals have fused past counting. Shared walls became shared tissue. Root systems braided under the plazas a thousand years ago and then stopped needing to be plural. Whether it is now one being wearing a city, or ten thousand beings who have agreed so thoroughly they function as one, is a distinction that only matters to something that still moves around and meets people one at a time.

It feeds the way the whole plateau appears to feed: light straight down, water straight up out of the rock, mineral carried in that water and deposited as tufa on its own lower surfaces. It grows to a size and is that size. It replaces a leaf exactly, in place, without the interval of bareness. It does not hunt, court, migrate, sleep, or wake. It has no predators because there is nothing left that it has not either absorbed or excluded; no insect troubles its flowers because an insect would need a life cycle, and this place has abolished cycles in favor of a single held condition.

Its one activity — if it can be called activity — is the tone.

At the center, in the dry circle around the spring that climbs twenty feet and becomes steam without ever falling, the paving is bone dry because that is the closest thing it has to a mouth, or an ear, or a throat: the place where the internal water turns wholly to air and the vibration is generated, fed up through every canal and aqueduct and hollow stem and sweating pore until the entire mountain hums at the threshold of pain. It is not calling. It is not warning. It is the sound of a body too large to have a heartbeat having something slower instead — a continuous pressure, maintained. To live inside it, they had to let it replace the small private sounds of their own bodies. Blood, breath, digestion, voice: all surrendered to the one note, until the note was their voice, communal and uninflected to any ear that still expects words.

You could, as promised, survive here. Drink. Eat the fruit they make without meaning you to. Sleep on a platform shaped by someone who slowly became the wall behind it. But you would not be joining a population. You would be a warm, temporary irregularity moving across a skin of streets — noticed the way a tongue notices a crumb. If you stayed still long enough, stopped fighting the wet heat, let your sweat cease trying to evaporate and your hearing cease trying to find anything underneath the roar, the place would begin its slow courtesy toward you, too. Moss in the seams by your sleeping platform, thickening. A root-hair fineness at first. A preference for the sunny side of the court. A reluctance to move in the morning that is identical at noon and at what would have been midnight.

They are not dead, and they are not waiting to be rescued from being trees.

They arrived at the exact shape of their own use, and then, exquisitely, they stopped — holding the inhale, holding the note, holding the noon — the citizens and the city and the forest one continuous, deaf, photosynthesizing body, large enough that its far side has weather, patient enough that your entire life would register to it as nothing more alarming than a brief, damp change in the texture of the sound.
