The people who live there look ordinary inside the cities, and that is the first misleading thing about them.

Inside a pressure fence the air is a little thicker, a little warmer. Snow falls straight. Bread rises properly. An old woman can carry soup across a covered market without the wind taking the steam off it, children can shout in the schoolyards, a man can grow soft around the middle tending ovens in a bathhouse built over a hot channel. It is dense, vertical, eighty thousand people stacked in stone-colored towers, and at street level it could almost be any cold city anywhere: wet wool, ozone, fried fat, wet basalt after rain, scarves, mittens drying on radiators.

Then the fence takes their ears.

Everyone born on the plateau learns the pop of the boundary the way lowland children learn a door slam. Going out, it is a small loss in the head, a thinning. Coming home, it is pressure returning like a hand laid gently over both ears. Their inner ears are connoisseurs of it. They can tell, half-asleep in a transit capsule surfacing at night, whether the field is running high because a storm is scouring the towers. They pause without thinking at thresholds. Babies cry at the change and are soothed. Teenagers dare each other to cross back and forth until they are dizzy.

Outside, they change shape.

Not literally — not at first — but in posture, in pace, in clothes. Double coats, like the grazers. Hoods that seal at the throat. Boots made for ground that lies about its strength. They walk on marked stone with a deliberate, testing step, even on paths they walked yesterday, because paths can have new crust over scalding water by morning. They stop often to listen. On the plateau sound is eaten by wet rock and open water, so a tick, a drip, a faint sigh of ice giving way is information. Their conversations outside the fence are shorter and spaced farther apart, not from reverence but from physics. You learn not to waste breath where the air is thin.

Their bodies are learning the altitude faster than their buildings can rise with it.

The firstcomers — and there are still firstcomers alive, people who remember when the slab was lower and the lowland clouds were not yet below them — have the careful hearts of lifelong smokers whether they smoked or not. They rest at landings. They know their pulse in their teeth. Their grandchildren, born at three and a half thousand meters, have barrel chests under their sweaters and an unhurried way of breathing that looks like laziness until you watch them climb a maintenance pylon in a crosswind, talking the whole way up. Their blood is rich. Their sleep is deep. They get headaches in the lowlands on the rare trips down, complain that the air there feels soupy and too warm and smells of too many finished things.

They are not hardy in the romantic sense. They get frost-nipped ears in August. They lose the feeling in two fingers mending a fence and laugh about it in the baths that night, soaking in geothermal water while the windows fog and the whole room smells of minerals. They sunburn badly under that white, close-feeling sun, even through cloud, and wear broad, stiff hats that give their silhouettes a mushroom look against the steppe. Their skin is wind-scoured at the cheekbones. Their eyes squint habitually at knife-edged shadow. Children are freckled violently by October.

What they do, almost all of them in some rotation, is finish the world.

This is not a metaphor they would use lightly. A third of any city can, in a given spring, be out walking ahead of a designed river: geologists in bright, patched parkas reading a channel scored into basalt the year before; ecologists carrying plover nests, absurdly calm birds and all, a hundred meters to the left; herd-drivers — tall, mostly silent people who smell of dung and wet leather and can whistle a whole line of grazers into turning without raising their voice — moving the herds off a fan that by August is scheduled to be a marsh. Bakers do it. Teachers do it. The mayor does it with her sleeves rolled up, seeding a bank-to-be with grass whose roots arrived in a crawler the night before, in root sleeves, with their microbes like a dowry.

They are comfortable working on projects that refute them. A watershed no living person will see completed is not an abstraction; it is a roster. You sign your name in the city archive under a reach of river, a soil basin, a future forest hollow, knowing your great-grandchildren will walk there and complain that you got the slope slightly wrong. Funerals reflect this. On the plateau the dead are composted, by law and by preference, in the soil houses at the lee of each tower cluster — warm, steaming, sweet-smelling rooms where crushed rock and moss and dung and a person become, over months, the beginning of a meadow somewhere that currently has no meadow. Schoolchildren visit on field trips without squeamishness. *This is Mrs. Alon,* a plaque might say, in effect. *She will be the north bank in forty years.*

It has made them patient, and it has made them fussy about dates.

They date everything. Maps by the month, some by the week. Letters begin with altitude as well as address — not from pride, though there is pride, but because the number changes and the post and the medical records and the foundation crews need to know. A love letter might end: *We are at 3,412 now. The lake by the east pylon went in the night. The geese found the mud first, of course.* They do not name places after heroes. They name them after what happened and when: March Braid, Two-Week Pond, The Hollow That Held. A name is expected to expire. To insist a feature will be there next year is considered not optimistic but rude, a failure to pay attention.

Children learn the rules as rhymes before they learn to read, and learn them so deeply they are embarrassed for visitors who break them: stay on the stone; herds have the road, always, even if the road is a transit plaza and ten thousand long-legged, steaming, double-coated beasts are filing through the edge of the pressure field while commuters wait with their shopping, patient as a tide gate; let your body acclimatize in stages through the lock-houses at the city rim over days, letting the field warn you gently and then not gently, or do not go out at all.

That deference to the animals is real and unsentimental. The plateau people do not think of themselves as the sole inhabitants, and not exactly as the senior ones either. The grazers were there before the forests by design. The burrowers engineered the gravel fans more thoroughly in five years than any crew could have. The eagles nest on the maintenance pylons because the pylons were rebuilt, quietly, at great expense, with ledges. A household will keep its windows dark during a midge emergence because the waders need to feed undisturbed in the new ponds. Dinner in summer is often goose, or engineered fish still tasting faintly of cold lake, or grazer milk sharp and rich, or bread — endless bread, the great lowland luxury made defiantly well up there — eaten in a warm kitchen while frost is already silvering the outside of the glass in July.

They are urban people who live surrounded by emptiness and like both facts. They can travel three hundred kilometers across uninhabited steppe and feel neither lonely nor free in any simple way, just *between*. Between a constellation of lit towers behind them and the next one an hour under the basalt ahead. At evening, when the rim clouds catch the light and the tableland seems to float, they gather — as city people do — in bathhouses, in markets, on roof gardens where crooked, slow trees grow in managed air and drop their leaves absurdly early. Steam rises. The ponds outside begin their faint musical cracking as they refreeze. Someone reads the day's rise aloud from the measuring station — two millimeters, a fraction adjusted somewhere far below — and it gets the small, satisfied murmur other places give to weather.

They do not feel they own the land. It is too new, and it knows it, and so do they. They feel, at most, employed by it: a dense, warm, loud-in-season people living in islands of thickened air on a cold wet stone that is still becoming a continent, making soil out of their dead, walking carefully ahead of rivers they have scheduled, yielding the street to herds, checking the map's date before they trust it — and going home through the pop of the fence with frost on their shoulders, already half-listening for what will have changed by morning.
