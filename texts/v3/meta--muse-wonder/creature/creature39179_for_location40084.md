At noon you would say that nothing lives there, or that only machines do.

Stand at the central junction long enough and the first inhabitants declare themselves not by movement but by occupation. The silo ledges are thick with pigeons, packed shoulder to shoulder in whatever narrow band of shade the concrete throws, their feathers puffed and beaks open against the heat. Below them, sparrows work the rail spur in nervous, dusty companies, picking spilled grain from between the sleepers. They do not sing. They hop, peck, scatter at a shadow, settle again. Everything here feeds in that brief, mechanical way, between long periods of waiting for the temperature to become survivable.

Then the tide drops, and the streets empty sideways.

Fiddler crabs come out of the drains in numbers that make the concrete look briefly animated — thousands of them, mud-brown and improbable orange, pouring from grates and culvert mouths with their single huge claws held up like tiny shields. They cross the hot yards at a scuttle, pause, scuttle again, feeling their way back toward the exposed mud. They have burrows in every softened bank and outfall, and the maintenance crews who clear those outfalls are not so much removing them as negotiating with them. By tomorrow the crabs will have begun the work again.

They are the lowest shift. Above them, in size if not in number, are the ones who eat them.

A monitor lizard owns the main canal the way a dog owns a street. She — and it is usually a she you see, long, low, gray-brown, her tongue tasting the wet air — makes her rounds at walking pace, unhurried enough that the crabs part before her and the gulls lift only at the last moment. She knows the tide gates, the shaded bends where fish collect when the pumps run, the warm culvert where rats cross. Her kind have lived along this canal long enough that the night crews recognize individuals by scar and missing claw. In the hottest hours she lies half-submerged with only her head and the ridge of her back above the brown water, indistinguishable from a piece of drowned timber until she moves.

The cats are her terrestrial counterpart, though no one would call them owners. They are a lean, heat-flattened, inherited population, long-bodied and narrow-headed, their coats thinned to a dusty shortness — tabby, black, a washed-out ginger — sleeping all day in the precise geometry of shade under parked trailers and container stacks. They wake as the sodium lights come on and the yards cool from unbearable to merely hot, and then they hunt as a loose confederacy: rats along the grain lines, geckos fallen from lit walls, crabs stranded too far from a drain. No one owns them and everyone feeds them. Fish heads appear beside the dormitory steps. A saucer of water materializes under a weighbridge. Kittens are born in the warm, humming spaces behind compressors and grow up unafraid of forklifts, fluent in the schedules of trucks.

Indoors, the tenancy grows denser and less visible.

Open the fertilizer shed at midday and the inhabitant is first a smell — warm, ammoniac, almost sweet with rot — hanging in the dark under the trusses. Look up and the roof void is restless with bats, a colony packed into the uninsulated heat, shifting and squeaking at the edge of hearing, their bodies making the shadows seem to breathe. At dusk they leave in a column so steady it reads first as a change in the air above the street, a thinning and thickening, before it resolves into wings. All night they work the channel and the brine pans, harvesting the moths and midges that rise off the water, and before dawn they return to the same furnace-dark roof.

Swifts scream through the ventilation shafts of the cold store and nest where the cold leaks meet the heat, in that narrow, permanent twilight between temperatures. Geckos take the lit walls after dark, flattened pale shapes arranged around every lamp, motionless for minutes at a time, then snapping with an accuracy that seems to edit the moths out of the air. Ants run the cable conduits in lines that never quite stop. Cockroaches live behind the compressors where it is always warm and always a little greasy. Mosquitoes — soft, persistent, rising in clouds from the condensate puddles under the air-conditioning lines — are perhaps the truest natives of all. They need no season and no rain. The buildings themselves make their water, drip by drip, all year.

And then, scarcest and strangest in this company, the people.

There are not many. A port this size laid out for a town has been given instead a few hundred permanent residents and a rotating skin of shift workers, and they have become, by necessity, as crepuscular as everything else. The dormitory blocks in the low quarter hold them through the day: four stories of sealed concrete around unplanted courtyards, windows shut against the heat and the biomass, air conditioners weeping their small constant rain onto the walkways below. Inside, in rooms kept cave-cool and curtain-dark, families sleep in shifts that follow the tide table and the cargo schedule more faithfully than the sun. Work shoes stand on racks above floor level. Children can read a pump cycle by sound. Everyone knows which street floods to the ankle when a gate is mistimed and which doorway has the best brush strip against the night insects.

They emerge in the evening like another species coming off a roost.

First the shutters, then the scooters uncovered, then voices — and the town abruptly has hands, faces, appetites. They cross between buildings by the shade-side routes even after dark, out of habit older than thought. They are not built differently from people anywhere else, but the place has edited them: a patience with heat that looks, to a visitor, like languor; clothes worn loose and salt-ringed at the hem; skin kept covered at midday and perpetually faintly damp; a gait that conserves. They eat what the barges bring — rice, oil, tinned fish, frozen meat, vegetables already wilting from the inland journey — and supplement it, many of them, with what the channel gives: crabs, small fish, shellfish gathered off the mud at low tide by older women who move out across the glistening flat with buckets, unhurried, knowing exactly how much time the water has given them.

Their children grow up in a world with no grass to fall on and have the reflexes of shore animals: they time crossings, read drains, never leave a ground-floor window open after dark, can tell bat from swift by the shape of the departure. They keep pet crabs in buckets for a day and let them go. They feed the trailer cats by name — different names on different shifts, so that one animal may answer, selectively, to three. In the market under the iron roof they walk through the slow hover of flies over the meltwater drains rather than around it, the way their parents walk through the evening drift of moths off the channel: not enjoying it, not especially noticing it, accepting it as a medium.

That is, finally, what lives there. Not a species but a relay.

The place is held in continuous occupation by tenants who partition it by hour, by tide, by temperature, by height. Crabs in the drains, rats in the grain lines, bats in the roof void, swifts in the shafts, lizards in the canal, cats under the trailers, geckos around the lamps, people sealed in their cool rooms through the annihilating middle of the day — each inheriting the same streets the moment the previous tenant withdraws. They eat from the same two sources, the sea and the cargo. They drink, directly or indirectly, from the same condensate, the same tanks, the same brown groundwater pushing up through the cracks. They have all learned the same lesson, which is the only one the town teaches: do not fight the heat, do not fight the tide, wait in the shade or the dark or the mud for your hour, and then move.

So at midday the town belongs to stillness and compressors and crabs crossing empty concrete. At dusk it belongs, briefly and completely, to everyone at once — bats streaming over the channel, trucks threading vapor with their headlights, cats unfolding from the trailer shade, people filling the market and the courtyards with cooking smoke and conversation, the pumps answering the tide below the streets.

It was never empty. It was only, most hours, being lived in by its smaller, quieter, more numerous residents, while the largest animal it supports rested out of sight, waiting for the light to fail.
