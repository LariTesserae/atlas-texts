At first you would not be sure you had met one.

You might pass them the way you pass everything else up there — as part of the terrain, part of the weather, part of the work going on in the warm dimness. A shape standing too still beside a steam vent. A low ridge that breathes. A stand of chimney trees with one trunk too many, pulsing out of sequence with the ring.

Then, hours later, the same shape is somewhere else. Not ahead of you or behind you, exactly. Just elsewhere, in a valley that hadn't finished folding when you left it, and now it has a different posture. It has put down roots in the interim. Or taken them up again.

That is the first difficulty: the inhabitants do not stay in a form long enough for a form to become a description.

The second is that they do not seem to experience this as a difficulty.

---

They are born — if born is not too settled a word — in the plating-lakes and the warm condensation pools, in the hollows that open in cooling flows and close softly around whatever is placed inside them.

Some begin as small, perfect mechanisms. You find them ticking in the mud at the margin of a hot runoff channel: pump-hearts no larger than your fist, valves opening and closing with wet precision, intakes straining bright water, outputs beading with dew. They beat for days without feeding. Then filaments break the casing. Roots explore. The beat slows, deepens, learns chlorophyll. In a week it is a turbine reed among turbine reeds, spinning its seed-head in water too shallow to turn it, adding its little charge to the marsh. In a season it may forget spinning altogether and become something that walks out of the marsh on jointed, gear-folded legs when the marsh drains through a new crack and has to be followed.

Others begin the other way. A seed falls from a fern as tall as a sail, splits on black basalt, and the thing that unrolls is not a shoot but a soft, jointed grub lined with coppery tissue, already carrying a faint charge in its segments. It eats rust-moss. It extrudes wire-laced silk behind it as it climbs. By the time it has crossed one knife ridge it has too many legs and too few, its back has begun to plate over with iron-rich scale, and it is filtering fog through bellows that pulse visibly under translucent skin.

Still others never have a small beginning you could witness. They condense, adult and entire, out of a tear in the vapor: moth-winged, deer-tall, herd-colored, stepping down out of the ochre lid-light as if the plateau had simply decided, in that valley, to have eyes.

It would be wrong to call these separate species.

Species is a promise that a thing will repeat itself. Nothing up there can afford to make that promise. The ground won't keep still under the promise. Instead there are lineages of *transitions*, families recognized not by shape but by the order in which shapes happen to them — this kind always roots before it walks, that kind spends a long mineral adolescence as a ridge of listening stone before it grazes, another kind passes from tree to herd to lake in a single rainy generation and is considered, by the others, almost indecently hurried.

If there is a people here, that is what a people is: a shared itinerary through matter.

### How You Know One Is a Person

Not by the face. Faces come and go; in some phases there are several, distributed around a trunk or along a flank, opening like piston-flowers toward warmth. Not by hands, though hands happen often — root-hands splayed over rock, drinking air; glassy forelimbs chiming against the Knife Fields; soft hydraulic grippers that grow around a broken companion and hold it together until leaves can do the holding.

You know by the *pause*.

Everything on the plateau is busy becoming something else. The inhabitants are the ones who can hesitate inside the becoming. Who can hold a phase a little past its natural surrender. A reed that remains a reed through a drought because it is listening. A grazer that does not root where it falls, though its bellows are slowing and its back is already cracking into basalt columns, because the herd has not yet finished crossing. A small walking pump, absurd and gleaming, that keeps beating for three nights beside a seedling that is failing to learn how to be a pump, sharing pressure through a temporary vein, until the seedling catches the rhythm.

Intention, here, is not the opposite of the process. It is a way of lingering in it.

They linger together. That is the other sign.

### The Rings

In the high forests and again, hugely, in the central basin, they gather — when *gather* is something their current bodies permit — in rings.

Chimney trees in a ring, heat shimmering from crown to crown in slow rotation. Grazers in a ring, bellows rising and falling one after another so that the whole circle breathes as a single, distributed lung. Standing stones in a ring on a fresh scree slope that, if you watch long enough through the sound-eating moss, you realize are not standing and not, in the mineral sense, stones: their copper-green interiors brighten in sequence, a thought going around, too slow for any one of them to hold alone.

A ring has no center. It may also, on a given evening, have no members in the same kingdom. Three trees, two herds, a landslide that is trying the event in several orders at once, a pool crystallizing into gears and politely not yet becoming a plant — and the pulse goes around through root-net and fog and shallow electric water, and something is decided.

Not spoken. Sound is useless up there; echoes arrive from places you haven't been and sometimes answer in a voice very like yours, improved. So decisions travel as color moving herd-wide like weather, as scent-pulses timed to the dripping, as changes in pressure you feel in your teeth. A valley can go briefly, beautifully copper as agreement passes through it. A marsh ticks with stored lightning. Moths rise from between the trees trailing sparks, spelling nothing, carrying charge from one side of a conversation to the other.

Then the ring loosens. Trees walk apart over the following month. Stones shed their bright interiors as new legs. The pool, its counsel given, lets green filaments thread its axles and unrolls leaves from its teeth, and small animals — who may themselves, in time, be counselors — move in.

### A Life, Loosely

A migration can take generations because a single inhabitant may *be* several of the generations.

She — and *she* is as provisional as everything else, a direction of care rather than a body — begins as a charged seed in the Loom Valleys, woven into a mat-bridge while she germinates. She crosses the gorge as part of the bridge, carrying others before she has ever carried herself. Rooted in the far bank, she spends a green youth refining: roots following a copper vein down through warm rock flour, trunk separating metals layer by layer, tin beading on her shaded side, excess heat sighing from her crown into the lid of cloud. Rust-mosses eat what she sheds. Birds hatch in her hollow places.

When the vein thins and a ridge begins, inconveniently, to rise through the grove, she does not die of the upheaval. She *is* partly the upheaval's answer to itself. Her trunk splits along fluted lines. The bellows she used for moving sap rearrange, with a wet, architectural patience, into lungs. She comes down out of the trees on six low legs, back plated with her own bark gone iron-hard, and joins the migration as an engine-grazer, filtering metal dust from the fog, precipitating shining dung that the forest behind her immediately colonizes.

She carries passengers now in earnest: seedlings rooted between her plates, moths resting in the warm lee of her, a clutch of pump-heart children beating in the condensation that gathers under her belly. She crosses basins that did not exist when she was a tree. She passes her own shed metals, taken up by reeds that were once pumps she tended. In a high valley where gravity has decided, locally, that water should flow along the underside of overhangs, she learns again how to stand — more upright this time, fewer legs, root-hairs drinking from air heavy enough to wring out by hand — because the valley is narrow and upright is the shape the valley is asking for.

She may be a person who remembers being a bridge. She certainly remembers in her body: the woven tension is still in her tendons; the copper vein is still in her teeth, which are replaced continuously, bright and machined, from the mineral reserve in her jaw. When she meets a young mechanism struggling to become a reed, the memory is practical. She knows the rhythm from the inside.

At the end — and the end can be postponed, sometimes for what in a softer country would be centuries — she slows in a warm basin and lets the slowing finish. Her bellows keep working for days after the rest of her has stopped answering. Then they root. Her plated back cracks into columns. Ferns unroll from the seams. Metals condense on the ferns and drip into a new plating-lake at her foot, where gears will crystallize as the melt cools, mesh, turn, soften, leaf.

Within a month there is a small warm hill where she died.

Her descendants do not visit a grave. They shelter in her. They hatch in the hollows that open in her cooling slopes. One of them, seasons later, will stand up out of the hill with her woven-bridge gait still faintly in its joints, and no one will find this ghostly. Continuity up there is not a soul persisting through bodies. It is a pattern of becoming that several kinds of matter have agreed to keep taking turns at.

### What They Do All Day

They tend the transitions.

That is as close as it comes to work, to culture, to worship — and it is all three, without ever separating into institutions that could leave ruins.

They carry seeds upstream to plants that have not flowered yet, because time in the deep valleys is out of order and upstream is sometimes a tense, not a direction. They repair fresh lava by laying their own shed skins over cracks until moss can take over the sound and the heat. They stand in steam vents as living pistons, opening and closing, driving air through underground chambers where blind, light-speaking fish move between reed-roots like sparks between thoughts. They place eggs — their own and others' — in humming hollows and return when the hollows open, having meanwhile been, perhaps, the ridge the hollow is in.

They leave tools everywhere, if you insist on calling them tools: a shed limb jointed like an instrument, still faintly warm, that will pry or pump or conduct for whoever roots near it next. A woven span. A polished channel in basalt where generations of bellies have polished a path to a condensation pool that exists only in certain fogs. None of it stays a tool. By the time a second traveler could find it, it has been plated, rooted, colonized, taught to bend toward the light. Their artifacts are indistinguishable from their children, their houses, their food, and their dead, because down at the level where things decide what they are, those were never different categories.

They do not build houses. They *pause* as houses. An elder in a rooted century is a dwelling with opinions, who lets swallows of copper-dusted moths roost in her crown and small grazers sleep against the warm side of her trunk, and who will, eventually, stand up and take the house elsewhere, apologizing to no one, the swallows rising in a slow spark-trailing cloud and resettling as she goes.

### What They Are Afraid Of

Not death. Death up there is visibly just a change of employment.

They fear *stopping wrong*. Becoming only one thing. A gear that refuses the leaf. A tree that will not walk when the vein gives out. A stone person who holds the mineral phase past the point where the ring needs their counsel in flesh, and wakes — if waking is the word — as a summit with weather of their own and no one left who remembers the itinerary back down.

The young mountains are full of these almost-people: lonely spires with too-regular columns, herds turned to scree mid-stride, a single perfect mechanism still turning in a dried lake, meshed with nothing, waiting with terrible patience for a green filament that never threads its axle. The inhabitants pass such places quietly, color dimmed, and sometimes leave a pump-heart child beating in the dust nearby. Not as a rescue, exactly. As a reminder that a rhythm is available.

It doesn't always work. The plateau is abundant, not merciful. It makes new kinds of itself faster than the old kinds can disappear, and it does not promise that any particular itinerary will be completed.

### At the Center

The closer you go to the permanent storm, the larger and quieter they become, until the distinction between an inhabitant and an infrastructure is a courtesy you stop performing.

Things move through the steam on many legs with the unhurried confidence of aqueducts. Crowned refiners stand in rings kilometers across, passing heat from trunk to trunk around a basin, and in the passing the basin's chemistry is governed the way a mood is governed in a body. Somewhere under the turning storm there are elders who have been stone so long that migrations navigate by them, and who may, on a timescale no visitor could witness, be preparing the immense, groaning preliminary to standing.

If you stand there long enough — in the green-brown noon, in the drip and the subsonic thrum, your own boots already heavier with plate, something faint and warm beginning at the seams of your coat — you stop asking which of the things around you are the inhabitants.

You understand the grammar at last. *Inhabitant* is not a noun up there. It is a verb that matter performs, sometimes singly, sometimes in rings, sometimes at the scale of a moth and sometimes at the scale of a range of blue-black teeth already green at the base, already refining ore into silent, light-speaking life.

The plateau lives there.

It lives there as herd and forest and factory and storm. It lives there as the child beating in the mud and the hill the child shelters in and the gear still turning in the hill's warm heart, waiting, not unhopefully, to become a leaf.

And it is not empty, and it is not alone, because it has learned — in every valley, in every phase, in every brief and stubborn person who pauses inside the great becoming to help another thing across — how to be many, without ever having to decide to stop being one.
