No one lives in the rooms.

That is the first, stubborn fact, and it takes a long time to stop correcting for it. You keep expecting the inhabitant to be withheld — shy, nocturnal, gone hunting, dissolved into the green when you look directly. A door that size ought to imply a shoulder that has passed through it. A stair ought to imply a foot that has worn it, even if the stair is root-matting and silica stalks and was finished yesterday. A cistern court ought to imply someone who will be thirsty later.

Nothing comes. Nothing has been. The footprints fill and close. The thresholds stay unworn because they are still growing across themselves. Inside the roofed enclosures the air is a little warmer, a little more scented, the floor gives off a faint fermented sweetness, and that difference — humidity held, shade made, rain guttered noisily away instead of falling straight — is the whole purpose of the room. It was not built to be entered. It is a way for the outside to be briefly, usefully *inside* something.

So if you ask who lives there, you have to turn the question sideways.

The walls do.

Not in the sense that something is hiding in them. The wall itself is the resident: a braid of species so tightly cooperating that you cannot say where tenant ends and tenancy begins. Stiff, hollow stalks rise first and take the wind-load. Lianas find them within hours and climb, tightening audibly, turning a bundle of poles into a column. Broad leaves sprout along the upper edge to steal light from whatever is rising next door, and their shade, in turn, trains the next generation of stalks into perfect, desperate verticality. Rootlets work down into the older construction below — last season’s fallen tower, last week’s drained basin floor — and soften it, mine it, pull the stored minerals back up into green tissue. The wall extends itself two meters in an afternoon, exuding at its pale, unfinished end, and in extending, it changes the watercourse at its base, which changes what can grow across the plaza, which changes where the next wall will be needed.

That is a life, if you watch it at the right speed. It just doesn’t move by walking. It moves by becoming more, and then by deciding — without deciding — to become less in exactly the place where less is useful.

The towers know this least and live it hardest. A tower is not a species. It is a temporary alliance with a death built into it. The core plants race upward because light up there is unobstructed and therefore worth any structural lie. The climbers race after them because a core is a free road into the sky. The crown leaves spread, drink sun, drip condensed vapor down the gutters, fatten tubers, drop fragments that root wherever they roll. And underneath, unnoticed, shade has been killing the conditions that made the base possible. Support plants yellow in sequence. The braid loosens. One warm evening the whole twenty meters sighs, lists with immense slowness, and lays itself down into a basin in a creaking avalanche of wet timber — and is not mourned, because it is not over. By nightfall the wreck is steaming. By morning it is colonized. Within days the corpse has stopped being a corpse and started being a foundation, slightly higher than the ground around it, slightly richer, already sprouting the pale first flags of whatever will dare the light next.

You could call the groundcover the modest citizen, if the tower is the ambitious one, but modesty here is also a strategy pursued at full speed. The plazas look empty and are not. That flat, springy, pale-green interlace is a working surface: solar collector, evaporation pan, floodway, nursery. It keeps itself cropped by its own growth habit, holds a film of water just below its surface, and waits — not passively, but in a state of continuous small adjustment, root hairs testing the flow — for the moment a basin upstream blooms and dies back and sends a warm flood sheeting across it. Then the plaza reveals what it was for. Spores germinate in the runoff. Fragments catch. In a day the open space is chest-high. By the second day the chest-high meadow is no longer a meadow; it is thickening here, thinning there, hollowing where water wants a channel, rising where light is being contested. It is differentiating into architecture again.

Everywhere, the inhabitants are doing this double work of building the place and being dismantled by it.

The rim-plants around a basin live for a single coordinated gesture. For weeks they hold, leaf overlapping leaf, wax-sealed, perfect, while the water rises behind them and the basin fills and the submerged stems ferment gently in the warm dark. Then they bloom — white, brief, indecently fragrant, in a place with no noses and no bees — and the flowering is apparently the signal, or the cost, or the timer. A strip of them dies back together, cleanly, as if a door had been cut. The basin empties in a rush. Everything downstream receives the news as water.

That is their communication. Shade is a sentence. A leak is a sentence. Hydraulic pressure squealing in a hollow stem, a root-mat going suddenly soft, a support species withdrawing in order — these are how the city talks to itself without a single animal nervous system anywhere in it. There is no mind presiding. There is something perhaps more unsettling: coordination without a coordinator, arising again and again because species that mismanaged sun and water here were eaten by their own buildings long ago, and only the interlocking habits remain.

Reproduction, for them, is weather. Nobody courts. Pollen rides overflow from a rim that blooms at the exact moment it fails. Runners cross a plaza overnight and start a new colonnade at a slight, experimental angle. Tubers drop from a tower crown, bounce down the terraces, and root in the wreckage. A fragment torn loose by a rerouted channel becomes, three basins away, the first green thread of a dam. Young is not a phase they pass through on the way to permanence. Young is the permanent condition. Everything you see has the translucent, yellow-green look of a thing unfurled this morning, because it was.

And old age is equally quick, equally public, equally useful. Nothing here gets to become a ruin. A dead aqueduct is immediately a raised bed. A collapsed court is compost heat and scaffolding. Rot is not the opposite of growth; it is growth’s supply line, the warm lower note you hear bubbling inside the cistern chambers, the city digesting its previous versions and feeding them, still warm, to its next one. If it has a memory, that is where it is stored: not in any living head, but down through the layers under your hand — root-fiber, stem-brick, leaf-masonry — a buried archive that is perpetually being read by being eaten.

So the inhabitant, finally, is not the tower or the plaza or the rim in flower. It is the turnover itself, wearing all of those shapes in succession. A single, valley-wide, many-species metabolism that seizes light because light is life, hoards water because water is transport, builds walls because walls manage both, builds rooms by accident because rooms are what walls sometimes agree to make, and then pulls its own masterpieces down before they can harden into history. It is loud. It drums its conduits, roars down its terraces, cracks its over-sapped beams, slaps its big leaves in the hot wind, and goes on at evening unfolding, extending, tightening — a citizenry you can hear growing from one end of the plateau to the other.

It does not miss the animals it never had. It is not waiting, despite the doors, despite the windows framing views for no eyes, despite the stairs arriving at platforms and stopping, as if someone might one day wish to stand there and look out over the cloud-sea below the rim.

It lives there the way fire lives in a hearth it is also eating. Completely, hurriedly, and without ever once being separate from the place.
