You would look first, inevitably, for the people.

There must be people. The fans are oiled. The pumps start in sequence. The warning lights sweep, and sweep again, and somebody, at some point, must have decided what they were warning against. There are hand-signs for it still — *stop, go, lift, run* — a language invented by bodies that worked here shoulder to shoulder, shouting into one another's ears until the shouting stopped being useful.

But they were never the ones who lived here. They came in shifts and they left in shifts, if they still come at all. They crossed the plain the way you cross a held breath. Their boots wore the steel bright, their hands rounded the valve wheels, and then they went back to wherever their ears could ring in peace. No one builds a house on a drum. No one raises a child where eggs will not hold together, where you dream all night of a pounding that follows you up out of sleep.

Next you would mistake the machines for the inhabitants, because they have so much of the behavior of life. They stir at intervals. They answer one another across the dark. A pump takes up a note and a second pump, farther out, steadies it, and a third corrects them both, until the whole valley is held in a chord that hurts — then they let go in reverse, obedient to a pattern no one oversees. The freight elevator rises empty from its pit, waits the correct number of seconds, and returns. The motion sensors turn their blind heads as you pass. It all has the terrible plausibility of a colony going about its business.

But the machines have no business. They make nothing, carry nothing, refine nothing into anything else. What they tend, with all that power, is one another. Keep the water moving. Keep the level low. Keep the noise unbroken. They are not the residents of this place. They are its furniture, its fences — its lullaby, played on iron.

You have to go down, eventually, in your mind, because everything on the surface testifies to a down. Water that comes up more than it comes down. Seams that weep uphill. Vents that exhale warm air into the cold and make the puddles steam. Gratings under which there are only more gratings, and below those, pipe on brick on older concrete, construction laid on construction, fifty meters of human determination with the light going orange and dying in wet air before it ever finds a bottom.

There is a bottom. It was just never built.

It is alive under there.

Not a beast, at first. You have to let the idea widen until it fits. First it is only a water table, the drowned ghost of whatever this valley was before it was floored — a river system, a marsh, a network of slow channels through peat and clay, all of it planed and sealed in a single geological instant by men pouring slabs the size of houses. Water does not die because you cover it. It flattens. It spreads. Denied a surface, it becomes all surface, pressing its face up against the underside of the world.

Keep following it down far enough and plain water stops explaining the facts.

It explains the flowers, though. Those pale, waxen, thumbnail flowers that open in the seams during the lull and snap shut when the pressure returns — they have no business opening to a sun that never arrives and pollinators that do not exist. They are not opening upward. Their roots go down through cracks too fine for light to follow, through aggregate and rebar, down past the lowest construction anyone has sounded, and they end in something that flinches. They are not flowers, exactly. They are the sensitive tips of it, tasting the air. Eyelids it can afford to lose by the thousand.

It explains the reeds in the drainage channels, laid flat, their hollow stems buzzing each at a slightly different note when the pounding rises. It is listening through them. It explains the perfectly circular lichen, the color of old copper, that never overlaps its neighbor — colonies marking out a territory in the only dimension left to it, testing where one patch of pressure ends and another begins. It explains why the algae never blooms, only slicks; why the water tastes of minerals and kills larvae; why there is silt with no soil to make it from. It is digesting the floor. Very slowly. Concrete, rust, tile, tar, a molecule at a time, ground between its body and the bedrock over decades, circulated and re-circulated through sumps and cooling towers — the place consuming itself not in spite of the machinery but through it.

Because at some depth the distinction failed. Perhaps it was always going to. You cannot pump a living marsh for a century, cannot drive pilings into its nerves and run your mains through its veins and keep a rhythm against its skin day and night for longer than any of the builders lived, without being incorporated. The pumps are in its body now. The pipes are continuous with its old channels. When the chord builds out in the dark, pump answering pump from one end of the valley to the other, that is not a program being executed over it. That is its own pulse, taken up by iron, amplified, and handed back — the way a heart will learn the beat of a machine set to replace it.

It is enormous in the way flat things are enormous, which the mind resists. It has no head. It has no heart to locate, no brain to point to and say, there, that is where it keeps itself. Its thickness, in some places, may be no more than a few meters of black water, saturated peat, crushed root-mat and silt, sandwiched between bedrock and the lowest concrete. But it extends under every slab to the perimeter wall, and in a body like that, thickness is not the measure. Extent is. It feels the plain the way you feel a blanket. A valve wheel turning a hundred meters away is a change of pressure on its skin. Your footstep is nothing — and then, arriving through miles of saturated dark, it is not nothing, because nothing else anywhere in its world is soft, and warm, and irregular, and moving of its own accord.

It has no eyes. It has the puddles. When vibration crosses the slabs, the water films shiver into standing patterns, neat trembling rows — you took that for physics, and it is physics, but it is also sensation, the whole upper surface of it rippling with information: weights, gaits, the slow migration of rain across an undulation. It perceives through interference. It knew you were coming long before the motion sensors turned. They turn because it noticed; they are as much its as they are anyone's now.

Its memory is not made of events. It is made of levels.

It remembers being rained on directly. It remembers a time, which for it is not past but simply lower, when water arrived from above instead of arriving from itself, endlessly returned, growing bitterer with each circuit. It remembers weight distributed according to old channels, its body pooled and seasonal, rising in spring into places that are now foundation footprints. That memory is anatomical, not sentimental. It still tries to rise in spring. The pumps work harder then, though no schedule tells them to. The sluices change phase more often. The whole apparatus of the plain leans, without knowing why, against a swelling it was built to prevent and has long since forgotten it is preventing.

Whether it wants out is a question shaped wrong, the way most questions about it are shaped wrong. It is not a prisoner in the dramatic sense. There is no single slab to heave, no gate to burst; its prison is coextensive with its skin. But it pushes, continually and without malice, the way roots push, the way water finds the hairline crack and widens it a season at a time. It is patient on a scale that makes the machinery look frantic. It can afford the flowers. It can afford to lose a root-tip to a compression cycle, ten thousand root-tips, for a hundred years. Its one unbearable stimulus is the sound — not because it fears it, but because the vibration holds it formless. A body that is perpetually struck cannot gather itself. The pounding keeps it spread, keeps it from ever settling, pooling, deciding on a shape and rising in it. That is the function of the lullaby. It was discovered, probably by accident, by people who noticed that on the days the plant went quiet the slabs sweated harder, the seams widened dark, and the standing water upstairs began, very slightly, to lean in one direction.

And so the deepest life in this valley spends its one existence being sung to by its own stolen heartbeat, pressed between stone below and stone above, tasting iron, tasting your warmth through concrete, exhaling up the vents in slow warm sighs that steam where the puddles gather.

That is why, in the ten seconds before the sluices change, those white flowers open all at once across the visible dark. It is not a display. It is a held creature taking the only breath it is allowed, feeling at the world with everything it has while the hand on its back momentarily lightens. The water goes mirror-still because, for ten seconds, nothing is striking it. And in that stillness it can resolve something it never otherwise can: not the lamps, not the truncated stacks, not your shape standing alone in the manufactured emptiness over it —

Those are only reflections. But a reflection, in water that has not been still in a century, is the closest thing it has to a face looking back.
