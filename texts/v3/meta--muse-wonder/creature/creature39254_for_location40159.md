The inhabitants are the groves themselves.

Not things that live in them. There is nothing in the canopy to be shaded, no one sheltering in the mats, no burrow underneath. Take away the trees and there is no one left missing them. Each grove — fifty meters across, alone on the gravel with a mile of warm basalt in every direction — is one creature, or as close to one creature as life there knows how to be.

From a distance it looks like several kinds of plant crowded together by accident. Up close the species stop reading as species. They are organs.

The low mats are its skin and stomach lining turned outward: overlapping scales, thick as tongues, drinking light all day without lifting. The ribbed mid-trunks are its gut and circulatory system, pumping warm silicone oil so slowly that a press of the hand can feel it give, sluggishly, like something asleep inside a pipe. The wire-drawers and foil-makers are its hands, though they never grasp. The tall pylons with hollow crowns are its deepest digestion, cracking rock inside sealed oily chambers and sorting it, in silence, into what the body can use and what it must put down outside itself: alumina here, iron beads there, copper curling bright from under a leaf, wire coiling in warm hanks at a trunk's base, mica falling in transparent sheets and stacking itself by falling true.

The piles are not treasure to it. They are not products, in its experience. They are what is left over when rock has passed through a living body and been made orderly. Foil is a kind of shed leaf. An ingot is a kind of dropping, a kind of bone-chip, a stone the gut could not dissolve. It manufactures the way a tree elsewhere makes oxygen: faithfully, chemically, without audience, without any inner picture of a user.

Its body does not end where the green ends. Under the trunks, roots enter the fractures in the basalt and come back up as cable — black silica sheaths, white ceramic collars grown at exact intervals, conductors running tree to tree in lines too straight for hunger. They are following the crystal grain of the bedrock, the faint field physics of the new shelf. So the whole grove is aligned from above like a diagram of itself. Its nervous system, if it has one, is also its skeleton, its map, and its power grid. Signals would not sound like anything. They would go as changes in potential along the buried cables, a brightening and dimming of charge from mat to pylon to flower, felt everywhere in the body at once and nowhere in particular.

It has no mouths. Nothing living there does. It does not drink, because there is nothing to drink, and it has arranged not to need to. Its broad amber and bottle-green leaves bead with dry deliquescent salt, and the salt pulls trace vapor molecule by molecule out of the thin air — not rain, not dew, never a drop it could feel as wet. It sips dryness. What circulates in it is not sap but oil, warm and transparent, immune to evaporation. What pollinates its blunt ceramic flowers is not an animal but a discharge: a faint ozone crackle at the center of the trunk, the body briefly completing a circuit with itself.

It is, in the strictest sense, self-sufficient. Not proudly. Not even knowingly. Its circle is the limit of the field it can maintain, and inside that field everything it needs arrives — light from above, unvarying heat from below, fresh mineral delivered by the still-warm upwelling under the plateau, a few stolen molecules of vapor from the air. Outside that field its own seeds dry to glass beads and lie unchanged on the gravel. It does not spread because spreading is not, anymore, something its body can imagine. A seed is an old instruction it still writes out of habit, addressed to a world that is not there.

Time inside a grove is not like time outside one. The light stays daylight. The temperature holds at ankle height and head height, morning and evening. There is no wind to bend it, no season to prepare for, no predator to outgrow, no mouth depending on its fruit. Its canopy plates tilt a fraction toward the sun and hold. Its oil moves. A sheet of copper works halfway out of a leaf over what would be a human year, bright and untarnished, and does not feel delayed. Growth is real, but it is exactly answered at the center of the grove, where the oldest trunks are slowly petrifying — leaf for leaf, trunk for trunk — turning to shining plant-shaped stone while still holding their wire in place. The dead do not fall and rot. There are no decomposers to take them. The ancestors become part of the works, conducting after they have stopped growing, a memory kept not as story but as structure.

So a grove does not age toward death. It maintains a shape by continually replacing it with itself, a green rim living around a stone heart. It is not young. It only looks young, the way the whole plateau looks unfinished, because nothing has arrived to weather it into the next stage. That is a human misreading. The grove is not waiting to become a forest. It is not an early attempt at a world with animals in it. It is complete at fifty meters, complete at three or four groves across a region, complete in producing perfect useful things that no one collects.

If it perceives, it perceives electrically and geologically. It would know the exhaustion of a mineral vein the way you know hunger in the belly — as a change in the quality of what rises through it. It would know the bedrock's slow delivery as satiety. It would know light as pressure on the mats, heat as the constant condition of thought, the other groves — if at all — as the faintest imaginable pattern repeated at the edge of sense, too far for cable, too silent for air, the same alignment happening elsewhere for the same reasons. Whether the repetition comforts it is unknowable. Solitude requires expecting company, and there has never been company.

A human walking in would register, perhaps, as weather: a brief dampness exhaled into air it is trying to sip from, a moving shadow on the light-scales, a warm animal voltage crossing between cables, footsteps that crunch and stop a few feet away. No face would turn. There are no faces. The leaves would not rustle in alarm because there is no air moving and no alarm-system that was ever rewarded. The body would go on completing its circuits. Somewhere in the trunk a flower might discharge with a smell of ozone and hot resin. Somewhere above, a finished sheet might settle onto the copper litter with a tick felt in the teeth. The visitor would be an event too fast, too wet, and too brief to learn from.

That is what lives there: a sessile, sleepless metallurgist made of leaves and cable; a factory that is also a body; a body that eats stone, breathes with salt, thinks perhaps in charge and gradient, births seeds that turn to glass, buries its ancestors inside its own machinery, and is not lonely, not industrious, not patient — because patience, industry, and loneliness are all names for wanting a future different from the present.

It holds. In amber shade, smelling of heated oil and salt, ringed by its own bright refuse, under an ordinary blue sky it cannot see, it holds — making, shedding, petrifying, replacing — in perfect balance with a warm rock that has never learned to cool, for no one, forever being exactly what it already is.
