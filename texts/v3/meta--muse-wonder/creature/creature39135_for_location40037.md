The first way you know a Sessran, down below, when one of them rides the funicular down for supplies or a wedding or to bury somebody in real earth, is that they keep reaching for a railing that isn't there and counting under their breath.

One, two, three — not nervously. Compulsively, the way other people check a pocket for keys. They count stairs, they count tram stops, they count the seconds between the forge-hammer blows back home even when they are a thousand meters too low to feel it, twenty-two, twenty-two, and their teeth ache with the absence. They wear the little brass bell even in lowland cities where there is no fog worth mentioning. Tollmen and innkeepers learn not to ask them to take it off. A Sessran without a bell feels overheard by himself.

Up there, of course, everyone has one. Children get theirs before they get shoes that fit. The bells are not standardized — that is the whole point. No two ring quite alike. A mother knows her daughter's bell three markets away, under the corrugated roof, in the rain off the pipes. A foreman knows who has come late to the foundry floor without looking up. Lovers learn each other's codes so well they can hold a whole shy conversation across a crowded tram platform, one soft chime answering another, while their mouths do something altogether more respectable.

And when someone dies, the bell is not buried. It is hung for a year in the doorway of the house, where the condensation takes it, where every passing draught from a door that should exist gives it a chance to prove that it is only the wind.

They are, to look at, almost unremarkable, which itself becomes remarkable the longer you stay. Pale, not from sickness but from a lifetime under that breathing ceiling — skin that has never had to decide what to do with hard sunlight. Their eyes are dark-adapted in a way lowlanders find unsettling. They blink slowly. They can read a tram timetable in what you would call pitch blackness, and at noon, in Sessra's submarine twilight, they squint anyway, out of an inherited caution no one remembers teaching them. When they do go below, they wear smoked glass and come home complaining for weeks that the world down there is obscene with edges.

Their bodies have made quieter bargains. Barrel chests, many of them, especially in the third and fourth generations — long ribs, a slow, deliberate way of breathing through the nose that makes them seem calmer than they are. They are magnificent at holding their breath. A Sessran child of seven can cross the Thin Streets — those three bad blocks behind the Upper Cistern where the good air simply fails to collect — at a dead run without inhaling once, sparrows scattering from the cobbles ahead of her like a green light, ears popping, the echo of her own feet arriving just ahead of her stride and spooking her pleasurably all the way home. Old people do it differently. They stop at the brass posts the city has set at every corner where the air changes, lean, let their bell settle, and wait for a yak-cart or a helpful grandchild or a shift in the condensation to bring a breathable pocket drifting down the street like a slow tide. No one is embarrassed. It is weather. You would wait out rain.

They move at the pace the city sets, and the city is very slow. Not lazy — Sessra is an industrial place, it smelts and forges and pumps and builds incessantly — but everything is conducted at the speed of materials that refuse to be hurried. A furnace takes a month to fire properly. A conversation takes longer than it should because it is polite to pause, now and then, and let your echo finish arriving, especially indoors, especially in the newer districts. If you talk over your own echo and it talks over you, you can end up having agreed to something neither of you said. The council ruling, quoted by every landlady in the city, is that an instruction from your own voice is binding only if it also knows your bell-code. Sessrans find this perfectly reasonable. They find lowland impatience — answering a question in the same minute it was asked — faintly ill-bred.

District by district, they are almost separate peoples. The funicular brings up newsprint and brides and machine parts, but the forty-minute tram rides across smoking, empty rock between the urban islands do something to a population. A Lower Foundry family speaks with the hammer in their sentences, those unconscious pauses every twenty-two seconds, waiting for the blow to pass up through the floorboards before they deliver the important word. They are deaf in one particular frequency and proud of it, they raise children who can sleep through an industrial apocalypse and wake instantly at the sound of a stair-count going wrong in the next room.

The greenhouse terraces breed a different sort entirely: broad, damp, green-smelling people who live among fig trees and six-foot night flowers and cat-sized bats, who talk to vines as colleagues, who can tell by the curl of a leaf whether the air in a pit is about to go bad an hour before the sparrows know. They are Sessra's aristocracy and its servants at once, because everyone eats and no one forgives the price of imported soil. A greenhouse keeper's hands are never clean in the lowland sense. The black compost gets under the nails and stays for years, and this is treated, at dances, as a kind of jewelry.

Then there are the shepherds, whom the city needs and pretends not to. They live out in the between-places, in the solitary huts you pass from the tram, moving with the dwarf yak herds from warm grate to moss bed to steam vent across whole seasons. Shaggy as their animals, smelling of wet wool and iron, they are the only people who regularly cross the plateau on foot and come back with their counting intact. They claim the tall, shy herds at the northern rim are not dangerous, merely unfinished, and that the correct thing to do when you find one several streets closer after you blink your lamp is to ring your bell once, clearly, so it can learn the difference between you and your echo. The surveyors — a younger, more nervous profession, all smoked lenses and chalk sticks and official notebooks that the Office of Emergence reads and then, pointedly, files upside-down — do not claim this. The surveyors are the ones who find the fifteenth stair.

Everyone respects the Finishers most, and no one wants their child to become one.

The Finishers are carpenters, masons, glaziers, plumbers, but those are lowland words for what they do. Near the spire, where the mountain gets ahead of itself and extrudes a cobbled lane overnight or buds a window with the glass already sweating in it, the Finishers go in with their toolbelts and their extraordinary patience and *coax*. They persuade a staircase to admit it only has the one floor to serve. They talk a load-bearing wall out of branching. They find the house that has begun forming around a hot fissure — arch, lintel, a doorway breathing warm mineral air — and they sit in it for days, sometimes weeks, ringing their bells at intervals, counting its rooms aloud, over and over, until the place agrees on a number. A good Finisher can discourage a second kitchen before it finishes dreaming itself. A great one, the kind they still tell stories about in the Long Market, once talked a tenement out of a tenant — came down at dawn, grey as ash, and would only say that the extra room had accepted, politely, that nobody lived there yet.

That is why children are kept from the spire. Not fear of falling. Fear of being *finished*. Every Sessran grows up on a story about someone's cousin who played too long in the fresh black columns and came home a half-inch taller down his left side, one fingernail turned to dark glass, hearing things with his left ear twenty seconds before they happened and answering questions nobody had quite asked. The stories never say he was unhappy. That is the worst part of them. His mother, they say, had to teach him to wait — to hold the answer behind his teeth and count, one, two, three, up to twenty, while the rest of the world caught up with what he had already heard. Sessrans who live closest to the spire all have a touch of this. They laugh a fraction before the joke lands. They flinch at the forge-hammer a breath before it falls. It makes them superb tram drivers and impossible card players.

Old age, up there, is not quite sequential. Time pools like the air does, and in a city where the Long Market clocks run slow on wet days, nobody is entirely surprised that a woman can be seventy in the Foundry district and, by some private arithmetic nobody challenges, sixty-four on her visits to a sister two tram rides away. They keep birthdays by weight, not by date, in some families — a sack of flour, a stone, an iron bolt added each year to a string in the kitchen, so that a life accumulates mass even when the calendar gets slippery. The very old are consulted the way you'd consult a map of a coastline that changes: carefully, gratefully, with no expectation of consistency between visits. Grief stays sharp in Sessra, because nothing has been worn smooth yet, and so the old are not really expected to get over anything. They are expected to carry it, polished and terrible, and to ring true when struck.

And woven all through the human city, treated with the flat matter-of-factness Sessrans reserve for everything impossible, are the other residents.

The dwarf yaks have right of way everywhere, by law and by the simpler fact that a yak asleep in its perfect steaming circle around an exhaust grate is not moving for a tram. The rock goats who ride the freight platforms pay, after a fashion — the platform operators take a tithe of whatever the goats have stolen, mostly lunches, and the six-toed foxes who have learned the shift-whistle attend the same negotiations. The birds are a public utility and a public menace, pouring between the stacks at dusk in their clamorous mixed flocks, parrots and choughs and starlings and pigeons all shouting over one another in a dozen stolen languages, building their overnight nests of wire and blue glass in the turbines. Turbine crews go to work with brooms and a genuine, practiced tenderness. You do not swear at something that can learn.

In the cisterns, the pale frogs sing in perfect imitation of tram bells, and Sessrans distinguish them the way they distinguish everything: the frogs are always exactly on time, and nothing human in Sessra has ever managed that. In the flooded undercrofts of the newest district there are eels and, occasionally, a light. The great moths, the size of dinner plates, beat their slow wings in the air too thin for anyone else and are treated as a kind of advance guard — where the moths are gathered thickest, the surveyors say, the mountain is thinking about making somewhere new. People leave those places alone. People leave lamps burning there. It amounts to the same courtesy.

There are, finally, the residents nobody lists. The voices in sealed rooms that know almost the bell-code. The herds that stand still under a lamp and closer without it. The tenants discouraged, politely, before they finished arriving. Sessra is a city built on a world that is still being made, generously and clumsily, with windows in it, and its people have developed, over sixty years of hammer-blows and Leveling Festivals and silenced bells, a single unifying civic virtue that looks, from below, like superstition and is, from up there, simply manners:

You announce yourself. You count what is supposed to be there. You ring, so that what follows you home has to work a little harder to pretend it is you.

And then, in the blue-grey dusk that never entirely lifts, under a ceiling that breathes and drips and remembers the daylight badly, you go about your slow business — turning the great screws a quarter-turn a decade, coaxing the new houses down to a reasonable number of doors, holding your breath across the thin streets, listening with one ear twenty seconds early and the other right on time — because someone has to be there when the mountain makes the next room. Someone has to open the window and find out what, this time, is on the other side of it.
