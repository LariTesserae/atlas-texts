You wouldn't pick them out, at first, on one of the long trams between districts. 

From a distance they look like people anywhere that work has made practical: dark, damp-proof clothes cut loose for the heat and buttoned tight against the spray, boots softened purple-brown like the ironwork, hair kept short or braided flat so it doesn't whip and sting in the constant wind off the falls. Women and men both wear a wide leather strap across the satchel, across the tool roll, across the baby sling — nothing carried is allowed to swing free and batter itself against a hip all day. Nothing loose. That is the first thing the city teaches a body.

Up close the differences accumulate.

Their shoulders are set a little forward, not from stooping, but from a lifetime of leaning in. To talk to someone near a millrace you don't raise your voice, you close the distance until your sternum is almost to theirs and you let the vibration do part of the work. They stand closer than lowlanders are comfortable with. They touch more, and more deliberately: a hand flat to a forearm to get attention, two fingers to a sternum to feel if a word was yes or no, a forehead briefly pressed to a child's forehead in a courtyard — not quite a kiss, a way of checking for fever and of being heard in the one register that never gets drowned out.

Their hands are what you notice if you stay.

The sign language here is not a fluttering of the fingers. It uses the whole arm from elbow to fingertip, big, sculptural gestures that can be read across a turbine hall or across one of the empty plazas when the tram bells are only flashes of light. As a result the forearms of lifelong residents are ropy and overdeveloped, the tendons standing out when they argue, when they joke, when they bargain at market without ever opening their mouths. Children's arms tire before their voices do. They wear the little chalk slates around their necks until they are eight or nine and strong enough to speak properly, which in this city means speaking without speaking.

At home, at the courtyard table, even hearing families sign. Partly it saves effort. Partly, over generations, it has become the language in which they are funny, or tender, or rude. Spoken language has shrunk to short, efficient bursts for use in the lee of walls — names, numbers, warnings, endearments of one syllable that can be felt as much as heard. Complex things migrate to the hands. A girl describing her day will be silent and enormous with gesture while her grandmother peels something into a bowl and answers with one floury hand, never looking up from the vibration of the knife on the board.

And they *are* deaf, all of them, but in a pattern so regular the water board could chart it by district.

Live twenty years by the Third Drop and the world loses a clean band of middle frequencies, exactly where the turbines sing. Live by the First and you lose more, lower, until human voices come through as texture without edges and only the very high sounds — a spoon on a cup in a courtyard, a dog's nails on stone, insects on the one quiet night of the year — arrive with a startling, almost painful clarity. Old people are not bothered by this. They call it peace, and they mean it physiologically. Their inner ears have thickened to the place. Newcomers, whose bodies haven't learned to filter, get the headaches and nosebleeds and sudden weeping fits in crowds, because for them the roar is still an assault instead of a medium.

Babies born in the bowl are soothed by it in a way that alarms visiting relatives. Put an infant in a bolted crib on the lee side of a house and she will sleep through shift changes, through freight cars shuddering the rails, through her father re-seating a sluice gate with a mallet two rooms away. Take her down the switchback road to visit cousins in the quiet country and she will lie awake all night, rigid, listening for the hand that is supposed to be pressed to her chest, until someone runs a bath or sets a kettle to rolling boil beside her and she can finally drop off to a lesser, insufficient rushing.

Adults are not so different. They travel badly below the plateau. Silence down there gives them insomnia, a feeling of falling in their sleep. Hotel rooms feel airless without trembling windows. Some traveling millwrights carry a small, clockwork water box — a reservoir and a fall of three inches, endlessly recirculated — not loud, but enough to tell the teeth that the world is still running.

For the work they do requires listening with the teeth, with the sternum, with the soles of the feet through bolted floors.

The most respected people in the city are not engineers, exactly, but tuners: older men and women who can walk a turbine hall with their palms out, not quite touching the riveted steel, and tell you three weeks before any gauge will that a bearing is beginning to sing wrong. They diagnose a cracked channel lining by putting a copper coin — the same coins used to shim doorframes — between their back teeth and pressing their jaw to the wet basalt. They feel hairline failures in their fillings. Their own enamel is often worn in strange facets, their jaws heavy, their necks thick from a lifetime of braced attention. When they retire, their hearing, such as it is, goes very fast, as if the body stops bothering.

That slowness you feel in the afternoons is in them, too. It is not laziness. Living inside a sound that never stops costs something neurological that has to be paid back in stillness. Between two and five they sit in the courtyards with their hands in their laps — those eloquent, tireless hands finally at rest — and let the vibration pass through. They move at the pace of freight on the towpaths, at the pace of the mules in their padded ear hoods plodding beside the canals. They do not hurry repairs, because nothing here can be finished; it can only be kept at the rate of its erosion. A man will spend a month re-cutting a single basalt block to fit a curve and speak of the mason who cut the first one three hundred years ago as if he had just missed him on the previous shift.

Their skin takes the city's seasoning. It stays a little damp even indoors, soft at the knuckles, darkened at the forearms where spray and sun alternate all day. Cuts heal slow in the wet and are bound with algae poultices from the sluice ferns. Lungs are strong and a little scarred; everyone coughs in the morning until the mist burns off, children and foundry workers alike, and thinks nothing of it. They smell, pleasantly to each other, of hot stone and machine oil and the green, warmed smell of their own courtyards: fig leaf, olive wood, wet laundry, reed mats drying on dark stone.

Age bows them along the lines of force. Doorframes walk two centimeters out of true in fifty years; so do spines. Old women by the First Drop lean almost imperceptibly toward their falls, as if still listening for something under it. Old men rest their weight through their heels, feeling the ground the way other people squint. When they die — and they live, despite everything, unremarkably long lives, as if the constant shaking discouraged more acute catastrophes — they are often found to have inner ear bones dense as river pebbles.

Socially they are organized by sound the way other cities are by parish. You are a Second Drop family, a Reservoir family; your sign dialect has a larger sweep because your hall is wider, or a chopped, vertical style learned in a narrow millrace. Marrying across districts is romantic and exhausting, because your deafnesses don't match — your husband literally cannot hear the pitch your mother calls your name in, your wife keeps turning toward a rushing you have learned to delete. Children of such marriages are prized as interpreters. Neighborhood rivalries are not about territory, precisely, but about whose fall is truest, whose turbine keeps the steadiest note, whose quarter endures the annual silence with the least panic.

Because they fear silence the way coastal people fear a receding tide.

On the night they stop the First Drop, it is the inhabitants, not the city, that are most revealed. They come out of the courtyard houses — houses that have turned their backs to the water for generations — and stand in the actual streets, unprotected, blinking. They speak and then immediately lower their voices, ashamed of how far a normal tone carries. Teenagers who sign obscenities the size of semaphore flags all year stand whispering with their hands clasped tight, to keep them from betraying nervousness. Dogs, who learned signs before they learned barking, bark and startle themselves. Old people weep at the sound of insects in the scrub, at a baby two streets away, at all the small, separate voices of the world that the roar usually, mercifully, holds together into one.

And when the gates open again in the small hours and the white fall rebuilds itself step by step, they do not cheer. They exhale. Shoulders drop. Hands unclench and rise again into easy, tired conversation. They go back inside their thick walls, to their courtyards with one fig, one olive shaped by shelter, to beds that have begun their almost invisible trembling again.

Not oppressed by the sound, not romantic about it. Inhabiting it, as they inhabit the altitude and the heat, with bodies slowly remodeled to bear a pressure that never lifts — a hand pressed forever, gently, against the sternum, which over enough generations has learned to press back.
