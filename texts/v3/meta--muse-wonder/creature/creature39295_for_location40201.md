At first you will try to give it a body in the wrong place.

You will try the houses. That is natural. They are the right shape for bodies. Some are better preserved than others — a courtyard still almost enclosed, a condensation stone still standing polished in its basin, the moss on the floor thicker and springier than anywhere else, as if it has been tended, or slept on. You lie down there the first night and it is faintly warm underneath, saturated and alive, and you think: something returns here.

But nothing returns. Or, it never leaves to return. In the morning that same courtyard is furred white and empty and the basin is full, untouched, filmed over with a skin of ice so perfect it has not even taken a beak-print. The moss is depressed in no place except where you depressed it. Your own shape stays in it for an hour after you rise, slowly lifting.

So you try the whistler.

That is the next most sensible thing. Something compact, furred, loaf-sized, in the moss on the high side. You hear it whenever your shadow crosses certain streets — a short, furious, two-note whistle, alarm and indignation together. It retreats as you advance. It is always just behind a wall. It arrives, because of the walls, from everywhere at once, but the *impulse* is local. You can feel a presence pulling back.

You will spend a whole green midday hunting it, if you are like anyone. Moving slow. Stopping. Listening past the enormous choir of the town for the one small voice that is not stone.

You will find the runs first. In the thickest moss, on the north sides where the ferns grow in ranks, there are tunnels the width of your forearm, roofed over with interlaced fronds, warm with rot and breath. They dive from courtyard to courtyard through holes left deliberately low in the walls — too low for a person to crawl comfortably, too regular to be damage. They connect the cisterns. They connect the sunny pockets where the white flowers open at noon to the deep shade under the lintels. Droppings like black rice. A cache of lichen scraped clean from a chimney and piled, absurdly neat, in a dry alcove. Gnawed fern stems.

And still no animal. Only the whistle, now behind you.

By the second day you begin to understand that sound is not a clue to it here. Sound is its camouflage and its weather. Everything in the bowl throws its voice. The frogs do it — the small black cistern frogs who call once at dusk and are answered six, seven times until the whole town is croaking and the real frog has long since shut its throat. The wind does it. The thaw does it. A stone contracting at midnight reports like a door closing three streets away. So a thing that wanted not to be found, in this place, would simply need to make a noise. The noise would go on living without it.

You are warmer by then, and your ears are ringing, and you have started orienting yourself the way you were warned to: by pitch. The low gate in your molars. The screaming alley you avoid. The chord of your courtyard — a fifth, a little flat by afternoon as the moss swells with fog. And somewhere underneath that learning, another learning is happening.

It is not in one house because it uses all of them, in rotation.

Watch the light. Ordinary daylight, thinned until shadows have violet edges, strikes the western houses first. The thaw begins there: drips, threads, the condensation stones sweating bright. The pools in the western courts go black and still. The moss there, frost-stiff all night, loosens and exhales.

That is where the tunnels empty in the morning.

Not all at once. Never all at once, nothing here is that crude. A stirring in the fern ranks. A tunnel mouth darkening as something passes under the moss without breaking its surface, a slow bulge traveling like a thought under a green skin. The white flowers in that courtyard open an hour before they open in the plaza, as if breathed on. Something drinks — you never see it drink, but a basin you have marked will be lower at noon than evaporation can account for, and its rim will be clean, licked free of pink algae in a broad, unmistakable arc.

By midday it has moved with the sun into the central houses, the greenest ones. This is its most habitable hour, and still cold enough to numb a hand in shadow, but inside its chosen walls the air is different. Less howling. The moss packing the chimney throat there has been recently arranged — you can tell, though you would be embarrassed to say how; the cushions are tamped in a spiral, thicker on the windward side, and the house-note has gentled overnight from a shriek to something almost voiced. It tunes its room before it rests in it. It has been doing this street by street for — you do not want to finish that thought. Winter cracks a lintel; spring moss grows into the crack; summer a house goes flat and is abandoned for a sharper one across the plaza; autumn a fallen stone re-voices a courtyard entirely and it moves in, whistling its approval from inside the walls.

It is not one loaf-sized thing.

It is a population that behaves like one thing, or one thing that has learned to be a population in order to survive up here. Call it a colony. Call it a family that never disperses. They are compact, yes, fur the color of wet stone on top and moss-green underneath where they press to their own gardens, blind as moles in any useful sense — eyes like black seeds under skin, because what would sight be worth in a place that vanishes into white river every evening? — and they navigate entirely by the instrument they live inside. They know this town the way you know the inside of your own mouth. They know which doorway is screaming today and which has been muffled soft by new growth. They press their bodies into the moss and feel, through stone, the low gate-note travel up from the plaza. A crack forming three houses away announces itself to them as a change in pitch a full season before it falls.

They are gardeners before anything else.

That is the work. Not building — the building was finished so long ago the corners have melted into grassed mounds. Tending. They scrape lichen from the exposed chimneys where it would brighten a note too much, and carry it to the shaded throats that need darkening. They pack and unpack moss with a patience indistinguishable from the weather itself. They keep the condensation stones clear of algae so the stones stay colder than the air and keep sweating drink. They nip the ferns back from the narrowest alleys — because those alleys must scream, that scream is part of the chord, part of the map, and if the town ever went entirely soft and muffled it would also go, for them, entirely dark.

And they eat almost nothing, which is what the place provides. Lichen. Fern tips in the brief summer. The pink snow-algae filmed at the edges of the standing pools, rich and strange. The small black frogs, occasionally, in the season when the frogs are careless — but mostly they leave the frogs, because the frogs are useful, because one frog at dusk can be a hundred frogs and drive off the white bird when it stoops too low, and because their croaking in the cisterns tells them, by echo-length, how full the water is without ever going down into the wet dark to check.

At night they come together more closely. That is when you hear the stone reporting like distant doors, one by one, in no pattern — and when, if you lie very still in a house they have not chosen, you can hear a nearer sound under it. A rustling that is not wind, because the bowl at night is locked and still. A settling. In the coldest houses, the Diplomatic ones they keep empty as buffers on the windward side, nothing moves. In the warmest court, the one whose basin never quite freezes all the way through, the moss will be lifting and falling in a slow, distributed breathing by midnight, a dozen small backs or one long one — the distinction stops meaning much under a hand's breadth of emerald cushion — all furred white at the tips by the frost that comes in through the roofless ceiling and does not quite reach them.

They are faintly warm. Not warm like a fire. Warm like a compost heap at altitude, like life insisting on a few degrees’ difference and defending it with stone on all sides.

They do not speak. The town speaks continuously, astonishingly loud, and they are the reason it has not wound down into a single moan or shattered into noise. Left alone, frost would wedge it apart and wind would worry every lintel down in a decade. Left alone, moss would choke every throat and the bowl would fall silent — which, up here, would be death; silence is what happens outside the lip, in the grey-pink emptiness. They are the felt and the finger on the string. A loose stone gets wedged with wet moss before it can clatter. A throat grown too sharp gets packed. A courtyard gone suddenly resonant after a fall gets investigated, whistled at from four sides at once, and either incorporated into the season’s new tuning or deliberately slumped, nudged stone by stone into a grassed mound, retired.

That is why it does not feel abandoned. Abandonment would require them to have been people. They are what people left possible — or what was already here in the moss tunnels, whistling at shadows, and for a brief arrangement borrowed human hands to stack the stones to the right heights. Hands could reach higher. Hands could fit boulders so patiently you cannot find the seam. When the hands left — down the mountain, into the clouds traveling beneath the upper slopes, for reasons that would have made sense at warmer altitudes — the original tenants simply moved up out of the scree and into the improved burrows, and went on with a much older occupation in a much better house.

In the fog, they are at their most present and least findable. The cloud-river pours over the eastern lip in the late afternoon and the town disappears one wet wall at a time until you are standing in whiteness and noise with your ears ringing, and then, close — closer than sound should allow you to judge — the whistle again. Not alarm this time. Locating. One note, answered from under a different wall, answered again from the cistern mouth, a relay going down the street you cannot see. They are moving through the whiteout far more confidently than you ever will, following the dropping temperature through their tunnels, heading for the night courtyard, calling the chord home.

If you stay long enough, they will begin to tune around you.

A doorway you pass every morning gets a fraction more moss on its southern jamb and its pitch bends, very slightly, away from the frequency of your voice. The house you have taken to sleeping in goes, over a week, subtly flatter, softer — because your body is a large warm obstruction in its chamber and they are compensating, or because they have decided a muffled room is the right room for a creature that startles at night. Your footsteps coming back to you off the far wall, doubled a moment late, will acquire a third, smaller pattering underneath them, keeping pace, retreating as you turn.

You will never quite see one clearly. You will see a fern rank shiver in windless air. You will see a tunnel mouth in the moss sigh shut. Once, at the dawn quiet — that twenty frozen minutes when the whole bowl holds its breath — you may see something cross the plaza pool’s black surface at the very edge of sight, low and hurried and the exact color of the stones, and the pool will hold its rings afterward for a long time, vibrating in patterns, as if the water were listening to where it went.

That is enough. Up here, sight was never the evidence. Presence is mostly elsewhere, and then, suddenly, under your hand when you lay it flat on a moss floor at noon and feel the cushion — deep, dry on top, saturated beneath — give not like vegetation but like a flank. Slowly. With great, geological patience. And hold still with you while the whole tuned town roars over the lip and through its stone throats, and the two of you listen to the season, legible as music, from inside its body.
