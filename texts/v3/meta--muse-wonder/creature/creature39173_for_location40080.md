If you ask who lives down there, a list would be the wrong kind of answer.

It would start out sensibly — people, goats, hens — and then it would have to keep going, because nothing down there lives only beside everything else. It lives off it, through it, inside the passages it leaves.

The pigeons are the most obvious citizens, and the least confined. They own the highest niches in the walls, round holes and low brick arches in iridescent rows, and they commute. All day they pour out through the holes in the vault and pour back in again, wings clattering against tile, dust and down sifting through the shafts of light. They are the hall's weather from elsewhere: rain on their feathers, cold air under their wings, a grain of something green from a field clinging to a foot. Below them nobody goes above so casually. The pigeons come and go, and everyone else watches them do it with the practical envy of neighbours who have chosen, for generations, a different bargain.

Below the pigeons live the rabbits in their wicker-fronted hutches, thumping warnings through the wood. Below them the hens, stacked in coops, opinionated at all hours, and the ducks who have claimed the damp corner by the drip-troughs and made a small, reeking, beloved swamp out of wet trampled straw where no swamp should be able to exist. They dabble in it as if it were a pond remembered by their feet. It fools them enough.

At floor level are the large lives around which the human day is organized. Goats, four and five to a slatted platform, wearing bells absurdly large for them, so that even in the dimmest aisle — and it is never dark, only shaded between the hard pools of daylight — you know exactly where a goat is thinking of going. They are neat-footed, fastidious, thieving. They know the herb pots are hung out of reach and regard this as a problem still under review. They know which drip falls where, and in warm weather a goat will station itself under a known bead of condensation with an expression of profound legal entitlement, mouth open, until it lands.

The two mules are older than most of the gates. They live in the central stalls where the traffic passes closest, and they have become furniture that breathes — patient, fly-twitching, vast-eyed, their hooves over years having worn perfect crescents into the brick by the tethering rings. Children lean against them to shell pods. Adults talk across their backs. When a mule stamps, the vault returns the sound a fraction larger and every hen comments and the moment rearranges itself.

The piglets are temporary citizens. Pink, loud, immaculate only in the instant after their tiled pen has been sluiced with its one bucket. They are loved without sentimentality and removed without ceremony: when a piglet reaches a certain size, a certain shout, it goes up the brick throat blinking into daylight, and another comes down. Nothing is allowed to outgrow the architecture. That is true of almost everything.

And then there are the people.

Not farmers who come down to a barn, exactly, though from above that is what it would be easiest to call them. These are the families of the hall itself — people whose soft shoes have helped dish the steps, whose palms have polished the black rope handrail, whose voices have learned the vault. They do not talk so much as project here, short and practiced bursts that carry over goat-bells and fodder-chopping, or they put their mouths to the clay speaking-tubes in the pillars and their words come out thirty paces away, intimate and disembodied, beside someone else's ear.

They are pale in the way of people who work in bright shade — not sickly, but sun-rationed, their forearms browner where the light shafts catch them at the fodder trays. They know the map of drips the way other people know streets. In cold weather, when the vault sweats, they can tell you without looking which cup will fill first, which trough the green-black stain belongs to, which animal is currently standing with its head under a falling bead. The first drink from any new drip is left for the frogs. That is not a superstition offered with a smile. It is spoken in the same tone as *no bare feet on the slats*, *no fire outside the hood*, *knock a gate twice before you open it, so what is inside can move back from the swing*.

Their children are everywhere, moving between the tiers with baskets nearly as large as themselves, absolutely certain of their footing. They learn the whitewash marks on the gates before they learn any other writing: this doe kidded in the cold month, this rabbit bites, this coop is the one with the hen who will sit on anything, including, once, a warm stone, with complete commitment. They rake droppings down the built slope behind the platforms. They carry water skins down and dung and old straw up, because if you bring something down, you carry something up — a rule the hall enforces physically whether you believe in it or not. They know which back stalls, warmed through the brick by the compost pit, are kept for newborn kids and lambs and chilled human infants alike, all of them tucked into the same borrowed heat.

Up on the gallery a woman works a treadle loom, weaving new pen-mats from reed and old rope while watching three toddlers and a pan on the hearth, and this is not a picturesque detail so much as the general condition. Nobody down there does one thing. The man chopping fodder — *chuff-chuff, chuff-chuff* — is also listening for a loose gate latch two aisles over. The old woman sorting husk and pod into sacks is also keeping count of the ducks. The girl hauling the shaft-covers open on their ropes, shifting the clean pools of light across the floor to warm a new tray of barley shoots, is also moving the day around for everyone else. Hands everywhere, keeping the passages just clear enough to move.

And around the people and the livestock, holding minor offices, live the tenants who were never invited and are now indispensable. Geckos flattened against the warm vault by the light shafts, patient as punctuation, taking moths. Swallows nesting in the stair throat despite generations of discouragement, swooping the whole length of the hall in one dark stitch. Frogs the colour of wet lime sitting gravely in the drip-bowls, eating the black flies that rise from the compost — the compost whose worms feed the hens, whose droppings feed the compost, whose warmth comes back through the back wall into the newborns' stalls. Crickets sawing away invisibly inside the fodder stacks, so the stacked straw itself seems to be quietly thinking. Mice running a second, mouse-scale architecture inside the brick cavities, with worn runs and hidden stores, an underground inside the underground. Bats folded in the one shaft bricked half-closed a century ago, waiting for the hall's day to tilt toward evening.

Even the green things are inhabitants of a sort, sparse and opportunistic and completely known. The fringe of moss no wider than a hand under the steadiest drip. The sharp-smelling herbs in pots hung just beyond goat ingenuity. The barley trays greened and cut, greened and cut. And the fig cutting in its cracked amphora by the brightest shaft — all leaf and ambition, tended out of proportion to its usefulness, never fruiting properly, kept anyway. Every crowded place keeps one citizen purely for hope.

From the gallery at feeding time, when the flat metal gong is struck and its thumb-smooth dent sings and the whole vault answers for a full minute — goats, hens, pigeons, rabbits thumping, mules stamping, children already moving with buckets, the frogs briefly reconsidering their posts — it becomes impossible to say where the keeper ends and the kept begins. The people feed the animals, and the animals heat the people. The compost heats both. The frogs tend the flies, the pigeons bring in the weather, the mice clean the seams, the mules hold up the centre of the room simply by being so calmly in it.

What lives there is not a family, and not a herd.

It is a single, densely inhabited body that has learned, brick by patched brick, drip by remembered drip, how to keep living in one warm, loud, sunken room without ever letting the room fill up — animal by animal, child by child, gate knocked twice, basket carried up, light uncovered, straw renewed — and to call that keeping, quite simply, home.
