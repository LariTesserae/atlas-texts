You would miss her at first. Almost everyone would.

For days you might walk the basin and think you had seen everything that lives there at any size — the egrets picking through the shallows, the monitor hauling himself importantly from pool to pool, the turtles clambering onto clods to bask — and you would have already passed her, probably more than once. You would have taken her for a part of the ground that had briefly decided to be something else.

That is because she has almost no vertical either.

Standing, if standing is even the word, her back comes no higher than your knee, and most of the time she does not stand. She settles. She lets her belly find the mud and her weight go out sideways, the way water does here, and then she is a low, grey-brown dome in a pan, a darker tone in wet clay, a slight interruption in the sheet of reflected sky. From a distance she looks like one of the old outcrops, polished and salt-stained. From closer, she looks like mud that has learned, very slowly, to breathe.

She is broad rather than long. A grown one is perhaps the length of a person lying down, but flattened, spread, made wide across the hips and shoulders as if the basin had pressed down on her ancestors for so long that they stopped arguing with it. Her head is wide too, blunt and low, no higher than the rest of her when she feeds, with a mouth that runs almost from eye to eye. Her eyes are small, dark, and set not so much on top of her head as at its upper corners, amber-ringed, with a third, milky lid that slides across against the glare. For this is a creature who lives in doubled light. She has to look down into dazzle as much as up into it.

Her skin is her country in miniature.

In the wet weeks it is soft, dark as chocolate clay, slick with mucus, filmed here and there with the same emerald and rust algae that colors the pans, so that submerged grasses seem to wave across her flanks when she lies in the shallows. As the water draws back, she dries in patches. Fine lines open across her back and sides, widen, join, until she is mapped in plates — dinner-plate, platter-sized, their edges lifting very slightly, clicking faintly when she shifts. Salt blooms white through her. It crusts delicate and pale along her spine, gathers in the deep fissures around her neck and legs, so that in the late season the oldest females carry a kind of pale geography on themselves, the rims of vanished pans drawn again in miniature. After rain it all softens and seals. She darkens. The cracks close. For days afterward, moisture stored in the thick outer layers of her skin seeps slowly out along the darkest seams, and she weeps, quietly, like the rocks do.

She is not beautiful in any lifted, branching way. She is beautiful in the way the basin is: reduced, patient, finished.

Her legs are short and enormously strong, set far apart, ending not in hooves or claws but in feet that solve the central problem of her life, which is not sinking. Each foot spreads when it takes her weight into a soft, webbed fan, three thick toes in front, the whole print a wide, splayed oval in the mud that fills with water almost as soon as she lifts it. Walking, she does not so much stride as transfer herself: a slow lean, a press, a sighing release, mud sucking softly. On a damp morning her trail is unmistakable once you have learned it — a braided line of round, flooded prints and, between them, the shallow furrow where her belly and tail have dragged, overwriting bird tracks, smoothing antelope prints, going pool to pool with no hurry in it at all. A flood does not rush here. Neither does she.

The tail is short, thick, flattened from side to side. In water deeper than her knees — which is to say, in water that would be nothing to you — she uses it with slow, powerful sweeps and becomes unexpectedly graceful, her head just breaking the surface, nostrils opening and closing in their high place at the tip of her snout, only her eyes and the salt-crowned dome of her back above the mirror. Mostly, though, the water is too thin to swim in. Mostly she wades, or lies, or pushes herself through shining wet clay that is more water than earth and more earth than water, which is her true element: the in-between.

She eats what the receding water leaves behind.

Her wide mouth is a tool for margins. She works the edges of pans head-down for hours, vacuuming up snails from the damp clay, freshwater mussels pried and crushed whole between ridged plates at the back of her jaw, softshell crabs, fallen seeds pressed into cracks, the black, nutrient-rich silt itself, which she takes in mouthful after mouthful and sorts with a huge, rough tongue. In the shallows she grazes the submerged grasses down to their pale bases and crops sedge tufts no other animal bothers with, salt and all — she has glands at the corners of her jaw that excrete the excess in thick, clear tears on hot afternoons, so that old ones often seem to be weeping steadily under the lone trees for no sorrow at all. Lungfish she digs out of their burrows when the pans contract, not often, but with terrible patience, scraping away at a little mud volcano for the better part of a morning. Catfish, stranded frogs, eggs left in the mud: nothing is refused. Nothing is chased either. She has no equipment for chasing. The basin delivers, and she is there when it does.

Because of her, other things gather.

When she lies in open water she is, quite simply, the only raised ground for a hundred yards, and life treats her accordingly. Dragonflies use the highest point of her back as a patrol post. Small frogs climb out of the water onto her in the evening and sing from her as if she were a bank. Egrets ride her — one, sometimes two, white and absurdly tall on that low grey island — stepping with exaggerated care as she moves beneath them, snatching at the insects her feet stir up, pecking salt and ticks from the fissures in her skin while she lies perfectly still, eyes half-lidded, in what looks very much like contentment. In the breeding season, weaver birds will hang over her for an entire morning disputing ownership of a strip of her shed skin as if it were the rarest material in the world. It may be. There is so little to build with here.

She shelters where shelter exists, which is barely. At midday, when the cicadas set up their tearing sizzle in the flat-topped trees, she will have aimed herself at dawn toward the nearest disc of shade and arrived by the worst of the heat, to collapse under it and let the insects and lichen-speckled bark and her own slowing blood keep the same hot hours together. She does not dig a burrow or make a nest or defend a hollow. She has, instead, a memory.

This is the thing about her that takes longest to understand: she knows the ghost map.

An old female may be sixty, eighty years old — the plates of her back grow in slow rings that can be read, roughly, like a tree's — and in that life she has crossed the same hundred miles of apparent sameness in a hundred different waters. She knows which depression will hold a hand's breadth when everything else is cracked, where the black bottom of an oxbow stays soft at the lowest ebb, which channel, indistinguishable now from baked clay, will be ankle-deep in a week. On late evenings, when mist fills the old waterways first and draws them in white across the dimming flat, she is already walking in them, unerring, following routes that do not currently exist. Young animals follow her at a distance in hard seasons. So, at a greater distance, do the antelope. She forgets almost nothing the water has shown her. The basin is ancient and has forgotten how to cut new rivers, and she is in agreement with it: nothing new is needed.

She is solitary the way the trees are solitary. Where are the others? At distances measurable in a long walk. Scattered across the floodplain in that same sparse arrangement everything here obeys — one to a good pan system, one to a reach of deeper channel — close enough that sound will do what legs should not have to.

For she can be heard, when she chooses, for over a mile.

Late in the afternoon, or on still nights when the frog chorus thins for a moment between one species and the next, she lifts her head a few inches out of the water, inflates the great pouch of her throat, and makes a sound that seems far too large for her. It is a deep, jug-like boom, lower than the deepest frog, felt in the soles of your feet through wet ground before it reaches your ears, followed by a long, resonant exhale like air sighing out of a huge, half-buried drum. On flat water with nothing to stop it, the call arrives disembodied. Another answers, minutes later, from invisibility. Then another, from further. On certain nights the whole basin booms very slowly in sequence, dome calling to dome across the mist, a conversation conducted at the speed of geography, about where the water is, and how deep, and whether it is coming or going.

The males — smaller, narrower, rarer, less given to staying — come out of the eastern channels in the season when the sheet water joins everything to everything, swimming and wading for days along the shining connections to find her. There is no display worth the name. No leaping, no brightening. He rests his chin for a while on the salt-white rim of her back; she either settles deeper, which is refusal, or raises herself slightly in the mud, which is everything. Her eggs, when they come, are not laid in any structure. She presses them one by one, soft-shelled and warm, down into the cracks in drying clay at the margin of a pan, and covers them by the simple act of lying over the place, on and off, for days, until her own weight and the swelling and sealing of the clay have hidden them entirely. They wait there as seeds and snail eggs and lungfish wait — in the held breath below the crust — and hatch, if they hatch, when the next water sighs in and softens the ground above them and calls them up through it.

What comes out is not yet her at all. The young are palm-sized, almost transparent, frilled at the throat with feathery, scarlet gills, all tail and appetite, wholly aquatic in a world of ankle-deep water. They live their first seasons in the pans as fish do, crowding down as the water crowds down, burrowing into still-damp clay and sleeping in cocoons of mucus and mud through the worst dry weeks, small volcanoes marking whole nurseries of them at the edges of the last pools. Year by year the gills shrink. The tail shortens. The back broadens and begins, unmistakably, to flatten under the long lesson of the place. Most die as frogs and fish die here, in enormous, unremarkable numbers, taken by herons, by snakes, by the simple failure of a particular pan. The few who persist eventually haul themselves out onto the mud on feet that have become fans, and walk, and do not go back to living in water so much as carry the water's habits with them onto land.

Nothing hunts the grown ones. There is nothing here large enough, and there is, in any case, almost nothing to get hold of: no neck to seize, no leg to hamstring, only that low armored dome that seals itself down into the mud at a shadow's approach and can stay sealed, breathing once in twenty minutes, while a whole afternoon of danger passes over it. Their deaths are slower. Droughts longer than memory. A pan poisoned too salt at the end, too many gathered in the last dark water. Age itself, which looks here like a gradual unwillingness to travel between pools.

And when she dies, she does not at first appear to.

She settles in some shallow place and does not rise, and the skin tightens and the plates dry and lift, and silt blows and washes over the immense still curve of her until she is a low swell, a polished dome, a bank no higher than a knee to be walked over, perhaps, by something that takes her for ground. Egrets stand on her. Grass finds the seams. Water stores itself in her hollows and seeps out for a long time after rain.

The basin has been worn down almost to its final idea of itself, and she is that idea, briefly moving: water, mud, light, and a life that spreads thin over great distances, that knows how to wait under a crust for the world to soften, that takes up less height than almost anything alive and, because of it, gets to see the whole sky.
