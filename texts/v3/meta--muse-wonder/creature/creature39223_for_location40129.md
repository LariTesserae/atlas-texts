Nothing that lives in the crack was meant for it, exactly. Everything arrived the way things arrive at that height — blown up, fallen in, lured sideways by shelter, or born there because a parent had made the same mistake successfully the year before. And then it stayed, because the crack, once you are inside it, keeps what the mountain above would kill.

It is stacked life, species pressed into storeys according to how much light and space their bodies require.

At the rim, in the last of the wind, are the snow finches. They live closest to the weather and are the most visibly nervous about it: white-bellied, grey-brown, endlessly shifting colonies that erupt from horizontal cracks in synchronized, hysterical bursts and settle again a second later as if nothing happened. Their nests are crammed so close that one bird's tail may brush another's chicks. Under them, a little deeper where the rock begins to hold shade, are alpine accentors — dunnock-quiet, mouse-colored, unremarkable until you are near enough to see the fine streaking at their throats — and rosy-finches, whose males in season carry an improbable wash of pink over breast and rump, like color remembered from a lower, warmer world. They pick seeds and frozen insects from the ledges, hop rather than fly in the confined places, and raise chicks that beg at every vibration.

The swifts own the air of the place. Thousands of them, dark sickles with almost no feet to speak of, made entirely for a life that does not land. They scream in and out of the slot at speeds that should dash them against schist, pulling turns inside the fissure, missing overhangs by centimeters, reading the walls by echo as much as by sight. Their wingbeats come back to them multiplied — one bird made three by the throat-shape of the rock. They nest in seams too narrow to look into, black slits where you know them only by a sudden eye, a breath of warm, musky air, a chick's beak probing out. A swift returns not merely to the crack but to the exact seam, the same hand-length of darkness its grandparents used, and rebuilds there with what the wind gives it: dry grass, sheep's wool torn off animals kilometers below, feathers, its own saliva for glue. In flight they harvest the updraft for a living — craneflies, aphids, moths, grasshoppers lifted off sunlit slopes whether they will it or not, all the small aerial plankton of the valleys strained out by beaks moving too fast to see.

Lower, where the light fails and stays failed, are the bats. They should not be there. It is too high, too cold, the nights too thin-aired. And yet there they are, small brown bodies packed head-down in cracks no wider than a human hand, fur beaded with condensation, wings folded like umbrellas of skin. In the day they shift and mutter and chitter to one another in sounds pitched at the edge of hearing, rearranging for warmth. At dusk — inside, a deepening of an already permanent dusk — they unhook and drop, need to drop before they can fly, and skim the black pool at the bottom without ever touching it, drinking on the wing. They live on the moths.

The moths are the crack's great unconsenting population. On warm days they come up by the ten-thousand, carried on valley updrafts, bright and dusty and utterly unequipped, tumbling over the rim when the air stills and sinking into the shaft like slow snow. Some flutter in the gloom for hours, bumping soft bodies against stone, landing on anything warm without fear. Some are eaten in mid-air. Some freeze in old ice on the floor, wings spread, patterns intact, perfectly preserved. Some end furred into spiderwebs or stippling the surface of the pool, struggling until a bat or a springtail raft reaches them. They do not belong, and the whole interior economy depends on them not belonging, again and again, in inexhaustible numbers.

Where no bird can fit, the rock crawls. Pale spiders live in the corners in loose sheet-webs that sag under moth wings and shed husks; they pick through the moth-fall methodically, long legs testing, unhurried in a place where food literally drops out of the sky. Harvestmen stalk the damp walls on stilts. Beetles with reduced, useless eyes feel their way through guano and crevices stuffed with the dry remains of grasshoppers, butterflies, craneflies, seeds — a larder they did not stock and cannot exhaust. In the black films where condensation runs, springtails gather in trembling grey drifts, thousands of minute bodies making the stone appear to move on its own. When they reach the pool they do not sink; they gather on its surface tension in living rafts, feeding at the edge of a moth's drowned wing.

Even the pool has its tenants and its visitors, and almost nothing down there is truly alone. Finch chicks in nests above gape blindly at sounds that are not their parents, because in the fissure every sound arrives amplified and displaced. Bats drop feathers of sound over the water. Spiders lower themselves thoughtfully onto anything new and warm that has learned to keep still — the second rule of the place, learned by every resident: movement is an alarm, stillness is landscape. Keep still and a moth will settle on your eyelash. Keep still and the shrieking vortex that exploded upward at your arrival will, after an hour, resettle seam by seam, web by web, until the screaming is a constant again and does not mean alarm at all, merely occupancy.

On the one ledge that gets morning shade, a raven keeps a stick platform, rebuilt each year, and a habit that looks almost like thought: it prises beetles out of cracks with a twig held in its beak. It is the largest regular, an outsider who has learned the pantry best, and everything smaller gives it a precise, respectful distance.

And underneath them all, barely qualifying as inhabitants and somehow the most startling: a film of green-black algae clinging to the wettest walls where light reaches perhaps twenty minutes a year; orange and sulfur lichen at the rim; thumb-sized cushions of moss campion above, flowering absurdly pink. Then, in the dark at the bottom where decades of guano have rotted down into something like soil — warmed faintly by its own composting, fed by drips and dead moths and bat dung — three blades of grass. Pale as maggots, nearly translucent, growing without light to speak of off warmth and rot. Not blown in to die, this time. Rooted.

That is who lives there: screamers and packers-in and pickers-through, accidental harvests and the things that harvest them, layered rim to floor so densely that a hand laid on the stone will cover a web, a husk, a feather, a breathing seam. Warm small bodies by the thousand, condensing their own breath onto the walls that shelter them, making the damp that makes the algae that feeds the springtails that feed — indirectly, patiently — everything else. A community assembled entirely out of what the sky threw away, that has learned, generation by exact generation, how to keep it.
