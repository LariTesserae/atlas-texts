Nothing that lives here lives upright for long.

Even the newest arrivals learn it quickly: keep low, keep wide, keep moving, and do your living in the brief seam between ice and water. What holds still gets heaved up by the ground, frozen into the peat, scoured sideways by the afternoon sleet. What settles gets left behind when the streams change course.

The first inhabitants are almost not visible. Lichen spreads in rust and sulfur coins over rock that split yesterday, patient enough to eat stone. In the wet hollows, cushion plants pull themselves into tight green felt, no higher than a boot top, their roots stitching a thin mat of black peat over gravel that has not yet become soil. Inside each cushion it is a few degrees warmer, a few degrees calmer, and tiny white flowers open there as if in another climate. Sedges do the same work in the meadows, short, dense, bright for a few weeks where meltwater lingers. Everything green makes a surface rather than a shape.

The animals follow that surface.

The wild asses come in strings of twenty and thirty, pale underneath where the low light catches them, dark along the spine. They graze with their heads low for a long time and bring them up all at once, ears turning, nostrils wide in the thin air. Their lungs do the work that altitude demands without complaint; they can run where a newcomer cannot climb a slope without stopping, mouth open, tasting iron. The smaller gazelles pick over drier ground, more nervous, scattering and reforming. Both herds are always on their way to somewhere else in the basin — to the next meadow darkening with water, to the lee side of a slab when the squall comes.

Around the marshes the life thickens until it is loud. Geese and ruddy shelducks nest right in the sedge, in numbers that seem impossible for so short a summer, and rise together when a fox shows at the margin — a sudden clatter and calling that fills the whole flat and then, just as suddenly, decides the danger is past and settles. Black-necked cranes stand in pairs in the shallows, tall but somehow still belonging to the horizontal world, grey against grey water. They mate for years, return to the same general drowning meadow, and rebuild on whatever hummock the new braids of water have left them. Marmots live in the fresh scree, whistling a warning that carries improbably far. Pikas cut sedge stem by stem and stack it to dry, whole winter economies laid out in miniature haypiles. Foxes quarter the edges at a trot. Wolves are mostly a change in behavior in everything else, and then, in the early dark, a shape moving without hurry. Ravens stay. Ravens ride the lift off every new cliff face, turn their heads as they pass, and croak once, the sound arriving clean a mile away.

None of them own a place. They own a route, a season, a memory of where the green line tends to appear.

The people have had to learn to be the same kind of animal.

They do not live on the plateau, exactly. They live *in* their machines, the way the cushion plants live inside their own held warmth. Two or three to a brine processor, a crew sealed into a long, low, riveted hull that crawls on person-high tracks, drinking lake water at the front and laying down clean warm water behind it. Inside it smells of hot electrics, diesel, wet wool, and tea kept going all day. The air is enriched just enough to think clearly. Outside, they wear oxygen-assisted masks and move with deliberate slowness, not from care alone but because haste is punished immediately — a few fast steps loading a filter and the world narrows, the iron taste comes back, hands fumble.

They are up from below for a season only. You can tell the new ones by their nosebleeds, their cracked lips, their headaches behind the eyes in the morning, the way they sleep badly and wake gasping in the first week even inside. The older hands have thicker blood and quieter habits. They speak less outside, save breath for work, gesture instead. They learn the gauges second. First they learn to scan the intake path for a goose settled invisible in the sedge, to read a darkening of gravel as last year's track already being erased, to tell at a distance whether those are asses queued to cross or just boulders in fog. A good operator can feel through the deck whether the ground under the track has gone to sponge.

For much of the day the crew are less drivers than herdsmen of a very large, very slow beast. The processor has its own appetites and rhythms. Its diesels labor and cough in air too thin for their design, its electric heart hums through the soles of your boots, its breath comes out white and flattens instantly in the cold. It follows lithium and borate as the concentrations shift in the lakes from week to week, the way the asses follow grass. It grazes kilometers apart from its fellows; at night the fleet is three lit smudges in an enormous dark, amber running lights blurred by fog, moving slower than weather. During the day you hear it before you see it, and only just — a low thrum swallowed by wet ground, a conveyor clinking at regular intervals, then wind again.

And, without anyone having planned it all at once, the other inhabitants have begun to include the machines in their routes. Ducks gather at dawn in the warm discharge pool at the stern, steam rising around them, in water that never quite freezes. Shelducks loaf in the lee of the hull during a squall. Asses queue, entirely unhurried, to cross in front of a processor, and the processor stops and idles until the last foal has picked its way over the tracks, because that is the law and also because in practice there is nothing else sensible to do. Drones rise to carry samples and spare pumps, the only fast things besides birds, and they veer automatically around nesting colonies and drop out of the sky if a crane crosses their lane. When the sedge is flowering and the geese are down, whole lakes are skipped. The richest brine in the basin is worthless if it lies under a thousand nests.

So the human day bends around the same narrow facts as everyone else's: melt by mid-morning, graze the green and the mineral while it lasts, weather-vane into the afternoon sleet and wait, stacks ticking, while a rock face somewhere sheds a new slab with a rifle-shot crack. By evening the crew dims their machine to a running glow and eats inside while the herds move down off the flats and the cranes call once or twice across the water, answering each other in the dusk that has looked like dusk since noon.

When the cold comes back in earnest, the bargain ends the same way for all of them. The cranes and geese lift and are gone south over ranges that would kill the people. The asses and gazelles drift lower. The marmots go under stone into the long sleep, the pikas to their hay. The processors turn, one by one, and crawl down off the plateau under their own power before the seeps ice shut and the tracks lock in — leaving no road, no foundation, no fence line, only a faint dark line over gravel already buckling with frost-heave. 

What remains is what can afford to remain: lichen on fresh rock, peat stiffening under clear ice, ravens and foxes and wolves making a sparser living in the wind. And underneath the frozen lakes, in milky water thick with salt and minerals, microbes keep their own slow summer going, waiting for the thaw to bring back the birds, the herds, and the lit, crawling factories — all of them returning, none of them settling, to a place that is still deciding how to be a place at all.
