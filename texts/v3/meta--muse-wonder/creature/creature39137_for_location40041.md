At first, nothing does.

That is the impression the place insists on, and for a long while it holds. No movement that resolves into an animal. No call, no scratching in the pipes, no flicker at the edge of sight. The warm pools held in the leaves are empty. The undersides of the leaves are empty. The cracked floor, the catwalk, the dark window of the control shed — empty. A human does not live down here, whatever the kettle and the clipboard and the single plastic chair outside the shed suggest. A human only descends, does what hands are needed for, and climbs the switchback again. The chair is warping from being left in the residents' weather.

The residents are the plants.

Seven of them, perhaps eight, if you count the one standing a little apart toward the north wall whose crown has not yet fully opened. They are given the whole floor the way orchard trees are given a field: not crowded, not touching, each with bare black stone all around it, room to walk a full circle without brushing a leaf. From a distance, in the pilot-light gloom, they appear as separate darknesses, darker than the dark around them. Up close they separate slowly into form.

They were not found here. They were made for here — bred, altered, selected until the ordinary rules loosened. They are not palms, though the crown suggests it, and not trees, though they stand on trunks. The trunk of a mature one is short and disproportionately swollen, waist-high, thick as a barrel, the bark — if it is bark — dark and perpetually wet-looking, plated and soft at once, as if it were storing water inside itself against a drought that will never come in this chamber. Nothing climbs it. Nothing nests in it. It holds the crown up into the rising heat and otherwise does nothing quickly.

The crown does the work.

Each unfolds leaves three meters long, broad as doors, rubbery to the eye, hanging under their own weight. Their color in true light might be green, but here, adapted to a slit of grey that never reaches them, they have gone nearly black — a black-green so deep it drinks what little illumination the valve-lamps throw. Dull silver veins run through them, not bright, not decorative, branching thick as fingers at the base and thinning toward perfect, unbitten edges. No leaf is chewed. No leaf is torn by wind. There has never been wind down here, and there has never been a mouth.

The leaves do not reach for light. That is the first disorientation, if you stay long enough to notice orientation at all. The older fronds angle outward and down, cupping themselves, making shallow living basins. Condensation collects there, drop by slow drop from the copper lines above, until each fold holds a small warm pool. For most of the long stillness between cycles the plants simply hold their water up in the air where they caught it. Then the pump breathes beyond the wall, the manufactured rain moves in its drifting band from plant to plant, the leaves grow too heavy or receive some slower signal of fullness, and one by one — never together, each on its own time — they tip. The held pool pours in a muted sheet down the frond's channel, over the swollen trunk, to the roots below.

It is drinking and irrigation in the same gesture. They catch the building's exhaled steam and return it to themselves.

Below, the roots do not behave like roots in soil, because there is no soil to speak of. They travel sideways across and into the cracked stone plates, pale, thickening, following the half-buried warm pipes the way a plant in another place would follow a watercourse. They grip the pipes. At points of contact they have flattened against the metal; where mineral water has crusted them they have whitened, as if slowly being turned to the same stone and salt that blooms around the floor vents. The great condensers are laced, foot by foot, into the plumbing they serve. It is impossible to say where the organism ends and the installation begins, and evidently that was the intention: a leaf-surface wide enough, cool enough, patient enough to coax vapor back into water, over and over, on a forty-five-minute breath.

They live on a timescale that makes animal life seem frantic and therefore impossible here. A leaf takes a week to open. A fern at the base of one trunk — brilliant, fleshy green in the cracks, a lesser attendant that has seeded itself in the perpetual drip-zone of the elder — unfurls over days so gradually that you cannot catch it moving, only remember it slightly less curled yesterday. There is no scramble, no season of urgency. Heat is always coming up through the floor. Moisture is always, eventually, falling. Their seasons are the pump cycles, two or three minutes of soft rain crossing the chamber, smelling of hot stone and green sap, almost sweet, and then the long hot stillness resumes, and they sit in it, dripping very slowly, growing at the edge of perception.

They do not flower for anything that flies. There is nothing that flies. There are no eggs under the leaves, no larvae in the cupped pools, though frog and mosquito should love this permanent summer. The shed holds hand-brushes for pollination laid out in size order, kept like surgical instruments, which tells its own story: even their reproduction, in the cultivated generation, is a task performed on them, carefully, by a visitor. Left to themselves they would simply stand and condense, stand and condense, perhaps for decades — lush, sterile, unwounded, a forest that permits almost no other life except the sponge-moss that drinks its drips before they can ring on stone, and the green ferns sheltering at its feet in the mineral runoff.

And yet at the north end, beyond the last spaced elder, they are beginning to stop being only that.

Where the wall split this season — fresh fracture, faces still glassy, seams breathing heat directly from the rock — young plants have started where no pipe was laid to guide them and no brush has tended them. A fern no bigger than a hand, flattened into a crack. A pale, blind shoot, not yet bothering with black-green, curling not upward toward the clogged slit above but down and inward, toward the warm seam. Its tropism is heat. Its compass is the gradient in the stone. Light here was always the accident — intermittent, dilute, undependable. Warmth and mineral water were the promise the chamber actually kept, and the new generation, unplanted, is orienting itself by that promise instead.

They are smaller, paler, less perfect than their engineered parents, and they are the first true natives of the place. The elders were installed. These are arriving.

So what lives here is a slow, solitary orchard learning to become a wildwood: a handful of huge, dark-leaved condensers spaced in the hot dark, holding rain in their arms, rooting into pipes, turning steam back into water with their bodies, attended only by their own shade-ferns and moss — and, at the edges, their feral young creeping not toward the sun but toward the heat in the broken wall, beginning, still in the first sharp youth of the place itself, to live there on their own terms.
