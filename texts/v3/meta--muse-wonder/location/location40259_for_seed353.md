It is built on a plateau that has forgotten how to be a mountain.

From a distance — from the dry approach road that switchbacks up out of the salt lowlands — it does not look like a forest, and it does not look like a factory, because there is no longer any difference. It is a single black mass filling an entire eroded caldera, valley-wide, perhaps thirty kilometers from rim to rim, worn so old and so flat by wind and time that the volcano underneath is just a shallow dish. Nothing sharp remains. Everything original has been ground down to a settled, final smoothness.

And on that smoothness, something has been made to grow until growing and making became the same act.

The pall comes first. Miles before the edge you enter it: a low, stationary cloud that never leaves the bowl. It is not weather. It is pollen, soot, shredded fiber, spores the size of dust, and hot exhaust, all held in by the rim and churned slowly by rising heat. Above it, the sun is white and merciless. Below it, there is almost no light at all.

You descend into noon darkness.

The temperature does not drop in the shade. It rises. Fifty, sixty degrees at the outer avenues, hotter deeper in. The air is desiccated, thin at this altitude, and it scalds the back of the throat. It smells of hot iron, ozone from the power lines, cooked resin, charred sugar, creosote, and a dry, peppery smell like paper left in an oven. There is no smell of rain, ever. What little moisture arrives on the night wind from the lowlands evaporates in the pall before it can fall, and the condensers — great finned towers grown, not built, their trunks wrapped in copper piping — catch it first and ration it downward in sealed pipes. Nothing here drinks from the sky.

No plant here lives on water in any honest sense.

They live on heat, on mineral slurry, on waste oil and slag and each other. Their sap is wax and black resin that does not flow until it is almost ready to burn. Their leaves, where they have leaves, are not green. They are black, blue-black, the matte black of a radiator, broad as doors and held underground-facing, or curled inside hot pits, to drink infrared from geothermal fissures and factory floors. Many trees are inverted in function: roots thick in the manufactured soil to mine warmth and metal, crowns above that are not crowns at all but cooling fins, chimneys, and hollow fluted towers that shed heat back into the pall with a faint, barely visible shimmer. In the true dark under the triple canopy you learn to see by that shimmer, and by touch, and by the phosphor strips the pruners embed in bark, because a naked white light will blister the shade-boughs and bring a whole lane down in defensive curling.

Every surface is plant, and every plant has been placed.

There is no natural substrate left to touch. If you dug — if you were allowed to dig, if the root-mat let you — you would go through a meter of shredded bark and slag-compost that was manufactured and laid down as soil, through living root pavement interwoven with old rail lines, through trunk foundations trained over steel armatures a thousand years ago and long since grown around them until the steel is bone inside wood. The warehouses are groves whose trunks were scaffolded in rows as saplings and roofed with their own fused branches. The cathedrals of the smelter district are hollow trees five stories high, their interiors lined with firebrick that the tree itself excreted, mineralized from its feed. The power station is a forest clearing only in the sense that its trees grow in a circle and all lean inward, feeding a turbine pit with falling resin pods. Fruit here is ore: hard, hot nodules grown around copper, tin, purified silicon, swelling on vines that run along conveyor trellises until automated harvesters clip them.

It is packed to the point of suffocation. There is no empty ground.

Between the foundry-groves and the seed lofts, between the cooling forests where black leaves tick and contract and the nursery terraces where seedlings push out of dry ash beds at visible speed, the lanes are tunnels cut this morning through living wall. By evening they will have narrowed. By tomorrow they will be gone unless the pruning carts pass again. The carts are slow, hydraulic, almost silent machines on root-rails; they saw, they mulch, they replant in a single passage, and their passage is the only reason a map can be drawn at all, and even then the map is printed on bark-paper meant to be composted within the week. Stand still too long on an unmaintained path and fine root-hairs will test your boots, your tripod, the hem of your heat-suit, with an industrious, mindless interest in incorporating you as trellis. Everything still must become structure.

And everything is in motion, though it makes very little noise.

This is the strangest part. A place this turbulent should roar. Towers live fast here — fed on furnace heat and ground factory waste, a cooling spire can rise twenty meters in nine days, flower in black, resinous cones, harden, hollow, serve fifty days as a flue, die, and be felled into its own shredder pit before it can rot. Vines overrun a decommissioned vat-house overnight. A whole avenue exhales, expands in the afternoon heat, and cracks its paved root-skin with a series of soft, deep pops like knuckles. Pollen fans — industrial blowers grown into the trunks of pollinator trees, since there are no bees, no birds, no wind strong enough under the canopy — turn slowly and push hot currents from male groves to female groves in a managed, artificial weather.

But density eats sound. Wood and leaf and fiber wall absorb it. The machines were built by people who had to live inside their own engine and made it muffled: slow gears, fluid drives, falls of material onto beds of shredded bark. What you hear, mostly, is your own breath in the mask. Then, near and far, details: the ticking of cooling leaves contracting. Resin dripping onto hot stone, a faint hiss. A distant, felt-more-than-heard thud as a dead flue-tree drops exactly where it was grown to drop. The low, continuous hum through the soles of your feet of the turbine pits, as if the plateau itself were an appliance left running in another room.

There is no animal life to add its layer. No insects in the resin. No birds in the black canopy — the heat, the dry toxins in the pollen, the managed sterility of the nursery air would kill them, and the gates filter even spores not on the register. Humans pass through in heat-suits, temporary and outnumbered to irrelevance. This is an ecosystem with only two kingdoms, and they have merged: plant and machine, each the other's tool. The plants do the smelting, lifting, cooling, storing, computing of stresses in wood grain; the machines do the sowing, pruning, mating, feeding, and killing.

Ancient and frantic at once, it balances. That is the rule you come to understand after a few hours in the dark lanes, sweating inside your suit while new growth audibly creaks somewhere above you and a lane you used that morning has already softened at the edges. Individual things here are violently short-lived. The whole is in its final form and has been for longer than any record kept inside it. Total mass does not change. Total heat output does not change. For every tower felled, a pit is already sprouting. For every factory grove harvested to stumps, the stumps are coppicing before the carts have cleared the end of the row. Erosion has done its work on stone and on intention alike: whoever designed discrete buildings and discrete forests is gone, their plans worn down to a single self-maintaining process that fills the dish of the caldera edge to edge, black and hot and dark.

At the rim again, climbing the service lift through the pall, you break out suddenly into blinding daylight and freezing, clean wind, and the contrast is obscene. Below you the cloud lid seals shut, featureless. Nothing shows. But you can still feel it through the lift cables — that packed, quiet, endless churning, growing its own walls in the dark and tearing them down, making and remaking itself out of heat and waste, needing almost no water, tolerating no animal, admitting light only as something to be shut out and replaced by warmth.

It does not need the sun. It has built a better, blacker source, and planted it.
