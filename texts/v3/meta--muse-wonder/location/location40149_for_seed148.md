At ground level it does not look like a town at all. It looks like something that has not finished deciding to be geology.

The land is low and cool and newly made — not eroded, not weathered, not yet hardened into its final opinion of itself. Where there should be stone there is a kind of translucent cartilage, pale and slick underfoot, still swelling in slow hills. Where there should be soil there is pulp. A shallow basin holds a shallow fog. Nothing drains properly. Water arrives as condensation more than rain: it beads on every surface, gathers in the cups of leaves, slips in cold lines down stems thick as bridge piers, and now and then lets go all at once inside, a brief interior shower that falls for thirty seconds, stops, and is absorbed.

In the middle of the basin, a seed-pod, a fruit, a root-bulb — the word changes depending on who is trying to sell you a map — has split just enough to admit a person sideways.

Inside is the town.

Inside is also almost entirely plant, and almost entirely empty. Both are true at once, and that is the first rule.

The pod is hollow. Imagine a cathedral if the cathedral were grown rather than built, and then imagine the builders, arriving later with their iron and timber and soot, had looked at the vaulted green darkness of it and decided not to fill it. The living tissue is everything at the perimeter: floor, walls, ceiling, buttresses, slow ribs curving up and over. Veins run through the translucent pulp of it, faintly phosphorescent, not enough to read by, just enough to know where the surface is. The center is void. A great, cool, damp air-space, perhaps two hundred meters across at its widest, though distances inside have a habit of behaving as if they were larger when you are crossing them and smaller when you are looking back.

The town clings to the inside of that surface. All of it. All the way around.

So there are streets above you, upside down in the dark, their lamps unlit, their chimneys pointing down toward your head across a hundred meters of nothing. There are streets under your feet, damp boardwalks bolted into living wood that is not quite wood. There are districts stitched sideways into the walls, houses in tiers, warehouses, a market under a leaf-canopy, a chapel, a water-tower grown from a hollow reed and banded in riveted iron. If you stand still for a while and let your eyes fail properly, you can pick out the whole urban pattern at once: layered, organized, dense with infrastructure at the skin, and utterly sparse in the volume. Long rope bridges and counterweighted elevators cross the void at intervals, but mostly there is separation. Platforms hang far from each other. Neighborhoods do not touch. Between one lit doorway and the next there may be a minute of careful walking along a root, and forty meters of black air below — or above — it.

Gravity, politely, follows the plant. It pulls outward, toward the nearest living surface. This means a person can walk up a street that curves, without any sensation of climbing, until the town they left is hanging inverted over the void and the town ahead of them has become the ground. Children find this funny in a way adults have learned not to discuss. Cats find it obvious. Pigeons, who arrived with the first settlers and promptly abandoned cooing, launch themselves from a roof, fall *up* across the hollow screaming silently with their wings, orient mid-flight to a new down, and land on a wall that from your perspective is a cliff.

Nobody speaks loudly here. It is not a law. It is an effect.

The industrial quarter occupies the lower — which is to say, one of the lowers — curves of the pod, where the plant’s vascular system runs thickest. Factories have been cut and coaxed into the tissue: sawmills that do not saw so much as persuade the fast-growing cane-wood to split along seams, fermentation engines in copper vats, loom-houses, a small foundry kept constantly cool and wet by sheets of transpiring leaf. Sap is tapped under pressure and drives pistons. Methane from rotting heart-pith turns slow flywheels. Belts of braided fiber run everywhere, muffled in moss, so the machinery is not heard so much as felt — a low, irregular thrum in your molars, in the soles of your feet, timed not to any human shift schedule but to the plant’s own pulse. When the pod contracts, which it does roughly every seven minutes, every engine in town gains power for a moment, every bridge tightens, every door swells fractionally in its frame. Experienced machinists time their hammer-strokes to it. Experienced thieves do too.

The plant is still growing, fast enough to watch if you are patient in the right way. A wall left alone for an afternoon will have advanced a finger’s width. New rooms bud, at first as soft knobs, then as blisters, then, if pollinated correctly, as hollow chambers with a skin that hardens over a week. This is how the town expands. Building permits are issued as seeds. You do not construct a house; you marry a seed to a location, feed it a specific diet of ash and river-silt and ground shell, sing or do not sing to it — opinion is divided and furious — and defend its young walls from the things that would eat it before it lignifies. A wealthy family’s home may have great-grandparents’ bedrooms preserved inside it as shrunken, bark-lined closets long after the family has grown new wings onto the structure. A poor family may live in a rented gourd that is still, technically, ripening, and sweeten the landlord’s heart-fruit in lieu of coin.

Cutting is forbidden, not morally but practically. A cut here does not stay a cut. It becomes an argument. Wounds in the substrate explore alternative shapes while they heal: a doorway lopped open on Monday may try out being a mouth on Wednesday. Staircases pruned too aggressively have been known to re-route themselves through the neighbor’s pantry, apologetically, overnight. So all building is done by persuasion, splinting, grafting, training new growth around iron armatures that the plant will eventually swallow and remember. Old girders run through living beams like bones through flesh. In the oldest district you can find a tram track, abandoned, that now grows small white flowers every spring from its sleepers, and occasionally, on very damp nights, carries a car that no company operates between two stations that were demolished before anyone now living was born. It makes no sound when it passes. The passengers look out with great interest. Nobody boards.

The fauna is abundant and almost entirely quiet. This is the second thing newcomers cannot adjust to — not the dark, but the populated silence.

Moths the size of dinner plates cross the central void on slow, rowing wings, pale as communion wafers, and are hunted in absolute silence by pale owls, by gliding marsupials with skin stretched between their limbs, by long-tongued bats who navigate by a sense that appears to be taste at a distance. Glass frogs crowd the condensation pools and sing below the threshold of human hearing, so you perceive their choruses only as a prickling at the edge of a headache. Snails with shells like stained glass process along the undersides of leaves, grazing the bacterial films, followed patiently by blind, cat-sized beetles who farm them. In the water-cups, in the cisterns, in the flooded seed-chambers below the factories, eyeless translucent fish turn in circles that, if you chart them for a week, spell the same shapes as the street plan above. Pilgrims come to have these interpreted. The fish appear unbothered.

Everything eats the plant, and the plant, slowly and without obvious resentment, eats everything back. Fallen leaves are gathered before they land — because, with gravity as it is, “fallen” is a local opinion — and pulped for paper, for fodder, for the fermentation engines. Dead animals are placed in specific hollows and are overgrown within days, their shapes persisting for a while as suggestions in the bark: here the long curve of a spine in a doorway’s grain, here, in a market pillar, something that was once very likely a hand. Dead citizens are handled the same way, by request, which is nearly universal. It is considered comforting. It may explain why some houses know your name before you have been introduced, and why certain streets, when walked at a certain age, feel remembered rather than navigated.

Light is the scarcest material in town. What the plant admits through its split seam and through thin places in its skin arrives greenish and exhausted, as if it has been chewed. For the rest, there are the veins in the walls, the faintly glowing sap-wells, colonies of a foxfire fungus that the street-lamp guild cultivates in hanging baskets and feeds on iron filings and grief — that is their term; chemically it appears to be sugar. The fungus brightens in the presence of strong emotion and has therefore been banned from courtrooms, bedrooms, and the stock exchange, a low, vine-roofed room where men and women trade futures in neighborhoods that the plant has not grown yet, and sometimes, increasingly often, never does. Your shadow in this light does not always keep pace with you. It prefers the plant’s timetable. It may arrive at a corner a few seconds early and wait, flattened against the living wall, trying out postures. Travelers are advised not to follow a shadow that beckons, even their own. Especially their own.

Rain happens inside when the pod exhales — a warm-cool sigh that moves through the void, sets every bridge swaying in sequence, condenses on every cool surface, and becomes weather. Farmers inside keep no fields; they keep vertical gardens stapled to the sunless walls, beds of fungus and fern and blind white asparagus, pollinated by hand and by moth. The harvest festivals are held in total darkness so as not to shame the crops. Music at the festivals is played on instruments made from hollowed stems and stretched membranes, felt more than heard, so the whole town, for one night, beats in time, and the plant, confused or pleased, beats along, and doors all over the hollow open and close softly like things dreaming of applause.

It is still becoming. That is the last and least comfortable fact. At the seed-seam, geological processes that should take epochs are proceeding at the pace of gardening: minerals are crystallizing in the pulp as bright, soft stones that harden a little more each year, drainage channels are inventing themselves, the basin outside is learning, experimentally, to be a floodplain. Under the factory quarter there are chambers that were not there last decade, full of warm, unformed tissue and arrangements of matter that resemble, without quite yet being, staircases, hearts, libraries. Tunnels dug downward do not reach bedrock. They reach earlier versions of the town, less ripe, where the streets are the same streets but simpler, the houses embryonic, the citizens — if the reports are to be dealt with at all — translucent, industrious, and completely unsurprised to see you.

The pod will open someday. This is understood the way coastal people understand the sea. When it does, light will come in all at once, the gravity will choose a direction and keep it, the void will become merely a valley, the upside-down districts will fall out of the sky of their own world. What is being built here, in the damp and the dark, with engines and seeds and silence, may not be a town that is meant to survive that. It may be a town that is meant to be *ready*. Or it may be that the plant is not sheltering the town at all, and never was — that the plant is using the industry, the warmth, the carefully tended fungus, the circulated sap, the generations of bodies returned to the walls, to assemble something inside itself that needs a city the way a chrysalis needs organized ruin.

At night — which is to say at the plant’s night, which is most of the time — the bridges are retracted, the districts draw apart, and the hollow at the center is left to the moths and the owls and the soundless, falling, rising traffic between. Then the town is just points of foxfire in a green-black sphere, widely separated, humming faintly through the tissue that joins them. You can stand on your own small platform, in the cool, with condensation gathering in your hair, and feel through your feet the whole structure contract around its emptiness, thoughtfully, like a hand deciding whether to close.
