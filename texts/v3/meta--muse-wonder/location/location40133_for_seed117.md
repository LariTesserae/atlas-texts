It is below everything else.

You go down to it by an iron stair bolted to a retaining wall, and with each step the air gets cooler and wetter and the light goes out of the day. At the top there might be sun. Halfway down there is only a green-grey dimness, as if under deep water or under a clouded skylight, and your breath shows. The stair rails weep. The steps have been worn into shallow troughs.

At the bottom is a court — not large. You could throw a stone across it, if there were a dry place to stand and throw from. Sixty paces, perhaps, wall to wall. But it takes an hour to cross, because nothing here is empty.

It was a cooling yard, once. A low work. Four walls of blackened brick and poured concrete, sunk below the water table of the town above it, built to hold water where it could be pumped, filtered, warmed, and pumped again through the engines of a small power house. The power house is gone to a single leaning shed. The yard remains, and because it sits so low, it has never been able to dry.

Water is not in one place here. It is the place. It stands in a dozen pools separated by narrow, slick causeways of concrete and iron grating, each pool at a slightly different height than its neighbor, which should not be possible and yet is constant. It seeps from the walls in dark vertical stains. It condenses on the cold undersides of pipes and falls as a localized, perpetual rain over the north corner, though nowhere else. It beads on dial faces, runs inside cracked gauge glass, drips from cable conduits, gathers in the bowls of upturned valve wheels. Underfoot, even on the walkways, there is a film of it. The concrete sweats through its pores.

There are no plants. Not a leaf, not a blade forcing itself through the cracks. Whatever tries to arrive from above in seed or spore does not take in this cold, mineral wet. Instead the surfaces have grown other coatings, simpler and older in feeling: rust-red bacterial mats that ripple very slowly in the outflow channels, white fungal fans in the joints of the brickwork fine as frost, black slime on the submerged iron, and a pale, almost phosphorescent crust where salt and lime have leached and dried and leached again for decades. Everything that looks soft is mineral. Everything that looks like fur is corrosion eating outward in threads.

The forms are settled. This is not a ruin in the process of falling. The fallen fell long ago. A gantry lies across two pools exactly where it stopped, and has become a bridge and a reef. Pipework that burst has been rounded by water until its torn edges are smooth as river stone. Rust has not flaked so much as flowed, concreted with grit and shell into a new, lumpy stone that binds iron to concrete to brick. Edges are blunted. Thresholds are grooved by feet and by flow following the same line for longer than anyone was here to maintain it.

And the machines have not entirely stopped.

That is the strangeness you adjust to second, after the wet. Under the shed, under a tarpaulin long since rotted into the shape of what it covered, a low electric pump still turns over — not loud, a hum you feel in the soles of your shoes before you hear it. Somewhere behind the east wall another turns in counterpoint. A flywheel in the pit beneath the grating rotates at walking pace, driven by the return water itself, clicking past a loose tooth once per revolution. Indicator lamps still burn here and there at ankle and knee height, amber and dim, their light doubling in the pools. Now and then, with no hand near it, a solenoid clunks and a sluice changes its mind a quarter inch.

It is mostly quiet, because water takes sound and holds it down. The pumps do not roar. They murmur through walls. Dripping is the loudest ordinary thing: plinks at different pitches from different heights into different depths, a slow, unsynchronized music. Occasionally a metal skin ticks as it cools. Occasionally, from above the walls, the town arrives as a muffled, distant pressure, no words.

Into this density of pipes and pools and hanging cables, life has moved so thickly that the machines and the animals are now hard to separate.

It starts at the surface film: water-skaters stitching the still pools, black beetles rowing in circles, mosquito larvae hanging upside down in the warmer cistern by the pump where the water steams faintly in the cool. Then snails — thousands, small as thumbnails, larger as fists, their shells encrusted white — grazing the rust mats in slow herds and leaving clean, dark tracks behind them. Crabs sidle from under the grating with bottle caps and valve seals clamped over soft parts of themselves. One drags a short length of copper pipe. Another has made a home of a hollow ammeter casing, its wires trailing like whiskers.

Frogs live in the gauge housings. You look at a dial expecting numbers and find a pale eye looking back, a throat pulsing behind fogged glass. Newts as long as forearms, slate-grey and spotted faint orange, lie draped over the warm motor casings three and four deep, utterly still except for the slow fan of their external gills in the damp air, and when the pump cycles off and the casing cools they slide, one by one, without splash, into the nearest pool.

In the deeper channels, eels keep to the flow. Glassy when young, then dark and muscular, they have learned the timing of the sluices and gather before a gate opens, hanging in the current in a woven mass, and pour through with it in a single coordinated pulse to the next level. Below them, blind white shrimp pick through sediment made of rust flakes and shed skins and the fine black grit that sifts forever from above. Rats — wet, silver, neat-footed — use the pipe runs as highways above the water, stopping to wash their faces on junction boxes. A heron comes at dusk down through the open well of the yard, though how it spreads its wings between the cables seems impossible until it does it, and stands in absolute stillness in the rain-corner, where the falling condensation drives small things to the surface.

There are nests in insulators. There are eggs laid in neat spirals on submerged warning signs. There are skins, shells, husks, and bones tucked into every bracket and conduit bend, layered season over season until some walls bulge with the accumulation. The air just above the warm pool carries a faint reek of iron, brine, ozone, and animal musk, close and intimate as a burrow.

The place has rules, learned by everything that lives here.

Nothing hurries when the pumps rest. When the pitch beneath the floor rises a half tone, the skaters clear to the edges, the eels align, the rats climb one pipe higher, because thirty seconds later the levels will begin — almost imperceptibly — to change. One pool sinks two inches. Another brims and spills over a lip worn exactly for this. The flywheel finds a brisker pace. In the rain-corner the dripping thickens to a shower as warm vapor hits cold iron above it, condenses, falls. This is its weather, generated by its own small circulation, and it can turn three or four times in an hour: stillness, rising, spill, rest.

You do not walk straight. The gates decide for you. A grating unlocked at your arrival may be sighing shut on its counterweight by the time you return. A causeway dry enough to cross becomes a weir. The animals expect this and have made it their calendar; their feeding and mating and migrations happen in these miniature tides between one tank and the next. Even the snails time their grazing to the exposure of fresh mat.

Stand long enough and your clothes go damp through without any rain touching you. Your notebook curls. The cold enters at wrists and ankles. The dimness does not lift, only changes color — greyer in the morning filtering down, browner toward evening when the surviving lamps take over. Reflections become more real than the iron that makes them: lamps, rails, the slow flywheel, a frog's eye, all doubled and broken in black water.

Nothing here is wilderness, and nothing here is quite machinery anymore either. It is a small, low, crowded engine that forgot to stop and was adopted instead — pool by pool, pipe by pipe, warm casing by warm casing — until the distinction wore as smooth as the stair treads on your way down.

When you climb back up, dripping will follow you most of the way. Behind you the hum steadies, a gate settles, something small drops into water, and the yard resumes its levels without you.
