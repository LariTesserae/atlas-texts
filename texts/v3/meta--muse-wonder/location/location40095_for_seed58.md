You enter it by going down.

From the rim it looks unfinished, which it is. A pit about three hundred meters across and eighty deep, cut twelve years ago into black, glassy rock for a geothermal plant that outgrew its plans. The walls still carry the tooth-marks of the excavators — long, parallel scores, drill holes in regular grids, bolts not yet rusted dull. Nothing has had time to soften. Every ledge is sharp. Every fallen fragment down on the lowest streets is angular as broken crockery, and when the rock lets go, which it still does, it doesn't crumble. It cracks, clean and loud, like a rifle in another room.

They built the town in the pit because the plant needed tenders, and the tenders needed beds, and by the time the second boiler house went up there was nowhere flat left to put anything except on top of something else.

It is the size of a hamlet and the density of a city center. Fourteen levels, if you count the pipe galleries and the roof-warrens, stacked in iron and poured concrete against the pit walls. You can walk from one end to the other in nine minutes, but it might take you an hour to go from the bottom to the top by the permitted route: switchback stairs, freight lifts, ladders bolted through other people's kitchens, alleys the width of your shoulders that turn three times before they agree to be a street. Houses share walls, then share those walls with cisterns, transformer sheds, coolant manifolds. A main steam line, copper gone green-black in the heat, runs straight through the second floor of the laundry and out the bedroom window of the house behind it. No one considers this an inconvenience. It is how the laundry gets its heat, and how the bedroom gets its rent discount.

At the bottom, it is thirty-nine, forty-one, forty-four degrees, shifting with the plant's mood. At the rim you can sometimes feel a rumor of outside wind. Down here the air does not move unless something pushes it. It sits. It presses wet palms against the back of your neck.

It is a dry place that is never dry.

That is the pit's private weather, and it runs all day and all night. The boilers give off heat and a thin, mineral exhaust. The cooling sheds give off great soft breaths of used water. It all rises — slowly, because the town is in its way, snagging on awnings and walkways and washing lines — up to the shade-net and the cold-water loop strung under the rim, where the air is finally, briefly, cooler. There it condenses. It beads under pipes, under girders, under the mesh itself. It gathers and falls.

And halfway down, in the rising heat, it evaporates again.

On most afternoons you can stand on the middle terraces and watch rain that never arrives: a grey veil starting below the rim, thinning, fraying, gone before it reaches the hot roofs. The real water is caught where it is born, in the drip-zones, under overhangs and along the cold pipes. Those streets are always dark and stippled. People put out umbrellas there, not against the sky, but against a particular leaking joint. Buckets are chained to railings by address. The first drip after a cooling cycle is thrown away — it tastes of iron and machine oil. The second is for washing. The third, if you are patient and your pipe is clean, you can drink.

Everywhere else it is condensation in pockets. A cool cistern sweats into a hot alley and makes a sauna of three square meters. A doorway breathes damp. Shirts hung indoors never quite dry and never quite stay wet. Concrete sweats. Paper goes soft in the drawer. Salt, left out, becomes a stone.

The light is dim in a way that has nothing to do with night. At noon, for perhaps forty minutes, a hard white disk of direct sun crosses the pit floor and everything metal becomes untouchable, and everyone goes inside or under tin, and the whole town ticks and pings as it expands. The rest of the day the walls take the sun and give back only shade. Work light does the rest: sodium orange in the boiler yards, green indicator lamps along the galleries, a bluish glare from the welding bays that stutters through steam, strings of low household bulbs in amber glass. Your eyes adjust. Visitors don't. They keep ducking, expecting a ceiling.

For all the industry it is a quiet place. The engines are massive and enclosed, lagged in asbestos cloth and sheet iron, and they make less a roar than a pressure you feel in your teeth. The loudest ordinary sounds are small ones: dripping, the creak of a walkway taking weight, a crane chain taking strain link by link, somebody scraping moss out of a pipe joint with a spoon. People speak low, not from custom exactly, but because the heat makes raising your voice feel like work. At shift change there are bells, wrapped in felt, struck once. You hear everyone stop at the same time. That synchronized silence is the loudest thing in the pit.

There is no animal life. None.

No pigeons have colonized the rim. No rats ride the freight lift down in the crates, or if they do, they do not survive the week in the lower heat and whatever sweet chemical rides the plant steam. No flies around the food stalls, because there are no food stalls in the open — everything comes down sealed, tinned, dried, or frozen, and goes into cold boxes straight from the lift. A dog was brought down in the early years. The story ends the way everyone expects, and no one has tried twice. It gives the crowded streets a strange cleanliness and a strange emptiness. Four thousand people live in a space you could cross in minutes, shoulder to shoulder in the galleries at shift change, and nothing moves that is not a person or a machine. No wings cross the light. Nothing scurries when you lift a crate. The meat in the mess hall has never been anything you could picture walking.

What grows, grows in lines.

Where a cold pipe drips reliably there will be a trough, and in the trough a row of pale, stringy beans, leaves nearly white, pollinated by hand with a paintbrush that is passed household to household. In the expansion cracks on the shaded wall a flat, black-green fern spreads, heat-loving, feeling more like wet leather than a plant. In the pipe joints, a bright moss, harvested for a bitter tea and for poultices. That is the vegetation of the pit: an agriculture of leaks. Gardens are not planted in soil — there is almost no soil, only rock dust and imported sacks guarded like flour — they are planted in plumbing. Move a pipe, and a neighborhood's crop dies. Reroute a cooling line, and a new green seam appears on a wall three levels down, and within a month someone has bolted a planter under it and claimed it.

It is always in motion, this small hot town. Not with weather from outside, but with its own cycles, as regular and as layered as tides. Cooling cycle: condensation, drip, collection, the middle terraces shining. Venting cycle: a sigh from the deep shafts that makes every loose sheet of tin on the lower levels lift and settle, once, in sequence, like a wave. Building cycle: a container house coming down on the crane at 3 a.m., a family moving their partition walls six inches to annex a dead air shaft, a new gallery being riveted out over an alley that had been somebody's only source of daylight — they will be compensated in water rights. Sleeping cycle: half the town works while the other half sleeps behind wet curtains, and then they trade, so the stairs never empty and the kitchens never fully cool, and there is always, at any hour, the smell of somebody's breakfast.

The rules down here are not written. They are temperatures.

You don't leave tools where the noon disk will find them. You don't sleep on the lowest floor in venting week if you can trade up. You don't fight over shade; shade is surveyed, deeded, inherited, fought over in court up at the rim, but never simply taken. You learn which handrails are decorative and which are pipes. You learn to tell, by the taste of your roof water, whether the north boiler is running lean. You learn to walk the drip-streets without looking up, and the dry streets without hurrying, because hurrying builds heat in the body faster than the body in this air can shed it.

At night — real night, up above — the pit glows faintly from below, orange and green, its little weather still turning over in the dark. The condensation forms a little faster. The rain that never lands falls a little further before it vanishes. On the middle terraces, in the coolest hour, people sit out on their iron balconies among the bean troughs and listen to the water bead and gather over their heads, and to the plant breathing under their feet, and for a while the heat is almost bearable, and the place is almost what it might have meant to be: a cupped hand of a town, held down in the young rock, keeping itself alive on its own recycled rain.
