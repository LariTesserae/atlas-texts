It begins under a salt field, where there is nothing to mark it.

Above is white, flat, blinding — a pan of dry mineral cracked into plates, hot enough at midday to hurt your eyes through closed lids. Below one of those plates, where the crust has buckled up a finger's width, is a seam that breathes out hot air. If you lay your cheek near it you will hear it before you find it with your hands: a roar, far away and too large for the crack it comes through.

The way down is not a tunnel so much as an interruption. You turn sideways, you drop your legs into warmth, you let the sharp edge of the salt take a little skin. Four meters down the walls widen and your feet find floor. Then you can stand, just barely, and understand that the place is a room.

It is perhaps twelve meters across, no more. A single pocket hollowed below the surface, dome-low in the middle, lower still at the edges where the rock folds down to meet coarse, black sand. It is hidden the way a tooth's hollow is hidden — not large, not meant to be entered, entirely separate from the desert that seals it. Once you are inside, the white world above stops existing. There is no shaft of sun. There never has been.

The light here does not change because it does not come from the sky.

Along the far wall the floor splits in a fissure the length of a sleeping person, and in that fissure the rock holds a permanent, dull heat — not flame, not lava, a cherry-red seam sunk deep enough that you only see its reflection on the wet-looking stone above it. It paints everything amber and then brown and then, at the edges, black. Crystals have grown fresh around it, young formations no water has ever softened: clear needles, sulfur-yellow fans, knife-thin blades of something transparent that rings if your sleeve brushes it. Their edges are still perfect. Nothing here has had time, or weather, to erode.

And there is no water. None. No drip, no seep, no dark stain of damp, no dew in the coolest corner because there is no coolest corner. It is warm all the way through, evenly, oppressively warm — the sort of warmth that lives in your lungs and makes the air taste baked. The fissure vents a dry gas, day and night in a place that has neither, at exactly the same pressure. It finds its way up through a cluster of hollow stone tubes and those crystal blades, and it makes the sound.

The sound is the real size of the place. The room is small and the sound is enormous.

It is not a howl that rises and falls. It never gusts, never falters, never finds a new note. It is one continuous chord, struck before anyone was here to hear it and held: a low roaring in the chest under a high, pure whistling that goes on and on in the teeth. Speaking is useless unless you put your mouth almost against another person's ear and shout in short bursts. Most communication here, for everything that lives here, gave up on air a long time ago.

Because — impossibly, in this dry heat, in this dimness — the far wall is lush.

It grows toward the sound, not the light.

A garden occupies the curve of rock beneath the crystal fans, sparse in placement and yet dense within itself, plant pressed to plant in a single hanging thicket. The rest of the floor is bare. Nothing grows in the middle. That emptiness is part of it; the room is mostly hot air and black sand and echo, and then this.

The plants have no sap. Cut a leaf — people have, the old cuts are still here, sealed like paper tears — and it does not bleed or weep. The leaves are dry to the touch, waxy, curled like shavings of horn, red and ochre and a dusty bottle-green. Some are broad hearing-fronds, stiff as fans, all angled directly into the vent's blast and trembling minutely with it, twenty-four hours a day without rest. Some are feather-ferns made of dry scales that rattle together in the updraft, adding a soft, incessant shushing to the roar. Some are swollen, segmented fingers that store something heavy inside themselves but are not succulent the way a cactus is succulent; squeeze one and it creaks instead of yielding. All the water here, if you can call it that, is locked. It moves only from body to body. It is never free, never rain, never a puddle. The plants crack it, molecule by molecule, out of the vent gas and out of the bodies of the dead, and they hold it in sealed tissue and do not transpire it back. Their roots do not seek soil moisture. They seek heat, and mineral, and the vibration in the rock. Where a root meets a crystal it will split and grow around the blade, and the blade will grow through the leaf in turn, so plant and stone hold each other still.

Smells collect because nothing washes them away: hot resin, crushed bitter herbs, warm stone dust, a faint sulfurous sweetness under everything, like a struck match that never finishes burning.

The fauna has learned to live inside the noise.

There are beetles, abundant in the thicket, smaller than a thumbnail and almost transparent, who travel the stems in lines and drum with their hind legs so you feel their messages as a faint ticking in the wood rather than hear them. There are pale, blind lizards the length of a hand who bask flattened on the warm floor near — never too near — the fissure, their throats pulsing in time with the chord, not out of distress but as a kind of tuning. There is a colony of dry moths who do nothing but hang in the rising hot current below the ceiling, wings out, never landing, carried and recarried in the same circle of air; their bodies are furred like seeds, and when one dies it does not fall, quite. The current is that steady. It desiccates in place, lightens, keeps turning for a while among the living until a wing tears and it snags in the fern and is slowly rooted through.

Everything eats everything else in a loop so tight it produces no waste anyone can find. The lizards take beetles. The beetles strip the dead moths and the old fern scales. The ferns root into the desiccated shells left in their own canopy. The droppings dry instantly to a fine, odorless powder that sifts down and is taken up again. Nothing rots. In the heat and aridity, corpses simply become thinner and more architectural versions of themselves, and the garden is full of these earlier tenants holding up the new growth.

They have no cycles here. The moths do not know night to emerge into. The lizards do not brumate. A frond uncurls to a certain size and then, instead of aging, simply persists at that size, trembling, for longer than anyone has watched. If this place has seasons, they are longer than animal memory.

And someone, at some point, found it and stayed a while — or several someones, spaced very far apart, because nothing they left has decayed either.

Near the emptiest wall are the built things, sparse, small, and low. A half-circle of flat stones, knee-high, not a fire pit — no fire could be heard or needed — but a listening wall, shaped to concentrate vibration into the small hollow behind it where a person could sit with their spine against the curve and feel the chord separately in each vertebra. A rack of lashed sticks still holds three hollow gourds grown dry and hard as wood, each bored with a different mouth; hung in the current they should have sounded, but the vent already occupies every note they would make, so they only shiver silently, their strings of beetle-shell beads clicking against them with a touch-language. In a niche: a flaked stone knife, edge still wicked, a woven basket of fern fiber containing nothing but a few sorted seeds as smooth as beads, a tally stick notched in groups of seven that stops mid-pattern.

The tools are pre-metal, hand-shaped, patient. Whoever made them understood the rule of the room intuitively: do not bring anything that rusts, rots, or needs washing. Do not bring anything that expects change. Sharpen stone on stone. Tie fiber with fiber. Sit with your back against the listening wall if you want to think, because your thoughts will have to arrive through your bones here.

Your own presence feels temporary in a way that is not frightening, just factual. Your sweat is the only free water to enter this place in perhaps a very long time, and it evaporates before it can darken your shirt, leaving you saltier and curiously un-thirsty in the thick, resinous air, as if the room discourages the idea. Your ears will ring for hours after the first minute, and then they will learn the trick the beetles know: you stop trying to hear *over* the sound and start feeling for the small movements laid on top of it. The brush of fern on fern, through your ankles when you stand near the garden. The lizard shifting its weight, through the floor. The tally-stick stillness.

Nothing announces itself. Nothing resolves.

Even now the gas keeps coming up through the stone pipes at its one unwavering pressure. The red seam keeps throwing the same dim amber onto the same crystal blades, which have not dulled, which will not dull. A moth keeps its circle. A fern keeps its angle into the blast. The chord holds, loud enough to erase the memory of silence, stable enough that after a while you understand stability not as peace but as a kind of pressure — a world that finished becoming itself early, sealed itself under a salt pan, and never needed to do anything again.

You could leave a basket here and come back in a hundred years and find it exactly as dry, exactly as patient, waiting in the warm dark under the desert, still listening to the same note.
