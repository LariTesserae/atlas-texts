It is above the clouds, and the light arrives before the warmth ever does.

You come up the last of the path on your hands in places — a stair cut into white rock, worn bowl-smooth by centuries of wet palms and wet feet — and step over the lip into a cirque no more than two hundred paces across. The whole world narrows. Sheer walls rise on three sides, black and amber and bone-white, streaked, weeping. Below you, nothing but a slow sea of cloud filling the valley to the brim. Above you, a sky so dark a blue at noon that the sun in it looks hard and small, like a hole punched in ice.

And in the bowl, impossibly, a town.

It is packed shoulder to shoulder into the curve of the cirque, tier upon tier, following the natural stairs of the place. The rock here is travertine, laid down by the springs over thousands of years — terrace upon terrace of shallow, rimmed pools descending from the cliff foot to a central basin, each lip rounded, each basin filmed with water. The town has not so much been built on the terraces as inserted into them. Houses of unmortared stone grow straight out of the pool-rims. A roof is the floor of the house above it. A street is a dry channel between two wet ones. Bridges of lashed poles cross alleys that are also aqueducts. Rope ladders, basket hoists, sluice gates, cisterns roofed with slate and horn — everything touches everything else. There is no waste ground. If there is a flat space the size of a table, something is drying on it, sleeping on it, or being born on it.

It is freezing in the shade and blinding in the sun, at the same time, on the same wall. The north-facing stone never thaws; it carries beards of clear ice year-round, blue in the core. Six feet away, sun-struck wet limestone steams faintly and hurts to look at. Every surface is white with mineral or black with wet or green-black with the thin algae that blooms and dies in a single afternoon. Your breath condenses. Your shadow is knife-edged. Inhabitants wear slit goggles of woven reed and polished bone, and their outer clothes are stiff, pale, crusted, because everything here slowly turns to stone.

The water is the reason, and the water will not sit still.

A dozen springs breathe out of the cliff at different heights, some no thicker than a finger, some wide as a doorway, all of them faintly warm at the source and skinning with ice a yard from where they emerge. The water carries dissolved rock in it. Where it runs, it builds — a milky film on a channel bed in the morning is a soft white ridge by evening, a hard dam in a week. Where it stands in sunlight, it lays down amber sheets. Where it drips inside a house, and it drips inside every house, it grows downward in slow translucent teeth from the ceiling and upward in blunt cones from the floor.

So the town is in a state of continuous, frantic, entirely silent unmaking and remaking.

At dawn the pools are sealed in ice patterned like ferns. By mid-morning the sun hits the eastern wall and reflects — wall to pool to white roof to pool again — until the whole bowl is lit from three directions and the ice begins to tick and split, though you have to put your ear almost to it to hear. Then comes the melt-pulse. It is different every day. A dam of night-ice gives way high up, a channel choked with fresh travertine shunts a spring sideways, a warm pool overflows its grown lip. Water finds a new way down through the town, and the town has perhaps an hour's warning, given in hand-signals.

Then everyone moves at once.

People pour from doorways that are already visibly narrower than they were last season. Gates of woven reed are hauled up, others dropped. Children carry armloads of hot ash from the dung-fires to throw on a freezing sluice. A man hangs from a rope under his own eaves, chipping with an antler pick at the stone his house grew in the night, chipping *underwater* where he can, because that is quieter. Baskets pass hand to hand. A roof is re-laid. A family's cooking pool is drained, scraped of its white rind, and refilled before the new current can steal it. Flags of undyed wool snap from poles — *this channel, not this one; close the lower cistern; open your grandfather's stair* — because no one shouts here. No one, in the open, even speaks above a murmur.

Above the town, balanced on the sunlit rim of the cirque, lies a great cornice of snow and spring-ice, beautiful, translucent, and metastable. The old rule predates any living elder: sound flies up the bowl and touches it. So there are no bells. No dogs that bark — there are dogs, small thick-coated ones the color of wet ash, trained to nudge and paw but never bark. Tools are wrapped in felt at the head. Even the constant, necessary chisel-work is done muffled, or wet. The loudest things here are drips, trickles, the soft slap of a reed gate settling, breath. Under all that activity, an immense quiet, as if the town were underwater.

And it is crowded with life far beyond its people.

Nothing much grows — that is the strange poverty under all this water and light. No tree reaches above a knee. There are cushion plants in the dry cracks, blue-green and tight as fists, flowering for three days a year. Lichen makes orange maps on the roofs. Moss survives only where a spring keeps it permanently wet and permanently in shade. The rest of the greenness is in the water itself: algae that wakes when the light strikes a pool, clouds to emerald soup in an hour, and blackens and sinks before freeze.

But everything *arrives*, or lives in the walls.

Cliff swifts nest by the thousand in the eaves, in the warm updraft of the chimneys, in holes deliberately left unchipped in the travertine. In the cold they sit torpid and silent, packed beak-to-tail like gray fruit. When the melt-pulse warms the air they come out all at once, not calling, just the soft tearing of wings, a river of birds following the new river of water downhill, skimming insects off its surface. Ibex walk the roofs as if the roofs were the natural continuation of the cliff, which, increasingly, they are; they lick mineral from the chimneys and sleep against the warmest walls. Marmots live under the floors and are tolerated because their burrowing keeps certain drains open. Pale salamanders, spotted gold, drift in the household cisterns and are never eaten — a house with salamanders has sweet water. Frogs no bigger than a thumbnail crowd the sunlit pool margins in their hundreds after a thaw-bloom of flies, throats pulsing, too cold or too high or too cautious under the cornice to sing.

The fuel is dung, dried in white-crusted cakes on every south roof, because there is almost no wood. The food is dried meat from below, cheese, bitter greens from the cracks, and the sudden feasts — insect, algae-fed fish fry no longer than a finger, swift eggs — that appear when a pool warms fast enough. The economy of the place is boom and freeze, measured in hours.

Inside, the houses are low, dark after the glare, and dripping. Condensation runs down the inner walls in clear beads and is caught in horn cups along the skirting. A small dung-fire burns in a stone hood, more for drying than heating; most warmth comes from bodies, animal and human, and from the spring-water piped in hollowed poles under the floor before it can freeze. Beds are stone benches softened with felt. Every interior smells of wet wool, mineral water, smoke, and the musk of the animals stabled behind a curtain in the same room. Stalactites have to be knocked gently from above the sleeping place, or they will, over a single winter, reach down and join the floor and divide the room in two. Some old houses have done exactly that and simply put a door in the stone curtain.

By late afternoon it is already turning. The sun drops behind the western wall in minutes, though the sky above stays hard and bright for hours. The pools stop steaming and begin, visibly, to skin. Mist that has been pouring in slow motion over the northern lip all day — a cloud trying to fall uphill into the sun and evaporating as it comes — thickens and settles. The swifts return to their holes in one last silent pour. New ice laces the channels. Somewhere high up a spring, pinched by freezing, finds a crack in the cliff and begins weeping through the back wall of a kitchen that was dry at breakfast. The family moves the grain, hangs a cup, signs to a neighbor. Tomorrow they will chip out a channel for it or chip out a new kitchen.

Nothing here is finished, and nothing here is new. The travertine terraces were old when the first house was wedged into them. The town holds its shape not by staying the same but by being repaired at exactly the speed at which water and cold erase it — deposition against the pick, ice against ash, the next flood against a hundred silent hands.

At night, when the cloud-sea below glows faintly with moonlight and the bowl is lit blue by ice and starlight, you can lie on a roof that is someone's ceiling and hear, if you listen with your whole body, the place making itself: drip, tick, trickle, the smallest shift of stone growing.

Not a sound rises to the cornice.
