The first thing is the light, and the second is that it makes no sound.

It is high, above most weather, in a valley that has not yet learned how to soften itself. The rim is a broken circle of black and pale-grey rock, uplifted too recently for erosion to have taken the edges off. Every ridge ends in a knife, every slope sheds long fans of scree that still show the bright fracture-face where they split. Between the rocks there is almost no soil, only ash-fine grit caught in cracks, and yet the whole bowl is green — improbably, densely, almost obscenely green for a place with no river.

You can see the entire valley at once from the rim: perhaps eight kilometres across, tilted toward a flat white floor at its centre. But seeing it does not make it feel open. It is crowded at every level.

The sun up here has brilliance without warmth. The sky is a deep, violet blue, nearly black at the zenith even at noon, and the light comes down hard and shadowless-white on exposed stone, so sharp that edges seem to cut out second, blacker shadows beneath them. Your skin feels the glare but not heat. In shade — under a leaf, under a machine housing — the air is finger-numbing cold. Breath goes thin and a little metallic. Everything lit looks overexposed; everything shaded looks drowned.

And it drinks the sound out of things.

The valley rock is young, vesicular, full of un-collapsed bubbles and hollows. The vegetation smothers what the rock does not. Ferns and moss and low, tight cushion-plants pack into every surface, layered three and four deep, leaves over leaves, fronds threading through fronds. A voice spoken at normal volume falls dead within a few metres, as if into felt. Your own footsteps make no report, only a faint compression in wet moss. There are no birds to lose in it anyway. Now and then a dark moth works silently between flowers, or a single pallid lizard is pressed flat to a sun-warmed cable conduit, but weeks could pass, it feels, between animal sightings. What tracks exist are old and softened at the edges, filled with water.

There is little water, and it is everywhere.

No streams cross the valley floor. No lake gathers. Instead, moisture arrives as condensation, as a night exhalation. Even on the brightest days, the undersides remain damp: water beading under leaves, darkening stone in pockets, collecting in the cups formed where broad, waxy leaves fuse at the base. The plants are built for hoarding. Their leaves are thick, blue-green, furred at the margins to comb fog from passing air. Root mats hold last week's dew like sponge. In hollow rocks, after a cold night, you find a mouthful of perfectly clear water, already going by noon.

That is why the green is densest around the machines.

They are industrial in scale but they do not assemble into a town, or even a proper factory. They occupy the valley the way the plants do: in systems, repetitions, networks strung across slope and floor, with only scattered shelters between them — low, windowless maintenance huts, half-buried in fern; a switching cabin; a store shed with its door grown shut by vines. The main work is done by arrays.

Rows and rows of condensation vanes rise through the vegetation, tall thin fins of brushed alloy, always beaded, always dripping despite the dry wind. Mirror fields tilt almost imperceptibly on their mounts, following the sun at a speed too slow to watch. Solar furnaces at the valley's centre focus that merciless light into sealed white chambers where nothing can be seen to happen, though the air above them stands in rigid, heatless-looking columns. Cable runs on short pylons from array to array, thick as a wrist, humming with charge you feel in your teeth rather than hear. Flywheels turn in open pits, enormous, magnetic-bearinged, perfectly silent, completing one rotation in the time it takes to look away and wonder if they moved at all.

Everything engineered here has been engineered for quiet. No exhaust, no clank, no venting steam. Motors are sheathed. Joints are fluid. Even the service crawlers that creep once a day along the pylon lines on rubberised tracks make less noise than a hand brushed over moss. Whether this is because loud sound would shatter the young, hollow rock, or simply because the people who built it understood that sound would not carry its warning far enough to be useful, is not obvious. The effect is devotional. A vast production with the volume turned off.

And production is clearly what it is. Condensate runs in transparent pipes, a slow pulse, downhill — though sometimes, watching a droplet inside an inclined section near the vanes, you would swear it hesitates, gathers itself, and climbs a few centimetres toward the coldest metal. Purified air hisses invisibly into storage bladders that swell across entire afternoons. The furnace chambers, opened on a cycle measured in days, yield pale ingots that crawler-haulers remove one at a time. It should feel busy. Instead its tempo is geological. The mirrors complete their arc. The bladders breathe out again at night. The flywheels store the noon glare and spend it, gradually, through the frozen dark.

The plants have learned the schedule better than any animal. The lushest growth is not in the sheltered gullies but pressed right up against the condensation vanes, where leakage makes a permanent damp halo. Vines have found the warm side of cable conduits. Mosses colonise the shaded north face of every pylon in identical green collars. Some fern species grow only inside the mirror fields, their fronds perforated in patterns that let concentrated light pass without burning — black spores dusting the mirror glass, cleaned away each morning by a silent wiper arm, collected, perhaps deliberately, in trays. It is hard to tell any more where cultivation ends and opportunism begins. The installation harvests water from the air for the plants to steal. The plants shade and cool the vanes, improving their yield. Roots keep the scree from sliding over the pipes. Mirrors keep frost off the leaf beds below them.

In the scattered huts, when you find one unlocked, there are signs of intermittent human presence but not residence: a sealed suit hanging like a shed skin, a logbook whose entries are dated weeks apart in the same hand, cups magnetised to a wall, a bed made up with hospital precision under a film of green dust. Someone comes up here, tends, leaves. The valley runs itself in their absence, at its own pace.

Toward evening — though evening is only a thickening of the blue, the light staying bright until it abruptly does not — the temperature drops below the dew point all at once. Then, for an hour, the place is briefly, secretly wet. Every vane, leaf, rock point and cable sheds droplets. Pocket pools brim. The moss releases its stored, earthy smell, cut with ozone and the faint hot-metal scent of the furnaces cooling. Condensation ticks and drips in the undergrowth, the only sounds the valley permits itself, each one near, private, swallowed immediately.

Then the cold seals it again. Frost feathers over the pools without cracking them. The flywheels turn on in the darkness. And overhead, with no cloud to catch it, the last light leaves the sharp young peaks in an instant, as if switched off along with everything else.
