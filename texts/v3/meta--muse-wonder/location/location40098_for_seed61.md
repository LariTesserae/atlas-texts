You hear it before you find it.

Not through open air — there is no open air here — but through stone. A low, continuous roar in the soles of your feet, in the wet rungs of the ladder going down. Thirty feet below the street, behind a cistern wall that looks like every other cistern wall, the sound suddenly gets a room to live in, and it becomes enormous.

The place is one chamber. That is the first lie it tells you, and then corrects.

It is perhaps nine paces long and seven wide, a vault of old rock and older brick hollowed out below the surface, and it is a town. Not a camp in a cave, not a hideout. A town, complete and formal, with a main street, addresses, a market, a mayor, a jail, a shrine, property disputes, and washing lines — all of it fitted inside this single wet room.

The main street is a line of planks down the middle, laid over the drain. The drain is the reason for everything. A black channel of slow water, finger-deep, iron-smelling, cuts the floor from the north wall to the south and vanishes through a grate no wider than a hand. Above the planks people step in soft, practiced places. The stone flags on either side are dished by generations of feet, slick as river rock, edged with salt-white mineral crust where the damp dries and dries and never quite dries.

For the walls do not stop weeping. Cool water beads on the vault, gathers in the mortar lines, slides down the brick in bright threads. In one corner it falls steadily enough to be called rain — a personal, indoor rain, always in the same square foot, filling a hammered copper basin with a clang that serves as a clock. Everywhere else is condensation: on timber, on rope, on faces. Your clothes are damp within minutes. Your breath does not quite fog. It is always the same cool down here, cellar-cool, stone-cool, indifferent to summer or winter above.

And there is nothing green. Nothing has ever grown here. No moss softens the wet, no root has found a crack. The only living film is simpler than that — a rust-orange bacterial scum in the drain, trailing fine hairs in the current, and a pale, almost mineral slime on the stones under the rain-basin that the townspeople scrape away with wooden paddles and throw, without ceremony, back into the water. The rest is rock learning to be architecture and architecture learning to be rock: calcite has sheeted over the lower bricks in waxy curtains, stalactite teeth have been worn blunt where heads pass, old carved steps have flowed together with new ones until you cannot say where the cave ended and the building began.

They built upward because they could not build out.

Three, in places five tiers of habitation climb the walls: sleeping shelves, storage lofts, work platforms, whole household corners partitioned by hanging blankets, salvaged doors, woven reed mats stiff with damp. Ladders and knotted ropes and a narrow stair of pegs connect them. A family may live entirely on a ledge the size of a table — cook-pot, bedding roll, tool chest, a grandmother — with its front step overhanging the hat stall below, which overhangs the street. Lanterns hang at every level, small oil flames in tin and clay, dim and amber and doubled in every wet surface. The light never really changes. They trim the wicks to an agreed height. Day is a bell rung from above. Night is a different bell. The flames stay the same.

Forty-one people live here at last count, which is also the count before, and the count before that. That is a rule, and it is not spoken of as cruelty. One birth, one departure. One death, one arrival down the ladder with a bundle. The chamber can hold this much breath, this much cooking smoke, this much noise, and no more — and so it holds exactly this much, year after year, in a balance that feels less like a law than like the water level in the drain: never rising, never falling.

It is loud in a way that rearranges how people are.

The roar is partly the rain-basin, partly the drain, but mostly something behind the north wall — a buried aqueduct, a diverted stream, a great weight of water moving forever through dark stone a forearm's thickness away. It fills the vault and reflects off it. Conversation happens at a shout, inches apart. Market haggling is a kind of cheerful bellowing. Lullabies are sung fortissimo into infants' ears, and the infants sleep, eventually, like millers. Whispering is impossible, and therefore suspect; a person trying to be quiet is assumed to be ill or plotting. Important things are announced. Marriages, debts, insults, apologies, the price of lamp oil — all declared up at the basin so the copper carries it. The town's privacy is not silence. It is the practiced deafness of neighbors pretending the roar took your words.

Mixed into it: the squeal of the bucket-pulley from the shaft, hand-looms clacking on the second tier where imported fiber is worked into rope and sacking, a smith — smith is too grand; a man with a hammer, an anvil stone, and simple iron and copper to mend — tapping out a rhythm he must feel in his teeth, kettles lid-dancing, somebody always sweeping water back toward the drain with a twig broom. Sound here is not background. It is another kind of density, one more thing nearly touching you.

Their technology is hands and patience. Wooden gears, stone weights, clay lamps, rope, leather buckets, knives and chisels of simple metal kept bright against the rust by constant use and constant oiling. Food comes down dried, salted, hard: grain, beans, fish, oil, fuel, timber. Nothing is wasted because nothing can be replaced quickly. A broken plank is a bridge, then a shelf, then a crutch, then firewood. Cooking is done on one small brazier near a flue-crack that draws, more or less, and the whole town smells permanently of that smoke — woodsmoke and lamp oil and wet wool and iron water and close human bodies and, underneath, the clean, cold smell of stone that has been wet for a hundred years.

Fauna is an event. A bat finds the shaft perhaps twice a year and spends a frantic night stitching the lantern light, sacred and terrifying to children who have only heard it described, until it is caught in a sack and released upward. Cave crickets, blind and colorless, live singly in the far corners and are left alone. And in the drain, in the deepest stretch under the planks, lives a single pale eel, longer than belief allows, that no one has ever seen entirely. A snout at one grate. A tail, weeks later, at another. It is fed crumbs. It is consulted. It may be forty years old. It may, the old people say with absolute certainty and no evidence, be the same eel that was here when the first shelf was built, which would make it the only citizen exempt from the count.

Nothing down here is rushing to become anything else. The rain falls in its corner. The drain does its slow work. The aqueduct roars behind its wall. The shelves are repaired in the same places with the same knots, the street planks replaced one at a time so the street is always old and always new in exactly the same pattern. Erosion and maintenance have reached an agreement. Even the arguments repeat — this family has disputed that family's dripping washing since before either family can remember, and the dispute is now a kind of load-bearing structure.

Leave by the ladder and the sound does something strange: it does not fade gradually. At a certain rung, where the shaft bends, it simply stops, cut off by stone, and the ordinary quiet of the upper world feels thin and exposed, as if you have surfaced from deep water into a place with no weather at all.

Below the bend, the town goes on at full volume — lamps lit, basin clanging, forty-one voices raised against the water — in its one hidden room, unchanged, at the bottom of the world it needs.
