There is a place you enter by going down.

Outside, its roof is barely a rise in the low ground — a broad, shallow dome of fired clay tiles and blackened chestnut, no higher than a haystack, slick in the cool air. Paths of beaten earth and split plank all slope toward it. There is no ground inside it that was not made by hands. Not a patch. The floor, the walls, the ceiling, the wet below the floor, even the air's way in and out, was placed there.

Push the low door — wood swollen tight in its frame, bound with rope, no lock — and the scale resolves and then unresolves. It is only one room. Forty paces wall to wall, maybe twelve men high at the center. You could cross it in half a minute. And yet it feels cathedral-large because it is almost perfectly empty.

Nothing stands in the middle. No furniture, no storage, no altar, no fallen debris permitted to stay. Just a vast column of dim, cool air, and around the far perimeter, where the curved wall meets the suspended floor, the life presses itself flat and dense. The emptiness is not absence. It is the main construction.

It is wet in a way that has nothing to do with rain that day.

Water moves everywhere at shin-height and below sight. Under the plank floor — wide boards laid over a vaulted undercroft, gaps left deliberately between them — a dark cistern breathes and shifts. Wooden sluices, hand-adzed, pegged not nailed, open and close on a slow tidal logic worked by counterweights of stone on nettle-rope. Cool water sighs in, spreads through clay channels, sighs out again toward the low fields. Condensation forms on the dome even when the sky outside is clear, beads on lime plaster, gathers, and falls.

It rains upward in effect, if not in fact: the floor sweats, the timbers weep at their joints, the tiles sing with drips. Your clothes are damp in ten minutes. Your breath shows. The stone steps at the door are hollowed into smooth bowls by centuries of wet feet, and the rope handrail has cut a polished groove clear into the doorpost. Nothing here is new, and nothing is ruined. Wood is replaced plank for identical plank, clay is re-daubed with flat paddles, a cracked tile is swapped in the same uneven red-brown firing as all the others. It is settled into its own weather.

The light is kept starved.

Three slits high in the dome, oriented with obscene care, and one oculus stopped down by a hanging wooden shutter that knocks and turns. For most of the day the interior is a grey-green dimness, the color of water seen through reeds. When a shaft does fall, it falls as a solid thing, full of dust and insect wings, and lands in a bright coin on the wet boards. The rest stays shadow: not black, but deep enough that the wall curves away before you can see where it ends.

And in that dim, the sound is enormous.

This is the purpose and the accident of the place. The dome is a resonator. The undercroft is a second, slower resonator under it. Every drip from the crown hits a different tuned hollow in the floor — some clay pots sunk mouth-up, some mere pockets in the vaulting — and returns as a note, a knock, a soft wooden boom. The sluices groan. The shutter claps. Water forced at pressure through a set of primitive reed valves along the north wall makes a low, unsteady chord that rises when the cistern is full and frays apart as it empties.

Over that, loudest of all, the animals.

They are abundant here in a way the empty air denies at first glance. Look at the edges. The inner wall is honeycombed near the top with nesting holes, each one shaped by a thumb in wet clay before firing, made for house swifts that come screaming in through the light slits at dusk and vanish into the curve. Below them, in the perpetual wet dark behind the wainscot of vertical boards, bats cluster so thick the wood seems furred. They leave at evening not in a stream but in a detonation, and for a minute the empty center is a vortex of wings loud as applause.

At floor level, in the channels between the planks, frogs. Hundreds. Pale, almost eyeless things that have lived generations in this made marsh, and larger green marsh frogs that find their way in through the sluice mouths and decide to stay. Their chorus, doubled and redoubled by the dome, is not background. It is physical. It presses in the ears. It makes the hanging shutter tremble sympathetically. When they are at full pitch you cannot hear a companion shout from ten paces away, but a whisper placed directly against the wall will travel the curve and arrive clear at the opposite side, late, as if someone else said it after thinking.

There is a food web turning entirely within the walls. Midges and craneflies hatch from the cistern scum and rise to the light coins. Frogs take them at the plank gaps with a wet smacking. The swifts take what escapes upward. Freshwater mussels, set in rows like cobbles in the intake channel by some patient ancestor, filter the incoming water and are themselves hosted by bitterling fish no longer than a thumb. An owl — there is always, it seems, exactly one owl — lives in the dead space above the shutter housing and drops pellets of swift-bone and frog-bone onto a single, traditional spot by the east wall. It is the only litter allowed to accumulate, and even that is cleared at the turning of the year with wooden spades.

Vegetation barely gets a foothold. Algae makes a black-green varnish in the runnels. One fern, inexplicable and therefore protected, pushes from a crack behind the main sluice wheel. A few reed baskets hang just inside the door slits, planted not for harvest but to tell the keepers by their leaf-curl whether the air inside has gone sour. Otherwise, the builders were ruthless: anything that rooted in the middle was pulled, because rooted things hold objects, and objects hold echoes wrong.

That is the first rule, learned with the hands more than the ears: keep the center clear.

If you set a crate there, set a bench, stand too long yourself in a group talking, the whole system falters. The condensation stops forming evenly and instead runs in cold lines. The frogs shift their pitch down, then stop in patches, and in the ugly, sudden quiet you can hear how loud the water under the floor truly is, knocking to get up. Old keepers say the building was made to listen to the low land around it — to gather its water, yes, and filter it, and store it against summer, but also to gather its sound: storm pressure, flood coming a day before it arrives as a subsonic boom in the undercroft, the mass movement of birds overhead as a shiver in the shutter. A filled room hears only itself.

The second rule follows: do not answer the frogs in your full voice. Pitch up, or speak in the whisper the wall prefers. If a human chorus competes, the frogs will match it, louder and louder, driven by the dome's feedback, until the bats drop from the walls in confusion and the water in the open inspection pit begins, visibly, to jump. It has happened. The boards remember it in a tide-line of silt halfway up the central supporting post — itself the only thing allowed to occupy the middle, a single black trunk rising from the cistern to the oculus, polished by hands that check, each visit, without thinking, whether it is still cold.

It is always cold.

Stay long enough and the dynamic of it enters your own timing. Fill and empty. Day dimming to a dimmer night in which the frog note separates into individuals, each one a throat, a pause, a splash. Timber contracting with a shot-like crack in the cool. A sluice pin, sharpened by hand to a wooden point, lifting on its rope and letting a new tongue of water chatter across the mussel rows. The building does not keep a steady state. It cycles, visibly, audibly, on periods of minutes and hours and seasons, and everything living in it has learned to feed and sing and sleep in the gaps between surges.

You leave by climbing again, pushing the swollen door out into air that feels, by comparison, thin and dry and strangely quiet. Behind and below you the dome goes on without you, as it has for longer than the village above it has used its present name: a small, total, man-made hollow in the lowest place, empty at its heart, wet at its skin, and loud with other lives making use of the echo.
