From above, there is almost nothing to see.

A low plain, cool and wind-planed, extends to a flat horizon the color of old bone. There is no soil in any ordinary sense. What looks at a distance like desert pavement, or a salt pan, or a field of sun-bleached stone, resolves underfoot into wood.

It is one surface. A single, immense, flattened organism that stopped growing up a very long time ago and began growing only sideways.

Rings run through it in slow arcs, hundreds of meters wide. Knots occur at intervals like sealed wells. In places the grain rises in a ripple no higher than an ankle; in places it has been worn by wind and grit until it is glassy, with the growth-lines polished to a dull sheen. There are no branches. There are no leaves. Erosion — wind, cold, ten thousand seasons of expansion and contraction — has taken everything that could catch, snag, or cast a shadow, and left the organism in its final form: a lid.

It is perhaps twelve meters thick at the center and tapers at its edges into the ground like the skirt of a bell. It is several hundred meters across. You could cross it in an afternoon and think you had crossed a particularly strange stretch of desert.

Under it is a city.

The entrance is a knot-hole near the northern edge, where the grain spirals inward and drops away. A ladder of pegged rungs goes down through living wood that gets progressively older, drier, and harder as you descend — first fibrous bark, then dense summerwood that rings faintly when your boot knocks it, then heartwood so old and desiccated that the tool-marks of whoever first widened this passage have not splintered in centuries. They are still crisp. Nothing rots here. Nothing can afford the water to rot.

At the bottom of the ladder the dark becomes total.

This is not dimness. There are no cracks, no phosphorescent fungi, no reflected gleam. The lid has sealed itself over its own interior with the completeness of a skull. Whatever light you carry in stays close to you, a small, nervous sphere that only shows how much it is not showing. The rest has to be taken by hand, by foot, and especially by ear.

Because it is loud down here.

Not constantly, and not with anything living. It is loud with structure. Wind moving over that flat wooden plain above finds the high knot-holes, the breathing pores, the old insect-free borings that generations of carvers widened into flues, and the whole hollow becomes an organ. Low notes arrive through the soles of your feet first — a pressure in the sternum, a hum in the teeth — and then bloom upward. The ceiling drums. The support pillars, each one a trunk that continued down through the vanished soil and into the floor, answer at slightly different pitches depending on their thickness. On a strong night above, the interior holds a chord for minutes at a time.

On still nights it is worse. Then you hear the wood itself. It contracts in the cool — for it is always cool here, a cellar cool that does not vary with the seasons — and the fibers complain. A crack will start somewhere far across the void, travel along a grain line with a sound like a distant musket shot, and echo until it seems to come from everywhere. Seed pods rattle in their storage lofts. Rope bridges creak against their pitons. A suspended house, settling a fraction of a millimeter on its bark-fiber cables, will groan through all its pegged joints at once, a sound so human and so large that your body insists someone has spoken.

The builders navigated by this. There are no lamps in the traditional districts, no soot on the ceilings. Instead the streets — the walkways of lashed and woven withies that run from pillar to pillar, well above the floor — have sounding boards set into them at intervals, flat panels of resonant root-wood that you strike with a knuckle-bone striker to announce yourself and to listen for what answers. A hollow thock means open air. A dull one means a wall near. A bright, singing reply means you are aligned with one of the main avenues of the under-city and can proceed, barefoot, following the carved guide-groove with your toes. The groove has been worn into a shallow trough by generations of feet. Every edge in the city is worn like that. Steps are saucers. Handrails are silk. Door-linths shine with the oil of palms that have been dust-dry for longer than anyone can remember.

For dryness is the second rule of the place, after darkness.

There is no water here. No well, no spring, no cistern of anything drinkable. The cisterns, when you find them — great hollowed root-bulbs set into the perimeter wall, stoppered with resin — hold lamp oil pressed from seeds, and a kind of liquid tar that was used for waterproofing hulls and roofs in some wetter elsewhere that the builders' ancestors perhaps remembered. The wood drinks anything aqueous on contact. Bring a full skin down the ladder and you can feel it grow lighter in your hands over the course of a day as the walls conduct the moisture away through contact, through air, through some slow capillary insistence of an organism that lives its whole life on the edge of desiccation and has learned to steal. Your lips crack by the second day. Your sweat vanishes before it can cool you. Cut yourself and the blood beads, thickens, and is gone into the grain of the floor like ink into blotting paper.

The plant manages on almost nothing. That thin living layer just under the upper surface condenses what it needs from night air drawn laboriously through the flues, and hoards it in waxy, resinous tissues that no mouth could use. Below, in the dark, it is mostly not alive at all — and yet the city is entirely made of it, and tends it like a crop.

Vegetation here is not decoration, or resource. It is substrate, architecture, furniture, textile, food store, and law. The floor is root-mat compressed and planed flat, its grain running in braided currents where a dozen trunks fused. The ceiling is the underside of the lid, ribbed with beams that are not beams but limbs that grew horizontally and were trained, pruned, and eventually persuaded to bear weight. The houses are excisions: spaces hollowed out of pillar-trunks with adze and stone wedge and patient charring-and-scraping, leaving walls a hand's breadth thick that sing when struck. Or they are additions: woven bark-cloth stretched over bent sapling frames and hung from the ceiling on cables, swaying almost imperceptibly, so the city has an upper town that dangles and a lower town that is carved, and between them a great deal of nothing.

That emptiness is deliberate, and it startles. For all its urban organization — and it is organized: there are districts, a market arcade run through a fallen horizontal trunk whose interior has been partitioned into stalls smelling of seed-meal and resin cakes; a granary quarter where the pods are stored in nets; a hall of records where history is kept as notched and knotted cords hung in rows that rustle faintly in the organ-wind; terraces of pale, blind shoots cultivated in troughs of pulverized heartwood, a crop that never greens and is harvested tasting of pepper and cold ash — for all that, most of the city's volume is void.

The buildings cluster at the pillars and hug the perimeter. The center is left open: a vast, flat, echoing floor of polished root-wood with a single shallow basin carved at its middle, not for water but for sound, a parabolic listening-place where a speaker standing at the focus can be heard whispering at the furthest pillar, and where, on wind nights, the chord of the whole structure gathers and concentrates until it is almost unbearable and the basin's rim thrums under your palms. Around that emptiness the suspended houses hang far apart, like lanterns that have forgotten how to shine. Between one creaking presence and the next there may be fifty meters of black air.

There is no animal life to fill it. None. This is perhaps the strangest thing, stranger than the darkness or the thirst. Dead wood on this scale should be a universe of beetles, borers, mites, ants. Here the resin that permeates every fiber is a sterilant, and the dryness finishes what the resin begins. No insects. No bats in the ceiling ribs. No mice in the granary nets — the nets hang undisturbed, their seed-pods intact, their cords unworn by teeth. And no people. Tool-marks, foot-worn grooves, mended cables, a child's handprint pressed into a resin patch beside a doorway at exactly the height a child would reach to steady themselves in the dark: all the evidence of hands, and no hands. Whoever built at this scale, with stone wedges and wooden pegs and bark rope and an acoustics more sophisticated than their metallurgy, has been gone long enough that their absence has itself become ancient and settled, another layer of the grain.

What remains moves, but slowly. That is the third rule.

The lid still grows — outward, not up — adding a ring at its buried edge in a good decade. This means the ceiling is, almost imperceptibly, descending, and the pillars are, almost imperceptibly, thickening. Houses carved a century ago have doorways a finger narrower than when they were cut. Suspended houses must be re-hung a cable-notch higher every generation, or their floors begin to kiss the walkways. A street that was straight in a grandfather's time takes a long, gentle curve in the grandson's, following a grain line that has asserted itself. The city was not built once; it was continuously negotiated out of a material that had its own slow intentions, and since the negotiators left, the material has been very gradually winning. In another few centuries the market arcade will close like a healing wound. The listening basin will shallow and go silent. The ladder hole will spiral shut.

Until then the place holds its chord. Cool, black, thirsty, resonant. Above it, the wind works over a wooden plain that looks like nothing, hides everything, and gives out — to anyone standing on that polished grain at night with their ear pressed flat to a knot — the muffled, enormous music of a town that is empty, built of a single tree, and still, below the threshold of sight, sounding.
