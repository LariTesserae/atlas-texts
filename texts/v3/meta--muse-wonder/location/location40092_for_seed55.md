It is water from the top down, and made ground at the bottom, and nothing in between that was not at some point placed, planted, or allowed to fill.

You enter through the surface — a wide, grey skin of half-frozen water, slack ice in dark plates grinding almost inaudibly in winter, open and black and rain-netted in summer — and you go down forty, fifty meters before the bottom levels out beneath you. The light goes with the first ten. What is left below that is not quite pitch dark, but the blue remainder of it, a dusk that has settled permanently into the water and never lifts. At noon it is enough to see the shape of a wall a few meters ahead. At night it is nothing at all.

The basin is low, and vast in the way that only flat things can be vast. The floor runs on in every direction below the murk, league after league of it, until sight gives up entirely and you are navigating by touch and memory and the slow slope of the ground. It has its own weather down here, unconnected to the weather above. Warm is not a word that applies — the water holds at the temperature where water is heaviest, just above freezing, year round, so cold that unprotected skin goes first bright with pain and then numb and then clumsy — but it moves in seasons of its own. Great slow currents shoulder through the deep fields at a walking pace or less, bearing silt. Sometimes for weeks a whole province of the basin is clouded out by a submarine weather-front of stirred mud, an underwater fog that settles a finger's thickness of fresh sediment over everything and erases tracks, tool marks, footprints, as if nothing had passed. Sometimes the water clears for a month and you can see down the long lanes between the plantings farther than at any other time, the rows going out of the dark at the edge of vision still unbroken.

Because it was all rows, once.

The entire floor is built. There is no natural substrate under it anywhere anyone has sounded. Dig through the silt — a meter of it, three in the hollows — and you do not come to bedrock or river gravel. You come to pavement. Flat stones, hand-dressed, laid edge to edge; or fired clay slabs gone soft at the corners after uncountable years underwater; or, in the poorer districts, rammed earth faced with woven reed matting that the cold has preserved black and fibrous in the mud. Under the pavement there is rubble fill. Under the rubble fill there is older pavement. The basin remembers being dry, or being shallow, in layers.

Above that made ground the makers terraced everything. Low field walls of unmortared stone, a knee high, a waist high, dividing the floor into paddies and plots that repeat until repetition itself becomes the landscape. Causeways run straight for distances that would take days to swim, slightly raised, paved on top, flanked by ditches. At intervals the causeways widen into platforms where roofless stone buildings stand in loose clusters, their timber lintels long since fallen inward, their interiors filled with silt and eelgrass. There are gate-posts without gates. There are stone stairs that climb three steps onto a terrace and stop at water on all sides. There are canals, main channels ten meters across with dressed stone banks, cut dead level across country that must once have been surveyed by eye and line and water-level alone. Nothing here was made with anything sharper than stone and bronze and fire-hardened wood. You can still see the peck-marks of the dressing hammers on blocks in the clearer districts, if you brush the algae away — shallow, patient dimpling, thousands of blows to a stone.

And then, at some point in its history, the builders let the water take it, or brought the water in, and planted it for drowning.

The vegetation is the second builder, and it has nearly finished its work. This is not a drowned dead place. It is lush past reason, especially given the dark and the cold — it has simply learned to live slow. Black kelp rises from the field walls in forests twenty meters tall, its blades broad and light-starved, almost purple-black, swaying in the current with a delay of minutes between the push of the water and the answer of the fronds. The paddies are meadows of eelgrass and stonewort, dense as turf, rooted in the deep silt that has collected inside the little stone enclosures exactly as soil once did. Every wall, every stair, every fallen beam is furred with moss and liverwort, cushion on cushion, so that after this much time the distinction between structure and planting has mostly been upholstered away. Red algae crust the stones where the light is marginally stronger. Green algae cloud the upper water in summer in a slow bloom that thickens the darkness below to near night and then dies back and snows downward, white flecks drifting for days through the still strata, settling on the kelp, on the meadows, on the causeways — the only rain this world has.

It is dense, layered, near-touching. You push between kelp stems and the fronds close behind you. You swim over a meadow and the grass heads brush the whole length of your body. There are places where dead vegetation has accumulated ungrazed, season upon season, into mats a meter thick, half-rotted in the cold — for decay here is almost as slow as growth, and nothing eats.

That is the fact that organizes the silence. There is no animal life. No fish in the kelp. No snails on the stonewort, no worms in the silt, no water-beetles, not even the shimmer of insect larvae under the surface ice. It is not that they are hiding. The world was stocked with plants — deliberately, in rows, in coppices, in hedges of pondweed along the canal banks — and never, or no longer, with anything to crop them. So the plants grow until they shade themselves out and collapse, and rot where they fall, and feed the next planting out of their own bodies, in a loop with no mouths in it. The water smells, when you bring a sample up into air, of green things and stone and cold mud. Nothing else. No musk, no carrion, no dung. It is a garden running on its own compost under a lid of dark water, and it has been running long enough that the species have sifted down to the ones that can tolerate dusk, near-freezing temperatures, and their own accumulating dead.

Everything else follows from the cold and the built flatness and the age. Erosion here is not dramatic. There are no cliffs to fall. The water has spent its entire tenancy rounding: wall-tops worn to swells, corners of cut stone gone to curves, inscriptions — there are a few, pecked characters on gate-posts and canal markers — softened past reading into suggestions of characters. The terraces sag. A causeway that must once have run crisp as a ruled line now undulates gently, having settled into its rubble core, and in a hundred places the pavement has bellied and cracked where the fill beneath subsided, and the cracks have filled with silt, and the silt has grown grass, so the roads are striped green along their fractures. Whole districts have simplified into a pattern you can read only from directly above, if the water ever cleared enough to let you: rectangles within rectangles, green inside grey, to the limit of the dark.

Sound travels strangely and mostly does not occur. There is the soft percussion of your own movement, muffled instantly. There is the tick and settle of silt grains. In winter there is the far groaning of the surface ice, transmitted down as a tone too low to quite hear, felt in the teeth and in the stones. Wood, waterlogged to the density of the water around it, sometimes shifts in a collapsed roof and knocks once against stone, and the knock carries a long way and startles precisely because the background is so complete. No calls. No choruses. No splashing except your own.

The rules of the place are the rules of deep, cold, still water over an old made thing. Pressure squeezes steadily as you descend the few deeper canal cuts. Warmth leaves you and does not come back. Fire is a story from another element. Metal left down here films over and flakes in slow scales; stone endures; wood endures longer than it has any business to, preserved black and heavy in the oxygen-poor bottom layers where even rot slows to a geological patience. Things fall straight down and stay where they land for centuries, buried by millimeters. Distance lies — everything looks close in the murk and is far, or looks like a wall and is a kelp stand, moving.

At the center, if center is the word for the lowest point of a basin this size, the fields give way to something like a plaza: a huge paved circle, its stones larger than anywhere else, its perimeter marked by a double row of standing blocks, all of them moss-mantled and silt-skirted. Kelp does not grow on the circle. Only a short dark turf of stonewort covers it, kept mysteriously even by the set of currents that converge there, and the water above it is the clearest and coldest in the basin. Nothing marks what it was for. It sits in the underwater dusk under its slow rain of white algae-snow, at the bottom of fifty meters of water, in the middle of a drowned, planted, hand-built continent that extends past the range of any single lifetime of swimming — still dividing itself into fields, still growing, still settling a fraction deeper into its final flattened shape, one cold, quiet, plant-thick year at a time.
