It starts at the locks.

Outside, the real sea stands higher than the land. You come in on a barge at ordinary sea-level, through gates of black steel sixty metres tall, and then you are lowered — not in a lift, but with the water itself — down into the Basin. The water sinks under you, cold and green, the walls of the lock sliding up past the hull covered in fresh concrete, painted depth-lines, stencilled warnings still crisp enough to read. When the lower gates open, you are already below the surface of the world.

Ahead is another sea. A made one.

The Low Sea is four hundred kilometres long. From the middle of it you cannot see the rim in any direction, only its weather. It has its own horizon, its own fog banks and rain squalls born from evaporation and steam, its own slow brown-green clouds that gather over the warm districts and drift toward the cold ones. The rim, when you do reach it, is not a coast. It is a dike, an engineered scarp of slag, basalt block and poured concrete running unbroken around the whole depression, with pump-houses at intervals like fortresses. Inside that wall, everything — bottom, shore, island, channel — was built.

It was a factory floor before it was flooded. That was only nine years ago, and it still shows.

Under clear water near the edges you can see it plainly from the boat: motorways running straight down the boat ramp and continuing under the waves, lane markings intact, gantries still holding signs for districts that are now thirty metres under. Rail yards. Container parks in perfect rows. Long warehouses whose roofs make the first islands you pass. The flooding was planned, slow enough to park the trucks neatly, to sandbag the archives, to strip out the worst chemicals and bolt down everything else. So the erosion is barely begun. Corners are still sharp. Paint is still blue and yellow and warning-red. Stair rails, pipe flanges, cooling fins, the teeth of gears in the open maintenance pits — nothing has had time to soften. Only a slick of algae, a thin fur of biofilm, makes the handholds treacherous.

The reason for it is heat. The Basin was built below sea-level, lined and plumbed, as a heat-sink for a continent's worth of industry — power stations, foundries, chemical plants, server cooling, smelters. Water is better at it than air, in the end, and cheaper than radiating to a warming sky. So they filled the works. Most production did not stop. It climbed. What could drown, was redesigned to work drowned. What could not, moved up onto roofs and towers and purpose-built platforms. The rest went on running underneath.

That is the strangeness of sailing it: it is empty, and it is busy.

You can travel for a day across the central reaches and meet nothing. Just open water, twelve degrees, wind combing it grey, the swell slapping at the barge, and below you an eighty-metre column of dimming green above an unseen pavement. Density falls away to almost nothing. A single line of buoys, blinking amber, marching out of sight, marking a shipping lane that follows a drowned motorway because the survey was already done. A sonar ping every few seconds. Rain stippling the surface so hard the water looks solid for a moment.

Then, distantly, a district announces itself by sound long before sight.

Underwater, the Basin is loud. Sound does not fade here the way it does in air; it arrives through the hull, through the water into your feet and teeth. The great circulation pumps — each one housed in a submerged cathedral of concrete, intake grilles the size of housing blocks — keep up a continuous bass throb that sets loose tools humming on deck. Turbines whine. Chains clank in flooded dry-docks still in use by submersible gantries. Pile-drivers go all day somewhere behind the mist, tok, tok, tok, building another platform. Propellers pass in the dark below, barges above, work-submarines below them, all announcing themselves with sonar so as not to meet in the murk. On deck you also get the surface share of it: diesel generators, crane alarms, gulls, the hiss of steam, the clang of containers being set down on a roof that is now a dock.

The islands are industrial and unmistakable. Cooling towers rise straight out of the sea in clusters, their hyperboloid throats steaming, bases lost in black water, colonies of cormorants nested in the internal latticework above the waterline. Smokestacks make thinner islands, with platforms welded round them at flood-height plus ten metres, and washing lines, and greenhouses lashed down against the squalls. A warehouse roof, hectares of it, becomes a port town: cranes along what were the eaves, dormitories in shipping containers along the ridge, a canteen, a chapel, a clinic, all supplied by boat. Where the roof sags, it makes a shallow lagoon, and there, finally, reeds find a footing — the place is poor in vegetation, the bottom too much concrete and tarmac and steel plate for roots, the water too churned — but in these roof-lagoons and along the dike toe, reed beds and salt-tolerant grasses have got in, thin and wind-bent, and algae paints every submerged surface olive and brown.

Between the islands, under the surface, the real country begins.

Dive in a work-sub — or in a suit, with a tether and a very good reason — and the light goes through its familiar sequence: glitter, green, blue-green, a dusk lit by strings of industrial floodlamps that were never switched off. Whole streets run with light down here, high-mast lamps still burning underwater, haloed in plankton. Radiator fields stretch for kilometres: rank after rank of finned heat-exchange towers, plates and pipes and ceramic vanes, standing on the old factory aprons, shimmering with waste heat so the water above them wavers like air over a road. Fish know exactly where the edges are. Outside them the water is cold; inside, a few degrees warmer and full of food washed off the surfaces. Shoals hang in the boundary layer in their thousands, turning as one.

Fauna got in with the filling water, through screens meant to be finer than they proved, and then it was deliberately helped: mussels, oysters, coarse fish, eels. It took. Abundance without much green to base it on — a food web built instead on plankton fed by mineral-rich inflow, on biofilm, on detritus, on the mussel beds that now crust every pipe and must be harvested by divers with scrapers before they choke the intakes. That harvest is a fishery now. So is the netting around the warm outfalls, where fish gather all winter. Cormorants and gulls work the surface; herons stalk the roof-lagoons; seals have learned the locks and commute, growing fat and impudent, stealing from the mussel barges. In the radiator fields, seen from a sub, the fish part around your lights in silver weather, and below them crabs pick over a car park where the windscreens are still, most of them, unbroken.

Nothing down there is still, because the water is worked like a machine part.

The artificial tide is the rule everyone lives by. There is no moon in the Basin's schedule; there is the pump rota. At six in the morning the great southern intakes open and the whole sea begins, very slowly, to lean south. Currents set in along the drowned roads — two knots down Foundry Avenue, three through the narrows between the twin towers of District 9, slack in the lee of the warehouses. At midday the flow reverses for the cooling cycle, and every boat, diver, fish-farm tender and submersible tram in the place has set their plans by it. Flags on the buoys show the state of the flow. A red board at a dock means the water under you is about to start moving hard enough to take a swimmer and keep them. Maintenance is done on the ebb-equivalent, when sectors behind mobile bulkheads can be dropped by five metres, exposing a shining, steaming acreage of machinery for six hours — workers go down in boots onto roofs and aprons suddenly become land again, gulls screaming in to pick at the stranded mussels — before the boards come up and the sea takes it back.

Weather rides on top of all this machinery and gets changed by it. Cold air off the open water meets the steam columns of the tower districts and makes standing banks of fog, white walls you sail into and out of, suddenly loud with unseen cranes. Summer rain falls warm over the heat fields and cold at the rim. Once or twice a year the wind gets a long enough fetch across the central emptiness to build a real storm, waves breaking against the stacks, spray going clean over the lower platforms, roof-towns battened and riding it out like ships that happen to be attached to the bottom of the sea.

At night it is easiest to understand. From a platform in District 17, the surface black and rain-pricked, you can see lights in three layers: the towns and towers above water, sodium and white; the shipping lanes sliding between them; and below the surface, dimmed and greened, the endless lit grid of the drowned works going down and away farther than the water will let you see — avenues, substations, pump cathedrals, radiator forests full of fish — still running, still cooling, sharp-edged and new under its thin skin of life, holding a sea in a bowl that people built, below the level of the sea outside.
