There is a room high inside the plant that does not appear on any of the painted diagrams.

You reach it by climbing past the compressor decks, past the heat exchangers that sweat oil and tick as they cool — though they never cool — and then sideways, through an insulated hatch no wider than your shoulders, into the thickness of the wall itself. The hatch shuts with a padded thud that the rest of the building immediately swallows. On the other side, the air changes.

It is smaller than you expected. Four metres long, less than two wide, a ceiling low enough that tall workers learn to duck under the cable trays without thinking. Everything is made. There is no mountain under your boots, though you are nearly four thousand metres up and the mountain is all around the plant; there is only perforated steel plate, cold-drawn and still sharp at the edges. The floor is grating. The walls are ceramic tile over riveted alloy, drilled in regular patterns. Even the slit that passes for a window is not a window — a laminated panel set into the outer skin, looking out onto another panel a hand's breadth beyond it, and grey high-altitude daylight leaking in between. The lamps overhead give the rest: a plain, white, working light, neither warm nor dim, the colour of an overcast noon. It is always on. It has no switch in here.

And it is cold in a way the rest of the plant is not. The compressors below throw off enormous heat, but this pocket sits in the intake path, and the air that is drawn through it has already been stripped, filtered, dried. Minus eighteen, minus twenty, steady. No water moves in this place. The chemistry of the plant is anhydrous where it matters — ammonia, hot oil, inert gas — and the room has been dried along with it. Your breath does not fog. Nothing condenses on the pipes, no frost feathers the corners despite the cold. Your lips crack within minutes. Your nose bleeds a little, eventually, if you stay, and the blood dries almost before you notice it. Metal touched with bare skin holds on.

Then there is the sound.

It is not loud the way an explosion is loud, or an alarm. It is total. A roar seated so low you feel it first in the sternum and the soles of your feet, threaded through with a high, continuous whistle from the turbine hall two decks down, transmitted up through the structure so faithfully that the wall panels themselves are singing. You wear plugs and then muffs over them and still the sound is inside the bone of your jaw. Your clothes tremble against your skin. Loose screws in your pockets buzz in sympathy. Speech is not difficult here; it is irrelevant. You can shout and produce a faint disturbance that your companion reads on your face more than hears. Workers communicate by pressing a palm flat to the wall and rapping, or by holding up pre-written cards, their corners worn soft — though remarkably little in this room is worn.

That is one of its rules, arrived at slowly, the longer you look. The place is new in a way that never advances. This is partly because it is, in plant terms, recent — cut and bolted during the last expansion, machining burrs still bright on the handrail, paint still glossy on the pipe runs. But mostly it is because nothing here is allowed to proceed. The temperature does not wander through the day; there is no day in the intake air. The pressure needle on the single gauge does not tremble. It points, and has always pointed, at the same fraction below the red. The roar does not swell or ease with load, because this room exists precisely to ensure that it doesn't.

Only a few things share the space with you, and they are spaced far apart, as if each required a territory of emptiness. At the far end, a valve wheel, chain-locked open, painted safety yellow. Along the north wall, three planters — if they can be called that: sealed steel troughs, each holding one low, stiff mat of engineered moss, blue-green and resinous and faintly plastic to the eye. Arctic stock, reworked for dry cold and bad air. They scrub a little carbon from the pocket and exhale a little oxygen and otherwise do almost nothing, growing at a rate that would need a decade to measure. Between the troughs, pale patches of sensor-lichen have been daubed directly onto the cold pipes, sparse as freckles, meant to blush if corrosion ever begins. They have never blushed. They stay the exact grey-green of the day they were applied, vibrating very slightly, each one, in its own spot.

Nothing else lives. No rat has found this place, no bird nests in the intake, no insect winters in the insulation. The sound sees to that, and the dryness, and the cold. The moss in its troughs is the entire ecology, and it is less an ecology than an arrangement.

If you stay still — and the room encourages stillness, because moving stirs nothing useful — you begin to see what the sound is doing to the air. Dust enters even here, mineral dust, metal fines, fibres from the filters below. It does not settle. It hangs. In the steady pressure of the roar, the dust gathers along invisible planes and lines, nodal surfaces, standing in the air in faint, spaced curtains that do not drift. From the doorway it looks like very sparse, very pale vegetation: a field of weightless seeds held mid-fall, forever. Reach out and your hand erases a patch, and over the next minutes it re-forms, grain by grain, into exactly the shape it had before. The filings near the magnetic conduit at the floor stand on end in stiff little tufts, like frost grass. They tremble. They do not fall.

This is when the room stops seeming like a leftover — a maintenance void, a forgotten gap between skins — and starts seeming intentional in a stranger way. The block in the middle of the floor that you took for a seat is too precisely placed, too massive, bolted through to the frame. A tuning mass. The moss troughs sit exactly where a hand's breadth of soft material would damp a particular frequency. The pocket itself is a shaped cavity in the body of the plant, a resonator the size of a person, cut so that the great machines below can shout into it and have their shout returned cancelled, steadied, held. The plant, left to itself, would find the note that tears it apart. This room sings against it, constantly, by doing nothing but being this shape, this cold, this full of unchanging noise.

So the equilibrium is not an accident of neglect. It is the work. If the roar ever stopped — if the needle moved, if the dust fell all at once in a soft grey rain, if the lichen ever had weather to report — it would mean something far below had already failed.

Workers who know the hatch exist check the gauge, rap twice on the wall to feel that the note is true, look at the moss to confirm it is still its patient colour, and leave. No one writes the date in the metal logbook chained by the door anymore. The last entries, in pencil, vibrate until the words go slightly double, and they all say the same thing in different hands.

*No change. No change. No change.*
