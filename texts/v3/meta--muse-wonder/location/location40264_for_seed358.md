You come to it from above, and for a long time there is nothing to see but flat farmland and a rim of spoil. Then the road tips down.

The pit is eighty metres below the plain. Three kilometres long, almost two across, cut in benches out of raw ground that has never been surface before. No topsoil down here. No rounded stone. The walls are fresh — pale limestone, blue-grey clay, seams of wet sand the colour of rust — still sharp where the excavators bit them, still showing the tooth-marks. Nothing has had time to weather smooth. In places the cut face is already growing things the earth made underground: clear gypsum needles pushing out of the clay, salt whiskers, iron staining that spreads overnight like a bruise.

It is too deep for the sun to work properly. Even at noon in summer the floor lies in a kind of humid shade, the light flattened by height and by vapour. In the morning the whole basin fills with mist to the second bench and does not clear; it condenses and rains back down inside itself. Strings of work-lights are left on all day along the haul roads, yellow in the murk, reflected in standing water everywhere.

Water is the condition of the place. Not a lake, not a river — water arriving from every direction at once, too much and too slow to leave. The floor is below the water table, so the ground weeps. Every bench has its seep-line, a dark wet band where sheets of water slide without ever quite falling. Trickles find the joints in the limestone and tick and pour. After rain — and above, it seems always to have just rained — the haul road runs like a shallow canal. Pump sumps have been dug at the low corners, deep black pits fenced with rebar, and even on a dry week they never empty. They rise a finger's width a day, patient, inevitable. The earth down here is still deciding whether it wants to be land at all, and mostly it wants to be bottom.

Because of that, everything dry is crowded onto everything else.

The only firm ground is the levees and pads the crews have built up out of crushed rock and compacted clay, narrow causeways between borrow ponds, settling basins, flooded cuts, and sumps. On those causeways everything the place needs is packed shoulder to shoulder, with no unused interval. Diesel pump-houses on skids. Office trailers up on concrete blocks. A workshop made of three shipping containers with its doors blown open. Fuel bowsers, stacks of pipe, a half-assembled dredge sitting on pontoons in brown water with its conveyor gantry reaching nowhere yet. Excavators parked nose-to-tail for the night, buckets down in the mud. Cable reels, pallets of geotextile going soft at the edges, portable light towers, a canteen trailer with steam coming out of its vent from five in the morning until dark. If you step off the boards they lay between buildings, you are ankle deep. If you step off the causeway, you may be waist deep in water that looks exactly as shallow as all the other water.

And between, under, and on top of all of that machinery: life, also packed, also leaving no gap.

The vegetation hasn't caught up — this ground is too new, too flooded, too trampled. There's algae, thick green and black mats that skin the ponds and make the clay treacherous. Spikes of new reed come up in the shallows in scattered, tentative lines. A little moss on the shaded faces. Nothing with a trunk. Nothing that could hold shade.

But animals arrived as soon as the first cut filled. Midges in columns over every pond. Dragonflies stitching the causeways, so many on warm afternoons that they tick off windscreens and hard hats. Frogs — in the sumps, in the tyre ruts, in flooded conduit trenches — leopard frogs, bullfrogs, chorus frogs packed into the reed scraps, all calling at once. Herons and egrets stand spaced only a few metres apart along the settling ponds, absolutely still in the industrial murk. Coots nest in a discarded cable spool. Swallows nest in the open ends of stacked pipe, in the eaves of the pump-houses, under the dredge gantry, coming and going through diesel fumes. Turtles haul out on floating pallets. Water snakes slide off the riprap when a truck passes. In the warmest basin, hand-sized fish — nobody stocked them, they came in with river water during the first flood — turn in water so shallow their backs break the surface.

And it is the sound, finally, that makes the density feel total. There is no quiet interval down here to put anything else in.

The pumps never stop: six large diesels and a rank of electric submersibles, a low throb you feel in the soles of your boots and in your teeth. Over that, the conveyor on the west bench squeals on a dry bearing all day. Backup alarms, water falling from bench to bench in thin curtains, generators, the hiss of pressure washers cleaning mud off tracks. And over and through all of it, organic and just as loud, the cicadas in the spoil trees up on the rim shrilling down into the pit, the frogs at dusk rising to a single continuous roar, the mid-morning uproar of blackbirds lifting off a pond in their thousands. You wear ear defenders to be allowed on the floor, and still you shout. Crews talk in a mixture of yelling and hand signals. Conversations happen at six inches. At lunch, in the canteen trailer with the door shut, you can still hear everything, muffled, as if the noise were another kind of water pressure pushing at the walls.

It is warm down there, always a few degrees warmer than the fields above, and windless. Air sits. Diesel, wet clay, warm algae, hot electrical insulation. Your shirt sticks by eight. Boots rot in a season. Tools rust between shifts. Paper in the site office curls and will not uncurl. Anything left on the ground for a week comes up green underneath.

Nothing moves fast, despite the noise. That is the strange tempo of the place. The pumps run day and night to achieve, at best, a drawdown of centimetres. A new spring opens in a bench face, not with a burst, but with a damp patch on Monday that is a steady pour by the following month. The clay floor heaves very slowly upward where the groundwater pushes from below, buckling a newly laid pad over a winter. Gypsum grows. Mud consolidates under its own weight at a rate the engineers measure in millimetres. They say it will be five years before this part can bear a foundation, ten before the final lake — this is supposed, eventually, to be a reservoir — settles to its intended shore. For now it is neither quarry nor lake nor building site, but something embryonic, with all its systems laid on the surface because there is as yet no inside for them to go in.

At the far eastern end, beyond the last causeway, the crews have mostly given up for this season. The ponds join up there. The water gets clearer, tea-coloured over white clay, and the only structures are a sinking survey platform and a line of marker posts disappearing into reeds that aren't there yet. Egrets are packed along a drowned fence-line, white bird after white bird, waiting. The pumps sound far away and somehow louder, bounced off the walls. If you stand there long enough the mist comes back down the benches toward you, erasing the light towers one by one, and for a moment the place forgets it was built by anyone at all. It is just a new, low, wet world, freshly opened, already completely full.
