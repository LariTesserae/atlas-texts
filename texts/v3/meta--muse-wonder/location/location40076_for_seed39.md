At first it's the air that tells you where you are, not the view.

It's thin enough that a short climb up a rubble slope leaves you standing with your mouth open, tasting cold iron and wet stone, waiting for your lungs to catch up. The light doesn't help you judge distance. A low ceiling of cloud hangs over everything, and what sun gets through comes as a pale, spread-out dimness, noon that feels like early evening. Nothing casts a hard shadow. Colors go flat and close: black rock, grey water, a short grass the color of old brass.

You are on a plateau so high that the country below it — valleys, forests, towns — might as well not exist. Around its edges the ground doesn't so much fall away as tear. New ranges shoulder up on three sides, raw and unfinished. This land has been lifted recently, geologically speaking, and it hasn't learned how to be a landscape yet.

The rock proves it. There is almost no soil, and almost nothing is rounded. Slabs of dark basalt and pale, glassy granite lie where they split, edges still sharp enough to cut a boot sole, faces unstained except where water seeps out of them. There are no real valleys carved here, no gorges. The drainage hasn't had time. Water moves, but it hasn't decided where to go.

And there is a surprising amount of water for a place this cold and this bare.

It weeps out of cracks. It condenses at night on every stone and freezes in a clear skin, then melts by mid-morning and runs again. It gathers in shallow, braided sheets that wander across flats of saturated gravel, change course around a fallen block, and vanish into a marsh. In the hollows are lakes without outlets, milky blue one hour and leaden the next, fringed with salt and ochre mineral crust where they have shrunk. Under your feet in the low places the ground gives — a sponge of black peat and root mat, soaked through, that releases a bubble of cold water around your ankle with each step.

Nothing grows tall. There are no trees, no shrubs worth the name. What lives in the ground holds itself flat against the wind: cushion plants no bigger than a hand, tight as felt, flecked with tiny white flowers; lichen in rust and sulphur patches on the fresh rock; a sparse turf of sedge and alpine grass in the wet meadows where the water lingers longest. In summer those meadows are the whole reason for everything else.

Because for a place so empty, it is crowded with animals.

They are not everywhere. For a kilometer you may cross nothing but wet stone and hear only your own breath and the ticking of meltwater. Then the flat opens and it's all movement. Herds of wild asses graze in strings of twenty and thirty, pale bellies, dark dorsal stripes, heads coming up together. Smaller gazelles pick through the drier gravel. Geese and ruddy shelducks nest in impossible numbers around the marsh edges, and when something startles them the sound is sudden — a clatter of wings and harsh calling that fills the basin and then settles again in minutes. Black-necked cranes stand paired in the shallows. Marmots whistle from fresh scree. Pikas cut sedge and stack it. Foxes work the meadow margins, wolves work the foxes' country in the early dark, ravens ride the updraft off every new cliff, announcing themselves with a croak that carries a mile in the thin air.

They are drawn, all of them, to the same narrow thing you are: the line where meltwater keeps grass green for a few weeks against the cold.

Into that same line comes the industry.

There are no buildings here. No roads, no fences, no power poles, no foundations. Building is pointless — the ground heaves a few centimeters a year, cracks open in the spring thaw, and floods in new places every season — and it is also forbidden. What comes up here has to be able to leave under its own power, and leave nothing behind.

So the factories walk.

Or rather, they crawl, on wide, low-pressure tracks as tall as a person, slow enough that you can outwalk them easily if you weren't gasping. A brine processor is a long, low hull of riveted steel and insulated pipe, a small plant complete in itself: intake pumps in front drinking from a lake margin, settling tanks and evaporator stacks amidships, a humming electric heart powered by derated diesels that cough and labor in the thin air, a discharge arm behind laying down clean warm water in a steaming sheet. Its running lights glow amber in the day fog. Its exhaust condenses instantly and trails as a white plume that flattens and disappears.

You rarely see more than one at a time. They work kilometers apart, a regional fleet spread over a town-sized emptiness, following concentrations of lithium and borate in the lake water as those shift from week to week. At night from a high slab you might see three of them — three small, lit smudges in a vast dark, moving slower than the stars seem to. During the day the sound reaches you before the shape does, and barely: a low, industrial thrum absorbed by wet ground and cloud, a faint whine of electric drive, the regular clink of a conveyor. Then quiet again, wind, a marmot's whistle.

Above them, service drones rise and fall, carrying samples, filters, a replacement pump. They are the only fast things here besides birds. They navigate around nesting colonies automatically, and they cut power and drop if a crane crosses their lane.

The strangest accommodation is at the stern of each machine. The waste heat and clean discharge make a pool that never quite freezes. Ducks gather in that artificial warmth at dawn, steam rising around them. Asses will queue, unhurried, to cross a processor's track — and by law and by simple practice, the machine stops and idles until they have passed. Its crew of two or three, bundled in oxygen-assisted masks when they step outside, spend as much time watching for nests in the intake path as they do watching gauges. If the sedge is flowering and the geese are down, whole sections of lake are simply skipped that year. The route next season will be a different faint darkening over the gravel, already being erased by frost-heave and new streams.

The weather does its own work on the same schedule. Almost every afternoon the cloud thickens, the temperature falls out from under the day, and a brief, violent squall of sleet scours the plateau sideways. Streams swell an inch and find a new braid. A rock face, saturated and frozen and thawed once too often, sheds a slab with a crack like a rifle shot, and by morning there is a new, untouched scar of rock for lichen to begin on. The processors weather-vane into the wind and wait it out, stacks ticking.

By evening everything is dripping. The machines dim to a running glow, the herds move down to the lee of the meadows, the cranes call once or twice across water. The cold comes back not gradually but like a decision, icing the seeps shut, stiffening the peat, turning breath to fog close to your face.

It is an old bargain, newly made: a young, wet, high place that has not finished forming, filled for a season with animals that have learned its brief plenty, and with moving factories that have learned they can only stay by never settling.
