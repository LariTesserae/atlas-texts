It begins flat, and then refuses to end.

You come to it up the last mountain range in the world — not peaks, exactly, but their roots. Whatever was once sharp here wore away so long ago that the stone has forgotten the idea of a summit. The ridges are rounded down to polished granite humps, pink and grey and black, grooved by vanished glaciers, split by frost into shallow plates. The path is a stair cut directly into that root-rock, wide enough for ten to walk abreast, each step worn concave by feet that are no longer anywhere. It climbs for days through cold, thin, ordinary daylight.

Then the stair arrives, and there is no descent on the other side. Only the Table.

It is a plateau so high that the clouds of the lower world move beneath its rim like a slow sea, and so vast that it makes its own weather above them. You can stand at the edge at dawn — dawn takes a long time here, the light spilling across the plain in a visible tide — and not see the far side, nor the middle, nor any curvature you can trust. The horizon is simply where the black-green ground gives up and becomes sky. Geographers below insist it is a continent. The people of the Table, insofar as there are people, insist it is one city.

Both are right, which is one of the local problems.

The surface is ancient beyond geology's patience. This is erosion's final opinion: nothing left to take. Rivers never learned to cut here; the bedrock is too hard and too old and too level, so water lies instead of runs, in dew-lakes an inch deep and ten miles across, in black peat pans, in lens-shaped ponds that appear overnight in the stone hollows and are gone by the following dusk. Rain does not fall generally. It condenses, in travelling pockets — a grey veil of drizzle a few streets wide moving purposefully across the plain, a sudden beading of moisture on every leaf and roof while the ground a hundred paces away stays frost-dry. At night fog rises out of the moss in rivers and flows, unmistakably, uphill, pooling in cisterns that were built open to catch it.

It should be barren. Altitude and cold should have scoured it to lichen and wind. Instead it is lush — but lush in the way of cold places that have had forever to specialize. The ground cover is a deep, sound-swallowing moss, knee-high in the wild districts, springy and dark, frosted white at the tips every morning and steaming faintly by noon. From it rise fern-trees with black trunks and fronds like green-black umbrellas, which uncurl only in the cold and fold tight when warmed. There are plate-lichens the size of millstones, amber and rust-red, that drink the dew and exhale a pocket of warmer air above themselves; whole neighborhoods are founded on clusters of them. Ice-flowers grow in the shade of walls, translucent, rootless, blooming at temperatures that kill other things. Everything is engineered by evolution to harvest water from air and light from a pale sun, and everything does it slowly, extravagantly, without hurry.

There are no animals. None. No birds over the dew-lakes, no insects in the fern-trees, no worms in the peat, no bones in the moss — and, once you notice it, no sound where sound should live. The Table is mostly quiet in a way that has texture: the tick of frost splitting stone, the creak of a fern-tree adjusting toward the light, a far-off dripping where a fog-river reaches its cistern, and then great continental silences in between, because there is nothing with a throat for miles in any direction and the moss eats echoes.

And yet there is a city.

It is urban the way a forest is urban if the forest had opinions about drainage. Districts rise from the plain at intervals of ten, twenty, sometimes sixty kilometers of empty mossland — a cluster of stone longhouses, a plaza of fitted basalt slabs, a market hall with a roof of woven fern-trunk, granaries, bath-houses fed by condensation channels, rope-bridges over peat pans, aqueducts of hollowed wood stilt-walking across absolutely level ground. Then nothing again. Then another district, with the same street-names, continuing the numbering of the last. It is all one municipality. Addresses here include walking-times. The Third District's east wall is a four-day journey from the Third District's west gate, and both post offices consider this local.

The construction is layered to a degree that becomes vertiginous when you look at a foundation trench: street compressed upon street, fossilized moss, old plazas become cellars, old cellars become the compressed black stratum on which new longhouses stand. The technology that maintains it is primitive and perfect — bronze saws, stone drills worked by water-weight, rope cranes, hand looms, counterpoised wooden lifts, water-clocks of dripping cedar buckets. Nothing is powered except by water, wind, gravity, and patience. Which raises the obvious difficulty that some of the aqueduct arches are four hundred meters long and built of single fitted stones the size of houses, and the tools to move them are, demonstrably, levers.

The inhabitants resolve the difficulty by not being in a hurry, or, depending on your botany, by not being inhabitants.

They are rooted when you meet them.

A citizen of the Table spends the long central portion of life as a tree — though "tree" is a lower-world word that undersells the tailoring. The trunk grows hollow and chambered; the canopy grows flat and insulative; the root system grows opinionated, tapping the fog channels, avoiding the old foundations, sometimes incorporating a doorway. Families are groves. A marriage is two canopies grafted for a century. Children are cuttings, carried in moss-lined bronze pots to a new plot and debated over by the neighborhood for a generation before planting. When a citizen finally uproots — a process that takes a season of careful communal excavation, of saws and levers and chanting that is mostly just creaking — they move, in the walking phase of life, perhaps forty kilometers over the next decade, trailing roots like a bridal train, and settle again where they will build, by growing, the next house they will be.

So the city builds itself out of its population and its population out of its city, and there is no animal life anywhere in the arrangement, only a vast, slow, green civilization that experiences a traffic jam as a matter of historical record.

This is where the Table begins to stop obeying the rules that got you up the stair.

Because a city whose citizens move at root-speed needs its distances to behave, and here they don't. A district left unattended for a winter is farther away in spring — not overgrown, not ruined, just *farther*, as if the plain between had quietly inserted more of itself. Roads must be walked regularly to keep them the same length. There are municipal employees — walking-phase citizens, dragging a measuring chain, ringing a wooden bell once a day so the road can hear itself — whose entire civic function is to insist, on foot, that the distance between two plazas remains three days. When one of them dies and is planted mid-route, a milestone becomes an ancestor, and the road, politely, shortens around the grave.

Verticality behaves the same way: as a temporary claim. Anything built upward is, over years, gently persuaded flat by the plateau's final opinion of mountains. Towers settle. Walls bow. The fern-trees cheat by growing sideways. The citizens cheat by rebuilding — endlessly, by hand, with bronze tools — on top of their own compressed previous attempts, so that the city rises measurably only in the sense that its foundations descend into history. A house here is not old because it has stood a long time. A house is old because, if you cut through its floor, you find the same house, again and again, all the way down, each version flatter.

Water has learned local customs too. It prefers remembered routes to downhill ones. The condensation channels work because the fog has been flowing into those cisterns for ten thousand years and sees no reason to change; divert a channel and the fog will flow, for a while, along the air above where the channel used to be, filling a ghost of a ditch, until the citizens plant a line of scent-leaved shrubs to teach it the new way. Rain pockets follow the avenues. Spores — which do all the work that insects never arrived to do — travel on those same damp currents in visible golden weather, whole pollination-fronts rolling across districts, so that a grove can receive, on a still cold afternoon, news and pollen from a family it has not touched in three hundred years.

And the scale does something to falling, and to light, that no one discusses at the lower altitudes of conversation. Things dropped from the aqueduct take too long to land, as if the ground were considering them. Sunset arrives in the western districts while the eastern ones are already in starless, moss-scented night, and the city uses this — messages are sent *into* yesterday evening by a fast walker with a lantern, occasionally, for ceremonial purposes, and everyone agrees not to make a habit of it. The daylight itself is perfectly normal. That is the worst part. Blue sky, white sun, honest shadows — except that shadows, on the Table, fall toward the nearest inhabited district instead of away from the sun, very slightly, the way iron filings lean. It helps you find your way. You stop mentioning it after the first week.

It is not a still place, for all its quiet. It is furiously active at speeds that don't include yours. Weather is born in the interior — spiral systems that form over the central peat pans because the moss breathes out warmth in patterns the sky copies. Fern-trees migrate their shade. Dew-lakes evaporate in geometric shapes. The bronze-saw crews of an entire district will spend your whole visit making one cut in one foundation stone, and make it flawlessly, and when you return years later the cut is complete and the stone has been a plaza for some time and there is a festival in progress that began before you left. Growth, repair, pollination, fog-harvest, road-insistence, the slow civic uprooting of elders — a hundred processes, all turning over each other, none of them loud.

At night the temperature drops hard and the whole continent-sized city ticks and contracts. The ice-flowers open in the walls' shadows. Fog gets up out of the moss and sets off uphill toward the cisterns, past the rooted citizens standing in their chambered trunks with their doors open and their canopies folded against the cold, dreaming at the speed of sap about the next street they intend to become.

If you stay long enough — and the roads, unattended, will make sure that leaving takes longer than arriving did — you begin to understand the Table's one law, the one under the erosion and the weather and the green urban patience of it:

Nothing here is finished. It is only, for now, flat enough to live on.
