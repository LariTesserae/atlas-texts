First you hear it from the plain, where there should be nothing to hear.

The country above is flat to the horizon in every direction — wind-packed snow in winter, grey-brown tundra in the brief summer, no trees tall enough to break the line. The road out from the coast runs straight for forty kilometres over permafrost. Then, without a hill or a valley to prepare you, the ground opens.

The Cut is eighteen kilometres long, two to four hundred metres wide, and sixty metres below the surface it replaced. It was blasted and dug within living memory, first as a sheltered corridor for a rail line and a gas pipeline — down here the wind cannot strip heat from the pipes or bury a train in a single night's drift. The walls still show how young it is. They go down in sharp benches, each ten metres high, the rock edges unrounded, drill-marks still visible in long parallel lines, frost only just beginning to split the corners off in clean, pale flakes. Nothing up here weathers fast.

You go down the service ramp at the north end in a slow series of switchbacks, and conditions change floor by floor. The wind stops. The cold does not. Cold air falls into the Cut and pools there, ten or fifteen degrees colder than the plain above on a still day, so that your descent is into a lake of winter you cannot see. The light goes with the wind. The sun, when there is one, clears the rim for only a few hours and throws the opposite wall's shadow across the rest. For most of the year the floor lives in a blue-grey dimness, lit flatly through cloud or not lit at all, with pools of orange work-light gathered far apart around the installations.

And the sound arrives before any of that. The compressor stations that keep the gas moving north are set into the east wall at intervals of several kilometres — low, long buildings of corrugated steel, half-buried, with exhaust stacks and intake housings the size of houses. Each one produces a continuous, tearing roar that the rock walls will not let escape. It travels the length of the Cut, reflecting bench to bench, thinning with distance but never actually stopping. A kilometre from the nearest station you still have to raise your voice. Five hundred metres away conversation becomes a matter of leaning close and shouting into the side of a hood. Inside the red lines painted on the concrete aprons, ear defenders are not advice. The noise is physical there. It comes up through the soles of your boots from the ground, it sets the loose ice crystals dancing off the pipe insulation, it makes the surface of your tea jump in its cup in the mess hut. Men and women who work a full rotation come up the ramp at shift's end hoarse from habit even though nobody, sensibly, has tried to talk for hours. They use hand signals down here as fluently as speech.

Between the stations there is almost nothing built, and that emptiness is the main fact of the place at ground level.

The floor of the Cut is entirely alive.

Not forested, not tall — there is nothing here over knee height except where waste heat leaks from a valve housing and a thicket of dwarf willow has taken the hint and grown to waist height, black twiggy crowns holding rime. Everywhere else the surface is a single continuous mat of vegetation, laid down deliberately in the first years to insulate the disturbed permafrost and since then gone thoroughly wild on its own terms. Sphagnum and feather moss, sedges, cotton grass, crowberry, reindeer lichen in pale sprawling patches. In summer the colours are dark and saturated — bottle green, rust, oxblood, mustard — in winter it all freezes into a muted, iron-grey carpet under snow that never quite covers the hummocks.

There is no bare ground. You do not walk on soil here; you walk on plants, metres deep in places, compressed by their own slow accumulation. The boardwalks that run the length of the floor, section to section, exist largely to protect that mat. Step off and you sink to the ankle in cold sponge, crush a structure that took twenty years to grow a hand's breadth, and open a wound that lets the summer in to thaw what must stay frozen. In the emergency shelters — small yellow cabins every two kilometres, each with a phone line, bunks, and a stove — the first instruction printed on the door is about staying on the duckboards.

It is damp here rather than wet, and that distinction shapes everything. There is no river. The moss holds what moisture arrives as intermittent rain, as melt from the benches in April, as condensation beading and freezing on the underside of cold pipes. Shallow black pools form between hummocks and skin over with ice by evening even in July. Drainage ditches run sluggish and tea-coloured and then stop, choked with sedge. Inside the sheds the walls run with condensation where warm air meets cold steel; outside, every railing, cable tray, and pipe flange grows a thick beard of hoarfrost that has to be knocked off with a mallet. Your boots are permanently damp. Nothing ever fully dries. Nothing ever fully soaks, either. It is a country of cold sponge and frozen surfaces.

The industrial presences recur sparsely along that green floor like punctuation. A pumping house. A rail siding with a single row of tank cars waiting, frost white overall, for the one slow train a day that creeps the length of the Cut at twenty kilometres an hour, its own diesel almost modest under the larger roar. A transformer yard humming inside a chain-link fence, the fence itself furred with ice. A storage shed with a living roof, sedge growing out of the gravel ballast the way it grows everywhere else. Pipelines run the whole distance on low concrete sleepers, two of them, lagged in aluminium cladding patched and re-patched, ticking faintly with expansion even in deep cold. Sometimes a maintenance crew appears — three figures in high-visibility orange, dwarfed by the width of the floor, separated from the next crew by kilometres of moss — and the scale resolves again: this is a valley-sized place, a small region with its own weather, not a site.

Animals mostly decline to live in it. The noise sees to that. A pair of ravens has nested for years on the warm side of Station Three's exhaust housing and commutes up and over the rim each morning, apparently unbothered. Arctic foxes den under the raised floors of the shelters where the mat is thin and the digging is easier, and leave neat lines of tracks in the frost between hummocks. Occasionally a reindeer strays down the ramp in autumn and has to be herded laboriously back out, bewildered, slipping on the boardwalks. Under the moss, though, the sound is another world. Dig twenty centimetres down — the researchers do, in their marked plots — and the roar is already muffled to a vibration. At half a metre there is only the dark, the wet roots, the slow fungal work, and the insects moving at the pace the cold allows. The mat is a roof. Most of the ecology here has gone underneath it, patient and deaf.

For processes down here are slow, all but one. The moss grows millimetres a year. The ice lenses in the benches heave a few centimetres a season and are surveyed, marked, and chiselled back on a ten-year cycle. A section of pipeline cladding rusts, is noted, is replaced two summers later. The permafrost beneath the mat, if the mat is intact, does not move at all — which is the point. Human time in the Cut is mostly waiting: waiting for the train, waiting for a gauge to move, waiting out the dark months under work-lights while the machines maintain their one fast, endless note.

In midwinter, from the rim at noon, you can stand on the silent plain and look along the trench as far as the haze lets you. Every few kilometres an orange light burns down in the blue shadow, small and steady. The moss is invisible under snow. The willow thickets are black smudges where the heat escapes. A thread of vapour rises from each station and is flattened and carried sideways, still inside the Cut, never quite clearing the walls. And the roar comes up out of the ground in front of you, out of all that green, quiet-growing emptiness, as if the valley itself — young, cold, cut open, and carpeted over — had been given exactly one thing to do at speed, and was doing it without pause.
