At first it reads as a mountain swamp that has gotten out of hand.

You are high — high enough that the clouds are not above you but below the rim, piled in a slow white sea against the outer slopes, and the light comes clean and ordinary and a little thin. And yet it is hot. Not alpine at all. The air is bath-warm, wet enough to condense on any cooler leaf, and the ground underfoot gives like a saturated sponge. Water is not in ponds, exactly. Water is near the surface of everything. It beads on stems. It runs in bright sheets over root-mats. It weeps steadily out of walls.

Because there are walls.

That is the second thing, and then it becomes the first. The swamp has terraces. It has towers. It has long, straight channels cut — no, grown — between raised basins, and arched bridges over them, and flights of broad, shallow steps leading from one level of water to the next. Wherever you look, the surface has been made into something. There is no bare rock to find, no soil in the ordinary sense. If you push a hand down through the green, you do not reach dirt. You reach older construction: densely woven root-fiber, packed and mineralized, then a layer of hollow stem segments laid like bricks, then a darker layer of composted leaf-masonry, hot with fermentation, then more of the same, going down. The mountain, if there was one, is long buried under what the plants have built on top of themselves.

And there are only plants. That has to be said because the place looks inhabited and it is not. No insects working the flowers. No birds in the towers, no frogs in the basins, no burrowers in the warm compost walls, no fish troubling the channels. No footprints but the ones you are making, and those fill with water and close behind you. Pollination here is water and wind. Reproduction is runners, fragments, spores carried in the runoff, tubers dropped from height and rooting where they roll. The entire region — basin after basin opening away from you across a high, uneven plateau, easily the size of a valley system — is one restless botanical metabolism, building for no tenant.

The buildings, if you can call them that, are sparse. That is what keeps it from being jungle. Between structures there is a great deal of open, low ground: plazas of interlaced groundcover, pale green and springy, kept cropped flat by its own growth habit, steaming faintly in the sun after every passing shower. Across those emptinesses, at distances of fifty or a hundred meters, single constructions rise.

Some are unmistakably functional, in a plant's terms. A tower of braided lianas around a core of stiff, silica-edged stalks, twenty meters tall, lifting a crown of broad leaves above its neighbors into unobstructed light. An aqueduct — a living trough of fused stems, its inner surface slick and root-lined — carrying a fast stream across a plaza at head height and pouring it, roaring, into a cistern walled with overlapping leaves sealed in wax. A dam across a gully, built of bundled reeds and mud-free fibercrete the plants exude and harden, holding back a mirror of brown-green water that seeps through a hundred controlled leaks.

Others are harder to justify. There are rooms. Enclosures with doorways and window openings oriented to the morning light, roofed with woven fronds, floored with matting root — and nothing inside but a slightly different humidity. There are staircases that climb a tower on the outside and arrive at a platform with no continuation. Colonnades of identical trunk-pillars, each one a separate plant trained by its neighbors' shade into perfect verticality, supporting a canopy that functions as a roof over an empty court where rain is collected and loudly guttered away through hollow stems. No one designed these as architecture, presumably. They are what you get when light-competition, water-channeling, support, and shade-making are pursued by interlocking species for a very long time at very high speed: forms that converge, again and again, on walls and arches and thresholds, because those are shapes that manage sun and water well.

And the speed is the real climate here. Over the heat and the wetness, this is a turbulent place.

Nothing is finished. A wall you pass in the morning is, by afternoon, two meters longer, its end still soft and pale and exuding, new leaves unrolling from its top edge like green flags snapping in the updraft. A channel reroutes itself as a living dam grows across it cell by cell; the water rises, mutters, finds the new spillway, and takes it with a sudden white rush. Towers do not so much fall as get deliberately undermined by their own support plants dying back in sequence — the whole structure sighs, lists, and comes down in a slow, creaking avalanche of wet timber and foliage into a basin, where it is immediately colonized. Within hours the wreck is a raised bed. Within days it is a foundation. Seedlings are not a stage here so much as a weather.

The sound follows from that. It is loud in a way that has nothing to do with animals. Water goes through hollow bamboo-like conduits and drums them. Water drops from terrace to terrace in a dozen permanent falls around any plaza, so that conversation has to be pitched over a constant fluvial roar. Stems under hydraulic pressure squeal and tick. Big leaves in the hot wind slap together like wet canvas. Now and then a structural member, grown too fast and full of sap, splits along its length with a crack you feel in your teeth, and a whole section of green wall shudders. Under it all, in the enclosed rooms and cistern chambers, you can hear a lower note: fermentation, bubbles moving in warm, enclosed water, the place digesting its own previous versions.

Young is written everywhere in its edges. Despite the depth of old construction underneath, the visible landscape looks recently-emerged, unweathered. New ridges of plant-mineral are sharp, almost serrated, their silica teeth not yet dulled by flow. Fresh spillways cut bright channels. The newest leaves have that translucent, yellow-green, just-unfurled look, and the newest towers stand at angles not yet corrected by phototropism, leaning into the wind like things built yesterday — which they were. Erosion has not had time to be an author here. Growth outruns it. Decay outruns it. Anything softening you see is rot, not weather.

The water cycle and the building cycle are the same cycle. Afternoon heat pulls vapor off every plaza and basin in visible veils; it condenses on the high towers and rains back down their leaf-gutters even when the sky is clear. A basin is allowed to fill until its rim-plants bloom — brief, white, scent-heavy flowers with no one to pollinate them but the overflow — and then a section of rim dies back in a coordinated strip, the basin drains in a warm flood across the plaza, carrying fragments and spores to the next foundation, and the empty basin floor, exposed to full sun for the first time in weeks, erupts. You can watch a meadow become chest-high in a day. You can watch it begin, by the second day, to differentiate into walls.

Rules, if you stay long enough to learn them:

Height is temporary and everyone knows it but the towers. Light is seized, not kept.

Nothing dry stays dry. A dead structure is not a ruin, it is food and scaffolding at once, and it will be incorporated before it can become picturesque.

Empty space is not unused. The plazas are solar collectors, evaporation pans, floodways. The sparseness is load-bearing.

And no room is for anyone. Doors open onto courts where the only event is rain arriving at a new angle. Windows frame views for no eyes. The city — and at regional scale, walking it for days from basin to terraced basin, it is a city, roofed courts giving way to open aqueduct country giving way to a district of towers so densely interbraced that their canopy makes a second, elevated plaza above the first — the city goes on building itself upward on its own high ground, louder and hotter and greener, indifferent to being uninhabited.

At evening the light goes normal and amber and, for perhaps twenty minutes, growth sounds seem to outnumber water sounds: a whole valley of stems extending, leaves unfolding, tendrils tightening audibly around new supports. Then the stored heat lifts a wind off the cloud-sea below the rim, the towers take it and sway together like masts, loose water is shaken from every surface at once in a brief, manufactured rain — and the building, somewhere out of sight, with a long woody groan, begins again to come down and start over.
