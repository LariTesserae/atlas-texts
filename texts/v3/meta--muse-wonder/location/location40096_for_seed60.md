It is a single low bowl of ground, no larger than a village green, and it is full to the brim.

From the levee that rings it you look down first only on green. Not a field of green, not a wood with green in it — green as the substance of the place itself. Canopy, wall, floor, roof-beam and stair are all plant. Where you expect soil there is a woven mat of roots, ankle-deep and springy, exhaling water with every step. Where you expect a wall there is a hedge grown so thick and so old that its inner branches have fused into pillars. Above, fig-like limbs have met and grafted over the center, leaving ragged skylights. Ordinary daylight falls through them, warm and yellow at noon, but it arrives broken, sieved through three and four layers of leaf, so the lower air is always in a green, moving shade.

It is warm down there, and wet without ever quite raining. The bowl sits low, just above a marsh plain, and water is never more than a handspan below the root-mat. In places it breaks through as still, black lenses between the roots. In other places it stands in shallow concrete sumps, sweats down pipework, condenses on every cool leaf and metal plate and drips, steadily, all day. Your shoes are damp within minutes. The air tastes of iron and sap.

Only when you push inside — and you must push, there is no empty path, only narrower and less narrow gaps between stems — does the second nature of the place declare itself.

There is a factory in the grove. Or there is a grove in a factory that has long since given up deciding which is which.

Low engine houses of riveted steel stand at intervals around the bowl, their roofs collapsed or lifted off by trunks growing straight through them. A row of cylindrical presses, tall as men, stands on a concrete plinth now cracked into islands by roots. Copper pipes run overhead, but half of them end mid-air and are continued by hollow, woody vines of exactly the same diameter, jointed with bark rings where the flanges used to be. Gauges still tick behind glass filmed with algae. Valves wear handwheels overgrown with moss, and are turned, apparently, by the slow thickening and thinning of the stems trained around their spokes.

It is not a ruin. Nothing here looks newly broken. The rust has gone to a deep, stable brown and stopped. The concrete is weathered smooth. The plants have not smashed the machines so much as studied them for fifty or a hundred years and grown into their habits. Trunks that were guided up scaffolding are square in cross-section for the first ten feet. Leaves trained against a radiator grille have taken its rectangular lattice. A whole hedgerow has learned to grow in a straight line along a buried cable run, its roots following the faint warmth.

And the machines still work, after their own slow fashion.

Deep under the root-mat a pump turns. You feel it before you hear the intake — a thump in the soles of your feet, then another, hours apart in its effects if not in its stroke. Water is drawn from the marsh lenses, lifted through the pipe-and-vine system, and released at the top of the bowl to fall back through leaf-chutes and woven screens. Resinous pods, grown in ordered thickets around the presses, swell, split with a wet crack, and drop a pale amber pulp onto conveyor slats made of flattened cane and old belt rubber. Somewhere, muffled by foliage, gears mesh.

The sound is the surprise. For a place that changes so slowly, it is loud. A continuous roar at conversational height: water hissing through a thousand leaf nozzles, hollow stems knocking together as pressure moves through them, the pump’s bass pulse, the creak and sigh of wood under hydraulic load, leaves rasping leaf as the whole canopy adjusts an inch toward the light. You have to raise your voice. The few people who tend the place — there are sheds for them, a control hut with a corrugated roof held down by living lashings, tool racks hung on a tree that has grown hooks for them — mostly use hand signs inside the bowl. Short, broad gestures. Point, wait, cut.

Because cutting is engineering here, and engineering is pruning.

To fix a leak you do not replace a pipe, you coax a new vine around the old one over a season and let the sapwood seal it. To increase pressure you thin a canopy panel and let more sun drive the transpiration lift. To shut the works down for winter you would have to persuade a single organism — for at ground level, if you follow the root grafts, most of what fills the bowl joins up into one immense, many-trunked individual — to go dormant, and it has not done so in living memory. It has negotiated terms with iron instead: it will keep the geometry of the plant, it will not crush the main shaft; in return the shaft keeps turning and brings it water even in dry months, and the presses take only the pulp it sheds anyway.

Ecology here has very little room for anything else. Epiphytes ride on the main plant, ferns fill the pipe elbows, mosses coat the north side of every tank, algae make the sumps opaque. But there is no understory in competition, no bare earth for opportunists. Every litre of light is accounted for. Fauna is correspondingly rare, and is noticed when it comes. A single grey heron lands sometimes on the tallest press housing in the morning and stands there, absurdly still above the noise. Small frogs live in the leaf axils where water gathers, and call rarely, one note at a time. Insects are few and specialized, mostly soft-bodied things that live inside stems. The absence is palpable — no birdsong chorus competes with the works. What song there is, is the works.

By afternoon the whole floor has risen, perhaps two inches, as sap pressure builds. The black pools shrink. Paths you used in the morning are closed by swollen roots at dusk, and new ones have opened elsewhere as stems relax. This is the fastest change the place allows itself, and it still feels geological. Leaves turn, pods ripen, a new buttress root frames a doorway over the course of a summer. Everything else is endurance.

You leave by climbing the levee again, ears ringing, clothes steamed damp and smelling of crushed green. From above, the factory disappears. Only the bowl of foliage shows, vibrating almost imperceptibly, letting off a thin plume where warm water falls back through leaves into shadow — a grove that breathes like an engine, an engine that has learned, patiently, how to grow.
