It is a bowl in the sky, and it is screaming.

You reach it last, after the long climb through country that has already given up on living. Four thousand meters up the air is thin and cool and cut clean — maybe five degrees, less in shadow — and the light up here is ordinary daylight, which makes it stranger. No gloom, no alien color. Just hard white sun, blue distance, black rock, and below the rim a drop to a plateau so dry it looks powdered. No snow despite the cold. No streams, no damp, no dark soil. Your throat closes within minutes of arriving. Your lips feel papery. The wind takes whatever moisture your breath makes before you know you’ve made it.

Then you go over the rim, down three meters of wind-polished stone, and the volume becomes physical.

The place is small enough to disbelieve. A natural amphitheatre, perhaps a hundred and eighty meters across, scooped out of the mountaintop — an old, settled hollow, not a fresh crater. Its walls have been smoothed for ten thousand years into flutes and organ-pipes and long curved ribs, basalt and pale rhyolite worn into shapes that happen, very precisely, to sing. The jet, dipping here as it crosses the range, hits those flutes and does not leave. It gets caught. It circles. It layers tone on tone on tone until the air in the bowl is less air than a standing, shaking event.

You do not hear it so much as get occupied by it. It hurts in the molars. It hurts in the sternum, where your ribs try to keep a separate rhythm and fail. Shouting produces nothing. Even the implanted kind of communication — bone-conduction, sealed helmets — jitters and drops. The old hands who work here speak by pressing their foreheads together, or by laying a palm flat on one of the speaking-plates set into the ground and feeling the words come up through the wrist. Most of the time, they simply don’t speak. After an hour your own thoughts feel loud enough.

And yet, in this dry, vibrating emptiness, everything that can live is living at maximum.

Most of the bowl is nothing. That is the first true thing. Black grit, glassy grit, stretches between presences with almost insulting vacancy — a cathedral floor after the furniture has been taken out. If you stand in the wrong ten meters, there is literally nothing at head height, nothing at knee height, only shaking air and dust being dragged in spirals so fast it draws lines. The vortex is not one vortex. It breaks and reforms twenty times a minute, throws up brief transparent funnels, drops them, spins grit devils that run along the wall and explode against the rock, sending a fresh note howling sideways through the chord.

Then you find a node.

At certain points — where the standing waves in the bowl cross and momentarily cancel, or pile up to a pressure thick enough to lean on — life has packed itself ecstatically. Low, lush growth, ankle- to waist-high, in clumps so dense you cannot see the ground inside them. No plant here transpires; none of them would survive giving water away, and in any case they have none to give. Break a stem and nothing wets your finger. You get a smear of cold, turpentine-smelling oil, or a bead of something silvery and non-aqueous that crawls away from the heat of your skin. The leaves are felted, rubbery, cool to the touch, many-chambered inside like lungs. Teal ferns with fronds that clench and unclench in time with the bass note. Ground-hugging mats of violet moss that are not moss, rippling constantly as thousands of tiny bladders inflate and sigh out dry gas. Pale balloon-trees, barely two meters tall, their trunks wrapped in insulating bark like cork, their canopies not leaves but clusters of taut, translucent sacs that drum in the wind and harvest — literally harvest — vibration. They eat sound the way lowland plants eat light. Their roots do not dig for water. They dig for resonance, seeking the exact depth at which the rock hums strongest, and they move: slowly in calm weather, obscenely fast here, a root-mat loosening, the whole plant slewing half a meter across the grit on a cushion of agitated dust until it finds a better note and locks down again with an audible *thunk* you feel in your shins.

Because nothing here is still. That is the rule you learn in the body before the mind gets it: stillness is what dies. The plants thrash even when they are rooted. The unrooted ones — tumble-pods, seed-bells, whole juvenile shrubs wrapped in their own spiny lattice — are thrown endlessly around the circuit of the bowl, planting for a minute, a second, tearing free. Dust is not lying on anything; it is performing patterns, Chladni figures blooming and dissolving on flat stones, spirals of silver spores hanging for a heartbeat in the shape of a fern, then scoured apart.

Above the growth, the fauna. Abundant is a mild word for it. The air at the nodes is thick with wings — but spaced, again, across a huge roaring vacancy, so that the bowl can be simultaneously empty and swarming. Glass midges in their millions, each one a speck of engineered chitin, forming brief, coherent swarms that spell shapes: a sheet, a column, a sudden hand. They are not only animals. Up close — on a speaking-plate, where a few settle and let you look — you see they are jointed at scales too fine for evolution to bother with, their wingbeats phase-locked to the dominant tone, generating lift from the sound field itself. They ride pressure the way gulls ride thermals, and some of them carry. They carry seeds, they carry pollen like black mercury, they carry the disassembled pieces of the place’s technology from one structure to another in bright, flowing streams.

There are larger things. Drum-moths, big as both your hands, ears covering most of their bodies, who let the noise pass through them and come out amplified, cleaner, tuned. Click-beetles armored like little bells. A kind of gliding lizard, membrane-winged, soot-colored, that has perhaps not touched the ground in generations; it folds itself into the vortex, sleeps on the wing in the warmest layer of the circling air, wakes to snap midges out of a swarm without breaking the swarm’s shape. Once, something high on the wall gives a cry that cuts the entire chord in half for a second — not louder, just *placed*, exactly into a gap in the frequencies — and every head, human and otherwise, turns. Nothing follows it. The cry is not repeated. It may have been a warning, a mating call, or a piece of maintenance.

Because the scattered buildings are maintaining this, or being maintained by it — the order of operations is not clear.

There are six, maybe seven low structures around the bowl’s edge, and the number changes. Not because you miscount. Because one of them, on your second circuit, has partly walked. They are squat, windowless resonators, igloo-shaped, made of a stone-colored material that is not stone: it knits a visible crack shut over the course of a minute, sweating a faint heat. Their doors are membranes that beat like eardrums. Their roofs carry arrays of hair-fine vanes, tuning forks, dishes that track nothing in the sky and everything in the air. None of this is technology you recognize the operating principles of. There are no cables, no panels, no hum of electricity as such — only pressure, phase, fields you can feel as a sudden wrongness in your inner ear when you pass too close, a sense that the air there is briefly *thicker* and holding you up at the ankles. A slab that should weigh a ton lifts, silently, on a cushion of focused sound, hangs while midges stream underneath it carrying bright granules, and settles again in a new orientation. A wall unfolds itself two centimeters taller to meet a shift in the wind. The structures are tuning the bowl. The bowl is feeding the structures: every surface is furred with the same oil-leaved growth, every vent exhales air slightly warmer and markedly calmer than what it took in.

It may have been a listening station, once. It may have been a power plant, harvesting the mountain’s permanent scream and beaming it somewhere you will never see. It may be older than the people who now pretend to run it — there are cairns here too, genuinely old, hand-stacked, each topped with a stretched animal skin gone to mineral, still vibrating faintly in sympathy. Someone, a long time ago, found a loud place and loved it correctly.

The rules emerge, as you stay, as prohibitions in your own nervous system:

Do not stand in an anti-node when the chord shifts; the pressure drop will empty your lungs more efficiently than drowning, and the local growth avoids those spots for a reason. Do not try to take a plant out of its clump — divorced from the shared root-mat and its tuned pocket of air, it goes papery and dead in minutes, its bladders caving, its oil drying to varnish. Do not expect a lull you can rest in. There are variations — terrifying crescendos when a gust stacks on the standing wave and every balloon-tree flattens and every moth dives for the mats — but there is no quiet. Quiet, the speaking-plates tell you, is the disaster scenario. If the vortex ever failed, if the resonators ever fell out of phase, the bowl would do in an afternoon what unprotected altitude desert does: freeze-dry, scour clean, empty out. The lushness here is not despite the violence. It *is* the violence, metabolized.

By evening the light goes ordinary-gold and the cold deepens and still nothing settles. The swarms grow denser and more architectural as the temperature falls, building brief warm shapes to roost inside. The plants close their felt leaves around pockets of their own made weather. The resonators retune, note by slow note, for the night wind, and the chord of the bowl drops by a third and somehow becomes more, not less, deafening — it moves into the frequencies your skeleton is for.

You leave by the one leeward gap in the rim, ears ringing into a silence that, outside, turns out to be merely the normal high wind of a dry mountain. Behind you the bowl goes on without pause: empty, crowded, cold, thriving, eating its own uproar forever.

You can hear it, diminishing, for another six kilometers of descent.
