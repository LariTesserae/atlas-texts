You don't find it from above. From above there is only flat, wind-polished cold — a white so uniform it erases distance, with a shallow depression where old snow has settled a fraction lower than the snow around it. The hatch, if you know where to put your hands, is under that depression, flush with the ice. It is not locked. It is just frozen shut most of the year, and the metal is the same temperature as everything else.

Below it, a shaft goes down farther than a ladder should. Rock replaces ice after the first few meters, then cut rock replaces rough rock. The air on the way down does not get warmer, which is the first wrong thing. It gets damper. Your breath comes back at you. Condensation beads on the rungs and refreezes into small clear collars. Somewhere out of sight, water is ticking down stone, one drop, then a long pause, then another.

The chamber at the bottom is small enough to hold in your attention all at once, if it were empty. It is not empty. It is full in the way a forest floor is full.

It is perhaps twelve meters across, and low — three meters of headroom at the center, less where the ceiling sags. The ceiling is basalt and old ice pressed together, smoothed by a very long argument between freezing and thawing. Roots have found the seams. Water has polished them. Nothing here looks carved or blasted in a hurry. The walls have settled into their final shape and have been that shape long enough for mineral skins to grow over repairs, over older roots, over the edges of things people left.

And in the dark, it is green.

Not bright green. Black-green, blue-green, the green of things that live under deep water or under snow. Moss first, everywhere moss — over the floor in a carpet so thick it swallows your boots to the ankle, over fallen branches, over the lower walls, up the legs of the few built things still standing. Then ferns, or things close enough to ferns, with fronds curled tight against the cold, unfurling so slowly you could watch for an hour and swear they had not moved. Horsetails in the damp hollows, jointed and hollow and beaded with frost. Low shrubs along the back wall with needle leaves and bark like wet rope, their canopies bent flat by the low ceiling and interlaced until they make a second, living ceiling a meter above the floor. Under them, lichens spread in pale coins over stone. In the shallowest pockets of meltwater, films of algae hold perfectly still, filmed at the edges with ice.

It is lush, absurdly lush for a place this cold and this dark, and layered the way rainforest is layered, only compressed down to the scale of a room. You cannot cross it in a straight line. You turn sideways between stems. Leaves brush your sleeves and leave cold moisture. Seed heads nod against your knees. If you stand still, the density stops feeling like separate plants and starts feeling like one continuous body you have been allowed inside.

There is no sunlight here. There has never been sunlight here. There is barely light at all.

Your eyes adjust to almost nothing: a pinprick of amber on a half-buried maintenance pod, another on the far wall, the faintest phosphor line where a service conduit enters the floor. That is all. The rest is shape learned by proximity — darker masses against less dark, the sheen of wet leaf when you move, the occasional minute flash as a condensation drop catches one of those indicator lights on its way down. The plants do not glow. They do not need to pretend at being luminous. Whatever feeds them is not arriving as visible light.

It is arriving through the floor.

If you kneel — and the moss is so cold it burns through fabric after a minute — you can see it where the carpet has been worn thin near the entrance: a lattice under the soil, filament-fine, silver but not quite metal, following the root systems and sometimes joining them, sometimes running in patient geometries no root would make. It is warm there. Not warm like a machine is warm. Exactly, narrowly above freezing, root by root, leaf by leaf, while the air a hand's breadth above the soil stays at twenty-five below. The cold is not kept out of the chamber. The cold is the chamber. It is simply negotiated with, locally, continuously, at a scale too fine to see working.

That is the technology of this place, and it is almost entirely absent to look at. Three egg-shaped pods, shoulder-high, sit scattered among the shrubs, their lower halves mossed over, their upper shells scratched and mineral-stained. A bench, or something that was once a bench, near the only open patch of stone. A hand pump that no longer connects to anything. A plaque by the shaft whose lettering has been filled in with ice so many times it now reads as braille. Nothing hums loudly. Nothing blinks in sequence. Once, perhaps every few hours, a pod will sigh — a soft depressurization — and a mist, finer than breath, drifts low over the moss for less than a minute and is gone. The plants take it. The excess condenses on the ceiling and resumes its slow ticking.

The stillness is the other wrong thing, after the greenness.

A place this vegetated should be loud with small lives. There should be insects in the moss, worms in the soil, something nesting in the shrubs. There is nothing. No fauna at all, not even the accidental kind. The quarantine is perfect and has been perfect for a very long time. Pollination, if the word still applies, happens by charge: on misting cycles the air itself takes on a faint static prickle, and pollen lifts from the low flowers — small, white, nearly scentless things that open facing downward — and moves in straight, unnatural drifts from plant to plant, carried by fields shaped like absent bees. Spores settle where the lattice wants them. Seeds fall and are drawn a few centimeters through the moss overnight to better ground. Nothing eats. Nothing rots the way it should; old leaves are dismantled from below, filament by filament, and returned to the lattice without smell.

So the sounds are only water, cloth, and your own breathing. A drop from the ceiling every twenty or thirty seconds, landing sometimes on stone with a tick, sometimes silently into moss. The creak of frost in the shaft above when the surface wind changes. At very long intervals, a deep, slow tick from the rock itself as it contracts. Your footsteps are muffled to nothing. If you speak, the vegetation takes the edges off your voice immediately.

Everything moves at the pace those sounds suggest. A frond takes weeks to open. Frost creeps in from the shaft a few millimeters a day along the ceiling, and the lattice pushes it back a few millimeters a night; the boundary between them is visible as a white lace line above the door that never quite advances and never quite retreats. A shrub on the east wall has been growing around one of the pods for longer than the pod has been obsolete, splitting its canopy politely around the shell. Condensation forms, freezes, thaws a fraction, reforms in a slightly different pattern. This is not a garden in bloom. It is a garden in equilibrium, holding a pose.

The rules here follow from that. You move slowly, because the paths are not paths, only temporary agreements between stems. You do not bring food in. You do not touch the pods, not because they are dangerous, but because the moss growing over their sensors took decades to arrange itself and the field adjusts to it now. You keep your lamp off; the indicators are enough, and after ten minutes the dark is not empty, it is textured — wet and dry, leaf and stone, open air and the places where the shrubs make the air feel closer. Your fingers go numb. Your collar gets damp and then stiffens. The place does not make itself comfortable for you. It simply permits you to be in it without immediately killing the things it is keeping alive.

Because that is, finally, what it feels like: not a wilderness, and not a machine. A pocket that was hidden below the cold on purpose, planted or preserved or started as an experiment and then outlived by its own systems. The builders left small, ordinary traces and then stopped being necessary. The lattice learned the roots. The roots learned the ceiling. The cold learned where it was allowed. And the garden went on growing in the dark, densely, quietly, without a single animal to witness it, at a pace too slow for grief or triumph — one drop, one filament, one unfurling leaf at a time.
