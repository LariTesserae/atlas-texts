It is always dark here, and it is not an empty dark.

It is warm, wet, close dark — dark that condenses on your skin, that drips from your hair, that fills your mouth when you breathe. At four thousand meters above a sea no one on the plateau has ever seen, the air should be thin and cold. It is neither. It is thick as broth, pressed down by the cloud-ceiling that has not lifted in living memory, and heated from below by springs, by marsh-gas, by the engines buried in the mountain. You climb toward it and the barren lower slopes get *warmer* as you ascend, until the wind coming off the top smells of hot iron, wet leaf-rot, sulfur, and animal musk, and carries a sound that never stops.

You hear the plateau long before you reach it. Days before.

### The Rim

The edge is not a cliff so much as a long, worn shoulder. The mountains that made this continent were ground down eons ago into rounded backs and smooth, elephantine domes, their valleys filled in with silt and slag and soil until only the gentlest swells remain. Black basalt, soft-shouldered by rain. Red clay worn to suede. Stone steps where rivers used to fall, now polished by a million years of mist into troughs that run perpetually with a finger's depth of warm water.

Everything weeps. Rock faces sweat in clear beads. Clefts exhale damp. Moss, three hand-spans thick in places, holds water like a sponge and releases it in slow curtains whenever something heavy passes and shakes the ground.

At the rim, the waterfalls go over and are never heard to land. They pour out of hanging marshes, out of perched black lakes, out of the mouths of condenser towers, and they fall into a darkness below the cloud-line where even their roar thins out and vanishes. Travelers stop at listening posts here — small, riveted iron shelters, the only buildings for a hundred kilometers — and press their ears to the shared wall to feel if the falls are running full. A full fall means rain inland. A thin fall means the factories are drinking hard.

There is no horizon. There is a direction where the wind stops echoing.

### A Continent That Makes Its Own Weather

Inside, the plateau is vast enough that it does not borrow weather; it manufactures it.

It is two, perhaps three thousand kilometers across — no survey has ever closed in the dark — rolling highland without a single sharp peak, cut by broad, ancient river terraces wide as provinces, pocked with warm lakes, drowned forests, and industrial basins that have been worked so long they have eroded back into landscape.

Heat rises from the engine valleys in great, invisible columns. It hits the cold underside of the permanent cloud deck, condenses, and falls again as a rain so warm and steady in those districts that the ferns there never dry, their fronds bowing and springing, bowing and springing under the weight. The rain runs off into marsh, into cisterns, into intake grates. The pumps lift it, boil it, use it to drive turbines, and vent it as steam, which rises, condenses, and falls again. Some of the water in a given lake has been through a turbine a hundred times. It tastes faintly of copper, and it is always bath-warm.

Between the rain districts lie the listening plains: seas of head-high grass-analogues — not grass, but ribbed, fleshy blades that click together when the wind moves them, millions of them, a dry, castanet chatter under the rain-roar. And beyond those, the deep forest.

The dark has remade photosynthesis into something else. The trees do not reach for light. They reach for heat. Their trunks spiral gently toward vent fields, toward factory walls, toward the warm bodies of sleeping herds. Their leaves are vast, soft, black paddles, hairy underneath, engineered by evolution to drink infrared and condensation at once. Under the canopy the density doubles: lianas thick as arms, dripping; fungus shelves that give underfoot like wet bread; bacterial mats in the hot springs that form the base of everything — sulfur-yellow (one imagines; no one has seen color here in generations) crusts that feed shrimp-like grazers, that feed blind fish in the marsh channels, that feed the great slow things that move through the trees at night-that-is-always-night, shaking rain loose in sheets.

It is lush in the way a mouth is lush. Enclosed. Layered. Too much life pressed into each cubic meter of air: spores, midges, seed-pods bursting with an audible *pop*, pollen that coats the tongue and tastes sweet and peppery.

### The Loud World

Silence, here, is a symptom of death. The healthy plateau is deafening.

The baseline is the rain on leaves, a continuous frying roar. Under it, the rivers. Under those, the industry: piston-valleys where ore-crushers the size of villages rise and fall in a rhythm you feel in your molars; rail lines where driverless freight trains, kilometers long, feel their way along at walking pace, keening against the rails, blowing low horns every fifty meters so that herds will move; condenser stations that inhale the wet air of an entire watershed through louvered throats and exhale it dry, sighing like sleeping giants.

Everything alive adds to it because everything alive navigates by it. Insects drum on hollow stems. Frogs — thousands of species — inflate throat sacs against tree trunks to use them as amplifiers. The canopy herds, eyeless grazers with sonar pits along their flanks and hides plated in wet, overlapping leaves of keratin, move in columns of hundreds, each animal clicking steadily, each click answered, so that a migration sounds like approaching hail. High above the canopy, in air no one visits, blind fliers with twelve-meter wingspans ride the factory updrafts, calling down in booms so low they are mostly a pressure in the chest, listening for the echo off the cloud-ceiling to tell them where they are.

Predators have learned the trick of rhythm. The softest-footed of them, a long-bodied thing that hunts the rail corridors, walks exactly in time with the pistons. You never hear it coming. You notice, instead, a half-beat missing in the world.

And the echoes come back. Sound carries obscenely far in this dense, saturated air, bouncing between cloud deck and wet ground for days. A crusher-station starting up two hundred kilometers away arrives at your camp as a faint, sourceless regularity in the rain — until, days later, your own shouted name returns to you off the far rim mountains, stretched and flattened, like someone else remembering you.

There are places where this fails. Marsh-gas pockets. Deaf zones, where a particular composition of warm fog swallows sound within a few meters. Entering one is like being struck deaf and blind simultaneously in a world where you were already blind: the roar cuts off, your own heartbeat goes muffled, your guide-rope goes slack-feeling even when it isn't. Nothing lives in the deaf zones for long. You find the bones by touch, perfectly preserved in the mud, still articulated, as if the animals had simply become confused and lain down.

### Industry, Old as Geology

The factories are not *on* the plateau, exactly. They have been here long enough that the plateau is partly made of them.

Strata in the river cuts alternate: basalt, peat, compressed ash, a layer of rusted machine parts worn to pebbles, peat again, a fossil forest, a layer of slag glassed smooth as river stone. The current industry mines the old industry. Autonomous excavators — slow, house-sized, entirely enclosed, because an open furnace here shows not as light but as a *hole* in the dark, the photon-eating spores in the air devouring visible light within centimeters — dig up the compacted remains of their predecessors, smelt them, and extrude new rails, new pump housings, new condenser vanes.

The production is fully scaled and entirely scattered. There is no capital, no factory-city. There are nodes: a pumping cathedral sunk into a marsh, its roof a garden of ferns; a lone smelter in a forest clearing, its walls sweated over with moss, with a village of twenty maintenance families living in felt huts against its warm flank; a rail siding with one hut, one water tower, one operator who has never met another operator, who communicates with the trains by striking a tuned rail with a hammer and listening to the answer come back through the ground.

Power is water and heat. Water is air. Raw material is the past. The product, as far as anyone on the plateau can tell, is the continuation of the process itself: rails maintained, pumps maintained, condensers maintained, so that rails and pumps and condensers can be maintained. Trains arrive at smelters loaded with old machines and leave loaded with new machines for the pumps. If there is an export, it goes over the rim on the waterfalls, pieces at a time, rusting as they fall.

The human presence is small and adapted. Eyeless by custom if not by birth — many families sew decorative patterns over the vestigial sockets — navigating by click-whistle, by rope-lines strung hut to hut, by an exquisite attention to air pressure on the skin of the face. Time is not measured in days. It is measured in condenser cycles, in the interval between migration-booms, in shifts of the great pistons, in the number of times the rain has warmed and cooled against the forest floor. A child learns geography as a sequence of sounds and smells and underfoot textures: *from the hut, forty minutes of grass-click, then mud that sucks, then the river that says its name over round stones, then the place where the air gets hot and tastes of iron, that's the pumps, don't cross the humming line.*

### Rules

Light does not travel. Make a spark inside a sealed box, open it: nothing. The spore-fog eats it. Stars, if any, are a rumor of the rim shepherds. Fire must be tended entirely by touch, and cooks judge doneness by sound and smell.

Sound *does* travel, further than loyalty. Never shout your true intentions on open ground; the rim will repeat them next week.

Heat is direction. If you are lost, walk toward warmth for industry, toward cool wet for forest, but never toward silence.

Water is never far and never entirely safe — too hot near the vents, too hungry (in the leech sense, in the current sense) in the marsh channels, and in the oldest cisterns, so recycled that drinking it gives people dreams in piston-rhythm for a night.

Nothing here is still. The herds move, the harvesters creep through the forest eating fallen trunks and excreting mulch warm enough to steam, the rain rises as steam and falls as rain, the plateau itself is mantled in a skin of life that digests stone and rust at the same slow rate. The erosion and the building have reached an agreement. The place is finished, and it is still being made, at the same volume, in the dark, without pause.

Stand anywhere on it — on the wet grass plains with the click-hail of a migration approaching through rain, one hand on a rope, your face turned up to a sky that presses back warm and close — and what you feel is not wilderness, and not a factory.

It is a single huge organism that long ago stopped distinguishing between the two, and is breathing, very loudly, in its sleep that is not sleep, on top of the world, in the middle of a night that never promised to end.
