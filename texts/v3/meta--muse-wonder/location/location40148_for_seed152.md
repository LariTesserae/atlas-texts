You would not find it from the street.

From outside it is just another stretch of blank wall in a low, warm town — sun-bleached mudbrick, a narrow wooden door with no handle on the street side, the top of a fig tree showing over the roofline. The town around it is still being made. Half the lanes are only packed earth marked out with twine, half the houses have walls but no roofs yet, piles of reed and pale, unfired brick waiting under palm mats. Nothing has been worn smooth. Corners are still sharp. Footprints still hold their shape in the silt.

You have to duck through the passage beside the door. It goes dark for three steps, cool against your shoulders, and then opens.

Inside is a single courtyard, perhaps twelve paces across, sunken a little below the level of the lane. It is enclosed on all four sides by new walls and the backs of small houses, high enough that most of the day is shade. Only near noon does a clean patch of sun reach the ground, and then it moves slowly from the west wall to the center and is gone.

The ground has not finished becoming ground. This whole quarter was marsh at the edge of the river plain until the banks were built up and the water drawn off, and the courtyard is still settling into itself. The soil is dark river silt, fine as ash when dry, black and giving when damp. It yields a fraction under bare feet. In the lowest corner, where the rectangle dips almost imperceptibly, a shallow skin of water collects after rain and lingers for a day or two, clear enough to reflect the wall. Elsewhere there is no standing water at all — just dampness that returns every morning, beading on the undersides of leaves, darkening the bases of the walls, condensing on clay pots left out overnight.

Because of this, you go barefoot here. Shoes would tear the surface. Flat stones, still rough from the quarry, have been laid as stepping places where the silt is firmest, with wide spaces of empty earth between them. The path changes every few weeks as the builders learn where the ground holds and where it sighs and sinks.

The building is done entirely by hand. Baskets of silt carried in from the river cut. Bricks pressed in wooden molds and set to dry under mats, still smelling of straw and wet earth. A half-finished room on the north side has walls only waist-high; green sap still beads where its roof poles were cut. There are no pipes, no glass, no nails that you can see. Water is carried in through the passage in the morning, pot by pot, and stored in the shade in porous jars that sweat slowly all day. Shutters are woven reed. Doors hang on leather.

Sparse, and yet lush. Nothing is planted close. A young fig stands alone near the east wall, already disproportionately alive — trunk as thick as a wrist, leaves broad and dark and overlapping, roots beginning to lift the nearest stepping stone. In the damp corner, a clump of elephant ear has opened three enormous leaves over the temporary pool. Mint crowds a jar by the kitchen doorway, basil another, both kept trimmed to mounds. A vine, planted the month the walls went up, has already reached the top of its pole scaffold and is feeling across the string line toward the roof, its tendrils curling and uncurling in the heat. Between these deliberate plants, pioneers have arrived uninvited and been allowed to stay: moss in the mortar where the wall never quite dries, ferns no bigger than a thumb in the joint of two stones, a single reed at the edge of the seep.

The spaces between are kept empty on purpose. Warm earth, a drying rack, a stack of terracotta tiles not yet laid. The emptiness is what lets air move, barely, along the ground.

It is almost silent. The mud walls are so thick, and so new and dense, that the town outside — voices in the lane, animals, the thump of brick molds — arrives only as a muffled pressure, not as sound. Inside, the loudest thing on most afternoons is a drop falling from a fig leaf hours after the last rain. A leaf turning over in a draft. The soft scrape of a basket set down.

And yet the place is full of animals, and busy with them. They are quiet kinds, or quiet here. Geckos live in the unfinished walls, pale as the plaster, emerging at dusk to wait motionless beside moths twice their size. Tiny frogs, green-black and no longer than a fingernail, sit at the rim of the seep and vanish into it without a splash. Snails work the damp base of the north wall in lines, leaving drying silver on the silt. Swallows have claimed the open eaves of the one finished house and cut back and forth through the rectangle of sky above the courtyard, taking insects on the wing. Dragonflies patrol the pool when it exists and patrol the empty depression just as faithfully when it doesn't, as if remembering the water. At night a cat from the next house crosses the roof tiles — audibly less than you'd think, one considered step at a time — to drink from the jars.

Everything is in motion, just at low volume and at body pace. In the morning the condensation dries and the bricks lighten by a shade. By afternoon the fig leaves have perceptibly turned toward the travelling sun patch, the pool has shrunk a finger's width, the vine has gained the length of a knuckle. A spider rebuilds the web the swallows broke at dawn. A gecko takes the moth it waited for. Mud, dug too wet the day before, slumps a centimeter in its mold and has to be pressed again. This is the rule of building here: you cannot hurry the ground. A wall raised before the course below it has dried will lean by evening. A floor laid before the silt has settled will crack across. So work happens in brief spells at first light, by hand, and the rest of the time everyone waits and watches what the place does on its own.

By late afternoon the light fails early inside the walls. Not darkness, exactly — a greenish, underwater dimness held in the leaves and the shade, the sky above still bright. The air stays warm against the skin, humid enough that cloth never quite feels dry, smelling of basil bruised by passing hands, wet clay, fig sap, and the faint iron smell of the silt. Frogs begin, not calling, just the smallest clicks from the wet corner. The swallows make a last circuit. The jars sweat in the kitchen shadow.

Nothing here looks old, and nothing looks finished. The mortar hasn't crumbled anywhere yet. No path has been worn in. No tree has dropped enough leaves to make soil of its own. It is a room made of earth that has only just stopped being river-bottom, occupied cautiously by people and plants and animals all learning its damp corners at the same time — a pocket of town still in the act of deciding what, solidly, it is going to be.
