It is first seen from the rim, a whole valley made of noise.

High, cool, blasted bright. The air up here is thin enough that the light does not soften — it arrives. Sun on wet stone, sun on white water, sun on mist thrown up and held. From above, in that glare, the basin looks packed solid. Not with trees. There are no trees, no grass, no moss, nothing green that is alive. It is packed with rock and water forced together until there is almost no empty ground between them.

The valley is perhaps four miles long, a shallow oval scooped out of the high plateau, walled by pale, weathered cliffs. Inside it, the floor has broken — long ago, and settled into the breaking — into tens of thousands of boulders, pillars, and leaning slabs. Some are the height of a person. Some are thirty feet tall. Basalt dark at the core, dolomite pale at the worn edges, all of them shouldered in tight against the next, interlocked at the base, rounded by an age of water. Between them are gaps a hand wide, a body wide, rarely more. No soil has ever been allowed to stay. Anything loose was washed out, ground down, carried away.

And through all of it, everywhere, a river that has lost its single body.

It comes in over the north rim, not as one fall but as a lake spilling evenly over a long lip of stone, and it shatters on the way down. By the time it reaches the floor it is already a hundred streams. Then it shatters again. It braids and rebraids through the boulder field in thousands of white channels, drops into slot pools, shoots under arches of jammed stone, reappears foaming. It never gathers, never calms into a proper river again until it leaves, squeezed, at the far end. The whole valley is rapids without a beginning or end.

That is the sound.

It is deafening, and it does not change. Not louder at midday, not softer at night, because the lake above buffers it, because the rocks have settled into positions they will not shift from, because the fall and foam and rebound have reached a perfect, permanent arrangement. A roar with a hiss inside it, and under the hiss a low thudding you feel through the soles of your feet and in your teeth. Standing at the rim you hear it. Stepping down into it, it gets into your chest. Deep between the pillars, reflecting back and forth off wet walls that stand only feet apart, there is nowhere for it to go. It packs the air the way the rocks pack the ground. Shouting produces only a pressure against your own ears. After an hour, your mind begins to edit it out, and then the world goes strangely silent while everything around you is visibly shaking — droplets jumping on pool surfaces, fine spray trembling off ledges, tiny concentric ripples racing across still pockets and colliding.

The water is not deep, mostly. Ankle-deep sheets sliding over stone shelves. Knee-deep runs, thigh-deep pools in the slots, cold enough to ache. But deep is not the measure of wet here. Wet is the condition. Every rock weeps. Condensation forms on shaded faces even in the bright sun and runs in beads. Mist from the rapids drifts in a permanent, waist-high layer through the lower passages, then thins and is remade. Rainbows hang at fixed angles where the spray crosses the light, not arcing and fading but stationed, quivering, in the same places hour after hour. Your skin is damp. Your clothes are damp, and at the same time the high sun is drying the shoulders of your jacket to a stiff warmth. Wet and dry at once, held.

Up close the stone is more various than it looked from above. Black where it is constantly washed. White with mineral crust where spray dries. Rust-orange seams, and a false, poisonous green-blue staining from copper in the water — color that from a distance pretends to be vegetation, and close up is just metal bloom on rock, slick and lifeless. In the quieter side-pools, where the current only sighs through a crack, there are films: amber, opalescent, slightly gelatinous mats of something simpler than a plant, grazing on dissolved stone. They pearl with trapped bubbles. They are scraped away whenever a pool shifts, and they regrow in exactly the same shape.

No path has ever been made here, and none could stay. No cairn would survive being noticed — it would just be one more stone among stones. There is nothing built, nothing broken that was ever made. Navigation is by other means: by the tilt of the floor underfoot, by following a slightly warmer current against your ankles, by putting a palm flat to a pillar and feeling whether its vibration is pitched up or down. Near the main channels the rocks hum at a low note you can feel in the bone of your hand. In the drier, higher jumbles at the valley edges — though dry here only means dripping slower — the note rises, thinner, almost a singing from wind finding the narrow throats between slabs.

That wind is the second engine, but it feeds the first impression of stillness. It pours over the south rim in a steady high-altitude stream, funnels into the slots, and is chopped into a constant tone. It does not gust. It has been shaped by the cliffs into something laminar and relentless. It carries spray horizontally in sheets between the taller pillars, polishes nothing — the polishing was done long ago — and keeps the temperature down even when the light is almost unbearable. Exposed rock in direct sun is warm to the touch. Step into shadow with wet feet and the chill goes straight up your legs. Breathing is work. The brightness makes you squint; the cold air makes your eyes water anyway.

Life, when it appears, appears as an exception startling enough to turn your head — though you will not have heard it come. A snow finch, pale grey, picking drowned insects from the edge of a foam pool, utterly unconcerned by the roar it evolved inside. It does not sing. Nothing here sings to be heard. In a sunlit backwater, a salamander the color of wet ash lies motionless under a ledge, its skin beaded, and is gone without a ripple you can distinguish from the thousand other ripples. Once, perhaps, a sure-footed, shaggy animal picking its way along the rim, looking down but not descending. The water is too mineral, too scouring, too loud for much to settle. What lives here visits, or hides in the few pockets the current forgot.

By late afternoon the light should be changing things, and visually it does — the white stone goes gold, the copper stains deepen, shadows pool blue-black in the slots. But the sound does not deepen with it. The mist does not lift. The streams do not slacken. The rainbows move a hand's breadth and take up another fixed station. It gives the place a quality outside ordinary weather. Not frozen — everything in it is moving violently, all the time — but invariant. Motion as a state, not an event.

You leave by climbing, and the brightness leaves with the altitude, and the roar begins, finally, to seem at a distance to be something the valley is doing rather than something the world is. At the rim, looking back, it is again that packed, glittering field: thousands of wet pillars, thousands of white threads of water, spray lit from above, no green, no roof, no road, no quiet corner anywhere inside it. Only rock settling into rock, water finding the same way down it has always found, and the sound of the two of them, together, not beginning and not ending.
