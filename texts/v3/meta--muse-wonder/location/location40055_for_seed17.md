It is a sea in the wrong place, held too high, and it has been there long enough that the wrongness has become a climate.

From above, it does not look like a lake. Lakes have an other side. This has a horizon. It lies across the top of a continental plateau, three and a half to four kilometres above the lowlands, filling what should have been a country of high valleys. The mountains that made it are still young — geologically just thrust up, unsoftened — and they rim the water in black, unworn arcs, their lower slopes continuing straight down under the surface as knife-ridges of basalt and volcanic glass. Nothing has had time to round off. There are no beaches, only shattered rock going in at a steep angle, and reefs that rise within metres of daylight and then drop away again into trenches.

The water is cold in a way that does not vary. Two degrees at the surface in full sun, two degrees two hundred metres down, two degrees in January and October. It is almost fresh, almost clear. There is no green in it anywhere.

### The emptiness

Nothing grows. That is the first rule, and it shapes everything else.

No weed on the rocks. No algae clouding the shallows. No phytoplankton turning the sunlit layer milky in spring. The water is so transparent that on a calm day from a boat you can see a submerged peak a hundred metres down still resolving, edge on edge, as if through thin air — and seeing it, you have no sense of scale at all, because there is nothing between you and it. No particles, no drift, no life visible at all for hour after hour.

The scale makes liars of the word *empty*. The sea is vast enough to carry its own standing weather: a permanent bank of fog fixed over the western shallows where the plateau rock is thinnest and geothermal warmth meets the cold, a fixed lens of cloud that hangs over the rim falls and never moves, rain that always falls on the same northeast quadrant and nowhere else. Sail across it — and people do, in low steel work-boats with sealed cabins — and you cross from one of its provinces to another over days. The light is ordinary daylight, sun up and sun down, blue at noon, grey when the deck-cloud thickens. It is the distances that curdle it. You can travel for six days and see only water, sky, and your own wake dissolving behind you because there is not even a current strong enough to smear it properly.

Then, on the seventh day, the fauna arrives all at once.

### What lives in place of plants

The food chain here does not begin with light. It begins with broken rock and bad water.

The plateau is still fracturing under its own weight, and where fresh basalt splits underwater it reacts, exhaling hydrogen and sulphur compounds into the cold. Around those vents — and, undeniably, around the warm, mineral-rich discharge pipes of the pump stations — bacterial mats spread in white and rust-orange sheets, the closest thing this world has to meadow. They are not plants. They do not photosynthesize. They eat stone chemistry, and things eat them.

So fauna is abundant here, absurdly so for such a barren volume, but it is abundant the way ore is abundant: concentrated, patchy, and separated by enormous sterile intervals.

Filter-grazers the size of dinner plates cement themselves to vent rock by the millions, opening and closing in absolute synchrony. Glass eels run in schools so dense they change the local colour of the water to smoke, then they are gone and there is nothing for a week. In the mid-water, untroubled by any need to come up for air that is thinner up here than it should be, live the bell-cows: eight, ten, sometimes fourteen metres long, blind, translucent-skinned, with ribs that show like the hoops of a lantern and stomachs faintly luminous from the bacteria they crop off the ridge-flanks. They are placid, solitary, and almost impossible to find until one is simply *there*, occupying the whole of your vision, having approached without displacing any sound you could distinguish from the sound that is already everything.

Whales would be the wrong comparison. They do not sing. Nothing here sings.

### The sound

Because the sea is not supposed to be up here, it is always leaving.

At the southern rim the plateau shears away and the entire drainage of the sea finds a series of notches in the rock and goes over. Not a waterfall so much as a distributed falling: forty kilometres of stepped cataract, water dropping in white terraces from turbine hall to turbine hall all the way down to the lowland plain, generating power on every step. The industrial plant that lines the rim — generators, surge towers, penstocks as wide as railway tunnels, switchyards fenced against the ice — has been running without interruption for a hundred and forty years. So has the pump network that returns lowland groundwater up through boreholes in the plateau, station by station, to exactly replace what the falls take away. Inflow, outflow, rainfall, seepage. The ledger balances. It has always balanced.

The ledger is deafening.

Above water you hear it only when you near the rim, a roar that gets into the fillings of your teeth. Below water it is total. Sound carries in this cold, particle-free sea for hundreds of kilometres, and the cataract-and-turbine chord — a fundamental so low it is felt in the sternum, with a shrieking harmonic structure from the generator halls laid over it — propagates through the whole body of the sea and, through the connected rock, through the plateau itself. Divers describe it not as a noise but as a medium, thicker than the water. Station crews are born to it, work in it, sleep inside it; their children learn sign language before speech and mostly keep using it, because shouting is a waste of breath. Helmets, hulls, and bones all conduct the same note.

It never changes pitch. That is the uncanny part, more than the volume. Storms do not change it. Ice forming at the shadowed margins does not muffle it. In a hundred and forty years of logbooks the spectrogram of the sea is a ruler-straight line. The fauna have evolved inside that line. They navigate by the interference patterns where the roar reflects off submerged ridges, the way bats use their own calls, except the call is provided for them, endlessly, by industry. They communicate by touch, by pressure-wave, by brief bioluminescent patterning — anything but sound. A bell-cow will drift for a month and never make a voluntary noise. It does not need to. The world is already speaking at full volume, saying one thing.

### The marks of people

Human presence is industrial but sparse to the point of rumor. Every few hundred kilometres, a pump station: a squat concrete-and-steel blockhouse on a reef or a gravel spit, its intake pipes going down into black water, its discharge steaming faintly where waste heat meets the cold. A jetty. A fuel tank. A mast with a light that is purely ceremonial, since no one navigates by sight at night in fog that never moves. Some stations have a crew of six. Some have been running crewless for decades, visited once a year by a boat that stays a day and leaves the keeper, if there is one, with mail and seals and bearings.

Between stations, on the sea floor, survey cables lie in straight lines for distances that make no human sense, pinned to bare rock, humming. Occasionally you pass a buoy tethered far below the surface, turning slowly in no current, listening to the roar and recording that it has not changed. Occasionally a wreck — not many; there has never been much traffic to wreck — upright on a ridge crest, its paint still legible, uneroded, unweeded, unrotted in the cold, looking less sunk than parked.

On land, if the rock that breaks the surface counts as land, the stations grow the only warm thing: exhaust stacks crusted with mineral salts, ice in the lee of every building in fantastic, wind-carved blades that reform identically each winter because the wind, too, arrives always from the same quarter at the same speed.

### The equilibrium

Nothing about it is dying and nothing about it is being born, quite. The mountains are too young to have been worn down and the sea protects their submerged flanks from ever being worn. The fish populations do not boom or crash; the bacterial mats expand to exactly the area of fresh rock and hot discharge, and no further. The water level, measured at a brass gauge bolted to the rim rock in the first decade of the works, has moved four centimetres in a century and a half.

Visitors expect a high cold ocean to feel dramatic. It does not. It feels administered. A continental volume of water, transparent, plantless, full of silent animals, pinned at altitude by pumps, serenaded at damaging volume by its own escape — held, indefinitely, in a balance so complete that even catastrophe has become theoretical.

You lower yourself over the side of a work-boat at noon, in ordinary sunlight, and the roar takes you. Below, the sharp black ridges go down and down through water clearer than air. A bell-cow crosses far beneath, lit from above, trailing a slow pulse of blue light along its ribs. The pump station behind you ticks with heat. The falls, four hundred kilometres away, speak through your skull in a voice that has not paused in your lifetime or your grandparents'.

And the surface closes overhead, flat and grey and exactly where it has always been.
