First you hear it. Before the ground is distinct, before you can tell where the glare of the sky ends and the glare of the land begins, the sound arrives — not carried on the wind so much as the wind itself, made audible and multiplied a millionfold.

It is a roar with structure in it. Low notes, factory notes, a continuous tearing of air through hollow things. Under it a clattering, as if sheet metal were being shaken in another room of the world, only there is no other room. Above it a thin, sustained screaming that rises and falls and never quite resolves into a note you can hold. It hurts in the teeth. It presses on the breastbone. After an hour your own thoughts have to be conducted at close range, by touch.

This is the high tableland, and the wind never leaves it.

It lies far above the cloud-basin countries, a continent lifted flat into thin air — three, four thousand metres up, tilted almost imperceptibly toward a distant, unseen escarpment. It is large enough to make its own weather and then argue with it. Storms are born in its middle for no reason the lowlands would recognize, travel a thousand kilometres across its back, and die against its own far rim. The sky above it is not blue so much as white with brightness; the sun is a hard, cold coin. At noon there are almost no shadows, only a darker glitter directly beneath things. At the edges of vision the light shivers, polarized off ten billion leaves, and the horizon seems to be cut from tin.

It is cool, spitefully cool for all that light. Ten degrees at midday in still air, and there is no still air. The wind strips warmth away as fast as the sun can lay it down. Frost forms most nights in the hollows of the root-mat, a brief, spangled whiteness that the plants steal before sunrise — cracking it with slow muscular flexures, drawing the melt down into wax-sealed chambers. For that half hour the whole plateau ticks and pings, a second, secret percussion under the great roar. Then the water is gone. There are no rivers here. No lakes, no marshes, no wet earth anywhere you could dig for it. Rain that falls — and it does fall, in sudden violent veils at the collision fronts — is intercepted, partitioned, drunk by leaves engineered like funnels, and locked away before it ever reaches the ground. What passes for sap in these plants is not sap at all: a slow, resinous oil, amber and bitter-smelling, that does its chemical work without water and makes the whole organism faintly flammable, faintly solvent, smelling of turpentine and hot coins.

For the ground is plant. All of it.

You do not walk on soil here. You walk on a living mat, knee-deep in places, elsewhere thick enough to swallow a leg to the thigh — roots and rhizomes and compressed generations of dead stems woven into a springy, resonant floor that extends, unbroken, from rim to rim of the continent. Probe it and you find no bottom that is not more of itself: pale cords, dark cords, nodes like knuckles, old growth gone to a dry, fibrous peat that smells of pepper. It drums underfoot. It transmits. A herd moving ten kilometres away announces itself as a low tremor in the soles of your feet long before the dust of its passage shows against the light, and every animal native to the plateau reads the ground this way, through vibration, because the air is useless for news. Nothing up here wastes breath shouting. Most of the fauna is functionally deaf, ears sealed or internal or simply vestigial, conversing instead in footfall codes, in drummed patterns on hollow stems, in chemical signatures laid down on the mat. You will see less of them than you feel. And yet they are everywhere — abundant in the way insects are abundant, a biomass threaded through every layer of the plant world.

From the mat rises the vegetation proper, and here is the plateau's paradox: it is total, and it is sparse.

The dominant growth is a reed — if a reed can be twelve metres tall and armoured in silica. Its stems stand in loose companies, each culm two or three hand-spans apart from its neighbour, hollow, jointed, tuned like an organ pipe. The wind crosses their open mouths and sounds them, each at the pitch of its length, and a stand of a million stems produces not noise but chord — shifting, beating against itself, swelling to a howl when a gust-front arrives in visible form, a dark wave racing through the reed-sea faster than anything could run, bending the heads down in sequence from horizon to horizon. The leaves are narrow blades with edges of opaline glass, grown to cut the wind rather than be cut by it. When they clash they ring. A whole field in a crosswind sounds like a distant industrial yard: tinsmiths, saw-teeth, loose cladding in a gale. Up close the leaves will open skin without any apparent malice, and the wounds fill instantly with the plant's resin and sting like a chemical burn.

Between the reed companies, which can stretch for days of walking, lie the glades: enormous emptinesses where nothing stands taller than your ankle and the root-mat is exposed to the full punishment of the sun, bleached silver-grey, ticking with heat it cannot keep. You can walk a whole morning in a glade and meet nothing upright. The space feels abandoned. It is not — the mat beneath is densest here, digesting light through buried green tissue, waiting, sometimes for decades, for the signal to rise. Then there are the scattered giants, so far apart that each commands its own parish of visibility: one to a valley, one to a skyline. From a distance they read as structures, as the only vertical punctuation in a world of horizontal roaring, and the eye insists on misreading them as built.

They are the mill-trees, and nothing on the plateau is stranger or more central.

Each is a single plant grown into the shape of a factory that has forgotten its building. A trunk like a riveted column, fluted, iron-dark, banded with metallic nodes where the plant has concentrated manganese and iron drawn up out of wind-blown dust. Branches that do not branch so much as *articulate* — long spars jointed like crane arms, perpetually re-trimming themselves to the wind, carrying sail-leaves that furl and unfurl with a clatter of slats. At the crown, a cluster of great pods hangs and turns, each pod a mill: seeds fed into it by conveyor-chutes of fused stem are husked by internal grinding plates, sorted by weight in airstreams, and expelled — flour-fine chaff to the wind, dense seed-shot rattling down hollow tubes into storage bulbs at the base, where the herds come to feed. Bellows-roots around the trunk pump air in slow, tidal contractions, driving the whole apparatus; you can hear a mill-tree half a kilometre off through your feet, a rhythmic industrial thudding underneath the general roar, a heart doing piecework.

There is no one tending them. There is no one who built them. The plateau has simply, over deep time, industrialized itself — evolved the division of labour into anatomy. Some plants are chimneys, venting the waste heat and spore-smoke of chemical processes conducted in root-chambers underground, where resins are refined and metals precipitated in the dark. Some are power stations: fields of black mirror-leaves, tilted to the sun, generating violent static differentials in the dry air, so that on bright afternoons the reed-heads wear coronas of pale fire and travelling animals pick their way between discharge points by inherited map, drummed into the mat by generations of hooves. The dust smells of ozone near these fields. Your hair stands. Small birds — earless, bright-eyed, flying in total silence — harvest insects stunned by the discharges, working the edges of the electric fields like gulls at a tide-line.

And everything is in motion, all the time. This is the plateau's settled state: turbulence as equilibrium. The landform is old, profoundly old — wind-planed escarpments, valleys filled and refilled with plant-peat, the slow geology of a place that has been itself for longer than most mountain ranges. Nothing about its shape is new. But within that ancient frame the living surface never rests. Gust-fronts tear seed from the reeds in pale horizontal blizzards. Pollen weather moves across the continent in yellow-grey walls a kilometre high, forcing the herds to migrate sideways out of its path, their drumming changing tempo as they go. Whirlwinds form over the dark mirror-fields, wander, and unravel millennia-dead sections of mat into spinning columns of fibre that rise until the thin air cannot hold them. Twice or three times a year a reed-sea will flash-burn — the resin, the dry fibre, the static, the wind all conspiring — and fire will run before the gale at terrifying speed, a bright line with a voice deeper than the world's normal voice, and behind it, within days, the mat sends up green-black shoots already seeded with silica, already humming in the first returning breeze. The animals do not flee the fires so much as surf their edges, eating the flushed insects and the heat-cracked seeds. Destruction here is not an interruption of the system. It is one of the gears.

The herds are built for this: low, wide, ballasted creatures with hooves like sled runners to spread their weight on the mat, grazing the seed-fall at the feet of the mill-trees, their bodies furred in overlapping scales of horn that shed both blade-leaves and fire-sparks. Predators follow them in the reed companies, long and nearly silent, communicating their hunts entirely through the drum of the ground, so that a kill may happen fifty metres away and register only as a brief, wrong syncopation in the floor. At night — short, brilliant with stars hard as salt in the thin air, and still, *still* loud — the plateau's smaller life comes up through the mat itself: burrowing things that live their whole lives inside the root-world, in a muffled red darkness where the roar is reduced to a vast, surrounding pressure, like the memory of an ocean. Some travellers say the only true silence available on the continent is down there, inside the plant, with the resin smell thick as syrup and the slow pumping of the bellows-roots moving the dark around you. Everything on the surface is adapted to deafening brightness and permanent wind. Everything below is adapted to being inside a body.

Stand anywhere on the tableland at noon and turn a full circle. Reed-sea, glade, a mill-tree alone against the white sky with its arms re-trimming, another not yet visible, dust devils working the far flat, a herd's worth of tremor arriving through your boots from beyond the edge of sight, the chord of ten million hollow stems shifting as a weather system of the plateau's own invention crosses its own interior. No water shining anywhere. No stone that is not already half plant-peat. No mark of any hand. Just process, at continental scale, running at full volume, in cold bright air, for longer than anything living can remember — a factory with no workers, a forest with no shade, a sea that learned to stand up and scream into the wind that made it, and goes on screaming, beautifully, without pause, because pausing is the one thing this world has never learned to do.
