You hear it long before there is anything to see.

Not a sound, exactly, after the first hour. A pressure. It comes through the soles of your feet, up the wet stone, into the teeth. It makes your ears ring even when you press mud and wax into them, it makes the water in shallow pools shiver in concentric rings, it makes conversation impossible except in signs, in touches, in writing quickly with a wet finger on a slate. When a cloud-shadow passes and it stops, all at once, the silence is so total your balance goes.

This is a plain that has finished eroding.

Whatever mountains were here wore themselves down so long ago that the land has forgotten slope. For sixty, eighty miles in any direction it is flat in the way only the oldest shield-rock can be flat — a peneplain, a billiard table of granite and ironstone ground down to its roots. The few rises left are whalebacks, inselbergs no taller than a house, their sides polished round, no sharp edge anywhere. Rain does not run off this place. It spreads. An inch of water can lie unbroken to the horizon, and because there is no gradient, it stays there, cold and tea-brown, until it soaks straight down or evaporates straight up.

It is always close to doing both. The water table is a foot below the crust. Dig with your hands and the hole fills, black and iron-smelling. Seep-lines weep from the whalebacks even in a dry week, beading on manganese-black varnish. In the morning the fog is so low and thick that it condenses on stone and metal and skin, and by midday — though midday is only a paling in the grey — it has condensed again. The light never quite arrives. The sun is a dim, wet coin behind cloud, shadows are suggestions. Everything is the color of things left out too long: rust, ash, bone-white salt, the dull green-black of wet rock.

And there is almost nothing growing on it, in any sense you would recognize.

Ancient leaching took the soil centuries of millennia ago. What is left is laterite gravel, quartz grit, clay pans sealed hard as pottery, and a mineral skin. When wet, the pans are coated in a slick black mat, cyanobacteria and iron-bacteria woven tight as felt. When it dries at the edges it lifts in pale, curling flakes. That mat is the bottom of everything. Snails graze it — millions of small, thick-shelled snails the color of the stone, leaving silver trails across the flats. Brine shrimp and clam shrimp hatch in water that was dust a day before. Midges rise off the pans in columns so dense they look like smoke. There are no trees, no grass, no reeds at the margins. The horizon is unbroken by any vertical living thing.

Which makes the animals stranger, and louder.

The pans are nurseries. After rain, and rain is frequent here — short cold showers out of a low sky, drizzle that lasts two days — every depression fills knee-deep at most, often ankle-deep, and warms just enough. Frogs come up out of the ground where they have been sleeping in cracked mud in sacs of their own skin. Not a few kinds. A dozen. Thumb-sized peepers that sit on stones. Heavy, warty toads that float with only their eyes out. A pale, almost translucent burrower that never fully leaves the water. For three, four weeks at a stretch they call together.

One pan sings. Then the pan half a mile away answers, not out of rivalry, but because the sound carries so perfectly across flat water and flat stone. The whalebacks are hollowed by wind and water into blind flutes and bowls, natural resonators. A frog calling inside a stone pan is calling inside an amplifier. Ten thousand frogs are a physical force. Perches shiver. Pots hum. Birds — ibis, herons, a kind of rail that runs rather than flies — work the edges in flocks, stomping to panic the shrimp, deaf to it in the way long-adapted things are. They have no external ears to hurt. Snakes swim pan to pan with their heads high. At night, by dim phosphorescence of disturbed algae — no, too cold for that, by the pale sky reflected in inch-deep sheet water — the whole plain looks like one animal breathing, disturbed everywhere by mouths.

Then a harrier passes low, its shadow crosses the water, and an entire square mile of frogs cuts out at the same instant. That silence travels outward like a wave. In it you can hear the individual small sounds that were buried: water dripping from a cairn, snail shells clicking, your own pulse. Then one frog, somewhere, starts again. The rest join in less than a minute, louder than before.

People have never lived here permanently, but they have always come here.

You find their marks at the sparse intervals emptiness allows: low cairns, head-high, built from water-worn cobbles and daubed white with lime so they can be seen in fog when you cannot hear a shout ten paces away. Lines of stones set in V's across a barely perceptible flow-way — frog traps, fish traps when the pans link up in flood. Circles of stone sunk two feet into the earth, roofed once with hide and mud; they are not houses so much as ear-shelters, built half-underground and entered by a bent passage so the chorus arrives muffled, bearable enough for sleep. Inside, the floor is swept clean, a hearth blackened in the center, a few chipped stone knives, bone awls, clay pots thick and badly fired, a bundle of snail-shell earplugs drilled and strung on sinew.

The people who made these come in the wet, in family bands, with dogs that have been bred — or culled — stone deaf and are the calmer for it. They speak while here mostly with their hands, even away from the water. Children learn to lip-read before they learn the seasonal names for the pans, which are named not for what they look like but for their pitch: High Tin Pan, Broken Bell, The Throat, Mother Pan which is so low you feel her in your sternum and your eyes water.

They harvest modestly and quickly: frogs by the basket, drying on stone racks; snail meat; eggs like black caviar scraped from the undersides of submerged rocks; the starchy, unpleasant cakes made from dried bacterial mat, peeled in sheets and toasted. They leave offerings in the resonator bowls — a shell bead, a smear of fat — not out of any doctrine you could ask them to explain in the noise, but because a bowl that sings back at you is plainly a place that listens.

There are rules that belong to the physics of the place more than to any law. You do not camp on the south side of a pan, because that is where its voice throws. You do not walk the sheet-water at night without a cairn in sight, because sound disorients — you will turn toward the loudest pan thinking it is the nearest, and walk in circles while the water rises around your ankles. You do not strike the whaleback stone with metal; it rings for minutes, sets off the frogs out of season, and brings rain, or seems to. You cover a corpse's ears with clay before burial in the shallow graves at the plain's edge, marked by a single flat stone. The dead have heard enough.

Late in the season the rain thins. The sheet retreats panwards by evaporation and seepage in a single afternoon, leaving a lacework of salt and dead midges and drying mat that crunches underfoot like frost. The frogs burrow backwards, one by one, the calling fraying at the edges of the plain first, then in the center, until only a few hoarse individuals are left croaking into stone bowls that no longer answer properly. The birds leave in long dim lines. The snails seal themselves to rock undersides. The cairns stand white in the empty, wet light.

But the ground is still humming a little, through residual water, through the holes it finds. Put your palm flat on the ironstone, anywhere, and you will feel it waiting just under the surface, cool and patient and inexhaustibly old — the next rain already on its way across a horizon with nothing to stop it.
