The first thing is the height, and the second is that the height does not cool you.

You come over the rim at nearly five thousand meters, where the air is so thin the sky has gone a deep, bruised blue at noon, and the heat comes up to meet you anyway. Not down from the sun alone — though the sun here is white, physical, a weight on the back of the neck — but up off the ground itself. The whole basin holds it. Dark volcanic rock on the slopes, white salt on the floor, a ring of worn, red-brown mountains closing it in on every side: for ten hours a day this place is a shallow oven built on the roof of the world.

It is an old lake that never quite finished dying.

From the rim it looks empty. A regional flat, twenty, thirty kilometers across, too large to take in at once, its far edge lifted and bent by mirage. Then your eyes adjust to the scale of it, and the emptiness reverses. There is no empty place down there at all.

The floor is tiled, edge to edge.

Part of the tiling is natural: salt hexagons, millions of them, raised rims ankle-high, each plate the size of a table or a truck bed, fitted together without a gap for as far as the light lets you see. Part is industrial, and it follows the same geometry so closely that from above the two are hard to separate. Evaporation ponds cut in long, perfect rectangles between the polygons — turquoise, cobalt, milky jade, a shocking arterial pink — each one a different stage of concentration, brine pumped up from meters below the crust and left for the high sun to do the heavy work. Causeways of packed salt run between them, and pipes run along the causeways, and solar arrays tilt beside the pipes, and low pump-houses squat in salt brick every kilometer or so, half-buried against the wind. No large town, no central factory on the flat itself. Just this scattered, repeated machinery, spread out until it becomes the landscape.

And between, under, and above all of it, life has packed itself into every remaining seam.

The water here is never generous. That is the rule everything obeys. There is no river crossing the flat, no true lake anymore. There is brine an arm's depth beneath the crust, so salty it burns cuts instantly. There are pockets where it breaks through — shallow eyes of clear, bitter water ringed in white crystal — and after the rare afternoon storms, there is a sheet of rain an inch deep spread over ten kilometers, turning the entire basin into a mirror for an hour before it sinks and vanishes. Most days moisture arrives only as virga, rain falling from towering clouds over the rim peaks and evaporating halfway down in blue veils that never touch ground, and as condensation in the coldest hour before dawn, when salt, pipe, and feather briefly bead with it and everything living comes out to lick.

Because water is pockets, life is pockets too, dense to the point of saturation around them.

Brine shrimp cloud the pink ponds so thick the water seems to move against the wind. Brine flies rise off the warm edges in black smudges, a million at a step, and settle again without a sound. Flamingos — three kinds, ankle-deep, heads down — work the shallows in lines of hundreds, thousands across the whole floor, their color taken straight from the algae in the ponds. Lizards the color of rust occupy every warm pipe joint. Swifts nest in the eroded cliff-bases where the slope meets the salt, and at the heat's peak they pour out into the thermals until the rising air itself seems feathered. On the sparse margin, where the salt gives way to stone, hard tussock grass grows in separate, yellow tufts, and a red halophyte creeps in mats no higher than a finger; vicuña pick between them, hawks nest on the pump-house roofs, and a small, pale fox learns the workers' shift changes by heart.

Vegetation could not be called anything but sparse. Animals do not need it. They eat each other, and the chemical bloom in the water, in a chain as tight as the salt tiling.

It is very quiet. This is the strangest part, given how much is happening.

Evaporation makes no noise. Solar pumps hum below the threshold where thin air will carry sound. The trucks that crawl the causeways, loaded with white salt cake and lithium carbonate for the processing sheds on the eastern shore, raise long plumes of dust that hang and dissolve in silence. Even the birds are economical. A flamingo flock lifting does it in a soft, collective rush, more felt in the chest than heard. Voices do not travel here; the flat drinks them. At midday, when the heat is worst, all processes seem to go on without accompaniment: crystals growing visibly along the pond edges, feather-white, a centimeter in a hot week; storm cells building, collapsing, building again over the rim; shrimp, fly, lizard, bird turning over in their endless, rapid exchange. You notice your own breathing, too loud and too fast in the altitude, and the faint ticking of the salt at dusk as it contracts — the only geology that still speaks, and barely.

The erosion here finished its argument long ago. The surrounding mountains are not jagged; they are smoothed, settled into their final shapes, their scree sorted and still. The salt crust repairs itself. Drive across an unmarked plate and by the next morning brine has wicked up into the tracks and frozen them over in new crystal, a scar already healing into pattern. Old pump-houses abandoned thirty years ago have not collapsed so much as been gradually salted into the floor, windows filled with white growth, roofs intact. Things do not rot in this dry heat, and they do not wash away. They encrust.

There are rules, learned in the body within a day. Walk the causeways; the beautiful plates between ponds may be a centimeter of crystal over black, caustic mud that will take a boot and the skin with it. Do not work at noon; even the machines rest then, and the workers — in UV hoods and reflective suits, faces powdered permanently with salt — sleep in the thick-walled sheds where it is merely hot instead of dangerous. Drink before you are thirsty, because at this elevation in this heat you can be in trouble while your mouth still feels wet. Shade is a material, as real and rationed as water. And never trust distance: in the clear, thin light a processing shed that looks twenty minutes away is three hours' walk, and a lake that appears, perfect and blue, on the western flat in late afternoon is only hot air bending the sky down to touch the salt.

By evening the basin changes without cooling much. The ground gives back its stored day into the night; the ponds stay warm to the touch; the flamingos go on feeding in the dark in their thousands, heads sweeping. Condensation begins to form on the cold metal of the condensers the company set on the rim — industrial nets and fins that harvest a few precious liters from air that seems to hold none — and below them, queues of insects arrive first, then birds, then the fox, in an order nobody had to teach. The stars at this altitude are not romantic. They are hard, crowded, and close, packed as densely overhead as the ponds and polygons are below, until the whole world, up and down, seems to have been filled in without a single wasted space.

Then, sometime before dawn, the salt ticks, the first flies stir on the warm brine, the pumps resume their inaudible work, and the heat begins again.
