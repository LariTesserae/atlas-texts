The land is new, and it knows it.

Not new in the way a forest is new after fire. New in the way a mountain range is new before the rivers have learned it. This is a high tableland lifted within living memory — a continent-sized slab of basalt and pale granite still rising, millimeter by deliberate millimeter, from a lattice of deep engines no one on the surface ever hears. It stretches past the horizon in every direction, far enough that it makes its own weather. Clouds are born over its center and die at its rim without ever having seen the lowlands below.

It is cold up here. Not deep-winter cold, but the persistent, thin-aired cold of altitude. Three or four thousand meters up, most days hover just below or just above freezing. Frost forms at night even in high summer, silvering the black stone, and melts again by nine in the morning. Your breath shows. The sun is ordinary — white, bright, normal daylight — but it feels closer for the thin air, and shadows have knife edges. When a cloud covers it, the temperature drops as if someone opened a door.

The rock has not been softened yet.

Everything is angular. Plains of fresh columnar basalt run for fifty kilometers without a rounded hill. Fracture faces are blue-gray and unweathered, so sharp they ring faintly if you kick them. Scree lies exactly where it fell. There are no old valleys, only shallow, tentative channels where meltwater is beginning to argue with stone. Lakes sit in depressions that haven't earned outlets yet — improbably clear, milky turquoise with rock flour, their shores just crushed gravel and wet silt. No beaches. No deltas. The geology is embryonic, held at the moment before erosion becomes a habit.

And it is wet, despite the cold, despite the stone.

Water is never far down. A boot print fills slowly. Mossy ground squelches over permafrost. Cliffs weep in bright lines where deep warmth meets a seam and sends it condensing, dripping, freezing into temporary icicles that grow and sigh off by afternoon. Every hollow has a pond. Every pond has a skin of ice by morning and open black water by noon, steaming faintly. Mist collects where the ground is warm and the air is not, pooling knee-high over the marshy flats, making the sparse vegetation look taller than it is.

What grows here is pioneer work.

Lichen first, of course — black, rust-orange, sulfur-yellow maps spreading across new lava, softening it by microns a year. Then cushion mosses in the wet places, dense green-black pillows that hold water like sponges. Stunted grasses engineered for frost and poor soil, their roots shipped up with intentional microbes. Everywhere the soil is being made on site: crushed rock, windblown dust, dead moss, dung. In protected lee hollows there are shrubs no taller than your shin, wind-pruned, flowering absurdly large for three weeks a year. Trees exist only near the cities, where the air is managed, and even there they grow slow and crooked.

Animals, impossibly, are everywhere the plants are not.

This was deliberate. Once the water table stabilized, the herds came before the forests. Long-legged grazers with double coats and huge lungs moving in lines of thousands across the stone steppe, following the melt. They trample seed into the silt. They fertilize entire watersheds. Marmot-like burrowers colonize the gravel fans, whistling from the entrances. Fox-sized predators with pale eyes work the burrow towns at dusk, and larger pack hunters work the herds, patient, mostly seen as movement at the edge of sight.

Birds own the warm places. Where geothermal vents keep a channel ice-free, ducks and heavy-bodied geese crowd all winter, and the marsh grass is beaten down by feet and wings. Sandhill-tall waders stalk the new ponds for engineered fish and the sudden, boiling emergences of midges that happen on still afternoons. Eagles nest — not in trees — on the maintenance pylons, which were quietly redesigned to invite them.

The abundance is loud in season and mostly silent otherwise, which fits the plateau. Sound doesn't travel the way you'd expect. The young rock and open water drink it. Wind is the main voice, and even that is softened over distance. You hear dripping, the tick of cooling stone, a far-off herd shifting gravel, a bird's wingbeat. Then nothing for a long while.

The cities interrupt this emptiness at enormous intervals.

You can travel three hundred kilometers across uninhabited steppe and then, without suburb or outskirt, arrive at a vertical town of eighty thousand people: stone-colored towers reconstituted molecule by molecule from local basalt, warm windows, covered markets smelling of bread and wet wool and ozone, schools, bathhouses built over hot channels, transit levels humming below the streets. It is unmistakably urban — dense, layered, lived-in — but each city is an island. Between them, nothing but maintenance shelters, herd stations, research camps.

They stay habitable not with domes but with something quieter and harder to point to: pressure fences. Walk into a city boundary and your ears pop. The air inside is a fraction denser, a few degrees warmer, held by a field technology that current lowland physics describes only with apologies and approximations. Snow falls straight down inside. Outside a gale may be scouring the towers; at street level scarves barely move. Buildings repair microfractures overnight. Foundations ride on rafts that re-level themselves as the plateau lifts beneath them.

Transit between cities is equally unobtrusive. Capsules drop into evacuated tubes under the basalt and arrive an hour later, silent. Above ground, cargo crawlers on repulsion skids move at night, lit only by running lights, carrying soil cultures, replacement parts, young trees in root sleeves. Nobody commutes by air unless weather work requires it; the sky belongs mostly to birds and to the steering arrays that nudge afternoon convection away from the youngest, most erodible basins.

For that is the real work here: not maintaining a finished place, but finishing one.

Each spring, engineers release designed rivers. Not floods — schedules. Gates open in an upland reservoir and a milky torrent goes down a channel that was scored into the rock last year, carrying exactly the sediment load calculated to carve this reach and deposit on that fan. Geologists walk ahead of the water with herd-drivers and ecologists, moving nests, seeding the banks-to-be. A valley that didn't exist in March has a braided stream, three ponds, and nesting plovers by August.

New vents open without permission. A walking path crosses crust thin as a dinner plate over scalding groundwater. A lake drains overnight through a fresh fracture and leaves fish flapping in scented mud, to the immediate delight of every bird within ten kilometers. The rules grow out of that rawness:

Stay on marked stone; new ground lies about its strength.
Give herds the right of way; the cities were sited around migration, not the other way round.
Acclimatize or don't leave the pressure field — the field will warn you, gently, then not gently.
And never assume a landscape feature will be there next year. Maps are dated by month here. Some are dated by week.

At evening the rim clouds catch the light and the whole tableland seems to float above a sea of weather, cities pricking on one by one as small constellations across the darkening steppe. Herds settle in the warm marshes, steam rising around their legs. The ponds refreeze with a faint musical cracking. Somewhere far below the basalt, the engines adjust their push by a fraction, and a measuring station records the continent rising another two millimeters toward a future watershed no living person will see completed.

It doesn't feel abandoned, or pristine, or finished. It feels begun — a vast, cold, wet, animal-rich world under careful construction, where the most advanced technology ever deployed is being used, quietly, to make something as old-fashioned as soil, rivers, and a place to live.
