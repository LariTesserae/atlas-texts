It begins as nothing at the top of the mountain.

Not a peak, exactly. The elevation has been worn down over a very long time into a broad, black shoulder of stone, high enough that the sky never quite becomes blue. At noon it is a thin, hard violet overhead, star-pricked at the edges. Below, in all directions, the slopes fall away into a country of dry basins and mineral flats, pale with salts that were never dissolved by any sea. There is no snow. There is nothing wet enough to freeze. The cold here is a clean, desiccating cold, drawn out of the stone itself, and it makes the basalt contract in inaudible stresses and the thin air — mostly nitrogen, a trace of argon — feel brittle in the lungs.

If you walked this shoulder for a day you might conclude it was empty. The wind, what little reaches this height, has polished some faces of the rock to a dull glass and roughened others into scales. Frost, not water frost but a faint rime of nitrogen compounds, gathers in the shadowed side of cracks and never melts, only very slowly sublimates back into nothing when the dim sun crosses. The light is low even at midday, filtered and distant, and it lays long, soft shadows that do not seem to warm the ground they fall from.

Then, in a shallow depression where three slabs lean together, there is a seam.

It is not geological. A disc, perhaps three centimetres across, set flush into the stone, made of something that is not quite metal and not quite ceramic. Its surface is the colour of old ice seen through smoke. Around it, almost erased, are marks: a ring of tiny indentations, a single line pointing downslope toward nothing, three small structures no larger than thumbnails — a post, a broken arch, a hollow cube open on one side — arranged with so much empty stone between them that they read less as buildings than as punctuation.

The disc has no handle, no display. But if you stay beside it long enough — and everything here asks you to stay longer than you intended — it becomes apparent that the disc is not *on* the mountain. It is the visible cross-section of something folded *into* it. The rest of the place exists in a direction that the mountain does not ordinarily have.

Inside is a room. Inside is also a landscape.

Which it is depends on how low you get, and how long you look.

At first accommodation, it is intimate, contained: a chamber you could cup in two hands from the outside, and yet when you are admitted — reduced, translated, however the old engineering phrases it, because your body does not squeeze so much as find it has always had a size appropriate to this place — it is perhaps twenty paces across. The walls curve, not smoothly, but in faceted planes of sintered regolith and iridium glass, meeting at angles that sometimes add up and sometimes, if you try to count them twice, do not. The floor is not flat. It has topography.

Kneel, and the floor extends.

There are low ridges of dark grit running like watersheds, shallow pans of compacted dust the colour of ash, a miniature escarpment where the surface breaks in tiny, perfect steps. From standing height it is texture. From an inch above it, it is terrain, with horizons. The dim light, which comes from no source you can find but seems to be an property the air was given and has not yet used up, falls across these micro-slopes and makes valleys. It is the same mountain, perhaps. Inverted. Or remembered. The ridges outside go down to basins; the ridges in here rise from them, as if the pocket were cast from the summit and the world outside were the mould.

There is almost no sound. This is not ordinary quiet, the kind that can be broken. Sound here arrives badly, late, and attenuated by something other than distance. If you scrape a boot on the grit, you may hear it a minute later, very faintly, from the wrong side of the room. If you speak — the sealed air is dry, xenon-heavy, tasting faintly of stone and ozone — your voice comes back to you thinner, with the beginnings worn off. Most of the time even that does not happen. The chamber has absorbed so many sounds over so many dilated years that they seem to be waiting in the walls, unsorted.

And it is slow in here in a way that is physical, not just felt. A particle of dust dislodged from the ceiling has been falling, visibly, for what your watch insists is an hour, tracing a path that is almost straight and yet curves around an absence near the centre of the floor. Outside the disc, centuries may be registering that fall as a single shiver. Inside, erosion proceeds grain by grain, processional. One of the tiny structures — there are a few more of them in here, larger from this vantage: a pylon no higher than your knee, a lens on a tripod of fused wire, a chair-like frame built for a posture no human body assumes — sheds a flake from its edge every very long while. The flake lands, and the dust reorganises around it over the next age.

The cold is not uniform. It is arranged. Near the walls you can feel your breath want to crystallise, a needle-cold that makes the fillings in teeth ache in phantom sympathy. Three steps inward, the air is merely cool. At the centre, above that absence the dust avoids, there is a stratum of impossible warmth that never spreads — you can put your hand through a boundary as distinct as a soap film and feel, on one side, winter, and on the other, something like skin temperature with no source of heat at all. The instruments, if they can be called that, maintain these gradients without power draw that any physics you brought with you would recognise. The pylon is not plugged in. It is, according to the faint etchings at its base, *persuaded*. The lens does not focus light; occasionally, without being touched, it focuses distance, and the far wall leans suddenly nearer without moving, so that the scratches on it — tally marks, a diagram of an orbit that closes on itself, a handprint with too many joints pressed into soft material long ago — are legible, and then, exhaling almost, it lets the room be wide again.

No one has lived here. That is increasingly clear the longer the place reveals itself. There is no provision for food or water, no waste, no sleeping surface that is not also a calibration. Even microbes have been edited out, not sterilised so much as denied a consistent chemistry to happen in. Near the centre the rules thin.

It starts with small betrayals. Your shadow falls toward the light when you pass the lens. Objects set down on the miniature escarpment roll, very slowly, uphill, and stop not at the top but at a point they seem to remember having occupied before you brought them in. If you walk directly across the floor toward the opposite wall, you will arrive, but the number of steps will not match the distance your eyes reported, and on one occasion — you will not be able to repeat it deliberately — you will see, across the intervening dimness, the back of your own head, a fraction of a second younger, still deciding to cross. Mirrors have been turned to face the walls. One has been turned to face itself, in a sealed loop of glass, and in the sliver visible at its edge there is a reflection of the room with the tiny structures in different positions, as if it were keeping a version where the work finished.

At the very centre, set into the highest of the low ridges like a survey marker, is the reason for the pocket. It is small enough to miss: a cube, a centimetre on a side, of vacuum that is not empty in the way the vacuum outside the mountain is empty. Colours collect at its edges that the eye attempts to name and then abandons. Within it, by the evidence of the few probes that were left half-melted around it like offerings, falling does not always mean the same direction twice, and cause is a local custom rather than a law. It is a sample of somewhere else, or of here, earlier, before the universe settled on its present habits — kept, refrigerated, slowed, at the top of a lifeless mountain because even its keepers understood that some things should be stored far from anything that could be harmed, and inside a fold where harm itself would arrive too slowly to propagate.

Every long cycle — and cycle here may mean a human lifetime outside — one of the scattered devices does a single thing. The pylon emits no light and makes no noise, but the dust everywhere lifts a fraction of a millimetre and resettles in a slightly more accurate arrangement. The lens turns by the width of an atom. The cube is measured again, by methods that do not touch it, and a new indentation appears in a row on the floor somewhere, among thousands, most of which, if the diagram on the wall is to be trusted, refer to measurements that have not yet been taken.

You could leave. The disc would reseal, the summit would resume being merely high and cold and empty, the stars would come back into the violet at the edge of noon. But the room would continue in its fold, twenty paces and a whole landscape across, its dust falling, its warmth staying exactly where it was put, its silence storing up the scrape of your boot to deliver, some time in the next hundred years, to no one at all — unless, in the different positions preserved in the mirror facing itself, you have already stayed, and are still down near the floor, watching the ridges extend toward a horizon that fits, entire, in the palm of the mountain's hand.
