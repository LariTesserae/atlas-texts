At first you don't see it at all. You hear it, and you feel wet.

You come up the last lift in polar night, at an altitude where the air is too thin to carry weather properly, and there is no horizon line, no skyline. Only black above black. Then the sound arrives through the cable, through the soles of your boots, through your molars — a pressure that makes the little bones in your ears ache before you can identify it as sound.

It is not one sound. It is millions, layered so densely it becomes a climate.

High shrieking, far above speech, sleeting down in pulses. A lower chattering like rain on sheet metal. Wing membranes snapping, soft and wet. Water dripping from every edge, amplified by stone. And underneath it all, a subsonic hum from the towers that you don't hear so much as lean against.

This is Halu Cirque. A town built in a newborn valley, for animals.

Thirty-some years ago the plateau split and extruded — whether by eruption or by engineered uplift no one down-valley agrees on — and left a shallow, open bowl eight kilometers across, walled by basalt that has not had time to learn how to be old. Nothing here is weathered. The rock is black glass and knife-edged slag, unsoftened, unworn, with no soil in its cracks, no channels cut for water. Erosion hasn't begun its work yet. The drainage doesn't know where to go.

So water simply stays. It weeps.

It is bitterly cold, forty and fifty below in the open, a cold that kills exposed skin in minutes, but the town is wet everywhere. The condensers see to that, and the bodies do.

The condensers are the closest thing Halu has to a forest, and there is nothing alive about them in the plant sense. Nothing green grows here — no grass, no moss, no trees, not even in the sheltered places. The light has been gone for eleven weeks and will be gone for five more, the altitude gives no shelter, and the rock gives no purchase. What rises instead, in dense rows across the cirque floor, are ceramic fins and matte-black vanes, taller than houses, grown rather than built by slurry printers that still inch along their rails at the valley's edge. They pull water straight out of air too thin and cold to hold it, using fields that coax vapor to condense without ever quite freezing. Each fin sweats constantly. Each one ticks and pings as a skin of rime forms, is shed, forms again. Melt sheets run down them in black runnels and collect in open cisterns, where blind, translucent shrimp the length of a thumb drift in water kept liquid well below its freezing point.

Between the condensers, the town proper is not built for people. It is built at settlement scale, street after street of it, but the architecture is roost, perch, and honeycomb.

Towers of fused basalt and self-healing nanoceramic rise in clusters, near-touching, their faces pocked with ten thousand nesting cells. Bridges arc between them not for walking but for hanging. Under every eave, in every lee, against every warm conduit, they are clustered: the veilwings.

There are more of them than the town. That is the only accurate census.

Each one is small — a body you could cup in two hands, fur the color of wet ash, wings that fold around it like a damp cloak — but they live at a density that turns individual animals into a surface. They coat the towers in a shifting, breathing insulation yards thick in places. They hang inverted over the streets. They fill abandoned printer bays and condenser housings. Their combined body heat lifts whole districts ten, twenty degrees above the killing air outside the clusters, and in that trapped warmth their breath condenses, rains back on them, runs off the towers in lukewarm, ammoniac streams. If you stand under a roost-spire long enough your suit will be soaked through with warm, stinking water while frost is forming on your shoulders.

They are engineered, or re-engineered, and their metabolisms are wrong for how loud they are. A veilwing takes four minutes to unfold a wing. Its heart, in deep roost, beats once every nine seconds. A flock does not startle; it wakes the way a tide comes in, one edge shivering, then a slow ripple of movement and calling that can take an hour to cross a tower face. And yet their voices never stop. Even sleeping, even torpid, they chirp and scan, chirp and scan, a constant echolocating murmur that rises in waves to a full chorus every few hours — a sound so loud at its peak that human conversation is abandoned entirely and caretakers speak through pressure-pads in their gloves, through taps on each other's forearms.

That chorus is why the town exists. A million small sonars, never still, pouring sound into new rock that has no cracks yet. The returning echoes are caught by the towers, translated by systems whose workings are mostly described to visitors as "not exactly computing," and rendered as slow maps of what lies beneath the cirque: water pockets, stress fractures, mineral veins forming in real time. Halu is a listening instrument that happens to be alive, and wet, and extremely crowded with other lives that live off the first.

Because where there are veilwings there is everything else. Mites move through the fur forests like herds. Pale beetles graze the mineral slime that grows — the only simpler-than-plant life that thrives here — in the warm condensate films on the tower bases, feeding on iron and sulfur and chemosynthetic bacteria. Gaunt, flightless shepherd-things, tall as a person and blind as moles, patrol the roost levels on long soft feet, eating the dead, grooming clusters apart when they grow too dense and begin to overheat, nudging newborn pups back into the warmth with their noses. In the cisterns, the shrimp are harvested by things like herons made of night, which stand motionless for hours in freezing black water and then strike once. You rarely see any of this directly. You see it in infrared smear on your visor, or you feel it brush your suit in a passageway, or you hear a new note enter the general roar.

The human part of Halu is small and embedded, almost apologetic. A few dozen caretakers live in pressure domes sunk halfway into the basalt at the cirque's southern rim, where the guano processors churn day and night, washing the towers' runoff into a potent, exportable sludge that pays for all of this. The domes are kept dim red inside, never white, never bright. The first rule of Halu is light discipline: a camera flash could blind a quarter-million animals and bring a chorus down like a collapsing ceiling. The second rule is softness: soft soles, soft voices, no metal on stone. The third is slowness. You do not run here. Nothing runs here. The printer-gantries creep. The floodwater from a burst cistern advances across an unchanneled street at a walking pace no faster than a crawl, finding no drain, spreading in a perfect black sheet until a shepherd-thing steps in it and redirects it with its body. Even the new towers grow at centimeters per week, extruding warm ceramic that steams in the cold and is colonized by veilwings before it has finished cooling.

Walk out, if your escort lets you, beyond the last condenser row to the open cirque floor at what would be noon if there were a sun. Your visor will show you almost nothing — a few infrared beacons, the heat-bloom of the town behind you like a low, wet fire. Ahead, the basalt runs unbroken to a wall you cannot see but can hear, because the veilwing chorus returns off it, faintly delayed. Your breath freezes in your mask. The rock under the ice is so new it still rings, faintly, when struck. No wind sounds like wind usually does; there is nothing for it to move through. Only the drip, everywhere, and the shriek, and the deep tower hum, and now and then the slow, thunderous sigh of a whole roost shifting its weight, ten tons of small bodies resettling against one another in the dark.

There is no dawn coming to change any of this. There is only the next chorus, and the next condensation cycle, and water finding, very slowly, the paths that in a thousand years will become the first streams of a valley that has not finished being born.
