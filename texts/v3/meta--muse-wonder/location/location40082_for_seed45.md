It is dark up here in a way that has nothing to do with night simply falling.

You come up through a sea of dry dust — not cloud, not fog, there is no moisture in it to wet your face — and you come out above it. Below you it moves in slow, regional swells, a pale ocean seen in starlight. Above you the sky is black and very close. The stars are hard, numerous, unblinking in the thin air, and they give just enough light to see by once your eyes have forgotten the sun.

And it is warm. That is the first wrong thing.

At this height the air should cut. Instead the wind that finds your neck is warm. The rock under your boots is warm. If you sit, heat comes up through stone that has never seen soil. If you put a palm flat, you can feel it as you would feel a sleeping animal, except there is nothing alive here to make it. The whole high country is exhaling heat from underneath.

This is a landscape that has not finished arriving.

As far as you can see — and you can see far, the scale opens all at once above the dust — there is a broad, uplifted plateau, miles across, tilting and doming almost imperceptibly toward a center that is not yet a peak. There is no single summit. There may be one tomorrow. Around the edges it breaks off into new cliffs that fall away into the dust-sea, and all across its surface it is sparsely marked by things that are still only beginnings: low mounds that are not yet hills, cracks that are not yet valleys, cones of black rubble no higher than a house that have not yet decided to become volcanoes.

Nothing here has been eroded. Nothing has had time.

The stone is raw. It breaks in blades and cubes and glassy shards with edges that can cut leather. There are no rounded boulders, no sand worn smooth, no gullies feathered by rain. When rock falls — and it falls often — it shatters bright inside, darker or more metallic than its outside, and lies where it landed. In places the surface rock is still streaked with unoxidized iron and copper, blue-black and peacock-green, colors that would dull in a season if the ground ever stood still long enough for a season to pass.

Instead the ground is in a state of continuous, turbulent making.

It does not erupt, exactly. It heaves. It ticks. A restlessness runs through it in waves. For an hour the plateau will seem solid, almost peaceful, and then a ridge a half mile off will shrug and shed its side in a long, silent slide. In this thin air the collapse makes almost no sound; you see the plume of black dust rise in slow motion and hang, and only much later, if at all, a faint brushing reaches you, like someone sweeping in another room. Nearer at hand, cracks open with small, precise snaps. They appear between one look and the next, a foot wide, ten feet, a hundred, breathing out hotter air that shimmers even in darkness. By morning a crack you stepped over may have become a trench with walls of fresh stone still ticking as they cool — though they never entirely cool.

You feel most of the motion in the soles of your feet. A low tremor that stops and starts. A tilt so gradual you only notice when the loose shards on a slope all begin, one by one, to creep in the same direction. Maps would be a kind of fiction here. Elevation is not a fact, it is a recent report.

There is no water. Not hidden, not seasonal — none. No snow despite the altitude, because the rock melts it from beneath and the air above steals what is left; no springs in the cracks, no damp in the hollows, no ice in shadow. Your mouth dries. Your lips split along lines that sting in the warm wind. The dust below is not a cloud deck; it is the mountain grinding itself into the sky and the sky too thin and dry to bring it back down as anything but more dust. That dust is why it is dark. It spreads above the uplift in a permanent, high canopy, turning day, far below, into a brown dusk, and up here turning even starlight into a kind of deep twilight. Once in a while a crack glows, dull red at its very bottom, just enough to show the edges of the stones around it — then dust sighs across and covers it.

There is almost nothing living to interrupt the mineral work. No grass tufting the cracks, no shrubs taking hold, no birds crossing the black sky, no insects. Wind sometimes carries things up from the lowlands — a dead leaf, a husk, a feather — and deposits them, perfectly preserved and desiccated, in a hollow, where they lie for a while like offerings before the next heave buries them in fresh rubble. In the warmest cracks there is a faint film, rust-orange and sulfur-yellow and a pale green so thin you doubt it, something simpler than lichen learning how to be alive on new rock. It does not spread. The ground moves too fast for it.

What grows best here is crystal. Overnight, along the lips of vents, salts bloom in white needles. Little sulfur flowers open around a fumarole that does not steam, only shimmers with heat. They are perfect for a few hours, untouched by wind or water, and then the stone shifts and they are ground back into the mountain.

Scattered across all this emptiness are the marks of people, but never many, and never for long.

A cairn, shoulder-high, built of the same black shards, now leaning because the ground under its left side has risen four inches since it was stacked. A low ring of stones for a windbreak, except the opening does not face away from the wind — it faces away from a crack that was not there when the ring was made. Charred wooden posts driven into rubble in a line that was once straight, meant to measure the rise, most of them now tilted at slightly different angles like a row of people losing their balance very slowly. Here and there a shallow pit dug and abandoned, a flat place where someone slept with their back to the warmest stone they could find. No tools left behind worth naming. No inscriptions that survive. The uplift takes a marker and makes it a ruin and then makes it geology again.

They are guides, of a kind. You navigate from cairn to cairn because there are no other landmarks that promise to stay. You learn the local rules quickly: do not camp in a hollow, however sheltered, because hollows are where the next crack prefers to open. Do not trust a slope that is quiet; trust the one that is already shedding, it has spent its energy for now. Walk in the middle of the old flows, where the stone is thickest. If the shards underfoot begin to ring — a faint, musical pinging as new stresses pass through them — move uphill, calmly, and keep moving until the ringing stops. If the warm wind suddenly goes still and the dust canopy above seems to lower, the plateau is about to breathe out somewhere, and you want not to be standing over its mouth.

For a place so violent, it is astonishingly quiet. The thinness steals sound. The dust steals what remains. You hear your own breath, labored at this height despite the warmth, you hear cloth, you hear the small clicks of stone expanding. Distant collapses, ventings, the birth of a new scarp two valleys over — for there are depressions forming that will one day deserve the name valley — arrive as pressure on the skin rather than noise. The turbulence is in your balance, in your eyes, in the horizon that will not hold a straight line. It is a storm happening in rock instead of air.

Stand still long enough at the center, where the dome is highest and the stars turn very slowly overhead, and you can sense the whole region rising. Not metaphorically. The lowlands are falling away. The dust-sea sinks, or you climb. New stone is arriving from below, pushing older new stone aside, splitting it, lifting it, spilling it off the edges to be buried and lifted again. This is pre-landscape — terrain before rivers have carved it, before soil has softened it, before life has signed its name on it. It has heat and height and darkness and motion, and it has not yet learned any of the gentler habits that would make it habitable.

It will, perhaps. In a thousand years the uplift may stall, the canopy thin, rain may find it, seeds may stay. For now it is an embryo of a mountain range, warm in the black, breaking itself open in near-silence under a sky it is still pushing closer to.
