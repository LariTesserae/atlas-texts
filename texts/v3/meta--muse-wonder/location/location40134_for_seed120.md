You find it by accident, and only because it is leaking.

High — impossibly high, where the air has given up being air and become a bright, thin ache in the teeth — there is a mountain that is not finished. It is not eroded. Nothing here is old enough to erode. It is being made, basket by basket, out of carried earth, broken slate, wet clay, dung, ash, crushed shell, and woven grass, piled into a steep, raw cone above the clouds. The slopes do not weather, they *cure*. They steam in the cold. Scaffolds of lashed bamboo cling to them like stitching. And at the very top, where there should be a point, there is instead a bulge, a swelling, a clay pot the size of a hut with no door you can see from the outside.

The entrance is underneath. You have to crawl up through a warm drainage pipe on your elbows to get in, and the pipe weeps on you the whole way.

Inside is six feet across. Inside is also, somehow, still going up.

That is the first rule: the room contains more altitude than it occupies. There is a floor of rammed, man-made soil — not dug soil, *manufactured* soil, kneaded by hand from charcoal dust, fermented leaves, ground bone, and roof-thatch rotted down in jars — but if you stand up too fast from it your ears pop. If you climb onto the low stone stool in the corner, your nose begins to bleed, thin and bright. On the stool, in a coconut half, there is frost. Below the stool, the floor is too hot to touch barefoot. Both things are true at the same time, packed in together, with maybe nine inches between them.

Everything is built. There is no natural substrate anywhere. The walls are coiled clay still soft enough to take a fingerprint, laid over a basket frame, patched with fresh mud that morning and already sprouting. The ceiling is a dome of interwoven living cane. The “rock” that weeps steadily into the central basin is not rock — it is a porous pot, fired badly on purpose, sweating water it pulls from a bamboo aqueduct that taps the clouds snagging on the scaffolding outside. Even the light is made: a dim, green-brown dusk filtered through layered leaves, oiled cloth, and woodsmoke, with one clay oil lamp burning animal fat in the middle, its flame leaning *downward* most of the time, licking at its own oil.

It is hot in here. Sauna-hot, jungle-hot, a wet heat that gets into your lungs and stays. Your clothes are soaked in minutes, not from the dripping — though everything drips — but from the air itself condensing on the cooler surface of your skin. You are the coldest thing in the room, so the room rains on you.

And it is *packed*. Lush does not begin to describe it. Plants grow on the walls, from the walls, through the walls, in trays, in hanging gourds, in the cracks between floor tiles, in the water basin, in the tool rack, in an old sandal left too long by the lamp. Ferns unroll as you watch, fast enough that you can hear the faint ticking of them. Orchids root in plain air and flower into the steam. Moss colonizes the hammer handles. A vine has taken the chisel. A broad taro leaf serves as the roof for a smaller, shadier garden underneath it, which shelters mushrooms that shelter a black beetle-garden of wet compost. There is no empty surface. Where there is no plant, there is a jar. Where there is no jar, there is a woven mat, a stone adze, a brush made of hair for hand-pollinating blossoms, a stack of unfired bowls, a bundle of straw, a sleeping body’s worth of blankets rolled and already mildewing beautifully. You turn sideways to move and still brush three wet leaves with each shoulder.

The sound is enormous for a space this small. Water hammers irregularly through the bamboo pipes, knocking. The porous pot ticks and sighs as it sweats. The kettle buried in the hot compost core at the center of the floor — there is always a kettle, there has to be a kettle, the compost is cooking itself alive beneath you — whistles through a split reed, stops, whistles again in a different note. Outside, the extreme-altitude wind is screaming across the unfinished peak, but inside the dome translates it into something lower and closer, a roaring in the walls as if you were inside a conch shell held to a fire. Leaves slap. Droplets fall up from the basin to the ceiling garden with a soft, reversed patter. The fresh clay of the walls makes tiny settling noises, pocks and sighs, birthing sounds. You have to raise your voice to hear yourself think.

Fauna is almost absent, which is why the pollination is done by hand, why the flies are painted on the wall in ochre to encourage the idea of flies. Almost. Very rarely — some say once a season, some say only when someone new has crawled up the pipe — a single white frog, blind, fat, the size of a plum, appears sitting in the frost on the stool at the highest point. It does not sing. Every other sound in the room stops anyway when it appears. Its throat pulses and the room’s pressure drops suddenly, painfully, as if the pocket had remembered it was at the top of the world. Then it is gone, and the kettle screams, and the drips resume, some of them falling sideways for a while until they remember how.

Because up is confused here. That is the deeper rule, under the altitude rule. Condensation gathers on the underside of leaves and falls *up* to water the ceiling. Smoke pools on the floor in a warm ankle-deep layer and the lamp burns down into its oil in search of it. A dropped seed sometimes fails to land, hovering, turning slowly, putting out a root toward the nearest wall and a shoot toward the basin, undecided. Yesterday’s rain is still falling in one corner, a localized drizzle descending very slowly through the thick air, each drop taking most of an hour to cross from leaf to floor, so you can duck under a drop in the morning and find it still there, trembling, at dusk. The builders say not to touch those. They are still deciding to be weather.

Nothing here is finished, and it must not finish. Each visitor is given, without explanation, a lump of wet clay and a handful of manufactured soil, and you understand instinctively that you are to add it somewhere — smear it into a wall, press the soil around a seedling, mend a pipe joint with it — before you leave. A stone is passed hand to hand and warmed. A length of cane is lashed one notch higher. The mountain below, the dome above, the garden within all proceed at the same pace: emergence as maintenance. If the making stopped, the pocket would dry, cool, equalize, remember its size. It survives only by being continuously invented.

Leaving is harder. The pipe, on the way out, is longer than it was coming in, and hot water runs against your face as you descend what feels like several thousand feet in the space of a body-length, ears clearing again and again. You emerge under the clay bulge at the summit into thin, blinding, freezing wind, soaked through, smelling of compost and orchids and animal fat, your hands still muddy.

From outside it is nothing. A swelling. A pot. A hidden, six-foot room at the top of a half-built mountain. From inside, you have been somewhere with its own summit weather, its own slow rain, its own extinct direction of fall — a world so dense and so new that reality, crammed in with everything else, has started to split at the seams and grow through the cracks, green.
