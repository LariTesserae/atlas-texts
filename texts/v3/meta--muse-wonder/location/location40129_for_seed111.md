At the top of the mountain there is a crack that was not there a hundred years ago.

The mountain is still being made — young, sharp, unsoftened, its summit ridge a serration of black schist and pale, freshly broken stone where no weather has yet had time to stain it. Up here the air is too thin to carry much of anything. It is cool even at noon, a clean, aching cold just above freezing, and a human breath comes out white and doesn't disperse so much as thin out and vanish.

The crack runs against the ridge, perhaps sixty metres long and twelve wide, forty deep. Cathedral scale, but inverted. Instead of rising into light, it falls into darkness. The walls are near-vertical, in places overhung, split so recently that the fracture faces are still bright — grey-white, granular, smelling faintly of struck rock — while the older faces beside them are black with lichen. You approach along the rim and at first it looks like nothing; a shadow line, a flaw. Then the sound reaches you.

It is loud in a way altitude should not allow.

Swifts live in there, thousands of them, and rosy-finches, and alpine accentors, and high, hysterical colonies of snow finches tucked into every horizontal flaw in the rock. In the deeper dark there are bats — improbably, stubbornly — small brown bodies packed in seams no wider than a hand. And below them, in the parts where no bird can fit, the rock is alive with smaller things: spiders in loose sheet-webs across the corners, harvestmen, beetles with reduced eyes, springtails gathered in trembling grey drifts on damp stone, moths, so many moths that on warm updraft days they arrive from the valleys below by the ten-thousand, carried up on the wind whether they will it or not.

That is, in fact, the rule of the place. Everything here is blown, fallen, or lured in.

At this elevation there is almost nothing to eat. The rim has a sparse skin of vegetation — orange and sulfur lichen, a few thumb-sized cushions of moss campion wedged where melt can reach them, their flowers absurdly pink and tiny. Inside the fissure, where light only enters directly for perhaps twenty minutes near solstice, when the sun aligns exactly with the slot, there is only a green-black algae film on the wettest walls. No soil to speak of. And yet the crack is dense, layered, near-touching life from rim to floor: nests made of dry grass and sheep's wool and their own feathers jammed into cracks, one above the other; ledges white and reeking with guano; webs furred with moth wings; crevices stuffed with the dry husks of grasshoppers, craneflies, butterflies, aphids, seeds — all the lowland detritus the wind strips off the slopes and drops, conveniently, into this sheltered hollow when the air finally stills.

It is an aeolian trap. A pantry at the roof of the world. The spiders know it. The swifts know it, screaming in and out of the dark at speeds that should kill them, missing the walls by centimetres, their wingbeats slapping back at them as echoes. The sound does not escape cleanly. The fissure is shaped like a throat, narrow at the top, bulbous in one chamber halfway down, and it holds noise, folds it. A single swift becomes three. A drip becomes a small bell. A chick begging becomes a chorus that seems to come from inside the rock itself. Stand on the floor — a jumble of fresh scree, slabs like broken slate roofs, feathers, bones, frozen moths perfectly preserved in a rind of old ice — and you cannot hear someone shouting two metres away for birds, for wings, for the constant papery rustle of invertebrates moving over stone.

And it is wet, but never with running water. Damp is a permanent condition, made by several slow processes conspiring. Snow lodges on the rim and melts grain by grain. Warm, animal breath — thousands of small warm bodies in a cold stone shaft — condenses on the walls and runs down in black films. Your clothes get wet just from leaning. Droplets form on the overhangs, swell over minutes, fall. There is a pool at the very bottom, no bigger than a bathtub, black as glass, fed only by those drips and by seepage through a hairline fracture. It never quite freezes, insulated by depth and by the faint, composting warmth of guano washed into it, and its surface is always stippled: a moth struggling, a feather turning in a current too slow to see, a springtail raft.

The darkness down there is not empty dark. If you stay still — and stillness is the second rule; move suddenly and the air explodes upward in a single shrieking vortex of birds, then resettles for an hour afterwards — your eyes adjust to a blue-grey gloom populated by movement. Pale spiders pick through moth fall. Bats shift and chitter and drop to drink without ever touching the pool. Finch chicks gape blindly at sounds that aren't their parents. Things land on you without fear because nothing large is supposed to be here: a moth on your eyelash, a spider lowering itself thoughtfully onto your shoulder to investigate this new, warm cliff.

Nothing built has ever lasted here. There is no shelter, no cairn that the frost hasn't heaved apart in a season. If people have come at all, they left only the simplest marks — a hand-hammered iron pin wedged in the rim rock and already split out again, a scratch where a crampon skated. The real constructions are animal and temporary and remade every year: mud-and-saliva nests, webs, the woven cups of finches, a raven's stick platform on the one ledge that gets morning shade, used for prising beetles out of cracks with a twig. Primitive tools for a primitive larder.

The whole system moves slowly, despite the noise. The fissure breathes once a day: cold air sinking into it through the long afternoon, pooling, then spilling almost imperceptibly out over the rim at night, carrying feather-down and moth scales with it. Ice in the deepest cracks expands in winter by millimetres and prises off another bright flake of schist. The pool rises a finger's width in the melt month and falls by evaporation none can feel. A swift returns to the exact black seam its grandparents used. A layer of guano becomes, over decades, a layer of dark, rich earth in which, impossibly, three blades of grass are now growing without light to speak of, pale as maggots, living off warmth and rot.

It should be a dead place — too high, too cold, too dark, too bare — and by any measure from the sunlit rim, it is. You look across the summit to other summits, all rock and ice and wind-scoured emptiness. Then you look down into the narrow split at your feet, where the air is thick with wings and the stone sweats and every surface crawls, and the screaming circles up around you undiminished, and it feels less like a crack in a mountain than like the inside of a body the mountain is keeping, quietly, for everything the sky throws away.
