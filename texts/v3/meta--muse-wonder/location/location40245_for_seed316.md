It is flat the way the ocean is flat when there is no wind — except there is always wind, and the water is never more than ankle-deep, and it goes on like that for a continent.

You can walk for a month in any direction and gain less height than a man standing. The horizon does not rise to meet hills. It does not fall away into valleys. It simply thins, loses color, and becomes sky, which is the same bruised, warm gray as the water. At some point during the second week the distinction stops being geographic. Up is where the sound is thinner. Down is where your feet disappear.

This was land, once. There were mountains here so long ago that their ghosts are only chemistry now — a little more iron in one band of mud, a little more salt in another, laid down in stripes hundreds of miles wide. Everything that could be eroded has been eroded. The rivers finished their work, then dried, then were forgotten by the ground they made. What remains is the end product of erosion: a skin of clay, silt, gypsum, and black organic mud packed so fine and so level that water, when it comes, has nowhere decided to go.

And water always comes.

It weeps up through the clay in cold spots on a warm day. It condenses out of air so saturated your lungs taste it. It falls in rain that arrives sideways, in sheets, in sudden warm veils. It stands. A finger's depth here, a hand's depth there, warm as bathwater, tepid with algae underneath. Under the mud, only inches down, is more water — briny, pressurized, ancient. Sometimes it sighs up through cracks and leaves white mineral lips around the hole. Sometimes it bursts.

The crust is a drumhead stretched over a drowned world, and the whole vast lowland is sounding.

That is the first rule here: it is never, ever quiet.

Not loud like a waterfall is loud, close and clean. Loud like being inside the waterfall, inside the thunder, inside a bell that is being struck by other bells on every side at once. The basin makes its own weather, and its weather makes sound, and its flatness gives sound nowhere to die. Storms are born out on the pan a thousand miles away — you can hear them being born. Their pressure waves travel across the unbroken water at terrible speed, losing almost nothing, picking up harmonics from rain, from boiling mud, from the crackle of sheet lightning crawling through ground fog. By the time they reach you they are not a noise coming from somewhere. They are a condition of the air.

The air is packed with it. That is literal. The atmosphere here is so humid, so warm, so saturated with mist and suspended spray and overlapping wavefronts that there seems to be no empty space left in it. You move through it the way you would move through a crowd — shouldering aside pressure. Your ears ache constantly. Your sinuses sing. Speak, and your voice comes back to you a fraction of a second later from the water at your feet, flattened and unfamiliar, then again from farther away, then again, until a sentence you said a minute ago is still out there wandering the pan, getting weathered.

In the worst places, where two or three storm-systems are grinding against each other beyond different horizons, the sound stacks. It becomes visible. The mist thickens into standing ribs and lattices. Rain hangs in midair in trembling curtains and does not quite fall. A hard enough boom from below — the subterranean water shifting in its caverns, the clay skin flexing — and the sheet-water on the surface jumps upward in a perfect, silent-looking disc, holds its shape for a heartbeat too long, and collapses. Dust and dry salt, on the rare raised patches, arrange themselves into rings, spirals, feather patterns, all vibrating, all updating themselves a thousand times a second. The world is drawing diagrams of its own noise and erasing them instantly.

Turbulence is constant, but nothing changes. That is the paradox the place has settled into. Waterspouts stitch cloud to pan and wander, sucking up black ribbons of mud. Micro-cyclones no taller than a house spin past full of reeds and white foam. Rain cells a mile wide hammer a circle of water to froth while, fifty feet away, the surface is a mirror holding a flawless, dim reflection of nothing. Mud geysers erupt, throw warm silt twenty feet up, and slump. Lightning goes sideways through the fog for minutes at a time, too diffuse to strike, just a long violet thinking in the clouds.

An hour later it is all level again. The mud, reduced over eons almost to a liquid already, flows back. The water spreads. Footprints fill, first with a rush, then with a slow, obscene patience, until the surface is unmarked. Storms sculpt the pan into waves, faces, temporary architectures of spray — and the pan accepts them the way a pond accepts a face leaned over it. It gives the shape back, briefly, distorted, and then it stops.

The light does not help you. There is a sun, technically, behind the perpetual high haze — a pale coin, a smear, a place where the gray is slightly less gray. Dawn and dusk stretch for hours and mostly change the temperature of the dimness. At midday it is like being inside a steamed window. At night — if the cloud breaks, which it does in patches, unpredictably — the stars are drowned almost at once by ground-mist rising to meet them. Reflections behave badly here. With the light so flat and the water so still between gusts, you will see a reed-bed that is not there yet, doubled below a reed-bed that has already been torn away. Mirages do not shimmer at the horizon. They squat at every distance. They have the same status as everything else.

Vegetation is sparse, ground-hugging, and sensible. Nothing tall survives the noise and the shear for long. There are reed mats that grow lying down, their stems jointed like fingers, pressed into radial patterns around every slight rise, as if the whole flora were perpetually bowing away from explosions. There are salt-tolerant creepers with leaves like wet leather, thick enough to deaden vibration. In the slightly deeper water there are algae films — green, rust-red, sometimes a shocking chemical blue — that tear, bloom, rot, and bloom again inside a single afternoon, popping with gas, adding their own soft percussion to the general roar. After a big pressure wave passes, whole acres of algae die at once and the water smells sweet and rotten and the surface goes briefly, horribly clear.

Animals are rarer than plants and mostly refuse the surface. The pan is full of life in the way a held breath is full of intention — underneath. Flatworms the length of canoes move through the mud below the crust, blind, deaf in any way you would recognize, navigating by the pressure grammar of the deep water. Things with shells burrow down and wait out years. The few birds are high, fast, and apparently miserable, crossing the lowland at insane altitude in straight lines, never landing, their calls arriving minutes after the birds themselves have vanished — so you are often startled by a cry coming out of an empty, dim sky directly above you, explaining a bird that is already gone.

And rarer still are the things the sound makes.

Because the sound does not only echo. It accumulates. It interferes with itself across a thousand miles of perfect reflector. In certain pressure-knots, where waves cross and reinforce, the spray and mud and mist are hammered for minutes or hours into cohesive shapes — a ridge that holds like a spine, a hollow that turns like a head, a quadruped ripple trotting alongside you at exactly your pace, made of standing water and vibration. The pan is forever almost-forming things and then losing interest. Walk long enough and you will be accompanied. Your own footsteps, amplified, phase-shifted by the crust, come back organized. Something made of your own noise learns your stride.

The only built things are apologies for this. Every few days of walking — or, just as often, not for a month — you find a berm: a low spiral of piled mud and reed bundles and flat stones, built by hand, by mouth, by dragged sled, by whatever people or near-people periodically attempt this place. The spirals are sound-shadows. Behind the thickest curve of a well-made berm the roar drops, suddenly, sickeningly, to something like the inside of a seashell, and in that pocket the air feels empty enough to frighten you. Your ears ring in the relative quiet. Your thoughts sound obscenely loud.

So the structures huddle there: a windbreak of woven reed, already rotting; a sleeping platform raised four inches — a mountain, locally — above the water; a stack of mud plates like a cairn, or an ear; sometimes a hut of salt-crusted hide over bent stems, half-dissolved back into the pan, its doorway always turned away from the prevailing pressure. No metal. No machinery. Anything hard enough to ring would be unbearable; anything complex would be shaken apart. Technology here has been edited down to mud arranged thoughtfully.

Some berms are inhabited, briefly. More are not. Some are inhabited in a way that is hard to verify, because the inhabitants communicate by pressing their palms and foreheads to the wet ground and letting the clay carry it, and because a voice behind a berm may belong to someone sheltering there now, or to someone who shouted there last season, whose words have been going around the basin and have only just come home.

That, finally, is the rule that replaces the others if you stay too long: sequence is a local opinion. Cause arrives when it can. Out on the pan you will hear the waterspout that dissolves in front of you an hour before it forms. You will hear your companion answer a question in an echo before you have finished asking it, and then watch them, puzzled, actually answer differently. Rain that has not fallen yet patters, faintly, inside the crust under your feet. The place is so vast, and sound is so imprisoned in it, and the surface is such a perfect ancient mirror, that past and future both just become directions sound can come from.

There is nowhere to go that is out of it. Lowering your head under the warm sheet-water does not help; the water carries the booming better than the air, and your skull becomes a listening instrument, and the deep caverns underneath are saying something enormous and slow that may be geology or may be weather in a sea that never sees the sky.

You stand up again. The water runs off you. The mist takes your outline, repeats it a few yards away, loses it. The roar finds the wet channels of your ears as if it had been waiting politely and resumes at full occupancy — storm, rain, mud, blood, breath, the pan flexing on its hidden ocean, the whole continent-sized instrument playing itself in dim warm light, packed to bursting with presences none of which need to be alive.

Level, and level, and level in every direction. Nothing finished moving. Nothing able to change.
