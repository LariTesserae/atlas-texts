It is hot at an altitude where it should not be hot.

You come up to it on a switchback road cut into white rock, breathing harder with every turn, the air thinning, and yet the heat does not thin with it. It thickens. By the last pass the dashboard thermometer is absurd, your shirt is stuck to you, your lungs work for less, and below you the whole high basin is shining.

There is no green in it. Not a tree, not a scrub margin, not grass in the ditches. The plateau is mineral from edge to edge — a broad, shallow valley three or four thousand meters up, floored in sinter and salt and pale travertine, rust-red where iron comes up in the water, mustard-yellow in the low places where sulfur crusts and breaks under its own weight. What looks from a distance like snowfields is salt. What looks like smoke is steam.

Water is everywhere and none of it is kind.

It weeps out of the rock faces in warm sheets. It condenses out of the bright air onto anything cold. It stands in terraced pools, milky turquoise and opal white and a clear, depthless blue, each rimmed with a beaded crust that has taken centuries to grow a centimeter. It runs in open canals down the middle of streets. It drips, constantly, from pipes, eaves, condenser fins, the undersides of walkways — a private, local rain that only falls under structures. The ground between buildings is too hot and too saturated to be dry mud or honest stone; it sighs. Small vents open and close in it. A boardwalk will be clear in the morning and by afternoon a new hot seep is darkening the planks from underneath.

This is why the town is urban and sparse at the same time.

On a map it is a city: districts, a grid, a power station, waterworks, freight yards, apartment blocks, a market hall, schools with shaded courtyards. On the ground it is mostly emptiness. No one builds on untested ground, and half the basin is untestable. Blocks stand a hundred meters apart across flats of crusted mud where only a mineral skin holds you above scalding water. Houses are low, wide-roofed, set on concrete stilts or thick sinter pads, their walls a patchwork of corrugated iron gone soft with rust, salt-blinded glass, lime wash renewed every year and already streaking. Between them the infrastructure does the crowding instead — pipes on trestles, insulated mains sweating into troughs, cable runs, raised boardwalks with handrails hot to the touch. Everything is connected by long straight lines across open nothing.

The industry makes almost no sound, which is the first thing you notice once you are inside it.

The turbines are underground, in chambers cut into the rock, taking steam directly from boreholes. The mineral works — lithium and borax and sulfur taken from the brines — are a series of low evaporation sheds and sealed centrifuge halls downwind, electrified, automated, venting vapor through tall condenser towers that bloom white against a white sky. There are no smokestacks roaring, no traffic worth naming. Vehicles are electric sleds on the boardwalk rails and they glide. Doors close softly because the humidity swells them. Even the geysers at the far end of the basin, which erupt on a twenty-minute cycle to a height of two stories, do it with a sort of exhalation and a rain of warm droplets, not a boom.

And over it all the animal life, which is abundant, is also quiet.

There should not be this much life without plants, but the food chain here does not start with leaves. It starts with mats — bacterial, not vegetative — in the hot runoff channels, slicks of orange and emerald and chalk-white that thrive at temperatures that would cook anything else. Brine flies gather over them in clouds so dense they look like a shadow on the water, but their wings make no audible hum in the saturated air. Tiny red shrimp pulse in the cooler salt ponds. And the birds come for both: thousands of them, white and pink-tinged waders with black bills, picking through the shallows on stilts of their own, lifting and settling in long, soundless rearrangements. They rarely call. Lizards the color of wet ash flatten themselves on the warm boardwalk posts, throats barely moving. Big black beetles cluster under every cold pipe where condensation beads, drinking in a slow, ordered rotation, the first one dislodged by a drip moving patiently to the back of the line. At dusk, moths come out of the rock crevices where they spend the day against the heat — pale, eyeless-looking things that fly inches above the warm ground, following the humidity, and the waders walk among them snapping quietly.

People have adapted their rhythms to match the silence. No one shouts across the flats; the wet air carries a normal voice oddly far and flattens its edges, so people walk closer to talk, or don't. Midday is an interior time, not because of siesta custom but because the light is intolerable — a white, vertical, high-altitude glare doubled off the salt pans, bright enough to hurt through closed eyelids, and the reflected heat comes up off the ground to meet the sun coming down. Streets empty. The steam hangs lower, trapped. Condensation rains hardest then, under the roofs, off the cold-water mains, a steady patient ticking into gutters that is almost the loudest sound in town.

Work happens at the bright margins of the day when the angle helps, and through the night under floodlights in the brine yards, where the air is merely hot instead of punishing. The rules are learned by the body quickly: never step off a marked path, however solid the crust looks. Never drink from a pool, however clear; one will be near-boiling, the next alkaline enough to take skin, the next sweet with dissolved metals. Touch metal with the back of your hand first. Shake your boots out in the morning for beetles seeking the damp leather. Leave a bowl under a condenser, not under a roof edge — you want the water the machines have cleaned by cooling, not the water the town has shed.

After a few days the strangest part is not the heat or the emptiness. It is how established it all feels. The sinter terraces under the oldest district have grown up around the foundations, sealing them in, so the first houses now sit in mineral bowls of their own slow making. Grandmothers point out pools that have migrated a street over in their lifetimes and bathing steps, now dry and crusted, that lead down into white stone. The power station has been rebuilt three times on the same pad because the boreholes wander. Nothing here is permanent on a human timescale, but everything has been here long enough to be weathered into a working truce.

In the evening, when the sun finally drops behind the western rim, the whole basin exhales at once. Steam rises in columns from a hundred vents and canals and rooftops, the birds lift in one slow white sheet and resettle on the cooling ponds, the bacterial mats give off a faint smell of iron and warm bread, and the town — its pipes dripping, its lights coming on in separated clusters like small constellations across the darkening salt — goes on quietly making power and salt and drinkable water out of a place that offers heat and poison in abundance.

You can stand on the boardwalk then and hear your own sweat hit the planks. Nothing answers it.
