It is a low country of black glass and standing water, and it is loud the whole way through.

You come into it from higher ground — not high, nothing here is high — and the valley opens ahead for miles in a dim, pearled light that never quite becomes day. The cloud deck sits a few hundred feet up, unmoving. No direct sun gets through it. The light arrives already scattered, gray-white, shadowless, so the wet stone shines as much as the water does and at first you cannot tell where the ground ends and the pools begin.

The ground was made recently. That is obvious in every cut and edge. This was once a sheet of molten rock, then a shallow lake over it, then the lake drained or boiled or slipped away through a crack somewhere to the south — recently enough, in stone terms, that nothing has had time to be worn dull. The floor of the valley is a pavement of basalt and obsidian, fractured into plates while it cooled, and from that pavement rises the dense part: fins.

Thousands of them. Tens of thousands, across the whole regional sprawl of the basin.

They are thin walls of black and bottle-green glass, shoulder-high to three times your height, sharp as broken bottles along their crests. Some stand alone like blades planted upright. Most are joined into crooked, parallel ranks, or into labyrinths, or into incomplete circles with a pool sealed inside. They are near-touching. In the thickest fields you walk sideways between them, pack scraping, hands careful, because a casual palm will open on an edge that has never been touched by wind- or water-wear. Your boots ring on the pavement, and your clothes are instantly wet whether or not it is raining.

It is almost always raining, or was raining, or is about to. That is not quite right either. The air itself is condensing. The fins are cooler than the mist that drifts in from the low marshes beyond the valley rim, so water gathers on their faces first as a beading sweat, then as clear rivulets that follow old gas-bubble grooves down to the roots. From every crest, drips fall. From every overhang, drips fall. Between the fins the ground is covered by a sheet of water an inch to a foot deep, unbroken for acres in places, broken in others into stepped pools held back by fallen shards.

There is no plant life to soften any of this. No grass in the cracks, no moss on the north sides — north means nothing under this sky — no reeds in the shallows. The color in the pools does not come from plants. One pool is milky turquoise with suspended silica, the next is clear over white precipitate, the next rust-red where iron is coming out of a fresh fracture and oxidizing in a thin mineral film. In the stillest corners that film gathers into a fragile, rainbow skin, and beneath it, if you look very long, there is a translucent bacterial mat, less than a living thing and more than a stain, slowly laying down threadlike stone. That is the whole of the lower ecology: things that turn dissolved rock into slightly different rock.

And yet the basin is deafening.

The fins are hollow. Not all of them, and not hollow like caves — hollow like bells. When the cooling rock trapped gas, it stretched it into long internal chambers, some sealed, some opened by a hairline crack to the outside. A drip falling from twelve feet onto a fin does not go *pat*. It goes off like a struck drum. A rivulet running into a crack sets up a sustained hum. Wind — and there is always a low movement of air down the valley, too slow to call a breeze — finds the edges and the openings and makes them moan in a narrow band, almost a voice, almost many voices overlapping at slightly wrong intervals.

Because the fins are so dense, nothing sounds once. Every ring is passed to a neighbor. A single falling stone the size of a walnut, somewhere you cannot see, will announce itself for half a minute, note to note through the ranks, getting lower and larger as it goes. Your own footsteps come back to you from three directions, late. Speech at a normal volume is useless past an arm's length. Anyone traveling here learns to shout, or to stop trying to speak at all and use hands, touches, pointed fingers. Past ten paces, though, a shout carries absurdly well, flattened and clarified by being conducted along the wet stone, so that a stranger a quarter-mile away will hear you more clearly than the companion at your elbow. Distance here is not measured by sight, under the mist, or by effort, across the maze. It is measured by pitch. Near things are dull. Far things ring bright.

Despite all that noise, nothing is moving fast. That is the strangest rule of the place. The water seeps, it does not flow. The pools rise a fraction of an inch over a day and drain as slowly. The cloud does not scud, it only thickens toward evening until the light goes from dim to a deeper dim and the dripping increases. The fins are not visibly weathering; a scratch you make will still be white and raw years later. Even the sound, for all its volume, is the product of very slow accumulations — a bead of water taking a full minute to swell large enough to fall, a crack drinking a cupful per hour and sighing it out as tone. You are inside a loud, patient process that will take ten thousand years to round off a single edge.

What lives here has adapted to patience and to noise.

Fauna is rare. You can walk half a day counting only the mineral mats and your own echoes, and feel, correctly, that you are being listened for. In the deeper pools there are pale, eyeless shrimp, nearly transparent except for a gut full of white grit, filtering precipitates with fans that move in exaggerated slowness against the cold. They do not flee a shadow — they have never needed to — but they fold instantly at a vibration in the stone. Gathering over them, perhaps once in a morning, will be the only large animal the basin supports: a tall, gray, wading thing, heron-shaped but not a heron, its head unfeathered and smooth, its eyes reduced to covered pits. It does not look for food. It stands in a ringing pool with one foot lifted for minutes at a time, then sets the foot down deliberately, *toc*, and listens to the returning pattern for the interruption that means shrimp below the surface. When it strikes, the whole circle of fins takes up the splash. Then silence under the noise again, for a long time.

Smaller, and rarer still, are the scrape-snails that travel the wet fin faces, radulas rasping inaudibly under the general clamor, leaving a faintly polished trail where the iron film has been eaten away. Their shells are made of the same black glass they move on, scavenged and cemented grain by grain, so they look like moving knots in the rock until they don't.

People have been here, though never many, and never for long. There is nothing to eat except what you carry, nothing to burn, nothing soft to lie on. But the basin lies across a low route between two watersheds, so travelers have crossed it since before tools were much more than held stones. Their marks are scattered and small. A cairn of flat shards at a place where three ranks open into a slightly wider aisle. A windbreak in the lee of two fins, made from fallen plates leaned together and chinked with wet sand. A circle of stones around a pool that runs, by local standards, sweet — less metallic on the tongue.

Most distinctive are the quiet huts. Here and there a previous party has found, or made, a place where two resonant fins face each other out of phase, and by packing the gap between their bases with sand and silt and broken glass they have cancelled the ring. Step inside the low, roofless enclosure and the volume of the world drops by half. Step fully in, lie down in the wet grit with your ears below the rim, and you can hear your own breathing again, and your heartbeat, startlingly interior after days of being conducted outward into stone. These huts are never large, never roofed — there is nothing to roof them with — and they are always already damp, but they are maintained across generations by strangers who will never meet: each sleeper repacks a little sand before leaving, adds a plate where a plate has slipped. The only building tradition the valley allows is the making of small pockets of less sound.

By evening, which is only a deepening, the mist comes down between the fins until you are walking in two mediums at once, water to your ankles and cloud to your waist, and every surface weeps at its maximum rate. The chorus swells without quickening. High notes from the thin crests, low booms from the great hollow ranks at the valley center where the original cooling was thickest, an irregular percussion of drips falling into pools inside sealed circles and amplifying there until the stone lip itself vibrates against your calf as you pass.

There, at the center, the ranks are so dense and so tall that the open scale of the valley is forgotten; you could be in a city of wet black walls built by no one, extending in all directions, full of bells no one is striking, in a light that has forgotten its source. And then a gap opens, a pool widens, the mist thins for a hundred yards, and you see it all again laid out — miles of young glass under low cloud, water standing and falling and standing, nothing green, nothing hurrying, everything sounding.

You leave with the ring still in your teeth. On higher, softer ground beyond the rim, where the first true soil in days makes your steps go suddenly and shockingly mute, the ordinary quiet of the world feels not like peace but like deafness, as if a part of your hearing that had been occupied, continuously and at great volume, by slow water on new stone, had abruptly been handed back to you empty.
