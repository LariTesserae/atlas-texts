It is a pocket, not a valley. At just under four thousand metres, where the mountain should have finished narrowing to bare rock and wind, a fold in its upper face holds still long enough for a forest to grow.

The fold is small — perhaps eighty metres across, a hundred and twenty from its upper lip to where it spills out again over a drop. You could throw a stone across it if the wind allowed a stone to travel in the direction you threw it. But crossing on foot takes an hour. Everything inside is near-touching. Everything has something else growing on it.

Three ribs of young rock enclose it. The stone has not had time to soften. It rises in black, angular blades and fresh scree, split along clean faces, the edges sharp enough to cut a palm. New fractures show pale grey against the weather-darkened surfaces, and after each strong gust a few more fragments tick and skitter down the slopes, coming to rest against moss or dropping silently into vegetation so thick it swallows the sound of their landing. There is no soil in any ordinary sense, only root, rot, stone, and moss holding itself to stone.

Inside the ribs, the air changes. The ridge above takes the weather at full force and pours it over. Cloud comes across the summit in a fast, boiling sheet, hits the lip, and falls in. It tumbles, reverses, tears open for three seconds on a view of nothing but more cloud, then closes. Rain does not so much fall here as pass through — sideways, upward in the eddies, briefly straight down when the bowl shelters into a sudden, complete calm. Then the next gust arrives over the edge like water over a weir and the whole place is moving again.

It is dark at midday. The cloud ceiling is low and black-green, and beneath it the canopy makes a second ceiling, and beneath that the undergrowth makes a third. What light remains has a drowned quality, as though it has filtered through deep water. Greens go almost luminous in it — moss green, fern green, the wet black-green of small leathery leaves — while everything else loses its colour. Your hands look grey. The rock looks black. Mist catches in the beams between trunks and hangs there in bright, drifting rags before the wind pulls it apart.

It is cool in the way that works slowly into you: seven, eight degrees, but damp, so that it reaches through layers. Condensation forms on any cool surface within minutes. Breath shows. Water here is mostly held, not flowing. There is no lake, no permanent stream, only moisture kept in suspension and in storage — fog condensing on leaves until the leaves tip and release it, moss that can be wrung out like a sponge, black hollows in the rock that fill during a squall and empty in a thin, temporary thread that disappears under the roots and is gone within the hour. For a few minutes after heavy rain the whole bowl ticks and drips at different pitches from a thousand surfaces. Then the wind shakes it all off at once and it begins again. Boots are never in standing water and are never dry.

The vegetation is lush past reason for the altitude. The trees are stunted — three, four metres at most, trunks no thicker than a thigh, bent into flag shapes by the prevailing wind — but they carry ten times their own visible weight in other plants. Moss coats them to the last twig, dripping in beards and cushions. Liverworts plate the underside of every branch. Ferns crowd the ground in layered fans, their new fronds still coiled, beaded with water. Dense, wiry bamboo grass fills the gaps between trunks at waist height. Bromeliads and orchids sit in every fork holding their own small, still pockets of water. Fallen branches do not reach the ground; they are caught in the weave of living growth a metre up and slowly absorbed into it, still recognisable as wood for a season, then only as a long green shape.

Nothing moves among it that is alive in the animal sense. There is no birdsong under the wind, no insect hum at the flowers — the few flowers are small, pale, wind-pollinated things that open without waiting for visitors — no tracks pressed into the moss, no droppings, no grazing damage, no spiderwebs, because any web built here would be torn out within the hour. It is a full ecology with the animal layer simply missing, plants growing and rotting and growing into their own rot, spores moving on the wind where seeds might have needed carrying. The lushness makes the absence more noticeable, not less. You keep expecting a bird to burst out of the grass. One doesn't.

And the place is loud. The wind on the ridge above is a continuous roar, rising into something close to a scream when it finds the notch between the rock ribs. Below that is the thrash of branches, the hiss of shaken water leaving leaves, the intermittent percussion of rockfall. And below that, incongruous and regular, is the industry.

An aerial ore tramway crosses directly over the bowl. No building stands here — the nearest station is somewhere below the cloud on the far side of the mountain — but two steel cables enter the fog on one side and leave it on the other, and along them, every half minute or so, a loaded bucket comes through. You hear it before you see it: a rising hum in the cable, a rattle as the hanger takes the strain, then the bucket itself swinging out of the mist, penduluming wildly in the gusts, ore shifting and grinding inside it. At the eastern rib, a single pylon leg anchors into the rock, rust scaled orange beneath a sheath of moss, its buried power cable giving off a faint electrical ticking in wet weather that you feel in your teeth if you stand near it. The machinery is unmanned, automatic, utterly indifferent. In the worst of the storm, when the cloud tears and the whole bowl thrashes, the buckets keep arriving out of the darkness at the same interval, swaying over the treetops, and vanish into the darkness on the far side. The one regular thing here is not natural.

That sets the rules of the place. You move between gusts, because during them you cannot always stay upright. You listen before crossing under the cable line, because a swinging bucket in fog gives you only seconds of audible warning. You do not follow water downhill, because any stream here is an hour old at most and will take you only to a root mat where it vanishes. You do not wait for the light to improve, because it won't — the darkness is not a time of day, it is the bowl's permanent interior weather, broken only when a rent in the cloud lets through a brief, directionless glare that flattens everything and is gone before your eyes adjust.

Outside the eastern rib, the forest stops the way a coastline stops. One step past the last flagged tree and the vegetation thins to a crust of lichen, then nothing, and the mountain falls away in wet black rock with the wind coming up it carrying rain from below. The pocket has edges. Within them: a small, dark, saturated room of green at the top of the world, shaking almost apart in weather that never finishes arriving, while overhead the buckets go through the cloud, loaded, on time, their cables humming in the gusts as if the mountain itself had been fitted with machinery and left running.
