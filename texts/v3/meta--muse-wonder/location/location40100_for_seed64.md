At 5,400 metres there is almost nothing to arrive through. The ridge falls away in black scree and old snow that never quite melts, the sky is a hard, thin blue even at midday, and your breath comes short on the service stairs no matter how slowly you climb. The plant — an air-liquefaction and ore-hoist station built into the summit rock — is a low, riveted shed of galvanized steel anchored with cables, humming inside its own weather. Outside, wind. Inside, machinery. Between the two, behind Compressor No. 2, there is a hatch that was meant to be opened once a year by a man with a wrench.

It is ninety centimetres wide. It does not lead anywhere.

You have to take your ear defenders off to work the seized latch, and that is the first mistake, because the sound does not stay in the engine hall. It pours out of the dark in a solid, physical column — not a roar that rises and falls, just one continuous industrial chord, a hundred-hertz drone through steel, a hiss of escaping pressure that never empties, a relay clicking at exactly the same interval it has always clicked. It has been so loud, for so long, in exactly the same way, that the leaves do not tremble so much as hold a permanent vibration, the way a tuning fork holds its note. Shouting changes nothing. The sound has no gaps in it for a voice to fit into.

And it is full of leaves. That is the disorientation — not a plant *in* the closet, but a closet entirely replaced by vegetation, packed from the diamond-plate floor to the conduit at the crown, from the back wall to the inside of the hatch itself, so that opening the door is like cutting into a loaf. There is no empty pocket larger than a hand anywhere in it. If you push your fingers in, you touch three different leaves and a warm pipe before you have gone ten centimetres.

It is, in its small vertical way, a complete terrain.

The floor is not floor anymore. Under a springy mat of root and compacted black cake — coal dust, cinder, powdered insulation, dried lubricant worked together into a soil that has never been wet — the steel plate makes a faint metallic hollow when you knock it. Roots run in braided rivers along the drainage grooves, thick as electrical cable at the main stems, thinning to white wire where they probe behind gauge housings. There are no puddles, no damp corner, no dew. The air that sighs out past your face is bone-dry, cool and stable on the skin — twelve degrees, always twelve degrees, regulated by a thermostat whose needle has not moved in any living person's memory — and it cracks your lips within minutes. It smells of hot copper, ammonia, ozone from the motor brushes, and a green, bitter smell that is not quite plant sap. More like heated refrigerant.

The canopy is at face height because everything is at face height. Broad, waxy fronds the size of dinner plates overlap in storeys, their surfaces dry as paper to the touch and faintly warm from the pipes behind them. Between them, a finer growth fills the interstices: moss in dense black-green cushions around every bolt head, lichen in pale rosettes on the bakelite junction boxes, vines that have learned the exact pitch of the pipe threads and spiral without a wasted millimetre. Lower down, in the dimmest stratum near the root mat, the leaves are almost black. Higher, where the one caged inspection bulb burns behind a fan of fronds, they go a saturated, bottle green, lit from behind so that every vein stands out like wiring. Dozens of small status LEDs — red, amber, green — burn steadily through the foliage at night-shift brightness, unblinking, giving the interior the look of a forest in which tiny, patient machines are watching from the undergrowth.

But they do not grow toward the light. That is the thing you notice second, and keep returning to. Every leaf in the closet is angled, with botanical precision, toward the back wall, toward the compressor. The fronds present their broad faces to the sound. The moss is thickest around the compressor mounting bolts. The vines climb *away* from the bulb, into the hottest, darkest seam where the exhaust trunking passes. Whatever this ecology is feeding on, it is not illumination.

It is feeding, as far as anyone who has ever logged it can tell, on the machine itself. A compressor weeps. All compressors weep — a molecule at a time, an aerosol of synthetic oil and refrigerant and metallic dust from the bearings, invisible, unending, exactly replenished by the reservoirs on the other side of the wall. The root cake drinks oil the way soil on Earth drinks rain. The fronds condense coolant vapour on their waxy undersides and draw it in through pores that open to pressure, not to sunlight. Their sap, if you crush a stem — they are strangely hard to crush, fibrous as cable insulation — is not green juice but a clear, cold fluid that evaporates off the skin instantly and leaves a smell of ammonia. Nothing here has ever needed water. Water, at this altitude, in this dry and poisoned air, would be the one useless thing you could offer it.

There has never been an animal in it. Not a gnat in the fronds, not a spider in the marvellous shelter of the conduit corner, not a beetle in the root mat, not so much as a dead moth under the bulb. The ammonia trace that the plants metabolise so happily is lethal at insect scale, and the sound — that sound the leaves have shaped themselves around — sits at a frequency that keeps the interior acoustically sterile. It is a lush place with the fauna simply deleted. The silence where birdsong, rustling, chewing, any of that should be, is filled completely and uniformly by machinery.

The human things are still in there, demoted to geology. A fitter's enamel mug, rim outward, has become a node where four vines meet and thicken. A short aluminium step-ladder, meant for reaching the high valves, now functions as a trellis and a kind of ridge-line, the only topography in the closet that rises at an angle the plants did not invent. There are shelves, brackets, a lubrication chart in a plastic sleeve gone opaque, a small clay pot — the original pot, people say, from the fern an operator's wife sent up in a supply crate decades ago to prove something living could stay on the summit. The pot is empty and cracked, half-buried in the root mat near the centre, and the plants are polite around it the way ivy is polite around a ruin, which is to say not at all.

It has been weathered into this state over a very long time, and now it has stopped. That is the deepest quality of the place, deeper than the strangeness of its diet: it does not change. A photograph wired to the inside of the hatch — dated, faded, taken with flash — shows the same frond, half-unfurled, that is half-unfurled today. The same leaf occludes the same pressure dial at the same angle. The drip of oil that should, by any ordinary accounting, eventually fill or foul or rot something, is consumed at precisely the rate it is produced. Growth and decay have arrived at an exact, humming stalemate. A leaf that browns is replaced at the speed of its browning. The temperature does not swing when the summit goes to thirty below in a storm. The bulb does not burn out, or if it did once, someone changed it for an identical bulb in an identical cage and the green dimness resumed without a seam. Outside, seasons scour the ridge and the light fails and returns. In here, behind the hatch, it is a single held moment of late maintenance-shift, cool and loud and full, sustained indefinitely.

You cannot enter it. That would require a body-sized absence in the vegetation, and there is none. You stand on the engine-hall grating with your face in the opening, breathing air that has been breathed by leaves and compressors for fifty years, your ears aching, your forehead almost touching a frond that will not bend back after you close the door — it is already pressing itself toward where the door is about to be, resuming its shape in advance.

You close it anyway. The latch takes. The drone in the hall drops by some small, unimportant fraction, and the leaves go on facing the back wall in the green dark, packed stem to pipe to stem, drinking oil-mist in an unchanging room at the top of the world that nobody needs to visit for it to continue exactly as it is.
