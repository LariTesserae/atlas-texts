It is a plain, but nothing under you is ground.

As far as you can lose it in the dark — and the dark gives out before the plain does — the valley floor is made. Cast concrete, poured in slabs the size of houses, joined with black iron expansion seams. Steel plate where the concrete wore through. Ceramic tile, tar, riveted sheeting, gratings set flush and long since polished bright. Under gratings, more gratings. In the maintenance shafts people lowered lights on cables thirty, fifty meters and found only older construction: pipe laid on brick laid on earlier concrete, going down until the light became an orange smudge in wet air and vanished.

It has been flattened by time into its final shape. There are no sharp corners left. Bollards are mushrooms of iron, their heads worn smooth and headless bolts sit like knuckles. Stairs have become ramps. Where factory walls once rose, their foundations remain as low, rounded curbs you can step over without noticing. Rain, foot traffic, floodwater, and a million passages of wheeled machines have planed everything to one continuous, gently undulating surface that sheds water in every direction at once. You can stand in one place and watch a puddle slowly migrate west, meet a seam, hesitate, and crawl east instead.

It is low, cool, and permanently wet without quite raining.

Water comes up more than it comes down. It weeps from seams. It condenses on every upright thing and runs in cold beads down pipes. It sheets across the slabs in films too thin to ripple, gathers in the depressions where vents exhale warm air, and steams there in the chill. The air smells of wet iron, hot oil kept at a distance, stone dust, and the faint green smell of algae. Your clothes dampen from the inside of your collar outward. Breath shows, is torn sideways by sound, and disappears into mist.

The sound is the first true terrain.

It is deafening in a way that makes near and far meaningless. A low pounding lives in your sternum and teeth, a cycle of pressure and release that never completes. Over it, turbines sigh through cracked housings miles away. Over that, water hammer knocks in buried mains — *clang, clang, clang* — arriving through the soles of your boots before it arrives through the air. When the sluices change phase, which they do every forty minutes or so, the whole plain lifts a fraction in pitch. Puddles shiver into standing patterns, neat rows of trembling droplets. Loose grit walks itself into lines along the seams. You do not talk here. You shout into an ear and watch the words fail. People who work a shift together speak with shoulders, with flat hands pressed to a forearm: *stop, go, lift, run.*

And yet it is sparse.

Most of the plain is empty. You can walk an hour across the wet slabs and pass nothing taller than your knee: a capped pipe, a valve wheel frozen open, a junction box humming to itself with one amber indicator fogged behind glass. Then, in the murk, a presence resolves — a pump house the length of a train, its roof long ago subsided into a shallow bowl that holds black water. A gantry crane stranded mid-rail, counterweight hanging, cable swaying though the air seems still. A row of cooling stacks truncated at different heights, none of them smoking, all of them breathing cold vapor from side louvers. Between these things, vast balanced emptiness. The eye finds no purchase. The mist does not hide density; there simply is not any.

The vegetation knows where to keep out of the way. It comes up only in the joints.

Grey-green tussocks force through expansion seams, flattened by vibration into rosettes. A kind of reed without height grows in the drainage channels, lying almost horizontal, its hollow stems tuned — when the pounding rises they buzz faintly, each at a slightly different note, and shed pollen not to wind but to the shaking itself. Pale flowers, thumbnail-sized and waxen, open in the brief lulls between cycles and snap shut when the next compression rolls through. In protected corners behind transformers there are mats of black moss and a lichen the color of old copper, perfectly circular, never overlapping. Nothing climbs. Nothing provides shade. The plants do not soften the place. They measure it, colonizing only those millimeters where the machine tolerates a crack.

There is no animal life to measure it with. No birds cross the low sky. No insects gather at the few working lamps — and there are few, a sodium light here and there on a bent pole, making a small wet halo in which rain that is not rain becomes visible. No rats in the channels. No frogs though the water is everywhere shallow and slow. The vibration would not allow eggs. The water, which cycles endlessly from sump to tower to channel to sump, carries a clean, mineral bitterness that leaves a white crust on concrete and kills mosquito larvae before they can twitch. Even the algae stays thin, a slick rather than a bloom. Once you understand the absence, you begin to notice your own body as the only soft, warm, moving thing for a kilometer in any direction, and the plain seems to notice it too. Motion sensors long divorced from any network still turn their blind heads as you pass.

Because it is still active. That is what keeps it from being a ruin.

Pumps start in sequence out in the dark, each one adding its note until the chord is unbearable, then dropping out in reverse order. A sluice gate on the northern berm sighs open and for six minutes the drainage channels run swift and brown with disturbed silt, though no soil exists here to make silt from — it is ground concrete, ground rust, the plain slowly consuming itself and circulating the powder. Fans turn behind mesh. A freight elevator with no building around it rises from a pit, empty, waits, descends. Warning lights sweep once across the mist, find nothing, sweep again. Every process is maintained, oiled, powered. Nothing is being manufactured that you can identify. No trucks leave loaded. No product accumulates. The industry has outlived its purpose and refined itself down to pure operation: keep the water moving, keep it level, keep it from ever settling and taking hold.

Toward what would be morning — the darkness thins from black to a deep iron blue, but the sun does not properly arrive, as if the sky above the mist is another slab turned face-down — you can see farther. The plain extends to a perimeter wall so distant and so low it might be a change in weather. Ranked beyond the pump houses are the silhouettes of further pump houses, transformer yards, pipe bridges stepping over channels on bowed legs, all fewer and lower the farther they stand, eroded toward the horizon. Regional in scale. A town built not for people to live in but for water to be persuaded, again and again, to go elsewhere.

Walk long enough and a rule suggests itself, not posted anywhere — the placards are all worn blank — but enforced by the place itself: do not stop the sound by listening for its source. There is no source. Press your palm to a pipe and the pipe is loud. Press it to the concrete a hundred meters from any machine and the concrete is loud. The slab is one instrument, struck from below by its own buried workings. If there is anything natural left in this valley, it is down there, under the total floor, under the pilings and sumps and old foundations, being pumped at, sung at, held at bay by noise.

At the deepest lull, just before the sluices change, those white flowers in the seams open all at once across the visible dark, thousands of them, for perhaps ten seconds. The water goes briefly mirror-still. In that stillness you can see them reflected, and the lamps, and your own shape standing alone in the manufactured emptiness — and then the cycle takes, the flowers shut, the plain resumes its deafening work, and the reflections break into light.
