It begins flat, and stays flat longer than your eyes know how to measure.

The land is a low basin, warm and newly dry, so young as geology that it has not yet learned how to erode. There are no valleys here, no gullies cut by rivers, no hills worn down into shapes. Only a vast floor of packed silt and pale clay, cracked into polygons in the dry months, dusted with salt in the lowest places, stretching until the heat itself blurs the edge of it. On a clear morning you can see weather happening *inside* the basin — a column of dust turning slowly twenty kilometers away, a stack of white cloud building over one district and dissolving over another — without ever seeing where the plain ends.

Everything else has arrived faster than the land has. The city came, and the scrub came, and the animals came, and they packed themselves into the new ground shoulder to shoulder.

There is no center. Long ago there may have been separate towns and farming villages, but they grew toward each other along well-paths and goat tracks until roof met roof, garden met garden. Now it is one continuous settlement at the scale of a small country: low houses of sun-dried mud brick, lime-washed in white and ochre and a dusty rose, courtyard behind courtyard, alley behind alley, for days of walking. Nothing rises much above two stories. Nothing needs to. There is always more flat ground, and the ground is still settling — in places a wall will slump a thumb’s width in a year, a floor will dome gently as the young lakebed underneath compacts and exhales its last buried damp. People build accordingly: thick walls, flexible reed roofs, beams that can be lifted and reset. A house is not finished so much as continually re-made.

There is almost no water to be seen. That is the first rule, and everything else is arranged around it.

No river crosses the basin. No lake shines in it. Wells are deep, narrow, and capped with heavy stone lids. Every house has a cistern sealed under its courtyard, cool and dark, fed by a roof that is not quite flat — tilted by the width of a hand toward a clay gutter, waiting for the three or four true rains of the year. Those rains, when they come, are a public event. Doors open. Jars are carried out. The alleys run ankle-deep for an hour, children shout, and then the plain drinks it all and is dry again by evening, smelling of wet dust and crushed leaves.

And yet, impossibly, it is green.

Not green like a forest after rain. Green like a garden kept alive by intention. Between the houses, in every shaded courtyard, behind every kitchen wall where rinse-water is thrown, the vegetation is dense to the point of insistence: prickly pear grown into living fences, their paddles overlapping; aloe and sisal; moringa trees trimmed hard and leafing again; baobabs at the oldest crossroads, trunks swollen with stored water, canopies flattened against the sky. Fig trees find the cracks beside cisterns and split them wider. Bougainvillea spills over limewash. In the market quarters, vendors sell greens grown in sunken beds under reed shade, watered by the cupful, mulched with goat dung and shredded straw so that not a drop meets the sun unprotected. From a rooftop — and rooftops are lived on, slept on, used for drying peppers and grain and laundry — the city does not look grey. It looks stippled: terracotta, white, and a thousand separate pockets of deep, defended green, each one marking where people live.

The plants and the houses do each other favors. Vines cool walls by ten degrees. A courtyard tree drops the temperature of the rooms around it and raises a faint humidity that geckos gather in at dusk. Fallen leaves go to the goats, goat dung goes to the beds, ash from the cooking fires goes around the stems against ants. Nothing is wasted because water makes everything that carries water precious.

The second rule is quiet.

For a place this crowded, this active, it makes astonishingly little noise. The streets are sand and swept earth, which swallow footsteps, hoofbeats, the creak of wooden cart axles. Mud walls swallow voices. There is no engine anywhere — no pump, no mill, no motor — only hand tools, stone, wood, rope, woven fiber. People speak at the volume a courtyard requires, and that habit carries outdoors. Midday, when the heat stands straight up and the light goes white and normal and merciless, the whole basin observes a practical silence: shutters closed, animals in shade, markets shuttered with cloth, millions of people resting in dim rooms while the streets empty to a few donkeys standing motionless with their ears turning. You can hear, then, the small sounds that are left. A wooden well-bell, muffled by design so as not to carry. Sparrows in the eaves. The dry rustle of a lizard through fallen sisal fiber. A woman grinding grain two courtyards away, stone on stone, rhythmic and soft.

But quiet is not stillness. The basin is in constant motion at a biological pace.

At dawn it wakes all at once and soundlessly, like an ant colony in first light: smoke threads up from a thousand cooking yards — brief fires, carefully fed, out by full sun — and porters and water-carriers and herds begin to move through alleys too narrow for any of them, somehow never colliding. Weaver birds commute in ribbons from the baobabs to the grain roofs. Swifts work the air above the well-yards where insects rise. Bees own the fig trees until nine o’clock and yield them, by an arrangement nobody negotiates, to the bulbuls. Termites are forever eating the oldest beams from inside and are forever being smoked out, the beams replaced, the city digesting and rebuilding its own skeleton year by year. In the outer quarters, where the houses thin just enough for thorn enclosures, goats are milked, sheep are driven out to the salty scrub, camels arrive from across the plain loaded with clay pots, salt, dried dates, and leave loaded with grain and cloth. Markets do not stay in one place; they rotate by weekday among the old village squares, so that goods walk themselves through the city and no single road wears down to mud.

The fauna is not separate from the population. It *is* part of the population. House geckos patrol every interior wall, pale and translucent, taking moths around the lamp niches. Cats hold hereditary rights to particular granaries. Dogs sleep in the exact center of alleys and are stepped over without comment. Donkeys know the well schedule better than their drivers. Pigeons erupt from rooftops in grey gusts and settle again. At dusk, bats come out of the baobab hollows and the abandoned towers of long-dead well-keepers, and the swifts give them the sky without a sound. Even the wild things stay close, because the city — with its shade, its stored grain, its cupfuls of spilled water — is the most generous habitat on the plain. Jackals trot the perimeter at night. Hares live in the prickly pear. A bustard may stride, stately as a tax collector, straight down a noon-empty street.

There are customs you learn only by staying.

You never pour water on open ground. You never take the last of a well’s daily measure, because the measure is calculated to let the deep damp recharge overnight through clay that is still, geologically, deciding what it wants to be. Shade belongs to whoever arrives under it, but a tree belongs to the grandchildren of whoever planted it, and cutting a living branch in the dry season is treated the way theft is treated elsewhere. Roofs are semi-public at night — in the worst heat, families sleep up there under the stars, and a stranger crossing a roof is expected; a stranger entering a courtyard is not. When a house is demolished, its mud returns to the street and its garden is inherited intact by the neighbors, to be kept alive until someone builds again, because a dead garden takes five years of saved water to restart and a living one takes almost none.

By evening the light lowers and goes amber across the flatness, and the basin makes its own small weather again: a warm wind rises off the cooling clay, moves through millions of leaves at once, and carries the smells of the day — dust, resin, woodsmoke, goat milk, crushed moringa, hot limewash cooling — from one end of the city to the other, so that for an hour the whole vast place smells like a single household settling down.

Night falls. Lamps are few and small. The stars over a land with no water and no elevation are enormous, and the Milky Way stands above a city that, from any distance, makes no glow at all — only a darkness denser than the darkness of the empty plain around it, pricked with breathing, rustling, the soft percussion of hooves on sand, and life packed into life, going on quietly in the warm.
