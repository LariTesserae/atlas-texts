It is reached only from above, and only for a short part of the year.

For days the way up is ordinary mountain: scree, snow patches that never quite melt, wind that makes your eyes water, air getting thinner until every fifty steps you have to stop and pretend to admire the view while your lungs catch up. Then the ridge narrows to a spine of grey granite, and set into the spine, so unexpectedly you almost walk past it, is a cleft barely wide enough for a person without a pack.

You turn sideways. The rock on either side is wet through, even when it hasn't rained. It weeps. Black streaks, green moss in the cracks, your shoulders brushing cold water. The sound starts before you can see anything — not faint and growing, but suddenly present, as if you had stepped through a door into a room where something was already shouting.

And then the hollow opens.

It's small. That is the first shock, after the height. You could walk across it in two minutes. Eighty, perhaps a hundred paces of floor, ringed almost completely by walls that go straight up another hundred feet, open only at the cleft you came through and at a notch opposite, lower than the rest, where the whole world falls away. Below that notch there is nothing for thousands of feet but cloud-sea and distant valleys reduced to creases. Above the walls there is nothing but sky. The place is a cup scooped out of the summit itself — an old glacial bite, long settled into its shape, the granite smoothed and rounded, striped orange and white with lichen that has had centuries to do its slow work undisturbed.

The second shock is how empty it is.

Not barren. Empty. The floor is lush — astonishingly lush for this altitude — but everything living hugs the ground. All the volume of the place, that great cylinder of air between the walls, is void. No trees; no tree could survive the winter wind that pours over the rim, and none has tried. No tall thing at all. You stand in a cathedral with no pillars, no roof, nothing between the carpet and the sky, and the emptiness has a physical quality. It makes you feel exposed and cradled at the same time.

The carpet itself is wet everywhere. Water is near the surface here, in a way it never is on the ridge outside. The walls condense it straight out of passing cloud. Every crack drips. A dozen small springs thread down the rock in bright, shifting lines, gather in runnels through the moss, and feed a single pool near the center, black and perfectly still at its edges, trembling where the largest fall lands. The fall is thin — a hand's breadth of meltwater dropping from a lip high on the shadowed wall — but the hollow is shaped like an ear, and it hears everything.

That is why it is loud.

The thin waterfall strikes a hollow slab above the pool and drums. Not metaphorically. There is a resonant cavity under the stone, worn out over ages, and each pulse of water produces a low, booming note you feel in your sternum, irregular, slowing in the cold morning when the melt is a trickle, quickening to a frantic tattoo by early afternoon when the sun has been on the snowfield above. The drips from the walls are amplified until they sound like stones dropped into a well. A loose pebble, kicked carelessly, reports like a shot and comes back off the far wall, and then again, softened. Shepherds who used this place — and someone did, once — could stand at the cleft and hear a whisper spoken at the pool. They could hear a sheep shift in its sleep across the hollow. There is no privacy in the Ear. There never was.

And over the water-noise, the animals.

They are the reason the emptiness keeps breaking. The walls are pocked with nesting holes, hundreds of them, dark punctuation in the granite, and they belong to swifts and snowfinches and a raucous colony of choughs with lacquer-red beaks. At dawn the whole colony leaves at once, and the sound of that — thousands of wings in a stone cylinder, every cry doubled by echo — is briefly almost too much to stand inside. They circle in the updraft that forms when sun hits the floor, rising without effort, black calligraphy against ordinary, bright daylight, no magic to the light at all up here, just thin-air clarity that makes every feather-edge sharp. Then they are gone over the rim to feed, and the hollow is void again, except for the drumming.

By midday the pool manufactures weather of its own. The water is cold — everything here is cool, even in high summer your breath smokes at dawn and your hands go numb washing them at noon — but the sun-warmed moss around it steams faintly, and a hatch of midges and pale mayflies rises off the surface in a column. That is when the swifts return. They come screaming through the cleft one after another at impossible speed, taking insects on the wing across the pool, wingtips skimming water, each pass setting off a fresh burst of echoing shrieks. Marmots in the boulder-field at the wall's base stand up and whistle alarms — a piercing, carrying note, answered from three directions. Under the sedges there is a rustle of voles. Twice a day, if you're still, a raven comes to patrol the rim, and once in a while, not often, something larger folds its wings at the notch and drops through the hollow in absolute silence, and every whistle and shriek cuts off at once, and the drumming of the waterfall goes on alone until the eagle is gone.

Nothing here is still for long. The place runs on a tight daily cycle, compressed by altitude. Frost films the pool at night even in July. By nine it has melted. By noon the falls are loud, the runnels have risen, threads of water are finding new paths through the moss, and a brief rain — often the inside of a cloud simply snagging on the summit and condensing as it passes — darkens everything for ten minutes and is gone. By evening the insects have been eaten or have drowned, the birds are settling into the wall with a noise like a crowd finding its seats, the meltwater slows, the drum under the slab drops to single, separate booms, spaced like a heart slowing toward sleep.

The plants live fast to match. For perhaps eight weeks the floor is improbable: moss campion in tight pink cushions, buttercups, white saxifrage in the wettest cracks, sedges gone almost luminous green, tiny ferns uncurling where spray from the fall keeps the rock permanently damp. It is a garden kept low by cold and wind, dense as a pelt. Put your palm down and the ground gives like a sponge and comes up wet and black with peat. Under the green, the soil is thin over granite. Everything roots shallow and holds on.

In one corner, where the wall curves to give a little shelter from the prevailing wind, people have left their marks. Not many. A rough circle of stones — a sheepfold, or the footing of a bothy — half-collapsed, its interior grown over with the same green as everything else. Three flat stones that might have been a hearth. An iron ring, hand-forged and rusted almost through, hammered into the rock at shoulder height, for tethering an animal or a rope. On the rim above the cleft, a cairn, and stuck into it a weathered wooden post carved with notches that could be a tally of seasons or of sheep or of visits. Nothing suggests anyone ever lived here through a winter. You couldn't. The hollow that shelters you from the wind in summer becomes, in winter, a trap that fills with spindrift until the pool and the fold and the floor are erased under a smooth white fill, and the drumming stops.

The rules of the place are the kind you learn with your body rather than from anyone. Don't camp in the center — the center belongs to the pool and the falling water and, at night, to a cold that pools there too, sinking off the walls. Don't shout for the pleasure of the echo more than once; the birds mind, and after the third shout the choughs will mob you, and their noise, in here, can make your ears ring for an hour. Drink from the runnels, not the pool — the pool is where everything else drinks, and at dusk the marmots and the finches and things you don't see come down to it in procession. And when you leave, leave through the cleft quietly. It isn't superstition, exactly. It's just that sound stays here a long time after its maker has gone, bouncing and thinning between the walls, and the last thing the hollow keeps of anything is its noise.

When you squeeze back out onto the ridge, the ordinary mountain wind hits you and the silence is startling. Behind you, sealed in its small stone cup at the top of the world, the waterfall goes on drumming to an empty room full of birdsong — getting louder, getting quieter, all day, all the brief summer, whether anyone is there to be deafened by it or not.
