It is high enough that there should be no heat left in the world, and there is nothing but heat.

You come up to it from below the clouds, and the clouds do not thin. They thicken, they warm. The last slope is black basalt worn round as river stone, though no river could have run uphill this far, and it is wet the whole way — not with rain that has fallen, but with water arriving continuously out of the rock itself. Every pore weeps. Every crack is beaded. Your hands, finding holds, come away slick and hot. The stone sweats faster than the sun can dry it.

And the sun is ordinary. That is the first wrong thing you can name clearly. Normal, midday light, hard and white and familiar, falling straight down through air that should be thin and freezing and instead presses on the skin like the air over a cooking pot. There is no dusk coming to relieve it. There has never been dusk here. The light simply is, at the angle it is, and the shadows lie where they have always lain, pooled black under eaves and awnings of leaf, unmoving.

Above the last rise: a plateau that is also a city that is also a forest, extending far enough that its far edge is lost not in distance but in steam.

It is regional in scale. Terraces step down — or up, your sense of that will not settle — for miles, valley within valley, each level walled, bridged, roofed, planted. From any high stair you can see other districts curving around bowls and ridges, dense with construction, and beyond them more, until the whole inhabited mountain resolves into a rim, and beyond the rim, impossibly, more rim, higher, enclosing sky below it. You can see down into cloud from the outer streets. You can also, from those same streets, look up at cloud. Both are true at once, and neither cancels the other.

Nothing here was built with tools. You look for tool marks and find none. The city is urban in every way except manufacture: there are aqueducts, cisterns, plazas, colonnades, tenement blocks stacked five and six stories against the cliff faces, alley systems, granaries, bathhouses, bridges over canals, market squares with permanent stone stalls. Only, the stone was not cut. It grew into these shapes, or was persuaded to. The walls are tufa and pale limestone fused with living root, the beams are trunks that bent in youth and then lignified mid-curve, still putting out leaves at the joints. Doorways are arches where two trees leaned together a century ago and fused. Windows are gaps deliberately left in a wall of interwoven cane, now hardened, never widening, never closing.

Mortar is moss. Not moss applied — moss that is structural, dense as felt, deep emerald and black-green, holding blocks that do not quite touch. Roof tiles are overlapping plates of bark, shed and reshed, layered so thick the roofs look thatched with scales. In places the buildings have no clear end and the vegetation no clear beginning: a house whose upper floor is entirely canopy, a street tree whose trunk contains three apartments hollowed by rot that stopped rotting, a temple — if it is a temple — whose pillars flower.

The lushness is not decoration. It is competition. Vines fill every vertical surface not kept clear by running water. Broad leaves, bigger than doors, stack in storeys over the streets, so that many alleys are green tunnels at noon, dripping steadily. Ferns erupt from gutters. Orchids — huge, fleshy, scentless — open in the joins of aqueducts. There are flowers everywhere and nothing to pollinate them. They open anyway, permanently, to the same size, the same color. They do not wilt. There are fruits, swollen and wet-skinned, hanging over plazas, and nothing eats them. They do not fall.

There is no animal life here. No birds in that enormous canopy. No insects in the flowers or on the water. No fish in canals so clear you can count the pebbles mortared into their beds. No lizards on the hot walls. No human either, though the city is sized for tens of thousands: worn steps, polished hand-smooth rails of root-wood, benches around fountains, sleeping platforms in the shaded courts. The polish is old and it is not increasing. Nothing is wearing anything down any further. The place has reached the exact shape of its own use and stopped.

And it is deafening.

The sound is not loud the way a storm is loud. It does not rise or fall. It is total, and it has no source you can approach. At the rim, you assume it is the waterfalls — and there are waterfalls, hundreds of them, sheeting off the plateau edge in white columns that vanish into cloud below without ever thinning. Near them, yes, there is roaring. But walk an hour inland, down (or up) through the terraced districts to the central basins where the canals run still and glassy, and the sound is precisely as loud. Inside a house, door shut — doors are woven screens that never fully shut — it is as loud. Underwater, in one of the bathhouse pools, head fully submerged in green-hot water, it is as loud, transmitted through the skull without loss.

It lives in the teeth. In the sternum. It is a single continuous tone made of water and stone and air all vibrating at the threshold of pain, a roar with a hum inside it and a shriek inside that, perfectly steady. You will shout to hear yourself and produce only the visible shape of shouting. After a while your ears stop trying to parse anything beneath it, and the silence underneath the roar becomes its own kind of texture: a world with all small sounds surgically removed. No dripping, though everything drips. No leaf-rustle, though the canopy is dense. No splash, though fountains throw arcs of water into basins all across the city. Those things happen in front of your eyes and contribute nothing. Only the one sound exists, and it does not change, and because it does not change, you cannot tell whether it is coming from outside you or whether the place has replaced something inside your head.

The water makes this possible and impossible at once. It is everywhere at exactly the same abundance. Canals run full to the brim but never over. Cisterns on the roofs — open stone bowls fed by bamboo-like hollow stems — are always at the same level, though water pours in visibly and drains out through carved spouts in four directions. The fountains do not recycle; you can trace their feed uphill to springs that emerge from plaza floors at a pressure that should, by rights, excavate the plaza. It doesn't. The spring rises, arcs, falls, vanishes into grates, and the paving stones around it are merely wet, eternally wet, with algae tracing the same arabesque patterns in the seams.

Rain also occurs, but only in certain streets, and upward. In the long district locals would have called — if there were locals — the Inverted Quarter, fine mist lifts off the hot stone in sheets and coalesces, mid-air, into droplets that accelerate toward the sky and strike the undersides of the leaf-canopies with an audible patter you cannot hear. Puddles there shrink by raining. Elsewhere, condensation forms on the undersides of bridges and falls as normal rain into canals that are already full, and the excess goes nowhere you can find.

Streets do not obey their own lengths. A stairway of forty steps, counted carefully, worn in the middle by ancient feet, connects a lower market to an upper residential terrace three hundred feet above it. The climb takes forty steps' worth of effort. The view from the top confirms the height. Descending takes forty steps as well, and brings you, sometimes, to a market that is the same market but entered from the opposite side, at an hour that is the same hour, with the fountain throwing the same arc — but the fruit in the stall, untouched, uneaten, unsold, is arranged in mirror image. Walk the other bridge and you may correct this. Or compound it.

The established feeling of the place is crucial. This is not a ruin in collapse, nor a wonder in the act of breaking. Everything is weathered to equilibrium. Edges are rounded. Stairs are bowed. The basalt rim is scalloped by water into smooth amphitheaters. The canals have cut themselves half an inch into their stone beds and no deeper. Even the reality failures are worn smooth, habitual, infrastructural — the city was clearly built accounting for them. There are streets that loop through the same plaza at two different elevations, and they have separate drainage, separate planting, separate neighborhoods built along each pass. There is a district that exists only as the interior of another district's cistern system, dry, windowed, with balconies overlooking water that flows through what should be their sky. People lived with this. They built handrails at the places where gravity locally reverses. Gardeners trained the vines to grow along directions that only exist on Tuesdays — no. There are no days. They trained them along directions that do not exist from outside the street.

Ecology, without animals, without decay, without change, is a closed green pressure. Plants grow to a size and are that size. Leaves replace themselves, you must assume, because fallen leaves exist on the streets in a fixed scatter — ankle-deep in the shaded courts, each leaf intact, wet, hot to the touch — but you never see one fall, and the depth never varies, and your footsteps leave prints that are already there. Seedlings hold at seedling height in the cracks, perfectly formed, photosynthesizing forever at the same rate the canopy shades them. The whole system breathes, visibly — steam rises from the forest-city in slow columns that do not disperse — but it does not progress. It is a lung held at full inhale.

Heat settles into materials differentially and stays. Basalt holds it brutally; stand barefoot on sunlit rim-stone and you will blister. The limestone of the inner city, kept wet by its own sweating, is merely fever-warm, bearable, faintly steaming where spray lands. Wood is body temperature. Leaf shade is not cool, only less hot, and saturated — air at one hundred percent humidity and far past comfort, so that breathing feels like drinking. Your sweat does not evaporate. It joins the general wetness. Everything you wear becomes the temperature of the place and stays wet and that is simply the condition now.

Denisty increases toward the center, where buildings and trees crowd so closely that the streets become vertical: ladders of root, switchback stairs between houses sharing walls, bridges of twelve feet spanning gaps of forty. Presence is layered there — roof gardens over markets over cisterns over houses over canals, five wet green storeys — and yet no crowding sound, no activity, only the roar, only steam moving just slowly enough to prove it is not moving at all. Each stratum drips onto the one below at a rate that the one below is already exactly accommodating.

At the center is the basin. Or a basin. A circular plaza, three hundred yards across, paved in white stone so hot it shimmers, ringed by the tallest structures: towers of fused trunk and tufa, nine, ten storeys, their tops in leaf. At its middle, a spring larger than the others rises straight up, a column of water a yard thick, climbing twenty feet and — not falling. It climbs, slows, and at its apex simply ceases to be distinguishable from the steam above it. The basin around it is dry. The paving at its foot is dry, bone dry, the only dry surface in the city, in a perfect circle, in that heat and wet. The roar is no louder there. That is how you know it is the source.

You can live here, probably. There is water you can drink — it tastes of stone and leaf, warm. There is fruit. There is shelter, unoccupied, maintained at the point of maintenance. There is no night to sleep through, no winter, no hunger cycle, no predator, no disease organism, nothing that bites or stings or sings. You would only have to learn to think at the volume of the sound, to navigate streets that politely insist on more space than the mountain contains, to accept that climbing down from the rim long enough brings you to the rim again, higher, looking down at clouds that are also above you, in normal light, in permanent steam, in a city grown rather than made, waiting at the exact pitch of itself, forever, without a single living animal to notice that it never, ever changes.
