You have to come up from underneath it.

For most of the ascent there is nothing to see except cloud. Not fog — a permanent, flat sea of it, white and unbroken, that lies around the foot of the plateau like water around an island. The lift climbs through it for an hour on cables gone green-black with age. Then the cloud thins, the light hardens, and the wall appears.

It is not a mountain slope. It is a cliff, eight, nine kilometers high, red basalt and black slag-rock banded like wood grain, warm to the touch even where it is in shadow. Old erosion has taken it seriously. Wind coming up from the cloud-sea for a million years has carved it into flutes and blades and hollow alcoves big enough to hold towns, though no towns hold there. Nothing clings. Nothing grows. Only the lifts, three parallel lines of cable and counterweighted cage, and the nesting ledges of the cliff swifts, thousands of them, tucked into cracks with their eggs laid directly on the hot stone.

You break out on top gasping, even if you were born to it. Everyone on the High Table is barrel-chested, slow-moving, pink with sun. The air up here will not do half the work air should do.

And it is warm. That is the first wrongness.

It should be frozen at this height. Instead the plateau holds heat the way an anvil holds it after the hammering. The rock is dark, the sun is unfiltered and ordinary and relentless in a blue that goes almost black at noon if you look straight up, and the whole continent-sized table — and it is a continent, 2,000 kilometers across in its long axis, large enough to make its own weather in the interior — absorbs the day and gives it back slowly through the ground. At midday the salt pans shimmer at forty degrees. At night the stone ticks and pings as it lets go. Water, if you spilled it, would not last. It would boil tepid in a sealed pan at this pressure, and in the open it simply thins and vanishes, hissing faintly. There are no rivers here. There are no lakes, no wells sunk to any water table. Every liquid lives under pressure, in riveted tanks, in waxed skins, in the closed loops of the machines.

That is why they built the factories up here, where no sane person would haul ore.

Down below, to make a vacuum you need a vessel. Up here the sky does it for free. The smelters of the High Table are open-air bowls of refractory brick, huge and shallow, where copper and tin and stranger ores brought up the lifts sweat and separate in the thinness without ever quite burning. Fire itself is reluctant here. It burns low, blue, and patient. So they learned to work with sun and pressure instead: fields of tarnished mirrors, acres wide, that crawl a fraction of a degree an hour to follow the light, focusing it onto crucibles until the metal inside goes white and silent. Solar furnaces. Vacuum stills like cathedral bells with nothing inside them but heat and vapor condensing pure on cold plates. A hammer press in Yesset takes three days to complete one stroke, its flywheel turning so slowly you can count the rivets passing, and when it finally falls you do not hear it so much as feel it through the soles of your boots, because the air is too thin to carry a proper sound.

Everything up here is mostly quiet. Your own breath is the loudest thing you own. Voices die within ten paces. Factories a street away seem mute behind glass, their pistons rising, their stacks exhaling, their signal-flags snapping in a semaphore language that has replaced shouting. Bells exist, but they are struck for the flash of polished metal, not the tone. People stand close to talk. They touch sleeves. They move slowly, deliberately, conserving the work of their lungs, and the whole pace of industry has taken that slowness into itself. A shift is measured by furnace cycles. A delivery is measured in weeks.

Between the towns there is almost nothing, and that nothing goes on for a very long way.

The interior is a mineral desert with no plant life at all — no scrub, no grass, no green stain in the hollows. Yardangs of red stone streamlined by wind. Pans of white salt and grey ash cracked into polygons big enough to park a train car on. Old roads, older than any living town, sunk a hand's breadth into the rock by the passage of iron wheels. Abandoned works out in the flats where a mirror field has been left to go blind, each heliostat still dutifully turned a little toward where the sun was on the day the water contract ran out. The erosion here is not violent. It is settled, finished. This place has been exactly itself for a long time, and wind has had time to round every insult soft.

The towns gather, when they gather at all, around lift-heads and smelter stacks. Low, dense knots of construction — houses half-buried, roofed with beaten copper and basalt tile, walls a meter thick against the daily swelling and shrinking of heat. Everything is repaired from everything else. A church in Orum uses a decommissioned condenser bell for a dome. Children play inside a hollowed slag bloom. Wires run everywhere at ankle height for the speaking tubes and the flag-lines. Because no wood grows here, they build with stone, sintered dust, cast metal too flawed for export. Because nothing rots quickly in the dry thin air, nothing is ever really thrown away; the oldest districts are layered, platform on platform, filled courtyards, furnaces become houses become cisterns for oil and coolant.

And despite the barrenness, despite no pastures and no fields, the place is crowded with animals.

The food chain does not start with plants. It starts with crusts — bacterial mats, iron-red and sulphur-yellow and a slick black that smells of hot pennies, growing in the warm fissures where geothermal breath comes up and in the runoff channels from the factories, eating rock, heat, and metal filings. On those crusts graze the herd things: long-legged, long-necked salt-lickers with lungs that billow visibly like bellows under their ribs, scraping stone with keratin tongues for a week to fill a belly. Slow herds of them cross the pans in single file, following mineral veins the way lowland cattle follow grass, their migrations taking a season to go from one town's slag heaps to the next. Predators follow at a distance, lean six-limbed runners that can hold their breath in a kind of internal pressure while they sprint, then must stand panting with their whole bodies for an hour.

The air, thin as it is, is full. Cliff swifts commute vertically, dropping two kilometers to hunt insects in the cloud tops and rising on the ferocious thermal that peels up the sunlit cliff face. Gliders with ribbed membranes launch from smelter stacks and ride factory heat plumes inland, nesting in chimneys, attuned so precisely to the industrial cycle that they lay when a furnace is lit and fledge before its three-month burn is done. Soot-moths in their millions paste themselves to the warm sides of tanks at night. Slag-beetles, blind and armored, live inside cooling waste piles for decades, eating their way through a single bloom over a lifetime, excreting a refined ore the townspeople harvest after them like patient farmers.

Scavengers own the towns outright. Dog-sized, feathered things trotting unafraid between the mirror fields, waiting for seal-failures, for a dropped ration tin, for warmth. They understand flags. They understand which workshops vent sweetness at dusk. The people feed them deliberately, not from kindness alone but because they clean what would otherwise sit forever. Nothing up here decays the way it should. A dead licker out on the pan will desiccate into leather and bone and still be there, recognizable, ten years later, slowly being worried apart by wind and beetle. The towns burn their dead in solar furnaces at noon, quickly, as a matter of hygiene and respect.

Travel is by rail, mostly, single iron lines arrow-straight across the flats between towns a hundred kilometers apart, the trains pressurized, wood-paneled inside with imported lowland timber more precious than the metal they haul, moving at a walking pace when the crosswind rises and dust begins to levitate. For the dust does that here. In the interior afternoons, when heat differentials build a weather system that belongs entirely to the plateau — a slow, revolving, nearly invisible storm with no rain in it, ever — electrostatic charge lifts fine red dust a meter off the ground and holds it there, hovering, so the trains appear to wade. Stones hum faintly if you press an ear to them. Loose chains sway toward nothing. Compasses in the central desert describe lazy circles and must be forgiven.

At the farthest interior, where the table rises another half-kilometer in a broad, imperceptible dome, the air is thinner still and the towns stop. There is an older city there, or the remains of a works so large it had districts, its furnaces cold for centuries, its mirrors cracked into a mosaic that still flashes at sunset, one panel after another as you approach, like something signaling in a code too slow to read. The herd animals avoid it. The gliders circle it but do not land. Warmth still comes up through its floors in winter. Whether it was abandoned because the ore gave out, or the lifts to that sector were cut, or people simply decided that this high, in this quiet, their thoughts went strange in a way they did not care for — barrel-chested children seeing stars at noon and hearing their blood too clearly — no record agrees.

You can stand there, in normal daylight, and see the curvature of the world's cloud-sea falling away on every side, and hear almost nothing at all except the ticking of cooling stone and your own heart doing the work of two, and understand the rule the High Table teaches everything that lives on it:

Move slowly. Seal what is precious. Waste no breath on distance.

What lasts here lasts a very, very long time.
