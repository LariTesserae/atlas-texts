There is no ground here.

That is the first thing you have to learn, and the first thing your feet forget. You can walk for a day across the High Yard — across deck, roof, causeway, terrace, and gantry — and never step on anything that was not put there. No soil. No rock. No original slope of the mountain, if there ever was a mountain. Far below the gratings at the edge, the struts just go down and down into bright haze until looking becomes a kind of inference. What holds this place up, and what it holds itself up *from*, is no longer visible from inside it.

It is very high, and very bright, and cold in a way that does not agree with the brightness.

The sun here is not warm so much as exact. At noon the light comes down white and vertical and makes every surface declare itself: white ceramic tile, pale enameled steel, brushed aluminum gone almost silver, glass in long greenhouse roofs, copper pipe sweating green at the joints. Shadows are black and cut clean as paper. Step into one and the temperature drops twenty degrees on your skin. Step out and your shoulders sting. The air itself stays cool, thin, smelling of hot metal and ozone and wet stone that is not stone.

Because everything is made, everything has an edge. This is a young place, unworn. The corners have not been rounded by weather or hands. Stair treads are still serrated. New bays of the factory districts rise at the perimeter with their rebar exposed like bright whiskers, their panels still bearing the ghost of protective film. Crane rails shine where the wheels have only passed a few hundred times. Even the oldest districts, near the center cisterns, look freshly assembled — people here replace rather than repair, unbolt and swap, and so the whole plateau has the unsettling look of something finished yesterday and also ten thousand people strong.

It extends. You can see that from any of the lens towers. Not to a horizon of land, but to a horizon of more Yard: district after district in a shallow, engineered bowl perhaps twelve miles across.

To the west, the Lens Fields, acre upon acre of tilting mirrors and solar furnaces, tracking so slowly you have to mark them against a chimney to believe they move. To the north, the Roost Rows, where housing and workshop and pigeon loft and battery stack are not separate buildings so much as one continuous terraced wall, five and eight and twelve stories high, perforated at every level. To the east, the Radiator Ridges — yes, ridges, they have geography — long fins of blackened metal rising thirty feet from the smelter roofs to dump heat into the thin air, humming faintly with expansion. South, the Cistern Steps descend in broad, shallow amphitheaters, each step a roof, a tank, a walkway, a garden gutter.

And everywhere, between, above, below: more.

Density is the local form of nature. There is no empty lot. A roof is a goat run is a condensation farm is a landing for swifts. A wall carries pipes, then nests, then a narrow market stall in its shadow, then a maintenance ladder, then a shrine made of retired pressure gauges, then sleeping pods for the night shift tucked behind a heat shield like swallows' mud cups. Walkways cross over alleys that cross over light-wells that drop to factory floors three levels down, where another walkway crosses. You are rarely alone and rarely in a crowd; you are simply always *among* things, layered presences pressing close without quite touching. Laundry, antennae, beehives, signaling flags, water clocks, drying racks for printed circuit sheets, cages for finches that are not cages but open frameworks the finches refuse to leave.

For all the industry, it is quiet.

That is deliberate, then habitual, then physical. The great presses in the Low Pits — and low here means only fifty feet below the main deck — are set in vacuum-jacketed housings. A press the size of a house completes one stroke per hour, forging lens blanks and turbine hubs with a force you feel in your molars but hear only as a soft, displaced *whump* of air, as if somewhere a giant book had closed. The monorails run on magnetic skids. The smelters are electric; there is no smoke, only shimmer. Turbines are buried in baffled shafts.

What you hear, mostly, is small: the tick-tick of cooling metal at dusk. The drip of water. Wings.

Water does not live here; it visits.

There are no rivers and no wells. 0.9 of a river, perhaps, spread through the whole place. At night the clear sky pulls heat out of every exposed plate, and by dawn the entire Yard is beaded. Condensation runs in runnels designed into the architecture — look closely and every cornice is a watershed, every roof ridge a continental divide. For an hour after sunrise the place drips in chorus, gutters chime drop by drop into cisterns, copper downspouts turn dark, the Radiator Ridges weep. Children and old people are out with soft brushes, shepherding dew. Then the sun takes it. By nine the decks are dry, the air is dry, your lips crack, and the only remaining water is locked in tanks, in pipes, in the damp north sides of vents where moss is permitted.

Vegetation takes what it can from that hour. It is sparse and tenacious and almost entirely opportunistic: silver lichen in the seams of tiles, in perfect circles around cold-water joints; tufts of engineered alpine grass in the overflow grates, kept cropped by the roof goats; a single line of dwarf junipers in a planter tram that makes a circuit of the Lens Fields, each tree shaped by wind and pruning into the same startled gesture. In the greenhouse roofs there are more ambitious things — tomatoes, beans, white flowers for the bees — but they live behind glass, in imported air. Outside, the green is a kind of punctuation.

The animals, however, have not been discouraged.

If anything, the Yard is theirs as much as anyone's. Swifts nest by the thousands under the eaves of the Roost Rows and pour out at dawn in one muted river, their screaming thinned by the altitude to something like tearing silk. Pigeons in oil-slick colors own the Cistern Steps. Sparrows live inside the signage. Large, pale moths — bigger than your hand, dusted like frost — sleep all day on the warm sides of transformers and are so numerous in some service corridors you have to walk through them gently, parting them. Bees commute in marked lanes between hive-walls and greenhouse ports. Cats, lean and long-legged for the ladders, patrol territories defined entirely by sun patches. And the goats, small white roof goats with black feet, pick their way along ridges and radiator fins with an air of municipal authority, eating lichen, trampling nothing, staring at visitors until the visitors move.

Where there are animals, there are insects, droppings, shed feathers, warmth in enclosed places. The sparse plants feed on that. The cisterns catch it. Nothing here is natural and the ecology is complete.

The work of the Yard is making — it has only ever been making. In the long factory sheds, under roofs that are also reservoirs, they make airframe spars and battery plates and optical glass and replacement parts for the Yard itself. Lines of workers in bright veils tend machines that tend other machines. A sheet of alloy takes a week to pass down one hall, heated, cooled, pressed, annealed, inspected by light passed through it at angles. Everything moves at the pace of the mirrors outside: patient, cyclical, nearly still. A crane crossing the main bay is an event you can plan lunch around.

This slowness is necessary, people will tell you, because of the light.

Here is where the place stops agreeing with itself.

They built high to get above weather, above dust, into clean sun for the furnaces and lenses. They succeeded. Then they built higher. At some point — no one has a date, the records become oddly duplicated after a certain year — the uppermost surfaces began to receive light that had not quite happened yet. Not much. A second, perhaps. Less.

You notice it first in shadows. At noon in the Lens Fields, your shadow falls slightly *before* you step. In the smelter sheds, a worker will reach for a tool her reflection in the polished housing has already lifted. The swifts, pour­ing from the eaves, sometimes vanish in a bright patch of empty sky between two struts and re-enter, audibly surprised, a few feet to the left, a few insects fewer in their beaks.

Mirrors must be recalibrate­d by animal observation: the bees, who navigate by a sun that for them is in the right place. Instruments are trusted only down-slope. The higher workshops keep two clocks, one mechanical and one a bowl of water evaporating, and average them.

No one makes a fuss. That may be the strangest part. A foreman will pause, hand hovering where the wrench will be in half a breath, and take it on the second try without comment. Cat sun patches are laid out with an extra square of carpet where the patch will drift against all solar reason on Thursday. The roof goats, several people swear, are older than goats should be, and may be the same goats.

Rooms, too, acquire a slight surplus. Maintenance crews mapping the under-deck find service bays that sum to more volume than the structure above them allows, clean, lit, stocked with parts for machines not yet installed. They seal them and mark the hatches with the date, and do not open them again until the parts are needed. A corridor in the Roost Rows returns you to its entrance if you walk it while looking at the floor; look at the ceiling pipes instead and it delivers you, correctly, to the hive-walls. Everyone knows this. There are no signs. Children are taught by being walked through it twice.

At night — and night here is sudden, violet, the whole white city going blue in minutes — the stored light begins to leak. Not electrically. The ceramic tiles, the lens blanks in their racks, the cistern water, give back a faint, sourceless brightness for an hour, enough to see by, so the Yard has two dusks: one solar, one material. During the second dusk the moths come out and gather in the places where tomorrow's sun patches will fall, waiting in cool shadow for warmth that — from their perspective, perhaps — has already arrived.

Standing then on a high walkway, between a hive-wall humming almost below hearing and a cistern breathing cold air up through its grate, you can see the whole impossible town of it: bright even in dimness, dense as a reef, quiet as a library, smelling of wet copper and hot dust and animal fur, dripping once, somewhere, a single leftover drop from the morning. No earth anywhere. Nothing that was not built, and yet birds, goats, moss, people, time itself all nesting in the joints.

Below you the presses complete their hour. *Whump.* Above you the first mirror, very slowly, begins to turn away from a sun that has already set, toward the one that has not yet risen.
