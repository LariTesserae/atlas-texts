You hear it before you see it.

Above, there is nothing to hear. A salt-white plain, cracked into plates, dry mineral dust grinding between your teeth, the sun ordinary and pitiless. No wind worth mentioning. No water anywhere — not a dark stain, not a reed, not even the promise of a well. Your shadow is short and sharp on the ground.

And then, from ahead, a roar.

Not water. Louder than water. A continuous, layered, grinding roar that rises out of the earth itself, until the salt plates under your feet begin to tremble with it.

The pit opens without warning.

It is a valley dropped straight down out of the plain, twelve kilometers long and perhaps two wide, its floor eighty meters below the desert. Fresh cliffs. The rock is still the color of a new break, unweathered, unsmoothed, spilling fans of rubble that have not yet had time to become soil. This place is not old. It is happening.

And it is entirely, impossibly green.

Not green on the ground. Green *as* the ground. Green up the cliffs, green across the floor, green hanging in ropes and curtains and bridges over empty air. Where the stone has just fallen, root-hairs are already white-knuckling into the cracks. Where there is no stone, there is root-mat, trunk, vine, leaf, husk, thorn, fallen trunk, and root-mat again. You cannot find the original floor of the valley. It is somewhere underneath meters of living and rotting plant, still being invented.

You go down by someone's old rope ladder — plant fiber, bone rungs worn smooth, a basket of stone blades cached at the top beside a cairn — and the sound takes you apart on the way.

First the cicada-class things, millions of them, in the cliff shrubs, a metallic shrieking that never pauses for breath because when one colony falters another takes it up a third higher. Then the frogs that are not frogs, dry-throated, inflating resonating bladders in the root hollows, *thok thok thok* like mallets on hollow wood. Then birds, hundreds at once, arguing. Then things you cannot identify: a whistling that rises until it hurts, a sawing, a rattling of seed-pods in a hot gust, a deep periodic booming from somewhere in the mat below, as if a very large animal is drumming on the earth — which, later, you realize it is.

By the bottom your ears are ringing even inside the noise. People here, the few who come here, pack their ears with black wax and speak mostly by gesture and by stamping on roots, sending sentences through the ground.

The light, when you reach the mat, is normal daylight, but filtered into moving coins through so many layers of canopy it seems to be raining brightness. Morning and evening the valley is in cliff-shadow and everything glows dully from its own surfaces — pale fungus, silver leaves, the amber eyes of animals. Midday the sun drops straight in and the whole pit steams with heat, though nothing is wet.

That is the first rule to learn: nothing here is wet, and everything is alive.

There has been no rain. There are no streams under the root-mat, no pools in the hollows of trees. Leaves are not soft. They are leather, wax, felt, blue-grey armor, curled shut around their own moisture. Trunks are swollen sealed casks. Cut one — you shouldn't — and it does not bleed water. It bleeds a thick, oily gel, almost syrup, that skins over in seconds and smells of resin and hot pepper. Fruit is dense as bread and sweet as fermented honey. Everything that animals drink, they take from inside a body: gel-fruit, sap, nectar thick enough to chew, blood. Their urine dries to yellow crystals on the leaves. Their droppings dry before they fall.

Above, the desert gives this place nothing but dust. That turns out to be enough. The pit is a trap. Every hot afternoon the valley breathes out — the fermenting depths of the mat, warm as a compost heap, warm as an animal belly, send a column of heated air boiling up the cliffs — and at evening the desert air slides back in over the rim, carrying mineral dust, salt, dead insects, seeds, ash from fires a hundred kilometers away. The vines at the rim are sticky, feathery, endlessly sieving the wind. What falls in, does not leave. Beetles the size of your hand patrol the rim shrubs, gathering windfall into the root system. Ants carry it down in shining rivers. The plants eat rock, dust, and each other, and keep all of their water locked inside their skins like miser's gold.

So the valley grows not despite the dryness, but sealed against it. A terrarium with its lid cracked toward the sky.

And it grows *fast*. That is the second thing. Stand still long enough — five minutes, ten — and a vine-tip near your ankle will have visibly curled, questing, found your bootlace, and begun to climb. Bean-like runners race across a fresh rockfall at hand-speed. In a clearing made yesterday by a falling canopy tree — and a tree falls every hour or so somewhere, with a crash that adds itself to the general roar and sends flocks screaming sideways — seedlings are already knee-high by the next morning, fighting in chemical silence, leaves unfolding with faint ticks and pops as the cellulose stretches. All that popping, all that cracking growth, is part of the sound too. The forest is audible because it is growing at the edge of violence.

It is turbulent in every direction. Updrafts tear loose storms of pollen, white moths, parachute seeds, biting flies. Canopy branches wage slow wars and then suddenly snap. The root-mat underfoot bounces: it is a trampoline of interlaced roots over hollow, fermenting darkness, and sometimes a section gives way and you drop a meter into hot, black, breath-stealing mulch full of white larvae, before scrambling out. New cliffs shed stones; roots split the stones; the split stones become new shelves for orchids that have no business on a cliff. Nothing has finished forming. Soil is being manufactured in real time out of leaf-fall and corpses, on top of geology that has not finished breaking.

Onto this, the animals have been poured without restraint.

They are in layers, like the plants. Mites in the leaf felt. Ants in seams in the bark, in your seams too if you stand still. Jewel beetles, hand-sized lizards with dry rattling tails, snakes the exact pattern of shadowed vine, proceeding through the canopy with no reference to the ground. Above those, troops of small, long-fingered mammals swinging and scolding, dropping husks. Birds everywhere and of every size, from hovering specks that drink syrup from flower-throats to huge, slow, bald-headed things that wait, with terrible patience, for the mat to collapse and expose the fermenting underworld. At dusk — though the noise does not fall at dusk, it only changes key — bats come out of cliff cracks in such numbers that they briefly eclipse the strip of sky, thousands of leathery wings making a sound like tearing cloth that you feel in your sternum.

Many of them are loud on purpose. There is so much competition to be pollinated, to be mated, to warn, to claim a branch for the three days before it falls, that everything here advertises acoustically. Flowers have resonating chambers. Certain trees drum their own hollow trunks with specialized branches in the wind, calling beetles. The big booming from below is a colony of burrowing, nearly blind herbivores, each the weight of a pig, thumping the root-mat in sequence so the vibration travels for kilometers: *here, here, here, the mat is thin, the mat is rich*. Predators thump false replies. You learn quickly never to trust a sound's direction. The whole valley is ventriloquism.

And some of the animals are plants, halfway. Snap-tendrils that take birds. Walking palms that inch, year by year, toward collapses where the light will be. Flowers that imitate the smell precisely — the *exact* smell — of a dead thing three days gone, and the flies come boiling. Seed pods that swell in the heat and detonate at noon, firing barbed seeds into trunks, into hides, into your sleeve. It is not hostility. It is saturation. Every niche has ten tenants and they are all shouting over each other.

Who builds, in a place like this? Almost nobody, for long.

You find the structures scattered and temporary: a platform lashed ten feet up between three trunks, its floor woven green so it will keep growing and need re-cutting weekly. A cache under a root: flaked stone saws, a bone awl, resin torches that nobody would dare light down here among the oil-rich trunks. (Fire is the one unspoken prohibition. A spark in pollen season, in this pit of wax and oil and dry husk, would not burn the forest. It would *detonate* it, valley wall to valley wall.) Rope bridges, abandoned mid-span when one of their anchor trees died. Little shrines in cliff niches, no more than a stacked stone and a bundle of feathers and polished seed-cases, left high above the mat where the sound is, by some marginal mercy, only deafening instead of concussive.

Everything built here is built expecting abandonment. The floor moves. The trees fall. A good campsite this month is a ferment-pit next month, exhaling heat and gases that make your eyes stream. So people — and you do see signs of them, footprints in the dust of a high root, a fresh-cut mark, a woven trap still twitching — move through like animals among animals, in small bands, wax-eared, oil-smeared against biting things, carrying water only as sealed gourds of gel and strips of dried fruit. They do not settle the valley. They visit it, harvest it, drum warnings into the roots ahead of themselves, and climb out before dark if they can.

Because at night the turbulence goes inward. The day birds stop and the night insects take over at twice the density, if not twice the volume, and the mat ticks and shifts as the burrowers work, and cooling gel-trunks contract with cracks like distant gunfire. Things hunt by vibration. Things hunt by the heat of your body against the general heat. Your best defense is to be high, to be still, and to be generously covered in resin so that you smell, sufficiently, like the valley itself.

By the time you climb back up the ladder, hand over bone rung, the roar begins to sort itself, just slightly, with distance. A mile out on the salt plain it is a single sound again, like surf on a shore that has no sea. Behind you, below the rim, new rock is cracking under new roots, new soil is steaming into existence, a tree is falling and a hundred seedlings are already racing for the hole it leaves in the sky.

The plain is silent. Your ears go on ringing for hours, full of a dry green ocean that is building itself, loudly, in a hole in the desert, out of dust and locked water and noise.
