It is a town in a hole that is not quite a hole.

From the plateau above, you don't see it at first. The land up there is flat, cool, wind-scoured stone the color of old bone, and then it simply sags. No cliff, no crack. Over three kilometers, the ground lowers itself in wide, worn steps until you are thirty, forty meters below where you started, standing on a floor that is even flatter. Everything sharp left here a very long time ago. The rims are rounded to ramps. The terraces are rounded to swells. Even the boulders that fell in some earlier age have been polished down to loaves.

It should be dark down here, or at least shadowed. It is not. The basin is floored and walled in white and pale yellow evaporite — gypsum, salt crust, fine talc-like silt laid down when this was the bottom of something that dried up. At mid-morning the whole cup fills with light and throws it back. There are two suns in effect: the one above, and the ground itself, glowing. In the lowest streets there is almost no shadow at noon, only a cooler, doubled brightness that seems to come up through your shoes. People squint upward out of habit, and downward too.

And it is cool, despite the brightness. Cold air sinks and stays. The basin holds the night well into the day. Your breath shows faintly on the lowest level even while the light is hard on the back of your neck. Stone houses keep that cold in their thick walls. The floors sweat.

That sweating is most of the water there is.

It rarely rains here, and when it does it is a brief, startling wash that runs instantly off the plateau and sheets down the ramps and is gone. More reliable is condensation. At night the white stone radiates, chills, and pulls damp out of the air. By dawn every shallow groove is beaded, every carved lip-channel is trickling at a thread's pace toward a cistern mouth. The town is engineered for that thread. Roofs — flat, slightly dished, rimmed — are not really roofs, they are collectors. Streets tilt a degree you can only feel in your ankles. In the hollows where the old lake bottom was softest, the moisture lingers longest, forming pockets: a salt pool no wider than a table that lasts three weeks, a dark damp patch under an overhang of gypsum where the stone stays black until June. No rivers. No wells that stay sweet. Just these temporary, returning damp places, mapped more carefully than property lines.

There are no plants. Not a tree in the basin, not grass, not a weed in a wall crack. The salt will not allow it, and the daily working of the ground would tear its roots anyway. What grows instead is lower and paler: crusts of halophilic fungus in the cistern mouths, orange and grey and almost mineral themselves, scraped off with bone knives; translucent mats in the damp pockets that thicken overnight and dry to a crisp film by afternoon. They smell faintly of iron and old cellars.

That, improbably, is enough to make the place loud with life — only not with sound.

Fauna here is abundant and almost entirely small, fast, and dependent on that fungal film. Brine flies rise off the morning pools in such numbers that the air over them looks granular. Black beetles with salt-white backs work the mat edges in lines. Stone geckos, the length of a hand, the exact color of the gypsum, live by the hundreds in the walls, their throats pulsing. Higher, the basin has its own interior weather of birds: swifts and martins that nest in the soft upper bands of the rim and spend all day cutting circles inside the cup, never needing to leave, feeding on the fly-hatch. At dusk a different shift comes out — hairless, long-eared rodents that lick condensation directly off stone, and pale moths so thick on market nights they powder everything left uncovered. Goats are kept, brought down from above, but they are visitors. The true citizens are the geckos and the flies and the swifts. You are never alone in a room here; something is watching from a crack, something is walking across the ceiling.

The town is built around and between the damp pockets, which is why, though it is undeniably a town — thousands of people, districts, markets, temples, storehouses, a system of signal mirrors — it feels so sparse. Settlement cannot spread evenly. A cluster of forty houses will hold tight to the north side of a reliable cistern, and then there will be four hundred meters of empty mirror-flat, blinding and featureless, that no one builds on at all. Then another cluster, around a well-house. Then emptiness again. From the rim at evening, with cook-fires lit, it looks less like one city than an archipelago that happens to share a floor.

The building material is the basin itself. Cut gypsum block, sun-dried ochre clay that bakes almost white, salt brick for interior walls that are meant to be temporary. Tools are stone, bone, and a little salvaged bronze gone green and pitted; fresh metal corrodes here in a season, wood splits as salt grows inside its grain, so nothing is made to be permanent in that way. Houses have no sharp corners — partly custom, partly necessity. Corners are planed, plastered, rounded by hand every few years. Doorways are low arches. Roofs are weighted, not joined, with smooth river stones that can be lifted off and reset. Everything can be taken apart by two people with hammers and rope, and regularly is.

Because the ground does not stay still.

This is the strangest bargain of the place: it is ancient beyond argument, settled into what looks like its final, flattened form, and it is in constant, silent turmoil. Night damp gets into the salt layers. Crystals grow in the dark with patient, enormous force. A floor lifts two centimeters between dusk and dawn. A wall that was straight in autumn bulges at the ankle by spring. In the heat of the day the crust dries, shrinks, sighs down again, and fine cracks run across streets like slow lightning. It is not violent. There is no rumble. The silt that covers everything, ankle-deep in the flats and swept into drifts against every wall, deadens all noise as it deadens footsteps. Houses shift without creaking. Pots topple without clattering, landing in powder. A whole market row can slump a hand's breadth in a night and be discovered only because the awnings are suddenly slack.

Wind adds the visible part of the turbulence. Cold air pooled in the basin and sun-heated air off the bright floor cannot agree, and by midday the flats breed dust devils — not one or two, but processions. Pale, almost transparent columns wander the empty spaces between districts, picking up silt, moths, drying fungal mats, loose gut-string and market litter, carrying them a hundred meters and setting them down in a new arrangement. They make almost no sound. The silt is too fine to hiss. Cloth snaps dully, once, muffled. You see a devil coming by the birds scattering and the geckos vanishing and by a sudden localized glitter in the air, and then it is on you, smelling of salt and cold stone, tugging at everything unweighted, and then it is past.

So the town lives in a state of continuous, quiet rearrangement. It is an anthill-energy, a bazaar-energy, but enacted in near-silence. Crews go out at dawn, not to build new things so much as to un-bulge walls, re-level floors, re-dig the condensation channels the crystals have closed, carry the silt drifts out of the doorways and back onto the flats where the devils will redistribute them again. Children are employed from the age they can walk to carry the roof-stones one by one to the marked stone-yard when a house needs resetting, and carry them back. The market does not have fixed stalls; it has fixed weights and fixed ropes, and its layout changes with the wind report shouted — in hand signs, mostly — from the mirror tower. People speak low, not from reverence but because raised voices carry absurdly poorly in the silt air and the white bowl at once. Close conversation, sign for distance, polished obsidian mirrors for district-to-district messages in flashes: that is the acoustic shape of the place.

Rules accrete around all this, practical first, sacred later, until you cannot tell which came first. You do not build on a flat, because the devils walk there — also, because your floor would heave. You do not waste the morning water even if a pool is uncovered, you cover it and register it, because uncovered water calls too many flies — also, because it is the god's eye and must be lidded. You do not make a sharp edge on anything offered to the town: not a house, not a loaf of the grey fungus-cake, not a tool left in the street. Sharp things catch wind and invite growth in the crack. You always leave a gecko-wall, one interior wall unplastered and full of holes, in any house you sleep in. A house without geckos gets flies; a house that gets flies gets fever.

By late afternoon the turbulence eases. The devils thin out, the swifts drop lower and lower until they are skimming the roofs, the silt settles, and the town's scattered districts light their small, smokeless fungus-fat lamps. The basin cools fast. Stone begins, almost imperceptibly, to bead again. Somewhere in a lower street a floor will be deciding, overnight and without a sound, to rise; somewhere a channel will be closing with salt; somewhere a new damp pocket will be forming in a place people had given up on, and in a week there will be a black mat there, and flies, and geckos, and an argument about whose cistern-kin have the right to scrape it.

Below the plateau, bright as snow, cool as a cellar, empty and crowded at once — it endures not by resisting its own restlessness, but by being rebuilt out of it, quietly, every single morning.
