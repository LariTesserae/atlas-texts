It is a single hollow high in the mountains, and you find it by accident because there is almost nothing to find from the outside.

At this altitude the wind is thin and bright and constant, moving over broken stone and short alpine turf. Then the ground folds. A lip of black basalt, rounded by an age of frost and water, curls over a north-facing cirque no more than twenty paces across. The sun has never properly entered it. Rain enters, mist enters, meltwater enters along a hundred hairline cracks, but light arrives only as a cool gray diffusion, bounced off wet rock, then bounced again off wet leaves, until it is less light than a memory of light. Inside, it is always dusk. It is always just after rain.

The temperature never finds a reason to change. Seven degrees, perhaps eight, in summer and in the buried middle of winter. The overhang holds the cold in. The saturated earth holds it. Your breath joins the air and stays there, because there is nowhere for the air to go and no wind has ever learned the way in.

Everything weeps.

Water beads on the underside of fern fronds and hangs there, swollen, refusing for a long time to fall. It condenses on stone, on moss, on the backs of beetles. It runs in clear, soundless sheets down the grotto walls, not in streams so much as a permanent condition of the stone itself. The basalt is black and glass-smooth in the seep-lines, ribbed with pale calcite where minerals have been laid down drip by drip over thousands of years. Each drip has worn its own small basin in the floor. Each basin is exactly deep enough to hold water, a snail, and the snail's dark reflection.

At the center is a pool, though pool suggests something open. This is more like a piece of night laid flat. It fills the lowest part of the hollow, edged with peat and liverwort, and it is so still that the drips from the ceiling vanish into it without a ripple — a living film covers the surface, tens of thousands of springtails and water-walking insects packed side by side, absorbing the impact, parting and closing. Under them, in the clear cold, blind shrimp move upside-down along the submerged rock, white as peeled roots. Fish no longer than a thumbnail, eyeless, the color of diluted milk, hang motionless above the silt. They have hung there, in some ancestral sense, forever. One replaces another in the same dark water, facing the same slow current that is not a current.

For the pool does not flow. Nothing here flows, exactly. Water arrives by condensation and seepage, and leaves by condensation and seepage, at a rate so perfectly matched that the level never rises or falls by so much as the width of a limpet. The limpets themselves cover the submerged stone in overlapping gray rosettes, grazing algae in slow circles. Above the waterline the snails take over. Below it, caddis larvae drag themselves in cases patiently fabricated from sand grains, fragments of shell, and bits of black grit bound with silk — the only construction here, and it is older than hands. From the ceiling, fungus-gnat larvae lower fishing lines of mucus and luminousless silk, bead after bead, until the dark above the pool is hung with a fine, wet curtain that moves with no air at all. Small flies blunder in and do not struggle long.

It should not be lush in this darkness, and yet it is, in the low, dense way of things that do not need to reach. Moss lies a foot thick over the floor, over fallen stone, over old wood that long ago lost any shape of a tree. It is not one moss but dozens, black-green, yellow-green, blue where the seep water is richest, growing in cushions and feathers and tight velvet scales. Liverworts spread their flat, wet tongues over every ledge. Filmy ferns, translucent as insect wings, open in the darkest corners where the rock perpetually sweats. Fungi emerge from the peat in pale clusters and stay — cold slows their rotting to a near-standstill, so a mushroom may keep its shape for a season, freckled with mites, eaten from within by larvae, still standing. There is no bare earth. There is no bare stone. There is no bare anything. Look closely at what you took for a leaf and it is coated in algae; the algae are grazed by rotifers; the rotifers are hunted in their millions by things too small to name without stopping the whole walk.

And you will stop. Not from wonder, though there is that, but because the hollow is packed in a way that makes simple walking an act of negotiation. Fauna here is not abundant so much as total.

The moss is inhabited at every level: springtails in white drifts that jump like flecks of ash when your warmth reaches them, millipedes coiled in the peat seams, isopods the size of thumbnails plated like wet armor, centipedes with their legs moving in slow, deliberate sequence, hunting by touch. Salamanders live under every liftable frond — long, black, gold-flecked, their skin beaded with the same condensation as the leaves, their eyes filmed and nearly useless. They do not flee. The cold has made a philosophy of slowness, here. A salamander may take a minute to withdraw one foot. It may decide, instead, not to.

The walls, up to where the overhang closes, are tiled with moths. That is the only word for it. Thousands of them, wings folded, gray and brown and bone-white, overlapping like shingles, like scales, like a second surface the rock has grown. Their wing-dust holds water in minute beads. Among them move spiders with abdomens like drops of amber, and harvestmen picking their way on stilts, and beetles that shine oil-green in the dimness. Higher still, in cracks too wet for moths, are colonies of snails with translucent shells through which their hearts can faintly be seen, beating at intervals that seem geologic. Every crack has its tenant. Every tenant has its parasite. Every falling dead thing — moth, leaf, shrimp, salamander — is met before it can settle by beetles, by fly larvae, by a white lace of fungal thread, and converted, without smell, without violence, into the next occupancy.

And yet it is nearly silent.

That is the strangeness that grows on you the longer you stay. A place this alive ought to make a sound. Instead the moss takes every sound and keeps it. No birdsong penetrates. No insect chorus rises, because everything that sings elsewhere is here in a mute or cold or subterranean form. What you hear is the drip from the ceiling — one, then eleven seconds later another, then eleven seconds later another — so regular it stops seeming like water and becomes a measurement of the place itself. What you hear is a soft, almost sub-audible rustling when the moths adjust, by the hundreds, a fraction of a wing-width. The chewing of snails, felt more in your teeth than in your ears. Your own pulse, offended by the quiet, coming forward.

There are no structures. Nothing has been built by anything with hands. A stone that looks placed was placed by erosion ten thousand years ago and has since been upholstered. Even the faint technologies belong to mouths and silk glands: the caddis in their pebble coats, the gnat larvae with their hanging snares, beetles excavating soft peat with spade-shaped heads, snails rasping stone to powder with a tongue set with tiny teeth — the same rasping, over centuries, that has helped smooth the seep-channels into their final form. Nothing improves on these tools. Nothing needs to.

Because the deepest rule of the hollow is that it has finished changing.

Not dead — densely, extravagantly alive — but finished the way a held chord is finished. The drip carved the basin; the basin now shapes the drip. The pool feeds the film that stills the pool. The salamanders eat the isopods at precisely the rate the isopods eat the leaves at precisely the rate the leaves are replaced. Winter seals the entrance crack with a skin of ice that is also there, in thinner form, in August. A fern spore that lands finds the exact damp ledge its parent occupied. If you came back in ten years, in fifty, the moths would be different moths, but the wall would wear the same pattern of moths. The pool would hold the same number of white fish, suspended in the same black water, under the same eleven-second drip.

You leave by squeezing back through the entrance slit, and the bright alpine wind hits you like a change of worlds. Your clothes are soaked through without any rain having fallen. You smell of peat and cold stone and faint iron. Behind you the hollow closes without a sound, its mist unparted, its surface unbroken — high, dark, wet, and full to the last fraction of an inch with a life that has nowhere else to go and no intention of going there.
