At the top of the mountain there is a sea.

It should not be there. The mountain is too new, too high, and too steep-sided — a broad volcanic cone that rose within living memory of the oldest stories, its black flanks still uncarved by rivers, still sharp where the lava folded and set. Near its summit the cone opens. Inside is a caldera eight kilometres across, rimmed with broken basalt teeth, and the whole hollow is full of water to within a few metres of the lip.

If the rim broke, the sea would fall. It does not break. More rain falls inside the crater than ever drains through its porous floor, and from below, heat keeps the balance strange and slow.

You enter the way everyone enters: over the lowest pass in the rim, through cloud.

The cloud sits in the caldera permanently. The elevation puts the lake directly inside the weather that other mountains only pass through, and the warm water below makes its own second weather of steam. Light arrives dimmed twice. Above the surface it is a silver-grey, late-afternoon light even at noon, soft enough that shadows have no edges. Below the surface it goes green, then bottle-brown, then a warm darkness. There is no glare anywhere. Eyes adjust and stay adjusted.

The water is bath-warm. In the depths over the vents it is hot enough to cook in, and those places are marked by strings of wooden buoys and avoided, but across most of the lake you can trail a hand over the side of a boat and feel a comfortable, body-like heat. It steams in the cooler mornings. It smells faintly of sulphur, wet stone, crushed leaves, and iron. It is very clear. Because the rock is young and uneroded, there is almost no sediment — no silt beaches, no mud deltas. The black stone goes down as cleanly as it comes up, and the water does not so much wear at the land as sit on it, waiting.

The town sits on it, in it, and underneath it.

The oldest town is at the bottom. You can still see it on a still day if you pole out to the middle of the lake and look down through twenty metres of green dimness: straight stone streets, low square houses of fitted basalt block, a stepped temple, market squares. It was built in the dry bowl of the caldera, in the brief generation between the eruption that made the hollow and the rains that filled it. The water rose slowly — a hand's breadth a year, sometimes less. Slowly enough to argue about. Slowly enough to carry the roof beams up to a new floor, and then another.

So they never left. They built upward.

The present town is that same town, continued. Stone towers rise straight out of the lake, some of them founded on those original houses, their lower storeys drowned and intact. Between the towers is everything that could learn to float or be suspended: dense-packed houses of woven reed and split timber, clay-plastered, roofed with layered palm and living moss; walkways of lashed logs; gardens on rafts. Almost everything is near-touching. A house leans its eaves against its neighbour's wall. A bridge is also a house extension is also a chicken run is also a trellis. There are no streets in the dry sense, only canals just wide enough for one boat, and raised causeways of pumice-block that sink to ankle-depth at the warm high-tide and emerge again six hours later.

The tide here is not lunar. It is thermal. The floor of the caldera breathes heat in a long, slow pulse — warming, expanding the water, cooling again — so the whole lake rises and falls by a handspan twice a day. Doors are built with that in mind. Steps expect it. You learn to read which walkway will be passable by evening.

Building is constant, but it is all hand-work. Stone is quarried from the rim with wooden wedges and rope and brought down in baskets. Reed is cut, dried on roofs, woven while wet. There is very little metal — a few precious bronze knives, cold-hammered, inherited, never rung like bells because metal is too rare and too loud. Tools are stone, bone, shell, hardwood hardened in fire. New construction is always added on top or tied alongside; nothing is ever demolished. The drowned ground floor of a house becomes a cistern, a fish trap, a cool storage room you reach by ladder through a hole in the floorboards, ducking your head under water that is held back only by a rim of clay and a hanging curtain of woven fibre. The submerged second floor becomes the place where grandparents sleep in the hot season, in hammocks strung just above the lapping internal waterline, half in and half out.

And some rooms are kept fully underwater, on purpose.

Go down with one of the divers — they go weighted with a net of stones, a reed tube to the surface tended by a child on a float, no hurry in any of their movements — and the lower town opens around you, quieter than quiet. Sound does not carry right. A wooden clapper struck against a hollow log makes a soft, internal thud you feel in your teeth more than hear. Voices don't descend at all. People communicate below with hand signs and by tapping patterns on walls.

In the submerged temple, air is kept in great inverted clay bells, wider than a person, hung from the ceiling beams. You can tip your head up inside one and breathe air that tastes of green plants and someone else's lungs, replenished daily with bladders carried down from the surface — animal skins, and the buoyant seed-pods of a local vine, sealed with resin. Candles won't burn down there. Light is brought in jars: a pale phosphorescent algae cultivated in the roof gardens, which glows just enough to find a face by. Prayers are performed in that thin green gloom, in water up to the chest even inside the air bells' refuge, because the ancestors are considered to live at the depth where they died, and you go down to be at their level.

The vegetation does not distinguish much between the town and the lake. It is lush in the particular way of a place that is always warm, always wet, and never bright: layered, overlapping, insistent. Reed beds thicken every shore that isn't vertical. Water lilies with leaves broad enough for a child to sit on — briefly, giggling, before the slow tip — crowd the canals until boats push through a living resistance. A freshwater kelp, copper-green, forests the drop-offs around the drowned streets, swaying in the thermal currents at a pace slower than a sleeping person's breathing. On the rafts, people garden intensively: taro in submerged beds, beans climbing house posts straight out of the water, fruit trees in barrels of pumice and compost whose roots dangle into the lake. Roofs sprout. Mooring posts sprout if left alone for a season. Any floating mat of vegetation left untended for a year becomes solid enough to stand on; left for ten, it accretes pumice dust and reed litter and becomes, functionally, a new small island. Elders can point to islands in the southern bay and tell you whose abandoned garden each one was.

New land is also coming up from below. Near the centre of the lake, exactly where the temple streets converge if you follow them down and across, the caldera floor is doming. A black cone, just a few metres high, entirely underwater, grows by millimetres. Bubbles rise from it in a patient thread. The stone there is newborn — glassy, razor-edged, coloured with iridescent films. Nothing lives on it yet except a white bacterial mat that ripples like cloth. It is measured with knotted cords every new moon. In a few hundred years it will break the surface. In a few hundred more it may lift enough to start draining the lake over its own slopes. The town keeps building in full knowledge of this, which gives all construction a temporary feeling despite its density. Everything is meant to be added to, floated off, or surrendered storey by storey, as needed.

Animal life, by contrast, is sparse, and treated as remarkable. There is one fish — pale, nearly blind, slow, about the length of a forearm — that lives in the kelp streets and eats algae off the drowned walls. Seeing one is good luck; eating one is forbidden, mostly because there are so few. There are small brown frogs that live in the roof moss and call only after the heaviest rains, a sound like a fingertip tapping wet glass, and then stop for weeks. A single species of tall, grey wading bird nests on the rim and comes down at dusk to stand motionless in the shallows. Children are hushed for it. That is nearly all. No insects bite. The warm water and mineral content discourage them, and the constant wet discourages most else. The presence that fills the animal-shaped space in the ecology is the plants: mats that drift a few metres a day and must be poled back into place, kelp that tangles an unwary swimmer with unhurried strength, lily roots that grope into houses.

Life adjusts to the slowness. Nothing in the caldera moves fast except falling rain — and even the rain, falling through saturated air onto saturated water, makes almost no sound. It stipples rather than drums. Boats are poled, never rowed hard. Markets open when the thermal tide is low and the causeways are up, and close when the water covers them again, which sets a rhythm no one argues with. Goods move house to house in baskets passed hand to hand across gaps in the walkways. A conversation might begin in the morning between neighbours across a canal, pause while both tend their gardens through the midday dimness, and conclude in the evening without either party remarking on the interval.

At night it gets no colder. The cloud holds the heat. The algae jars are hung out along the canals, faint green lanterns reflected in black water, and the steam rises up through them. From the rim path, looking down, the town is a dense constellation at water level with another, dimmer town directly beneath it, aligned street for street, tower for tower — the living one lit, the drowned one dark, both warm, both inhabited after a fashion, with the slow thread of bubbles rising between them from a mountain that has not finished being born.
