It is called the Anvil, and you do not walk up to it so much as get lifted, very slowly, out of the weather.

The last approach is by counterweighted elevator up a cliff of black glass. Below is cloud — not fog, but the actual top of the cloud layer, white and level and close enough to look walkable. Above is more sky than should be possible. Thin, pale, normal daylight made hard by altitude, with no softness in it. About halfway up, the car passes out of the cool damp and into the heat, as if through an invisible lid. Your ears pop. Your shirt, wet from below, begins to steam dry on your back.

The mountain is still being made.

That is the first rule here. This is not an old volcano with a town in its crater. It is a new dome, still extruding, still shouldering itself upward in the tropics at almost six thousand meters. The rock arrives from below too hot and too glassy for anything to root in it. It cools in ropes and plates and shattered black panes, sharp as broken bottles, and almost before it has set the surveyors are on it with tripods, and then the builders with jacks.

Because of that, the Anvil does not have foundations. It has feet.

Every building — house, refinery, chapel, bath, warehouse — stands on clustered iron legs, screw-jacks, stone piers in iron shoes, with gaps underneath where you can see the ground breathing heat. The legs are painted with white measurement lines. Once a week men move through the alleys in single file, quiet, reading the lines, calling numbers to a boy with a slate. Where the dome has risen three centimeters, the building is cranked up two. Where it has risen unevenly, one corner is left to tilt until the Thursday crew comes. Doors acquire extra steps, then lose them. Windows that looked over an alley last year now look into a wall of fresh basalt, warm to the touch, faintly ticking as it cools.

There is barely any flat land, and what there is has to do everything at once. That is why the town is so dense it feels held in a fist.

At the summit the cooled top of the dome is perhaps two kilometers across, a buckled plateau of obsidian, pumice the color of bone, and sulfur-stained stone, and across it the town is packed roof-to-roof, factory-to-house, without gardens, without trees, without weeds in the gutters because nothing green has learned how to live here yet, and imported soil burns or blows away. Buildings lean over streets so narrow you can touch warm clay tile on one side and riveted copper sheathing on the other. One structure's cooling tower is another's bedroom wall. A bakery uses waste heat from the glassworks. A laundry hangs over a smelter vent. Second streets run across joined rooftops, planked and railed, and third streets below go in shadow even at noon, lit by slits and by the orange seams of hot rock showing between paving slabs.

The industry is everywhere, and yet the place is oddly hushed.

They make power here, and glass, and salts, and machine parts, and a very pure silicon drawn from the fresh stone while it is still chemically naive. But thin air and heat do not permit hurry. The machines are therefore enormous and slow: flywheel presses that turn once a minute and seem to think between strokes; beam engines nodding in their houses like patient animals; distillation columns forty meters high where a bubble rises, pauses, rises again. Everything is lagged in felted ash, cork, ceramic wool, to keep the heat in or out, and that lagging drinks the sound. What you hear is small against the size of the industry — the soft clink of a chain, a hiss from a valve, water dripping into an iron tank, the distant, irregular ping of contracting rock. At night you can hear the dome itself, very faintly, through the legs of your bed.

It is hot in a way that contradicts the altitude, and this creates local heresies of physics that the residents simply accept.

Geothermal heat comes up through the dome; black stone stores the equatorial sun; the wind that makes it over the rim has already been scraped warm across kilometers of bare rock. Shade is still shade and a handrail in full sun can blister, yet water boils lukewarm here. Kettles sing at a temperature you can almost put your hand in. Cooking takes forever and is mostly done by ground-heat or pressure vessels, great clamped pots that sigh in every kitchen. Rain, on the rare afternoons when cloud overtops the summit and spills across it for twenty minutes, does not so much fall as precipitate suddenly out of a whiteout, beading on hot roofs, vanishing into steam where it strikes the street, collecting for a frantic quarter-hour in every gutter, cistern mouth, tarpaulin sag and condenser fin in town.

Water is not scarce exactly; it is intermittent, architectural.

In the dry mornings — most mornings — the town lives on stored water and condensation. Cold pipes brought up from deep shadowed clefts sweat all day, and their sweat is caught in zinc channels. The power station runs long black radiator walls that face the night sky; by dawn they are furred with dew, and boys scrape it before the sun takes it back. In the wet hour, bells ring, not urgently, and everyone has a job they do without speaking: open this hatch, swing that spout, unroll that net. Then it stops. The clouds sink below the rim again. The streets steam. The cisterns under the buildings tick as they settle.

In that damp margin — in condenser channels, around warm pools caught in roof basins, at the mouths of steam vents — is the closest thing to a living landscape. Not plants. Mineral gardens. Needles of salt grow overnight in the exhaust from the brine works, white thickets an inch high, exquisitely branched, dissolved by the next wet hour and grown again differently. Sulfur flowers open as yellow crusts around vents, then harden and flake. In the warm runoff channels, bacterial mats make slow tapestries of rust, black, cream and a poisonous green, slimy, heat-loving, smelling of iron and eggs. They creep a few centimeters a month. The Anvil's gardeners are chemical workers who keep them out of the drinking intakes and let them alone elsewhere, because they are the first thing here that is not either stone or built.

Fauna is rarer and treated as news. A condor sometimes works up on a thermal from below and circles — beneath the level of the rooftops for a while, obscenely large against the clouds, before it finds the updraft off the dome and rises past the chimneys. White moths live in the cool pipe-ducts and come out at dusk in twos and threes. There are wall lizards in the sunniest districts, introduced accidentally in crates a generation ago, now precious and protected by custom; children leave bottle-caps of water for them. Rats live only in the upper, temperate floors. Anything lower, and the ground is too hot for their feet.

Living here is an agreement with verticality and patience.

You learn not to dig. Down is hot, then hotter, then not survivable. Graves are in cold chambers cut sideways into an older, dead spur on the north rim, and even that is filling. You learn not to leave metal in the sun, not to trust a map printed more than a year ago, not to build a wall you cannot raise. Old ground floors are constantly being surrendered: first they become storage, then kilns, then curing rooms for brick and chemical cake, then they are sealed with a small iron door and an offering of salt while the family moves up one story and cuts a new entrance from what was a landing. Many houses are thus younger at the top than at the bottom by decades, wood and bright tin above, below them darkening layers of masonry slowly being taken back into the mountain.

At the eastern edge the town simply stops, and the new earth begins.

There are survey stakes, a fence of warning bells that stir when gas breathes out of a crack, and then half a kilometer of raw dome no one has built on yet — folding, creasing, pushing up fresh spines in the night, its surface still too warm to walk at noon. It steams after the wet hour like a herd of sleeping animals. Sometimes a whole slab will lift with a sound so low it is felt in the teeth more than heard, and stay, and the surveyors will wait three days, five days, and go out in asbestos-soled boots at dawn with their painted poles, while behind them the Anvil waits with timber already cut, pipes already threaded, a street already named.

From the rooftop streets in the evening, when the machines have slowed to their night pace and the heat comes up through the soles of your shoes, you can see the whole settlement lit in windows and furnace-mouths, dense as a circuit, contained as a ship. Beyond the rim there is nothing but distance: cloud ocean below, black lesser peaks breaking through it, stars coming on too quickly in the thin air. No fields. No forests. No river. Just this one improbable, industrious town, sweating, condensing, cranking itself patiently upward on its jacks — riding a mountain that has not finished deciding how tall it is going to be.
