Before you see it, you hear it.

You hear it from the rim road, twelve kilometers out and still in what the maps, optimistically, call empty country. A low pressure in the sternum first. Then a roar that resolves, as you descend, into layers: the bass churn of pumps and transit tubes, the mid-range clatter of construction and deconstruction, the high, constant hiss of cooling water hitting hot surfaces. By the time you pass under the first inhabited arches it is 105, 110 decibels at street level, a physical weather of sound. Unprotected conversation is impossible. Unprotected ears will be damaged in an hour.

Everyone here is protected. The standard implant is a selective gate in the inner ear, grown not manufactured, that cuts the city down to a manageable brown murmur and lets voices through only if they are addressed to you, bone-conducted, subvocalized. Children are born shouting and learn, by age three, to sign with their whole forearms while their mouths make small, precise shapes no outsider can hear. Pigeons would go deaf here. There are no pigeons.

This is the Basin.

It is not a city with suburbs. It is a former inland sea, a warm, low depression two thousand kilometers across, now filled. Filled is the word. Shore to shore, rim to rim, horizon curving away under its own haze, there is no countryside inside it, no park, no vacant lot, no interval. Density is not crowding here; it is law. Any volume left unoccupied for more than fifty-eight minutes is reclaimed by the municipal substrate and re-offered, re-grown, re-filled. A room wants a body in it. A corridor wants traffic. A roof wants a market, a cistern, a nest, a landing pad, another roof.

The buildings are mostly low, five to nine layers up and as many down, because elevation is precious in the wrong direction here — the floor of the Basin is already forty meters below the outside sea level, and the air pools heavy and hot. But low does not mean spread thin. They pack shoulder to shoulder, interlocked, bridged, tunneled, stacked in terraces of sintered regolith ceramic, black solar glass, self-healing alloy gone rainbow with heat, and a bone-pale bio-concrete that sweats in the humidity and knits itself shut when cracked. Streets are not gaps between buildings. Streets are buildings that have agreed, temporarily, to be hollow near the ground.

And temporary is constant.

About four percent of the Basin is dismantled and rebuilt every day. Not renovated — unmade. Programmable matter, vat-grown structural tissue, and swarms of assembler motes take a tower down to slurry overnight and raise a clinic, a tenement stack, a transit throat in its place by morning shift. Neighborhoods migrate at walking pace, facades rippling as internal partitions flow. An address is not a location; it is a trajectory. You do not say where you live, you give a predicted path with error bars, and the mail, which is mostly physical because the air is too noisy and wet for clean transmission, follows the probability.

This should be chaos that tears itself apart. It isn't. It has been doing this for three hundred years. The churn is the settled state, the way a river's rapids keep the same shape while no water stays. Certain great flows never change: the dawn surge of bodies from the down-level dormitory warrens to the heat-exchange fields; the afternoon rain; the nightly reversal of the canals. Old women who have never left their district can predict, to the minute, where a market will condense tomorrow, in the same way farmers once predicted weather.

Because the Basin makes its own weather. Ninety billion human bodies — and that number is argued about constantly, proudly — plus industry, plus dark roofs under a normal, white-yellow sun, pump heat into trapped air until it rises, cools against the rim mountains, and falls back as rain. Every day, between two and four, warm rain comes down in sheets so dense the upper bridges close. It drums on ceramic, it floods the stepped plazas in minutes, it turns every weeping wall into a waterfall. Then the pumps take it, audibly, joyfully, down through gratings big enough to swallow a bus, into the cistern layers, through the cooling loops, out again as steam from a thousand vents. Nothing is dry for long. Clothes are designed to be worn wet. Paper does not exist outside of sealed archives. Mold would eat the world if the cleaner-fauna weren't faster.

There are no plants. Not one blade, not outdoors. That is the strangest thing once you notice it — a warm, wet, sunlit place with zero green. No trees for shade, no moss on the wet stone, no weeds in cracks. The cracks do not allow it, and neither does the ecology that replaced it. Food is vat-meat, vat-grain, algae paste grown in sealed interior farms behind glass. Pollen is a biohazard category. In its place there is a mineral tidiness, scraped clean by life of other kinds.

For life is abundant; it is just not plant life, and not all of it is human.

The air at head height belongs to filter-kites, blind, sail-winged things the size of dinner plates, engineered from bat and ray stock, that sieve spores and particulates and are harvested weekly for the protein in their gut sacs. The walls belong to gecko-sized wall-runners that lick condensation and excrete it clean into gutter veins. Every canal — and canals cut the Basin in moving braids, black water smelling of iron and ozone, barges and pump-stations and floating tenements crowding them bank to bank — every canal teems with armored eels that eat sewage and are eaten, in turn, by the heron-machines and the poor. Down in the service dark below the lowest inhabited layer, something much larger moves in the warm flooded tunnels: tunnel-moles, hairless, sightless, strong as horses, bred to drag cable sleds and to find, by smell, the dead who were not reported. They are treated with superstitious affection. Their handlers are a hereditary guild.

Smaller still, everywhere, is the true population: mite-swarms, ant-swarms, roach-swarms, some biological, some mechanical, most by now an undecidable blend, that clean skin from floors, food from teeth, corpses from corners, data from obsolete surfaces. Walk barefoot and your soles will be groomed before you reach the corner. Drop a meal and it will be disassembled in forty seconds, the larger fragments carried off by beetle-porters with barcodes grown into their shells. Lie down in public — people do, in shifts, in hammocks strung over transit lanes — and you will wake with your ears cleaned and your pockets inventoried but, by ancient swarm-law, untouched.

At the center of the Basin, where all the water eventually gathers, there is no park and no monument. There is the Sump: a shallow, steaming, artificial swamp three hundred kilometers wide, roofed in places, open in others, where the cooling systems of a continent-sized city finally give up their heat. Reactor towers stand in the reedless water like islands. Heat-fauna crowd there that live nowhere else — red, translucent fish in the outflow channels, amphibious things basking on pipes too hot for a human hand, whole rookeries of filter-kites blackening the steam. Floating districts anchor in chains, rising and falling with the thermal tides. The sound there is worst of all, a roar of water and venting so total it becomes, through the implants, a strange silence at the center of perception. People go there to think. They say the center of the noise is the only quiet place in the world.

There is no outside view of it. The Basin is too big to see across and too dense to leave an edge unbuilt; even the rim is stacked with arrivals, quarantine stacks, desalinization throats drinking from the exterior ocean and weeping fresh water inward. Ships dock at the rim and their crews, stepping into the noise and the wet heat and the unending motion, often weep — from the sound, from relief, from horror.

You learn to move by current, not by map. You learn which human rivers run which way at which hour, which bridges will exist this afternoon, which roofs accept foot traffic when the rain hits. You learn to read the smell of a district — ozone for fabrication, yeast for food vats, hot iron for transit, sweet rot for the down-levels where the moles are kept. You learn that privacy is not space; there is no space. Privacy is a negotiated dampening of sound and swarm-attention, a courtesy.

At night the light does not go out, it changes medium: less sun-glare on wet glass, more sodium and bioluminescent signage crawling with updated destinations, buildings advertising their own future demolition dates. The churn slows to two percent. The rain stops. The canals reverse. Ninety billion people, more or less, breathe in a depression in the earth that has become a single, turbulent, deafening organism — not growing outward anymore, because there is nowhere outward left, but growing inward, denser, faster, forever rebuilding the same place into a new one.

In the morning your street will be somewhere else. Your building will have traded a floor with its neighbor. The rain will come at two. The sound will never have stopped.

That is not the city failing to settle. That is what settled means here.
