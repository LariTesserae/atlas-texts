It is a town in a hollow, and there is no one in it.

You come down into it out of thicker country — warm, low ground, the air already wet on your skin before any rain — and the vegetation does not thin. It changes intention. What was jungle becomes wall, roof, pavement, arch. The trees stop growing like trees. They square their trunks at the corners of lanes. Their branches meet overhead in vaults and stay there, leaf-tiled, root-buttressed. Vines have thickened into cables, spans, balustrades, bridges over nothing. Broad leaves overlap like shingles. Bark has been polished by passage into something almost like stone steps, and the steps are worn concave in their centers, established, old enough that the wear itself has been grown over and worn again.

It is urban without ever having been built. There is a market row of hollow stalls, each roofed with a single curled leaf, each counter a flattened root still living and faintly pulsing. There are towers — four, five stories of braided liana and fused saplings — with round windows that dilate slowly in the normal daylight. There are doorways. Too many doorways. Some are tall enough for a giraffe, some are knee-high slits in a hedge, some open in the middle of a trunk and lead only into another doorway facing back at you. There are lanes between the plant-buildings, a plaza, a fountain court, proper drainage channels cut — no, grown — into the living ground.

But no tools made it. No cut ends, no nails, no glass, no wire. Everything is rooted, budded, secreted, or woven while still green. If there is mortar, it is sap. If there is rope, it is a vine that will object, slowly, to being loaded wrong.

And the town is empty in a way that feels architectural. Density has been removed from it. The lanes hold nothing. The plaza is a vast, swept oval of root-mat and pale, packed earth, forty paces across with not a shrub, not a fallen leaf, not a single body in it. Outside the hollow, the green world presses in total, plant on plant on plant, substrate and ceiling and food and grave. Inside, there is this deliberate clearing, this negative space held open tree by tree, as if the forest learned the shape of a city by learning where not to be.

The moisture is intermittent and everywhere at once. It does not rain so much as condense. Leaf-gutters drip into leaf-cups. Moss beards hang dark from the eaves and squeeze themselves out at noon with an audible ticking. Shallow pools collect in root basins, in the cups of bromeliads set like cisterns along the walls, in the footprints — if they are footprints — pressed into the soft verge. Between rains the town steams. The wood swells. Doors that fit in the morning are stuck by afternoon, or have grown a new threshold across themselves in the night.

It is loud before you see why.

At first it is insects: cicadas in the wall-trees, a bright metallic drilling that comes not from any branch you can point to but from inside the trunks, as if the buildings themselves were hollow instruments. Then frogs join from the cistern-leaves, hundreds of them, glass-green and throat-bloated, none visible until a whole facade flickers with their pulsing. Birds live in the roofs in such numbers that the tiles rustle. Not perched on the town — in it. The walls teem. Split a dead leaf and there are beetles, ants hauling white eggs through a crack in the bark, a sleeping bat folded into a flower bract, a snake the exact color and patience of a vine. The fauna is abundant and almost entirely concealed. The empty streets belong, apparently, to sound alone.

And sound is the weather here.

Twice a day, and always after a burst of rain, the chorus gathers itself. It begins with one frog making a note too large for its body in a lane behind you. The note is answered before it finishes — not echoed, answered early, the reply arriving a breath before the call that causes it. Then the cicadas find the pitch and saw at it. Birds drop out of the canopy in spirals, screaming as they cross the plaza, not landing, just using the open space to turn their voices in. Something large and unseen bellows through a hollow tower and the windows all over town flare open. The volume becomes physical. It is deafening in the strict sense: after ten minutes your own thoughts arrive without consonants. Dust lifts off the root-pavement in synchronized hops. The pools dimple as if rained on from below. Leaves shiver off a fine mist of spores, pollen, and water that hangs in the sunlit air and makes every sunbeam look solid enough to lean against.

Distance stops working in the sound. A whisper from the far bell-tower arrives pressed against your ear, obscene in its intimacy. Your companion shouts beside you and you watch the words leave her mouth and get lost, politely, somewhere around her chin. There are streets where the chorus doubles and streets, two steps sideways, where it is cut off as cleanly as by a door closing — sound-shadow, cast by buildings that have grown baffles, scoops, resonant hollows. One lane loops acoustically. You hear your own footsteps approaching you from ahead for as long as you walk it, always about to arrive.

This is how the town is built.

Nothing here cuts or carries. Things call, and the plants grow toward the shaping of the call. A sustained note thickens a vine. A rhythm prunes it. The frog-colonies in the cisterns keep the water-channels open by singing stones — small grit and seeds — out of them with vibration. The birds maintain the plaza by never allowing their uproar to drop, in that one place, below the level at which seedlings can stand the shaking. Predators hunt by it. There is a lean, long-legged cat, green-eyed, almost silent, that waits in sound-shadow and strikes at birds made careless by their own noise. Owls — in daylight, wrongly — hunt the cat by listening for the one moving patch of quiet inside the din. Ants sweep the streets in black, organized rivers between choruses, carrying off every fallen body before rot can start, and the buildings feed them for it through nectar-windows that open only on their trails.

The place is active in all its layers at once. A stall grew a new roof last night because a mating pair of howler monkeys argued in it until dawn. A bridge sags, is sung taut again, sags differently. Flowers the size of cartwheels open on the south walls and turn, creaking, to face not the sun but the loudest tower. Seeds burst overhead in the heat with little pops that the frogs immediately imitate, then the cicadas, until the whole town is reproducing a sound whose origin has already stopped. It learns noises the way other forests learn seasons. If you speak here — and you should not, during chorus — you will hear your sentence come back the next morning from a wall that was not there the night before, grown a little more curved, a little more like the inside of your mouth.

There are rules, none written, all enforced by consequence.

Walk in the middle of the lanes, where the sound keeps the ground clear and the walls cannot brush you with their inhabitants. Do not touch a green door twice; the first touch is the plant smelling you, the second is it deciding. Do not shelter from rain under the big leaves — that is where the water is being aimed, and where the frogs fall, ecstatic and heavy, when the cisterns overflow. Leave before the second chorus if you want to leave by the same street you entered. The streets do not move, exactly. Their echoes do, and after enough days the plants follow their echoes, and one morning the lane home is a wall of white roots listening very carefully in the wrong direction.

By late afternoon the light goes green-gold through the canopy vaults and the town is at its most beautiful and its least inhabitable: every tower humming, every pool ringing at its rim, birds crossing and recrossing the empty plaza in a single braided scream, the plant-city feeding, courting, hunting, repairing itself at a volume that makes your ribs feel like thin, badly made doors. In the center of the plaza, where nothing grows and nothing stands, the air is visibly dented by noise. That is the town square. That is the inhabitant.

You were never looking at an abandoned place. You were looking at a body with its one enormous organ held open to the sky — and the emptiness was not absence. It was the space the voice needs to live in.
