There is a door at the bottom of the wall that most people take for a cupboard.

It is knee-high, made of blackened cedar planks held together with wooden pegs, set into the plinth of the grinding-house where the lane turns down toward the dry riverbed. No steps lead to it. You have to crouch in the dust, work the peg latch with two fingers, and go in sideways on your knees. The lane behind you is cool and pale and almost silent. Then the door swings inward, and light and sound come out together, as if the little dark seam in the wall had been holding back weather.

Inside, there is exactly room for one standing person.

Not room to turn comfortably. Not room to raise your arms. The chamber is perhaps five feet across, but that measurement is theoretical; almost none of it is empty. You stand in a person-shaped hollow worn into the middle of the floor, and on every side — to your ankles, your shoulders, your face — the place is packed solid with things that hands have made.

The floor under your feet is a mosaic of fired clay tiles, each one stamped before baking with a thumbprint, a wheat-head, a spiral, a crude eye. Centuries of knees and soles have dished them smooth. The walls are brick under lime plaster under other walls that were built later, by accumulation rather than by plan: rows of small clay tablets wedged edge to edge, each incised with a name or a prayer or a date no one can read anymore; hammered copper and brass plaques nailed over the tablets; carved wooden toggles; woven wool ribbons gone stiff with dust; bundles of reed tied with twine; strings of handmade glass beads; little clay houses, clay hands, clay ears, clay hearts, each no bigger than a walnut, slotted into every gap. Above, the dome is more of the same, brick disappearing under hanging clusters of tiny clay bells and hollow gourds, so close overhead that tall visitors feel them brush their hair. There is no stone, no earth, no original surface left visible anywhere. Even the air shaft is lined with glazed pottery. Even the crack where a plant has rooted turns out to be a broken offering pot.

It is blindingly bright. High in the dome, a window of sliced alabaster no wider than a hand admits the daylight and softens it to milk, but the light does not stay up there. Nine oil lamps burn in niches behind pierced clay screens — tended from a hatch on the other side of the wall, never allowed to go out — and their light, and the daylight, strike the hundreds of polished plaques and bead strings and glazed surfaces and are thrown back and forth until there is no shadow left to stand in. The brass nearest the standing-place has been rubbed mirror-bright by sleeves and shoulders. Farther out it has gone green and brown. You see yourself broken into a hundred small, overlapping reflections, none of them whole. After a minute your eyes water. There is nowhere dim to look.

And it is loud, with a loudness that does not come and go. On the other side of the north wall is the chanting hall, where the tone has been kept up, in relays, longer than anyone in the town has been alive — one voice taking the note before another lets it fall, day and night, a single low vowel sustained through the shared wall. The builders understood what they were doing. Forty clay resonating jars are buried mouth-inward in the brickwork, tuned by size and by the thickness of their necks, and they catch that note and build it, each jar adding its own hum an octave or a fifth away, until inside the little chamber the sound is no longer something you hear so much as something that presses evenly on your ribs, your teeth, the backs of your eyes. Your own breathing joins it. If you speak, even to whisper your name, the dome takes the word and returns it at the volume of a shout, flattened and prolonged, and the hanging bells answer with a faint sympathetic shiver. Most people, after the first startled noise they make coming in, say nothing more.

The air is cool and completely dry. The walls are three feet thick; the chamber sits low, below street level, and neither summer noon nor winter night seems to reach it. Your mouth dries within minutes. There is a smell of lime dust, old lamp oil, warm copper, and the faintest ghost of incense absorbed so deep into the plaster that it can no longer be separated from the smell of the building itself. Dust hangs in the alabaster light, but even the dust seems settled into its arrangement, drifting without ever quite landing, stirred by the vibration in the air.

Almost nothing lives here, and what does lives sparingly. No spider builds in the bright corners. No ant crosses the copper strip set into the threshold; no mouse has ever nested in the ribbons, though the whole town outside is full of mice. The noise and the light and the dryness keep the chamber emptied of animals as efficiently as a sealed jar.

The plants are three, and they may only technically be alive. In a niche at elbow height, a resurrection rosette sits curled in its own clay cup, brown and papery and tight as a fist, waiting for a wetting it never receives. By the air shaft, a caper sprout has found the one crack in the pottery lining and put out two pale leaves, no more, trimmed back year after year by the dry moving air to exactly those two. And among the offerings on the eastern wall, woven in with the wool ribbons generations ago, are bundles of dried herbs — marjoram, rue, a grey local sage — which are vegetation and offering at once, never rotting in the dry air, never quite finishing becoming dust. They give the place its only softness, and it crumbles if touched.

That is the governing rule, understood without being written anywhere: *do not touch.* Not because the objects are precious individually — most are crude, many are broken — but because the packing is the place. Each tablet leans on a bead string, each plaque is wedged by a clay heart, each ribbon holds a bundle that holds a bell that has not fallen in a hundred years only because nothing has shifted. Custodians add new offerings from the hatch behind the lamps, working by feel, sliding each small thing into the one gap that will take it, so that the hollow in the middle grows imperceptibly narrower as the centuries pass, and is kept, by the wearing of the floor tiles, exactly the shape of the people who come to stand in it. Nothing is removed. Nothing is rearranged. Decay here is so slow — dry, cool, airless between the objects — that it has reached a kind of truce with accumulation. A ribbon fades at the rate a new ribbon is added. A lamp is trimmed to the same flame. The note does not fall.

So the room does not change. Pilgrims came out of it two hundred years ago blinking and temporarily deaf, describing the same glare, the same pressure of sound, the same two leaves by the shaft. The town outside has re-roofed itself, changed its language at the edges, let the riverbed go fully to cracked mud and salt. Inside the wall, it is still the same cool noon, the same chord, the same packed silence of objects — because the silence is not in the room, it is what the room does to you. You stand in the only empty space for as long as you can bear the light, feeling your heartbeat find the frequency of the jars, and when you finally duck out backwards into the lane, the ordinary world returns in pieces: first the muted street noises, absurdly quiet; then the wide, dim sky; then the smell of dust and dry weeds, and your own small shadow, restored to you, walking beside you up from the riverbed.
