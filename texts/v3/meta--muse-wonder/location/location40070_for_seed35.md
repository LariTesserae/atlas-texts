It is below the plain, and you hear it before you find the way down.

Not from a distance as a roar getting louder, but all at once, at a certain unmarked step on the dry track, as if you had crossed the lip of a bell. One step: wind in sparse saltbush, your own boots on grit. Next step: a held chord filling the teeth.

The pit is not large. Eighty metres across, perhaps thirty deep — cathedral-scale, if the cathedral had been blasted rather than built. A single bounded bowl cut straight down into warm rock, so young its walls have not learned to soften. Every fracture is still bright. Ochre, rust-red, sulfur-yellow stone in knife-edged shelves, fresh talus at the angles with the corners still on it. Painted survey numbers, white and stencilled, have not faded. The cut marks of drills are still clean spirals in the rock. It smells, impossibly and permanently, of recent blasting — hot stone-dust, cordite ghosting the air — though no one has blasted here in any living memory. It has been exactly this fresh for as long as anyone has kept records.

A switchback stair of expanded steel mesh goes down. The rail is hot through gloves.

Light never properly arrives. At the rim it is hard desert noon; ten steps down, the geometry takes it. High scaffolds, gantries, and a tangle of pipework throw the sun into amber slats, and below that a constant brown haze of dust hangs — not settling, not blowing away, held in permanent suspension by vibration. Sodium work-lamps burn at all hours along the walls, dim orange in the haze, drawing a second, tighter ceiling under the sky. It is always this dim. Morning does not brighten it. Night does not darken it. The lamps have burned so long their glass has sugared.

Warmth comes up from below and out from the machines together — a steady thirty-one, thirty-two degrees, dry as inside a kiln. It shivers visibly over exhaust stacks and then, strangely, does not disperse. The heat haze keeps the same shapes. Sweat vanishes before it can run. Your lips find the taste of pennies, ozone, hot oil, guano, sulfur. There is no water anywhere in the place, and every part of it testifies to an expectation of water: dry standpipes, cisterns open and white with mineral crust, hoses cracked into leather lace, a concrete sump at the lowest point with a drain at its centre that has never been wet. Pumps still run — industrial, purposeful, huge flywheels turning — pumping hot air and dust in a closed loop from one dry pipe to another with great seriousness.

The machines are not in a factory. They are fitted into the bowl wherever the rock would take them, layered so densely there is no single floor. A turbine house wedged into a notch. A row of compressors bolted to a ledge barely wider than they are. Catwalks crossing catwalks, cable trays, riveted tanks, a small foundry furnace breathing orange through its seams, conveyor galleries angling down to a grated shaft at the bottom from which the deepest note rises: not built, that one. Found. A fissure in the earth exhaling a constant, scorching, perfectly even wind. Someone, once, built an engine around a geological throat.

And then the animals took the rest of the space, which was all of it.

They are abundant past reason, dense as the machinery, near-touching, arranged. Swifts nest in open pipe-ends, row after row, their mud-daub replaced with dust and machine oil worked to a dark cement. Bats hang from the undersides of gantries in warm, breathing curtains despite the lamps. Pale lizards occupy every sun-warmed — machine-warmed — plate, tails aligned with seams in the metal. Crickets the size of thumbs live in the cable trays, blind, milk-white, thighs like springs. Moths crowd the sodium lamps in a perpetual slow orbit, singed and unsinged at once. Beetles move along the conveyor belts in both directions, riding up, walking down, riding up. Below the grating, where the hot wind rises, thousands of small dry-winged insects hold station in the updraft without rising or falling, a living column.

Nothing here moves freely, exactly. Each creature has a pitch, a perch, a cell, a note. The crickets stridulate against the compressor rhythm. The swifts cry on the off-beat. Wing-cases, turbine blades, the sigh of the fissure, a loose panel buzzing in sympathetic resonance halfway up the north wall — together they make the chord, and the chord never changes. Not louder at dusk, not thinner at noon. Ninety decibels of equilibrium. You cannot whisper in the pit; the air won't carry anything so temporary. You shout and your voice is simply absorbed, becomes briefly part of the held note, and is gone without echo.

Vegetation persists where it can, sparse and stubborn and wrong. At the rim, saltbush and a few thorn trees lean away from the noise. Inside, in cracks where oil has soaked the rock black, grow leafless grey plants with swollen stems and flowers like tiny cupped ears, always turned toward the loudest machines. A dry, colourless moss — if it is moss — coats the inside of abandoned ducting. It feeds, apparently, on heat and vibration and iron filings. When a compressor cycles, the ear-flowers tremble in unison. They have no seeds anyone has found. They are simply always there, the same number, in the same cracks.

The human presence is scattered and slight against all this: three corrugated operator huts, doors open, logbooks inside swollen with identical daily entries. A shrine the size of a mailbox at the stair foot, containing a bolt, a feather, and a hearing-protector worn smooth. Faint white arrows painted on walkways. A canteen counter with cups chained to it, cups dry for decades. No ruins, because nothing is allowed to ruin; the vibration shakes dust off every surface forever, the animals pick every filter clean with their bodies, the lizards eat the moths that fall, the beetles eat what the lizards leave, the swifts eat the beetles that stray upward, and something — no one has seen it arrive or leave — replaces any creature that dies within the hour, taking up its exact position, its exact portion of the sound.

That is the rule of the place, once you've stayed long enough to feel it in your sternum: it does not progress. The rock does not erode because the animals and machines together are constantly worrying it back to sharpness, claw and coolant-drip and maintenance that no maintainer performs. The population does not boom or crash. The furnace does not cool. The fresh smell does not fade. A dropped tool will be found, years later, in the same orientation, bright-edged, vibrating almost imperceptibly against the grating where it fell, coated in nothing.

At the bottom, beside the sump, the air is loudest and strangely clearest. Stand at the grate and the hot wind from the earth presses your clothes to your body and the chord separates for a moment into its hundreds of parts — insect, bird, blade, bat-murmur, the human-scale hum of generators — each one performing its note at its station, none beginning, none ending.

You can leave whenever you like. The stair is right there. But the last surveyor wrote, in the identical logbook, in the identical hand, under a date no one can verify: *It is not that it has stopped changing. It is that it finished, and this is what finished sounds like.*
