There is an ocean in the sky, and it has never moved.

Not a lake on a mountain. A sea, at altitude, spread across the top of a continent-sized plateau four thousand metres above the cloud plain, held there by plants. From below all you see is the plateau's flank disappearing into weather, and a slow, perpetual spill of white water coming over its edge in a hundred waterfalls — and the same amount, always, falling forever without the level above changing by a hair.

Up top, the light is ordinary late-morning light. That is the first wrong thing. It is always late morning here. Cool, clear, green-gold light, sun fixed at the angle of about ten o'clock, shadows laid down in permanent bars through the water. The water is cool, too — eight, ten degrees — so cold it aches in the teeth and numbs fingers within minutes, and so clear in the open places that you can see a kilometre through blue-green emptiness until the next presence resolves.

And there are not many presences. That is the second wrong thing, because the vegetation is total.

The sea has no mineral floor. Far below the surface the light fails into a slow green dusk, and there the bottom is root — root laid over root, braided, fused, polished by centuries of current into smooth, undulating terrain, hills and basins and long channels worn to a glassy finish. Nothing is sharp up here. Erosion finished its argument long ago. Every channel holds exactly the shape the water wants, and the water holds exactly to the channel. In places the root-mat rises in towers — trunks thicker than cathedrals, spaced kilometres apart across open pelagic plains, their bark grown over with ribbed, shell-hard plates. Between them: nothing. Just water, light, a sparse snow of plankton, and distance. But drop anywhere, touch anything, breathe the water in through a filter and taste it, and it is plant. The water itself is faintly resinous. Microscopic green life thickens it like broth. Fine root-hairs, almost invisible, span even the emptiest columns of water in webs so delicate they rebuild as fast as you tear through them. The surface, seen from below, is not open sky. It is a ceiling of interlocked leaves the size of fields, their undersides furred and dripping, letting the sun through in shifting-looking panes that never actually shift.

This is the substrate. Everything lives in it, on it, or as part of it. There is no shore that is not a root-rim, no island that is not a crown of fused trunks breaking the leaf-ceiling to make a breathing-hump above the surface, where the air is thin and mountain-cold and smells of sap and ozone.

And the forest is industrial.

Not built — nothing here is built. There is no timber cut square, no metal, no ruin, no artifact, no straight line anyone intended. But along the great permanent currents you find the pump-groves, and the sound reaches you long before the sight: a vast, continuous, mechanical roaring that never rises and never falls, the loudest steady sound in nature, felt in the sternum as much as heard.

The pump-trees stand in ranks along the inlets, each one a hollow trunk forty metres across, and inside each, grown woody turbine vanes turn on living axles, driven by the current and by slow muscular contraction of the trunk itself, sucking deep water up and jetting it out through lateral vents in a permanent, boiling upwelling. Strings of oxygen bubbles rise from them in unbroken columns. Black-leaf boilers cluster where the light strikes strongest, their concave leaves focusing sun onto sap-chambers that hiss and tick and vent steam into the water in rhythmic puffs that have never varied their rhythm. Further down-current, sorting-reefs do the work of a factory floor: tier upon tier of filter-fans wave in sequence, passing fallen debris hand to hand, so to speak, along slow conveyor mats of overlapping leaves — husk to the crushers, mineral grit to the rim-ward channels, soft rot down into digestion pits where the water shimmers with heat and enzyme-cloud. Electric pylons — dead-looking, leafless trunks impregnated with mineral salts — stand singly in the open plains, kilometres from anything, building charge in a crackling corona that discharges at intervals so regular you could set a clock by them, if clocks changed here. Their blue-white forks stun drifting shoals, which the root-mat then quietly absorbs.

It is mechanised, electrified, scaled production. It produces oxygen, sugar, heat, current, and itself, at industrial volume, forever, and it has no operators and no output anyone removes.

The fauna has fitted itself into the machinery. Abundant, but spread thin across the vastness — you travel long emptinesses, and then abundance arrives all at once, in its appointed place, where it always is. Glassy fish hang in the pump upwellings, swimming hard against the artificial current and going nowhere, mouths open, eternally feeding. Armoured grazers the size of barges patrol the root-hills on paddle limbs, cropping the turbine vanes and conveyor leaves at exactly the rate the leaves regrow; their shells are gardens, furred with algae, barnacled with small pink anemone-flowers that are half plant themselves. Diving birds enter from the breathing-humps above, black-and-white, loud even underwater with the snap of their wings, and take fish from the stunning-fields in the half-second after each discharge — they have learned the interval the way their ancestors learned it, because the interval has never been different. In the digestion pits, pale blind eels knot and unknot in the warm outflow. Everything has a station. Everything is at it.

Because nothing here progresses. This is not stillness — the water moves violently, the turbines scream, the birds dive, the conveyors creep, the whole sea is in furious motion. It is motion without change, a standing wave the size of a continent. A leaf detaches and falls, and is already being replaced at the same rate by the leaf beside it, and falls in the same turning path the previous leaf fell, and lands where the conveyor expects it. A grazer eats a channel-fan down to the rib and the rib is regrowing under its mouth. Rain falls on the leaf-ceiling from the sea's own weather — for the sea is vast enough to have weather of its own, private slow cyclones of warmed water and spore-cloud that circle their appointed basins — and the rain is exactly the evaporation, and the waterfalls over the rim are exactly the inflow from the condensation-nets of root and leaf that trap the plateau's cloud layer far below and lift it, pump by pump, back to the top. A map made here would never go out of date. A footprint pressed into the root-mud is smoothed away at the same steady rate everywhere, neither fast nor slow, so that after the appointed interval every footprint is equally gone and equally, faintly, still there.

Even injury is rationed. The electric pylons crackle, the boiler-vents scald, the pump intakes can take a limb — but they have always been there, in those places, at that strength, and every animal alive up here was born knowing the geography of danger the way it knows its own gut.

At the rim, on a clear interval between discharges, you can hang in the water at the lip of the world and look down past the falling white columns into cloud, four thousand metres of it, while behind you the green sea extends to a horizon that curves with no coast, pump-groves roaring their one unchanging chord, light coming down in permanent bars, cold in your teeth, plant-sap on your tongue, and the certain, bodily understanding that if you came back in a year, or a hundred years, or never — it would be mid-morning, the turbines would be mid-turn, a bird would be mid-dive, a leaf would be mid-fall, and the ocean would be exactly, deafeningly, peacefully itself.
