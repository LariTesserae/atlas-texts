It is an ocean where there should not be one.

Not at this height. Not on top of the world.

Far below — so far below that the distance stops feeling like height and becomes weather — there is a low country of pale dust and old, eroded stone. And then the land rises all at once, not in foothills but in a single continental refusal: a plateau four kilometers high, black and unfinished, and across the top of it, filling it from rim to rim, a sea.

It is still being poured.

The sky above the High Sea is almost always raining. Not storm-rain, exactly, though there are storms, but a permanent, engineered deluge — broad curtains of it drifting for thousands of kilometers, hammering the surface white, then thinning to a silver veil, then hammering again. The light is ordinary daylight, which makes it stranger. A white sun. Blue-gray clouds unspooling fast. No alien color to explain it away. Just noon, clearly lit, on an ocean suspended in the thin air at the top of a continent, being made.

The air up here is cool and thin. It bites in the throat. The water is cooler — eight, ten degrees, numbing through gloves in minutes — and it tastes, at first, like nothing. Not salt. Not sweet. A blank mineral taste, faintly metallic, faintly of ozone, like licking a new battery. The salt is coming. The chemistry is coming. It just hasn't finished arriving.

Under the surface the land has not learned to be a seabed yet.

This is raw geology, drowned before it could weather. Basalt plains run for hundreds of kilometers without a grain of sand on them, their cooling cracks still sharp enough to cut cable. Canyons that were dry for a week are now black flooded gorges. Spires and mesas that were mountains last season stand as islands now, their flanks unscarred by surf, no tide-line, no barnacle, no smoothing. Every edge is new. When the uplift shifts — and it shifts weekly, groaning up through the water in slow, plate-wide shrugs — new rock breaks the surface already steaming in the cold rain, glassy and knife-edged, and for a few days it is the only solid thing in five hundred kilometers of moving water.

Because it is empty. That is the hardest part to hold in mind about the scale of it. The sea is vast enough to have its own internal weather, its own gyres that behave like seasons, its own hurricanes that are born in the middle of the water and die there without ever finding land — and in all of that volume there is almost nothing. You can travel for weeks over swells the size of valleys and see no island, no mat of weed, no wreck, no buoy, no structure. No birds. Not one. No fish breaking. No insects in the rain. The horizon closes and opens and closes, and between one horizon and the next there is water, and weather, and the sound.

It is loud in a way that has nothing to do with life.

Rain drives so hard on the swell-tops that the whole surface hisses. Rivers are still falling into the basin from the higher rim, white cataracts dropping off basalt shelves straight into the sea with a roar you feel in your teeth a kilometer away. Where warm new rock meets cold water there are perpetual booming collapses underwater, pressure cracks traveling for miles. Thunder rolls almost without stopping somewhere, not near, not far, just as a condition of the place. To talk on deck you shout. To think, you go below, and then you hear it through the hull instead: a deep, irregular drumming, as if the ocean were still being hammered into shape from above.

And below the surface, it is not calm. It is turbulent all the way down.

Currents here do not settle. One day a warm, clear stream runs east at three knots a hundred meters down; the next morning it has reversed, gone milky with suspended mineral, and dropped twenty meters. Whirlpools open over new vents and wander. Upwellings lift bottom water so fast that the surface bulges and rains upward in a brief, impossible fountain before it collapses. The nano-machines are part of it — you cannot see them, there is nothing built anywhere to see, but you can feel what they are doing to the water.

That is how the technology works here. Not as towers or stations. As a change in the rules.

The water holds together a little too well. Droplets on a rail stand up tall instead of flattening. A wave crest will keep a hard, almost geometric edge for a second too long before it breaks. Near the plateau rim, where the sea should by any sane physics pour off the edge in the largest waterfall in the world, it does — and then it doesn't. The outflow shears down fifty meters, slows, curves in empty air, and is lifted, steaming, back into itself. An invisible recirculation, continent-wide, patient as gravity. You can put your hand near the boundary field and your skin prickles, your fillings sing, and the rain in front of you falls sideways.

Everywhere, at a scale too small to see and too large to avoid, the water is being edited. Machines in the rain fix nitrogen as it falls. Machines in the upper currents split and recombine, oxygenating, desalting in one province, salting in another, laying down dissolved calcium where the future reefs are supposed to be. They eat raw sunlight and raw rock and excrete readiness. This sea is not an ecosystem yet. It is a recipe still being mixed.

So the vegetation, where it exists at all, is lonely and provisional.

Far apart — sometimes a thousand kilometers apart — there are the first plantings. Rafts of engineered reed-kelp anchored to new spires, each one a dark green island no bigger than a town, trembling in the swell. Cold-water moss spreading hand-widths across fresh basalt, almost black until the sun hits it and it flashes emerald. In the shallows over a drowned plain, a sparse forest of translucent stalks, still leafless, swaying in a current, filtering minerals, waiting for a light regime and a salt balance that have not quite stabilized. From the air they look like punctuation. Stray marks on a blank page. Most of the sea has not even that much. Just sterile, fertile water, full of nutrients and empty of mouths.

The sterility has a smell. Rain and hot stone and clean mud. No rot. No brine-and-life smell of a real coast, no guano, no low-tide funk. Nothing has died here in quantity yet, because nothing has lived here in quantity yet. The silence underneath the loudness is biological. You keep expecting, at dusk, some call across the water. It never comes.

At dusk the storms show you how unfinished the physics still is. The engineered fields stitch lightning between cloud and swell in long, branching, too-regular patterns, correcting charge as the weather tries to tear itself apart. Rain falls in one spiral arm of a storm and rises as vapor in the next. A waterspout holds station for an hour, perfectly vertical, drawing a column of sea up into a cloud that is simultaneously feeding the sea back down a kilometer away. Conservation, brutally enforced, beautiful to watch from a very long way off.

Maps are useless after a month. Soundings are suggestions. An island that was a navigation hazard in spring may have subsided and become a reef by summer, or risen and split into an archipelago of steaming black teeth. The sea floor is still deciding what height it wants to be, and the water — patient, planet-scale, held up here by will and mathematics — is deciding what a sea is supposed to taste like, push like, reflect like.

One day there will be coasts. The rain will stop being constant and become merely frequent. The currents will find their habits. The sparse green rafts will seed, spread, tangle, begin to smell like a living shore. Something will eventually be allowed to eat them.

Not yet.

For now it is only this: a cool, loud, empty, half-invented ocean at the top of the world, lit by ordinary noon, falling on itself forever, roaring into its own new rock — vast enough to lose a weather system in, and young enough that the water itself still seems surprised to be here.
