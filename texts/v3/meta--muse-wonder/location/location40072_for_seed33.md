It begins long before you can see it, in the thinning.

You climb for days through country that gets simpler as it rises. First the last gritty pines give up. Then the last grass, then lichen, then even the color goes — red rock to gray rock to a pale, almost white stone that holds no warmth. The air stops carrying things. Smells do not travel. Your own breathing does not travel. At a certain height your footsteps make a clean, close sound against the stone and then unravel, and above that height they make no sound at all unless you press your ear to the ground later, when the sound arrives, small and lost.

And then, where there should only be a summit, there is a city.

It spreads across a roof of the world too broad to be a mountaintop. From its gate you can look across plazas, roof-lines, terraced districts, bell towers, market colonnades, a valley's worth of building, and still not see the far wall. From outside — though no one can quite agree afterward where outside was — it looked no larger than a village perched on a needle of basalt.

The sky above it is not blue. At noon it is a deep, bruised indigo, and the stars do not leave. The sun is small, white, and factual; it gives light but very little heat. Shadows here are long, cool, and precise. They fall across streets at angles that have more to do with the hour inside the buildings than the hour in the sky.

The city is called Vaith, in the valleys. Up here the name loses letters if you say it too often.

It is built — undeniably, laboriously built — and built without any sign of a machine. Dry stone fitted to dry stone so tight a hair cannot enter. Black basalt for foundations. Pumice, light as lung, for upper walls. Roofs of red slate and beaten clay tiles. Timber beams blackened by cold rather than fire, joined with wooden pegs and rope and a gray mortar that is not quite mineral. There are aqueducts, cisterns, bridges, staircases cut with a patience that implies generations. There are no nails. There are no wheels, no hinges of metal, no glass. Doors turn on stone pivots. Windows are shutters, lattices, deep embrasures left open to the thin wind.

And it is empty in a way that makes the building feel more, not less, urban. A street of forty houses will have smoke from none of them, washing from none of them, but every fifth door is swept. A market square big enough for a town has its stalls roofed and ordered in rows, their counters dusted, their measures set out — stone weights in descending sizes — and nothing to sell except a fine, pale grit that collects again within the hour however often it is cleared. Districts continue the pattern: a cistern quarter of domed reservoirs, all dry, their interiors lined with salt flowers and frost; a quarter of dyers where the vats are pits of colored dust — indigo dust, madder dust, ochre — that stains the hands for weeks but washes off instantly in water, if there were water; a quarter of high, narrow houses built around courtyards that exist mostly to hold a single, carefully raked emptiness.

There is no water in Vaith. The wells go down and keep going, and what rises from them is a column of colder air that hurts the teeth. The fountains are carved with fish whose mouths are stopped with stone plugs. In the cisterns, instead of a waterline, there are tide-marks of a different kind: a ring of tiny handprints in dust at varying heights, as if the level of something invisible rose and fell for years. Rain is a rumor from below. Moisture arrives only as rime at night, feathering window ledges and the north sides of statues, and it does not melt in the morning. It sublimates, vanishing straight from crystal to nothing, with a faint ticking you feel in your molars.

Nothing green lives here. What looks at a distance like moss in the joints of walls is a mineral felt, blue-gray, that creeps a finger's width in a century and eats stone so slowly the eating is a kind of repair: it excretes the gray mortar, grain by grain, in its sleep. Gardens are enclosures of sorted gravel, five colors raked into waves around a single uncut boulder. In winter — which is only a longer, darker version of the cool that never leaves — those boulders grow beards of needle ice on their lee sides. The ice sings against itself when the wind finds it, at a pitch most people hear as pressure behind the eyes.

The fauna is rare, and rationed by the altitude. Height-hares, the size of cats, the color of the white stone, with ears so thin the light goes through them pink; they eat the mineral felt and are eaten, occasionally, by the silence. That is how the locals would have put it, if there were locals. Their predators are never seen in the act. You find a hare-shaped scatter of fur beside a wall and no tracks. Moths come up from nowhere on still evenings, large, dust-colored, winged less like insects than like flakes of mica that have learned a rhythm; they gather around unlit windows and tap to be let in. In the wall cavities live choir mice. They do not squeak. A whole colony singing together — and they do, at certain seasons, for reasons that may be mating or weather — produces only a sympathetic vibration in hollow objects nearby: a bowl left on a table hums, faintly, in harmony.

Because it is silent here, you notice how much is moving.

Vaith is not still. The wind works constantly, but a wind with almost no substance — it moves dust in spirals, worries shutters, finds the Aeolian cracks built deliberately into tower walls and worries from them a tone too low for hearing. Heat, such as it is, leaves the stone at dusk and the whole city ticks and contracts, doors easing open by themselves on their pivots, stair treads settling. On some nights whole streets are found in the morning to have exchanged a house: a pumice facade that stood by the cistern steps now stands by the dyers' pits, its door still warm — no, not warm, *less cold* — its interior swept, its hearth laid with unburnt kindling. No rubble marks the change. No one hears it happen. Dust-falls slide soundlessly down the outer slopes in broad, slow fans, and occasionally a slide goes the other way, grit creeping up a scree slope grain by grain over an afternoon to rejoin the city above, as if recalled.

Altitude does not stop at the gate. That is the first rule, and you learn it in your body. Walking a level plaza, your ears will pop. Crossing a room can leave you breathless and faintly elated, as if you have gained a hundred feet. Certain streets are higher than the roof they run beside; you climb three steps to a doorway and look down into a courtyard that is, by every measure of your knees, above you. People who stayed here long — and there are signs everywhere of long, careful habitation: worn thresholds, hand-polished rails, niches for lamps worn smooth — people here must have learned to route their days by breath rather than distance. The shortest way was the lowest way. A shortcut uphill could cost you a nosebleed and a memory.

For above a certain interior height, memories begin to misbehave in predictable ways. You forget the most recent thing first, as on any mountain, but here the forgetting has an address. Leave a word in a high room — say the name of the street you came by — and you may find it there when you return, not remembered but present, a faint thickening in the air in the shape of an interrupted exhalation. The upper stories of the tallest houses were left, in the end, entirely to storage: not goods, but pauses, unfinished sentences, the ends of songs. Opening a shutter in one of those rooms can release a smell that has not existed at this altitude for centuries — woodsmoke, river mud, a woman's hair after rain — which pours downhill, invisible and devastating, and is gone.

Geometry follows altitude, politely, and then not at all. All staircases in Vaith go up, including the ones descending to cellars. This is not a trick of perspective; your calves know. To get below a house you climb until the climbing folds and you are, with a lurch in the inner ear, under the floor you started on, looking up at a ceiling hung with roots of stone that grow down toward a nourishment no one has named. Two travelers walking the same street in opposite directions will pass different buildings and can both, afterward, draw accurate maps that do not agree. The far wall recedes in proportion to your certainty about reaching it. The gate, however, is always easy to find and always nearby, which after the second day stops being reassuring.

Shadows detach first. Yours will keep up for most of the morning. By afternoon, in the long dim light, it may be half a street behind, flattened against a wall, resting. It will catch up at dark, when there is nothing to cast it, and you will feel the small cold weight of its return across your shoulders. The towers' shadows do the same at civic scale, sweeping the plazas on their own schedule, and it is considered — again, by a citizenry attested to only by maintenance — bad luck and bad manners to stand in a tower's shadow when the tower itself is in sun. What stands in it is not you, exactly, for the duration.

Time is built, too, and layered like the districts. Bells hang in the towers, wooden bells, stone bells, one great bell of compressed ash that could not make a sound in this air if it were struck, and it is never struck; their silence is rung, at intervals, by the opening of shutters all over a quarter at once, so the hour arrives as a change in the light through lattices. Market day returns every nine days and also, in the high market, continuously: you can enter the high market on a morning when the stalls are only dust and stone weights, and find the air above the counters dense with the gestures of commerce, transactions proceeding at the level of elbows and the brief eclipse of one body passing another, and leave with your arms aching from carrying nothing home.

Nothing prevents you from staying. The houses are sound. Kindling is laid. Stone beds are carved with a hollow for the hip, and the mineral felt, harvested and dried, makes a stuffing that holds the shape of the last sleeper for a long while before very slowly taking yours. There are instructions, of a kind, cut into lintels all over the city, in a script that reads most clearly when you are out of breath: *Sleep with the mouth closed. Weight every door you love. Do not bring water up; it will try to return faster than you can. If a street offers you downhill, thank it and stay. If you meet yourself on a staircase, let yourself pass — you are higher than you think.*

At night the stars are large and unblinking and nearer than the far wall. The rime feathers grow. The choir mice set the bowls humming in empty kitchens. Somewhere a shutter opens, somewhere a house is preparing to be elsewhere, and the whole high city, dry and cool and silently laboring at its own continuance, gains another few feet it will never admit to — rising, as it has always risen, out of the top of the world, into whatever the world has a top *against*.
