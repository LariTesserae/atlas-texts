You hear it long before you can place it, and then you never stop hearing it.

Coming up the long grade from the lowlands — a single gravel road, switchbacking through nothing — the sound starts as a pressure in the teeth. Not one sound. A river in full melt, several rivers, a waterfall somewhere off to the east throwing itself over a lip of new rock, wind working across a high open plateau, and underneath it all a low industrial hum that you feel through the soles of your boots when you stop. By the time you reach the top you are shouting to a person standing a meter away. By the second day you stop trying. People here point, gesture, write on fogged whiteboards, wear ear defenders around their necks as casually as sunglasses.

Sunglasses are not optional either. This is high country, cool and thin-aired and almost painfully bright. At this elevation the sun does not so much warm as expose — 8, 10 degrees in the middle of a summer afternoon if you're out of the wind, cold on your ears and hands, blinding on every wet surface. And everything is wet. The light comes back up at you off a thousand shallow pools, off saturated moss, off braided streams spread like hammered tin across the flats, off the pale sky itself. In June it never really leaves. Midnight is just a thinner, bluer version of noon.

The plateau opens slowly, which is the scale of it — not a single vista but a whole inland region unrolling feature by feature. Ten, fifteen kilometers of rolling high ground between two distant icecaps that sit white and flat on the horizon like more sky. No trees. No tree could. No fences except where the power company has fenced an intake. Between the scattered buildings — and they are very scattered — there is mostly emptiness.

Except the ground is not empty at all.

The ground is entirely alive. That is the first rule you have to relearn. There is no soil to step on, no bare earth in the ordinary sense. From the road edge to the foot of the youngest black lava ridges, the land is sealed in a continuous plant skin, a few centimeters to half a meter thick, spongy and soaked through. Woolly moss in deep emerald and olive, sedge in the wetter hollows going yellow-green, tiny arctic willow flattened by wind into mats no higher than your ankle, cushion plants beaded with white flowers, cotton grass nodding in the bog pools. Even the rocks wear it. Even the low concrete weirs wear it where the spray reaches. Step off the boardwalk and your boot sinks, squeezes out clear brown water, and leaves a footprint that will still be legible next summer. In places, they say, ten summers from now. The moss grows that slowly — millimeters a year — though the water moving through it never stops.

This is young land, barely a landscape yet. The ice left it late, and in some valleys the volcanoes have re-paved it more recently than that. The bedrock is raw: black basalt with its columns still sharp, pale rhyolite stained orange around hot springs, rubble that hasn't had time to round into hills. Because of that the water cannot decide where to go. The rivers have not cut canyons; they wander, braid, reunite, spill sideways into marsh, vanish into moss and emerge twenty meters on as a spring line weeping from under a lava front. Every depression is a pond. Every slope weeps. After rain — and rain blows through two or three times a day, sideways, brief, glittering in the sun even as it falls — the entire plateau seems to exhale mist, and the metal handrails and turbine housings condense and drip as if sweating in the cold.

The marsh-edge is everywhere and nowhere. You are always at the edge of standing water. Bog pools mirror the sky so perfectly that walking the duckboards feels like walking between two lights. The water is cold enough to ache, stained tea-color in the peatless pools, milky blue-grey with rock flour near the glacial streams. You can hear it trickling under the moss even where you can't see it, a second, intimate sound inside the larger roar, if you kneel — which you don't, because kneeling would crush a decade of growth.

The industry is what brought the road, and the reason anyone lives up here year-round, but it does not look like a town. It looks like a few strong objects set very far apart in the wet green. At the center of the plateau, half-buried to escape the wind, is the power station: a long, low hall of corrugated aluminum and concrete, its roof already freckled with moss at the edges, its doors painted international orange. Inside, the turbines make conversation impossible and the floor vibrates pleasantly through your knees; outside, the tailrace throws a permanent white plume and a permanent rainbow where it rejoins the river. From it, a line of pylons marches south, wires singing deafeningly in the wind when you stand under them, each tower standing on a gravel pad that the moss is patiently, inevitably trying to recolonize. 

The rest is smaller: an intake dam no higher than a house, holding back a reservoir the color of milky jade; gauging stations like metal cupboards on stilts in the streams; a warden's hut with a turf-thick roof and a fuel tank and a flag that never stops snapping; a bridge over the main fall where the spray has made its own extra-lush garden of moss on both railings and the warning sign — EAR PROTECTION, SLIPPERY, NO STOPPING — is already spotted with lichen-colored rust. Workers live in a row of cabins by the station for two-week rotations, their drying room full of steaming socks and boots, their windows running with condensation every morning. Everything manufactured up here is either galvanized, anodized, or slowly being taken back by green.

Animals have mostly opted out. That is not a tragedy, it's just the terms. A pair of ravens works the power station roof and shouts, hoarsely amplified, into the turbine roar. Ptarmigan in mottled summer plumage flatten themselves in the sedge and hope you are as unobservant as you are loud — you won't hear them go. Whooper swans sometimes stand in the far bog pools, white and improbable, utterly silent at any distance you can manage. In a warm hollow, midges find you for an hour. An arctic fox was seen last August. The sheep belong to the farms a thousand meters below and only occasionally, bewilderingly, appear up here as small wet flecks on the horizon. Life, here, put almost all its effort into the plants and the water and left the rest as visitors.

The etiquette follows from that. Stay on the gravel, stay on the boardwalk. Fill your bottle from the tap in the hut, not the stream below the station. Shout, or don't talk. Wear the dark glasses even when it's cloudy, because the cloud is bright too. Expect to be wet from the knees down no matter what your boots promise. Expect the river to be louder and higher at four in the afternoon, when the day's melt arrives from the icecaps, than it was at dawn — the one reliable daily event in a place that otherwise changes at a geological pace.

Because for all that noise, all that moving water, nothing up here is in a hurry. The moss thickens imperceptibly. The new lava fields get, decade by decade, a deeper green. The braided channels shift a braid at a time. The reservoir silts grain by grain. The turbines turn and turn at exactly the speed they were set to. You come over the grade shouting against the wind, dazzled, your ears ringing, and the plateau is simply continuing — bright, cold, soaking, deafening, alive from edge to edge in a layer you could hold in your hand, and mostly, magnificently, unoccupied beyond that.
