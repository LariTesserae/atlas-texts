It is a bowl, high up, no larger than a grove.

You find it through a cleft in the ridge where the rock has worn into two smooth shoulders, just wide enough to turn sideways and pass. Beyond that the ground opens and drops — not far, perhaps twenty paces across, thirty from rim to back wall — and then stops again at stone. A hanging shelf. Below the lip, on the outside, the mountain falls away in warm haze and cloud moves slowly underneath you, snagging on other ridges. Above, the sky is ordinary and bright. For a few hours a day the sun looks straight in.

At first it seems empty. That is the density of it: not barren, but spaced.

There are six tall plants, no more. They might be tree ferns, or something that arrived at the same answer. Each has a single black trunk, wet to the touch, furred at its base with moss, and then an extravagant crown — fronds six feet long, deep green, beaded, arching out and drooping at the tips as if exhausted by their own lushness. They stand so far apart that their crowns do not touch. Between them the floor is visible: dark peat, slick basalt worn into shallow bowls and channels, pools of clear water an inch deep, mats of liverwort the color of bottle glass.

What grows low grows large. A single leaf can cover your forearm. Ferns unroll as big as hands. Orchids cling to the trunks in clumps of wax-white and bruised purple, their roots exposed, drinking from the air. Moss is everywhere the water goes, thick enough to take the print of a foot and hold it, slowly, for minutes before it swells back. But there is always stone showing through, always air between one living thing and the next. A cathedral arrangement, pillars and clear floor.

The back wall weeps.

It is not a cliff so much as a curve, the inside of the bowl rising up smooth and stained — rust, black, pale green where minerals have dried in fans. From a hundred places water emerges without any visible source, condenses and gathers and runs. Near the top it is just a darkening, a shine. Lower down it has organized itself over a very long time into threads, then into thin curtains that slide over the rock without breaking. The channels they follow are polished. In some the stone has been eaten into flutes so regular they look carved, but no one carved them. They are the result of the same water taking the same path for longer than the plants have existed.

Behind that curtain, somewhere in the rock, there is a hollow.

You learn this with your ears before you understand it. The bowl is loud.

It is not loud outside the cleft. The approach is wind and distant birds and your own breathing. Inside, the falling water fills the whole space with a continuous roar, pitched low and high at once — the sheet hiss of the curtains, the tick and plink of drops falling from the fern tips, and under it a booming resonance that comes back at you from the hidden chamber every time a larger drop strikes the pool at the base. Conversation at the center is possible only close up, mouth to ear. At the rim, by some trick of the curve, it falls almost silent again. Two steps out and you hear it as if through a door closed behind you.

The central pool is oval, black, still at the edges, dimpled constantly in the middle where the main curtain lands. The water should ripple outward and be done. Instead the rings move very slowly, as if traveling through something thicker than water, and they keep their shape all the way to the stone margin before vanishing. Sometimes a ring begins in the center with no drop you can see arrive — a soft *thoom* from inside the rock, a moment later, then the surface lifts.

Nothing here hurries except the sound.

The air is warm and saturated. Your clothes become damp without rain. Condensation forms on the undersides of leaves and falls in fat, irregular drops, minutes apart, loud enough to startle. Mist does not roll in; it is manufactured locally, breath by breath, where sun hits the wet wall and turns the seep to steam that rises a few feet, cools against fern fronds, and rains gently back down onto the same plants that fed it. A closed system of weeping and returning.

By midday it is lush to the point of gleaming. Every surface has a skin of water. Snails the size of thumbnails, almost transparent, travel the trunks leaving silver that dries to nothing. But animals are rare. You may wait an afternoon and see one sunbird, iridescent and furious, visit one orchid and go. Or a frog, mottled green-black, sitting motionless in a rock mortar of water under a fern, blinking once an hour. It does not call. It doesn't need to; any small sound it made would be found and magnified and carried around the bowl until it belonged to the place, not to it. Life here keeps quiet and keeps apart.

The strangeness is not dramatic. It accumulates.

The big leaves turn, over the course of a morning, not toward the sun that moves across the opening, but toward the loudest part of the waterfall. When a new thread of water finds a new flute in the rock and the pitch of the wall shifts, the new fronds unrolling that week come up angled subtly toward it. The pool reflects the sky correctly, but it also reflects the underside of the fern crowns twice — once faintly, where you would expect, and once again, displaced, as if there were a second, stiller pool just beneath the first. Look long enough and your own voice comes back to you from the back wall, not as an echo, but softened and slowed, your last word arriving after you have already started listening for it.

On the floor near the entrance, half sunk in peat, there is one made thing. A hammerstone, fit to a palm, its end battered white, and beside it a natural hollow in the basalt where generations of hands — it must have been hands — ground something wet into paste. Seeds, perhaps. Medicinal leaves. No hut, no wall, no fire scar, no path beyond the cleft worn smooth by passing bodies. Whoever came here came with almost nothing and left almost nothing and did not stay. It seems right for the scale of it. You do not build in a room that is already listening this hard.

Old is the other feeling. Established. The lichen on the rim grows in concentric maps, orange and grey-green, each ring a slow century. The trunks are scarred at the same height by an ancient rockfall that must have thundered in the bowl unimaginably, and they have grown around the bruise without closing it. The water channels are deep enough to lay a finger in. Nothing is being violently made or unmade. The mountain has finished this corner of itself and settled into maintaining it.

If you stay — and the place encourages staying, there are dryish ledges at the edge where the roar becomes a purr in your sternum — time dilates to match the dripping. A fern frond that was furled at noon is furled a fraction less at dusk. The mist thickens enough to soften the plants into mere verticals, then thins. The pool's slow rings keep crossing each other without interference, passing through as if each occupied its own layer. At dusk the sunbird may come back, or it may not, and the frog may descend into its mortar until only its eyes break the surface.

You leave by turning sideways again in the cleft. Three paces out, the loudness is gone, cut clean. Below you the clouds continue under the ridge, and the warm wind is ordinary wind, and the enormous quiet of the high country resumes. Behind you, unseen, the bowl goes on collecting itself drop by drop, leaf by patient leaf, sounding itself forever into the hollow rock.
