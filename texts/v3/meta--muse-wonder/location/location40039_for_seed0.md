It is dark at noon here, and it has been dark, in the practical sense, for as long as anything has lived in the valley.

Not night-dark above. If you could climb — hand over slick hand, through forty meters of layered leaf — you would come out blinking into a hard, warm sky. But almost nothing lives up there except the briefest fliers. The life is below, under three canopies stacked wrong on top of each other, in a green twilight so complete that color has given up on brightness and gone instead for saturation. Leaves are black-green, bottle-green, green so deep it looks wet even when it is only warm. Their undersides flash maroon and copper when the wind turns them. Flowers do not waste themselves on pale. They are waxy red, bruised purple, a yellow so thick it seems to make its own small light. Which, some of them, almost do.

This is a low place, a long crack-valley running perhaps twelve kilometers between fresh ridges, and it is young in a way you can feel in your shins. The rock has not been rounded yet. Basalt and pale limestone shoulder up through the soil in teeth and blades, unweathered, edges that will take skin cleanly. New spines split the forest floor where the earth has recently shrugged. Fallen trunks do not lie across stone so much as get impaled on it. In places the valley wall is still a scree of yesterday — sharp gravel, bright fracture faces, no lichen — and small stones tick down it all day, set loose by heat, by roots, by the constant trembling passage of animals.

Because there are animals. That is the first rule, and the second, and it outranks the darkness.

Overwhelming is too passive a word. The valley is *occupied*, at every height and in every interval. The air between trunks is not empty; it is trafficked. Beetles the size of thumbs blunder from leaf to leaf with their wing cases open. Moths, dusted and blind-looking, crowd any paler surface. Ants make living roads up bark and across gaps on bridges of their own bodies, and where an ant road crosses a slug trail, there is a brief, silent war. Frogs — hundreds of kinds, or one kind in hundreds of tempers — live not in ponds, for there is surprisingly little standing water, but in the cups of bromeliads, in curled leaves, in the inch of rain held by a split seed pod. They are green and glassy and orange-toed and they call from inside the plants, so the plants themselves seem to be shouting.

Above them: lizards that bark, geckos that click, snakes that go through the leaf litter making a sound like poured sand. Monkeys, or things doing the work of monkeys, crash in troops through the mid-canopy and never fully land. Bats pour through the trunk layer at dusk in a river that lasts twenty minutes and bends around trees without thinning. Birds you will never properly see announce themselves as disembodied whistles, booms, maniacal laughter. Something large and hoofed and mostly nocturnal pushes through the understory at night, rooting up tubers, pursued closely by something else large that prefers to wait in absolute stillness by game trails, breathing through its mouth.

And under it all, the insects make the weather.

The sound is deafening, not as a metaphor. Conversation, if you attempted it, would be a physical effort, like talking beside a mill. It comes in strata. At the bottom, a continuous sizzling rain-stick hiss of cicadas and crickets so dense it stops registering as individual calls and becomes a texture of the air, a pressure on the eardrums. Above that, the frogs pulse — the whole valley inhales and then, on some signal you cannot hear because it is underneath the noise, erupts. Above that, periodic and shocking, the howler troops and the great night birds let off territorial roars that shake condensation out of the leaves overhead. You feel those in your sternum.

Then there are the incidental sounds, which are constant: fruit hitting the floor like dropped tools. Nuts cracking. Branches giving way under monkey weight and crashing down through three stories of growth, startling a spiral of birds who startle a different troop who set off a different alarm. Water dripping. Always water dripping, though it may not have rained for days.

That is the water economy of this place:  damp, not drowned. There is no river on the valley floor, only a chain of seep-holes where a fresh fault squeezes out a clear, mineral-tasting bead that fills, spills a handspan, and sinks again. After a true storm — and storms do roll over the ridge and dump themselves without ever really reaching the ground as rain — everything runs for an hour in silver threads down bark, and then is stored. Stored in tank plants. Stored in bamboo-like stems that knock hollow when animals bite them. Stored as fog, which the warm valley manufactures nightly when cooler air slides off the new ridges and meets the breath of all that vegetation. By morning every leaf is beaded, every spiderweb is a necklace, your hair and skin and clothes, if you had them, would be soaking without a drop having fallen. By mid-afternoon the floor is only humid, treacherously slick, breathing steam in the rare shafts where light does get through in a solid, dusty column and dies before it can be useful.

The plants have adapted not to light but to the traffic. Many of them need to be hit to reproduce. Seed pods that split only when a howl hits a certain frequency, or when a monkey troop passes and shakes the branch in rhythm. Vines that grow toward vibration and wrap the warmest trunk. Huge, soft, dark leaves positioned to catch drips and funnel them to a central throat where frogs live and fertilize them with their droppings. Trees whose bark peels in musical strips, so the wind and the passing animals keep up a dry rattling that warns the root-feeders off. There is a fungus — pale, almost luminous in the permanent gloom, though never quite enough to read by — that fruits explosively after the bat river passes, throwing spores into the disturbed air: white dust, a brief snow in the dark, smelling of yeast and old blood.

Turbulence is not an event here. It is the baseline state, and calm is the anomaly that frightens everything. The valley cycles through feeding frenzies that move like weather fronts. A mast tree will, without seasonal warning, ripen all at once in the dark, and for two days that hectare becomes a brawl: parrots and toucans screaming down, pigs underneath, monkeys fighting in the branches, wasps drunk on split fruit, the floor carpeted with pulp and opportunistic seedlings and bright-winged butterflies lapping at rot. Predators drift to the edges of it and take the distracted. Then the tree is bare and the front moves on.

If the chorus ever stutters — and it does, a half-second hitch moving outward — every small thing freezes. That silence means something large is moving that does not call. It is the only real darkness in a place already dark.

There are no structures. None. That is strange given how clever the hands are here. In the clearings where the sharp rock comes through in anvils, a tribe of heavy-browed, black-handed monkeys systematically carries nuts and river-stones up from the seeps, seats the nut, and hammers. The stones are used, worn smooth in the palm, and dropped. Young ones learn by sitting shoulder to shoulder and stealing the cracked halves. Elsewhere a glossy crow-like bird fishes termites with a thorn held crosswise, discarding it the moment the colony soldiers swarm. Leaf-cups are folded, drunk from, let fall. A small striped mammal stacks nothing, but will wedge a stone to lever bark, then leave both stone and bark behind.

No nests persist; the monkeys sleep in a new crook every night, the birds roost in whatever dense tangle stops the rain-drip, eggs are laid in warm leaf-mold and guarded by a body, not a building. Anything built would be eaten, rotted, or shaken apart inside a week. The tools are real but they do not accumulate. Knowledge lives only in hands and watching eyes, passed troop to troop. When an old hammer-monkey dies, one particular stone might be picked up for years afterward by others who saw her use it, and then one rainy season it sinks into the mold and is gone without consequence.

What holds it all together is not permanence but replacement speed. The soil on the young rock is thin and absurdly rich, full of fresh minerals, and in this warm damp dark things grow at a rate that feels indecent. A fallen giant is outline within a month, a soft green hill of ferns and fungi within three. Saplings race each other for the brief wounds in the canopy with stems you can almost see lengthening on a humid morning. Broken limestone gets rooted in, split, and toppled by figs. The valley is still being made — small quakes rearrange the seeps, a ridge face slumps and opens a new sun wound and a hundred species pivot to exploit it before it closes — and the life prefers it that way. It is not a finished forest. It is a construction site where everything is also the crew, tearing down while building up, eating the scaffolding.

Go in and you would not last by being quiet, or still, or careful in the usual senses. You would last, briefly, by being loud in the right register and wet and moving with a front instead of against it; by drinking only from leaves that frogs refuse; by never trusting an apparently empty patch of floor, because emptiness here is not absence, it is preparation. By learning that dark does not mean cool, that dry does not exist, that a stone might be a tool, a weapon, an egg case, or asleep.

Above all you would learn to listen through the deafening until it stopped being noise and became a map: water ahead where the frog pitch drops, a fruit fall coming where the monkeys go suddenly courteous to one another, danger where the map tears.

And still, under the map, under the sizzle and the roar and drip, there is that newer sound the valley keeps adding — rock ticking as it cools at night, roots prying a fresh crack a fraction wider, the earth not yet decided what shape this place will finally take, while everything living shouts on top of it as if volume alone could hold it together.
