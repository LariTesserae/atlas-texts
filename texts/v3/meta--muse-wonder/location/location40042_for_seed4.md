It begins as nothing on the surface.

A low marsh in the cold, flattened under its own wet. Sedge and bog-cotton laid down in brown mats, pools skinned with new ice, hummocks of moss just high enough to keep above the water. No trees standing. Only roots, and below them more roots, going down into black peat that has never quite become soil.

You would walk past the place. You would walk *over* it.

Because the city is under the mat.

Not buried, exactly. Held. In late autumn the water rose just enough to lift a raft of living peat — sedge rhizome, moss, tangled root, last summer's stems woven through this summer's — and before it could settle again, cold came and locked the edges down with ice. Underneath, a pocket stayed open. A single low room, no wider than a small bedroom, no higher in most places than your knee if you could get yourself in there on your belly.

You can't stand. That is the first rule. The second is that you have to go in blind.

### The Room

The entrance is a slit where the mat tore from a hummock, sharp with new roots, pale and unsmoothed, still smelling green where they snapped. Peat water weeps through it in a slow, black trickle. Inside, the air changes at once: still cold enough to make your teeth ache and your breath fog and vanish, but not freezing. One or two degrees of borrowed warmth. It comes from below — from mud, from composting plant matter, from bodies — and it condenses everywhere. The ceiling drips without rain. The walls sweat.

The ceiling is the underside of the world. An inverted meadow. Root hairs hang down in fine curtains, white tips still growing, searching. Thicker roots cross like beams, like rafters, like the ribs of something that swallowed a field. Between them is packed moss, living on the top side, dead and golden and slowly rotting on this side. Everything architectural here has been grown first and used second. There is no stone in the place worth the name. No bare earth that is not already threaded with fiber.

The floor is also plant, but flattened by use. A skin of trampled reed and peat mud, glassy with water, sloping almost imperceptibly toward a rill that cuts the room from end to end. The rill is no wider than two fingers. It runs black and clear by turns, over sand grains and seed husks, under bridges, and disappears through a root sieve at the far end where you can hear it, very faintly, falling farther down. All the drainage in the city — and there is careful drainage — leads to it.

For a long time, after your eyes have given up, you think the place is empty.

It has the sparseness of a town after an evacuation. Lanes kept ostentatiously clear. Wide, muddy plazas with nothing in them. Gaps of dark between one structure and the next, so each stands alone in its own cold puddle of air. You can hear almost nothing. A drip counting itself. A soft creak as ice shifts somewhere far above the mat. The tick of frost working in a root.

Then the fungal seams begin to show. Not light, really. A greener dimness along the wettest roots, a smear of phosphorescence in the grain of rotting reed, enough to make edges separate from other edges. Enough to miss by.

### What Was Built

They built low, because the ceiling is low and because water rises.

Along the rill, on stilts of bundled sedge stem, are the storehouses: round, high-floored, roofed with overlapping moth wings and broad dead leaves sealed with mud. Inside them, sorted with an almost insulting neatness, are seeds. Sedge seed separate from rush seed separate from the fat, pale corms dug from below the floor itself. Some stores are woven baskets. Some are hollowed stems stoppered with chewed pulp. One — the largest — is roofed with a human bottle cap, rust bloomed across its crown, its crimped edge dug into the mud as both cistern and vault. It collects ceiling drip, one bright bead at a time, and does not overflow.

Houses are not houses so much as decisions made in roots. Where a root forks, the space beneath it has been lined with moss felt and dry grass, gnawed smooth. Where the mat hangs lowest, someone has propped it with a chewed stick and excavated a chamber underneath with a domed roof of interlaced fibers. Covered runways connect them, roofed tunnels of bent reed, just high enough for a vole, a shrew, a line of beetles moving single file. The tunnels do not go straight. They kink around wet spots. They have passing places. They have side drains cut at an angle to the rill, tiny canals lined with shell fragments so they will not collapse.

There is a communal hollow inside the thickest root. You can tell it by the polish. Generations — no, not generations, a single season of feet — have worn the entrance lip shining. Inside, nesting material is banked deep: shredded leaf, fur, feather, seed-down, all of it slowly steaming with body heat. Latrines are dug at the cold end of the room, downwind of what little wind finds its way through the entrance slit, and they are regularly capped with fresh peat. Middens of husk and bone and broken snail shell form low, sorted ridges at the city's edge. Nothing is wasted near the water. That too is a rule, followed without signs.

The tools for all this are lying where they were left. Thorn awls. Splinters of bone honed on grit. Freshwater mussel shells with one edge chipped to a scraper. Grass rope, twisted tight enough to cut your skin, tied off with knots that have swollen shut with wet. Drag sleds made from a single curled leaf, its stem still attached for a harness. No wheel. No fire. No metal except the bottle cap and, half-buried in the central plaza like a plow left in a field, a bent safety pin the size of a gate.

At first the sparseness reads as poverty. Then you realize it is spacing. Every structure keeps its distance because the ground moves.

### The Plant Is Not Finished

This place is young. That is written in every sharp, pale, unworn root tip, in the unrotted brightness of last summer's stems still holding up tunnels. The mat above is still growing downward, even in the cold, even in the dark. Roots lengthen by a fraction each day, seeking the warm air rising off the communal hollow. A runway that was clear at dusk will have a new root hair curtain across it by morning. A storehouse stilt will be embraced, then tilted. Doorways slowly close like mouths.

So the city is in continuous, quiet negotiation with its own material. Gnawing is not destruction here, it is maintenance, a kind of civic duty carried on in shifts, always at the edge of hearing. Walls are re-woven. Supports are chewed back. One bridge over the rill — three stems bound with rope and daubed with mud — sags under a new weight of moss and is being rebuilt, stick by stick, two feet to the left, while traffic still crosses the old one. The entire settlement has migrated perhaps a hand's breadth since it was founded in November, away from a seep that opened in the east wall. You can see its old foundations: rings of pressed mud, already going soft, already being reclaimed by white fungal thread.

Nothing here is intended to last past spring. When the ice loosens, the mat will drop or tear, the rill will become a flood, and the room will fill black to the ceiling in an afternoon. Which is why everything portable is bundled. Why the stores sit on stilts but also float. Why there are exit tunnels — three of them, that anyone who lives here seems to know without looking — angling up through the peat toward hummocks that stay above water longest. The city is built to be abandoned correctly.

### The Population

And then, once you have been still long enough that the cold has gone out of your immediate skin and into your deeper bones, the emptiness fails.

The roof of the largest storehouse moves. It is not leaf, it is moths, hundreds of them, wings folded tight, packed shingle-fashion against the drip, their bodies making the insulation. Under a loose flap of bark along the wall, beetles are stacked in a slow, breathing layer, legs tucked, shells beaded with condensation. The wall itself has eyes. You thought the root bark was knotted. The knots blink, or shift, or turn a damp gold eye toward the warmth of you.

Frogs are sealed into the mud floor in individual cells, each in its own scooped hollow lined with slime that has gone glassy with cold. They do not move, but their throats pulse once, and then a long time later, again. Salamanders lie in the wettest runways like discarded ribbons, skin shining. Snails have glued themselves across the mouths of empty houses and become the doors. White springtails cover one cold corner in such numbers that the peat seems frosted, and then the frost flows around a stone. Woodlice commute under everything. Centipedes hunt through the roof beams.

The builders themselves are voles, mostly — blunt-nosed, short-tailed, smelling of earth and green stem — with a smaller, sharper population of shrews who do not so much live in the city as police its edges with hunger. A harvest mouse has taken the top of the bottle-cap cistern as a lookout and woven her nest into the rust. Field mice come down the exit tunnels from the world above with cheekfuls of seed and leave again before the root doors are gnawed back into shape. Nothing large can get in except a weasel, and there is an entire district of false tunnels near the entrance that exists for the weasel, dead-ends that smell richly of mouse and end in cold water.

Fauna is not an addition here. It is the census, the labor force, the livestock, and the weather. The insects farm and are farmed. In a side chamber kept warmer than any other by a banked mass of shredded reed and dung, fungal gardens pale as curd spread across the walls in branching sheets, tended and harvested by beetles, which are in turn harvested, tidily, by the shrews. Vole droppings feed the fungus. Fungus feeds the beetles. The rill carries the excess away and brings in, after every thaw-pulse from above, a new rain of drowned seeds, stunned flies, minute crustaceans flipping in the shallows. Foraging parties time their work to that drip-pulse. You can hear them if you stop trying to hear: a rustle-pause-rustle along a covered runway, claws on reed, a root tapped twice as a signal that needs no voice, because voice carries up through the mat to owls and foxes listening on the ice.

Predation is constant and nearly silent. A centipede takes a springtail swarm one body at a time. A shrew vanishes into a side tunnel and the tunnel, briefly, is a commotion of felt and breath, and then is still, and the shrew comes back alone, licking its needle teeth. Even the plants participate, after a fashion: the root hairs grow fastest toward animal warmth, so a sleeping cluster will wake, hours later, gently netted, and must chew itself free before it can forage, leaving the cut hairs behind as fresh lining.

There are more living individuals in this one low room than on acres of the marsh above. They are just keeping their distance. Territories are maintained by scent posts — tiny, deliberate smears on root posts at every lane crossing — so the city can be full without ever being crowded. Each house, each storehouse, each fungus chamber has its own empty margin of mud around it. The darkness does the rest. You occupy only as much of the place as your whiskers can read. The rest belongs, provisionally, to someone else.

### How Time Goes

There is no day. There is a tightening and a loosening. When wind presses the ice above, the mat flexes down a fraction and every drip changes its rhythm and the rill rises against its banks, and work moves to the high lanes. When the cold deepens beyond a certain point, the whole population draws back into the communal hollow and the fungal chamber and the outer city goes empty and clean and beautiful in the phosphor-dark, lanes swept, stores sealed, bridges lashed down, waiting.

Sleep is not all at once. Neither is waking. The city operates in overlapping small shifts, so that at any moment someone is gnawing a root back, someone is carrying a seed twice her own weight across the bottle-cap plaza, someone is dragging a dead beetle toward the fungus beds, and someone large — by the standards of this place — is motionless in a doorway, listening with her whole skull through the root to the dangerous surface world.

You, being huge and warm and breathing like weather, are a problem the city is politely declining to solve. Runways near you have emptied. Scent posts are being refreshed behind your back. The harvest mouse has left her lookout. If you stayed long enough, a new lane would be chewed to give you a wider margin, and then another, until your presence had been zoned out to the edge.

You can't stay. The air that is warm enough for them is thin with their breathing. Your own heat is already calling roots toward you.

Back out through the slit, the marsh receives you without any sign that it noticed. Ice, sedge, a few black pools holding nothing of the sky. Under your knees, under a hand's thickness of living plant, the lanes fill again. A root taps twice. The drip resumes its count, and the storehouse roofs rearrange their wings against the wet, and the city — young, dark, improvised, and absolutely metropolitan in its intentions — goes on being rebuilt at exactly the speed at which it is being grown over.
