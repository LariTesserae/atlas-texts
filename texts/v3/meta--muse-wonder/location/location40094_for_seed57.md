It begins under a mat of vegetation, in the lowest part of the low ground, where the marsh never quite becomes a pond.

You could walk over it without knowing. Duckweed seals the surface so completely it looks solid — a green floor, cool through your shoes, giving slightly. Where it parts, there is not open water underneath so much as a darkness full of stems. Root-hairs, reed bases, fallen leaves suspended at different depths, all of it packed close, shoulder to shoulder, with no empty water anywhere.

The opening is smaller than it should be. A slit between two stones, a hand's breadth, where last spring's flood cut a fresh channel and something below gave way. The limestone there is still white at the break, sharp enough to catch skin. No rounding yet. The place is new, in geological terms still hurting.

You get in by putting your face down into the cold and turning sideways.

Inside, it is a room.

Not large. Perhaps three paces across if paces meant anything underwater, but you cannot pace it, because you cannot quite stand, and after a moment you are not sure which surface would be standing-on anyway. It is wholly filled. Water to the stone on every side, water through the tangle of plants, water in the lungs if you stay — and you do stay, which is the first wrong thing, because after the initial burn and panic the water begins to give back a thin, mineral breath, cold as well-water, enough to think by, not enough to speak easily. Spoken words leave your mouth as slow silver beads and do not rise. They sink, and lodge in the crevices overhead.

There is almost no light. What light entered with you closes behind the duckweed like a lid being set down. The dark here is not empty. It has a grain to it, a faint resistance against the eyes. And yet things are growing, lushly, impossibly, in it.

Ferns unroll in the water column as if in humid air, their fronds beaded and slow. Moss makes a deep pelt over the stone, black-green, then — only when your hand passes near, disturbing the current — briefly, vividly green, as if it remembers sun at the touch and then forgets. Rootlets from the reeds above have pushed through cracks and not stopped; they braid down, divide, divide again, until the chamber has an interior scaffolding of pale roots, and between the roots hang gardens: algae in long, cold veils, freshwater sponge the colour of mustard left in shadow, filaments of fungus like wet thread, leaves that fell years ago and have not rotted, only softened into leather.

Everything is close. Your hair tangles almost at once with something. Your knee finds sponge, your elbow a nest of empty snail shells, your back a curtain of stems. Seeds are stored in every hollow, unsprouted. Silt has settled not only underfoot but along one wall and in a crescent on the ceiling, fine as ash, and in that silt are tiny, distinct footprints going up.

The gravity in here has opinions, and they change by the hour.

At first the silt stays down. Then, with a current so slow you could watch a mote cross the room between one thought and the next, the pull shifts. The ferns lean sideways in unison. A loose pebble detaches from the floor and falls, very patiently, toward the left-hand wall, where it clicks and sticks. The little structures built along that wall now make sense. Someone — or several someones, or something with the patience of roots — has been here before, and has built for more than one down.

There are cairns the height of a thumb, stones balanced on what is now a vertical surface. There is a gate made of two snail shells and a lintel sliver of bone, opening onto smooth stone. There is a ladder of twigs lashed with rotted cord, leaning against nothing, its top buds open underwater into three pale leaves. In another crevice, woven rushes make a small dam across a crack that is not leaking. On a flat slate near your hand, tally marks have been scratched in clusters of seven. There are more tallies than the height of the chamber, counted in any direction, could account for. No metal. No plastic. Nothing made that could not be made by fingers and teeth and time.

Fauna is rare, and therefore enormous in significance when it comes.

A single blind fish, no longer than your little finger, the colour of the water itself, makes a circuit of the room. It passes through the fern garden without bending a frond. It passes through your open hand — not around it; through, with a brief cold pressure, like being remembered by something. Then it resumes. Later, much later, a white isopod walks across the ceiling that was briefly a floor, carrying a green seed larger than itself, and disappears into the roots. Nothing else. But the empty shells suggest more, or suggest that shells arrive here from elsewhere, washed in from ponds that have not existed for a long while.

It is mostly quiet. Quiet in the way that makes your heartbeat impertinent. Occasionally a bubble escapes the deep silt with a soft, thoughtful tick. Occasionally, from far above or far below — direction gives up early in this place — a root creaks as it thickens. Once, while you are there, a rain begins on the marsh. You know because the darkness gets denser and the cool gets cooler and hundreds of tiny impacts arrive all over the inside of the chamber at once, though no rain is getting in.

At the centre there is no centre. That is, the roots all grow toward a point about a body's length from every wall, and stop just short of it, their tips curled. The plants lean there. The silt drifts there when gravity forgets itself entirely. The blind fish's circuit tightens around it on every seventh pass. In that place the water is colder and slightly thicker, and if you put your hand in it you have the acute, embarrassing feeling of interrupting a sleep. Something the size of a fist is there, or the shape of where something the size of a fist was. A seed. A heart. A knot in the world's plumbing. The longer you stay, the less helpful the distinction seems.

Time behaves like the currents: it moves, but barely, and in eddies. Your trapped breath-words are still sinking past the ferns when you have already, somehow, been here long enough for moss to have made a faint green map of your shoulder where it pressed. The flood that made this place is still, in some sense, arriving — white stone still cracking a hair at a time, new root still nosing through, the water still carrying in a suspended load of leaf and seed to pack the last gaps. It is a young place pretending, with its deep growth and its little gates, to be very old, and the pretence is becoming true faster on the inside than out.

Leaving is a matter of finding the slit again, which is now above you, now behind the gate of shells, now apparently under the slate with the tallies, until you stop looking for the way out and look instead for the way that is most densely grown — because the entrance, faithfully, overgrows itself to keep the dark in. You turn sideways. The cold lets go of your lungs with reluctance. Above the duckweed the ordinary marsh resumes, loud by comparison, which is to say a frog clicks once and stops.

Behind you, under the green mat, the room stays full. It does not drain. It does not collapse to the size of the crack that leads to it. Somewhere in its packed, drowned thickets your sunken words are still descending toward a floor that will, by the time they land, have become a wall, settling beside other words, other breaths, keeping them — the way it keeps unsprouted seeds and fallen leaves and rain that never fell here — perfectly, coolly, and for much longer than it should.
