From above, it does not look inhabited at all. It looks like a wound that learned to be green.

The mountain is still young. This whole high shoulder of it rose recently enough — in the slow language of stone — that nothing has had time to soften. The slopes are unworn scree and black, glassy blades of rock, ridges that split in clean teeth against the sky. Clouds move below the summit most afternoons, in a white, silent sea, and above them the light is ordinary and pitiless: clear alpine sun, hard shadows, air so thin that sound seems to lose interest in traveling.

Then you come over the rim, and find the Cup.

It is not large. Three hundred paces, perhaps, from wall to wall — a shallow volcanic bowl tucked into the top of the mountain, sheltered from the high wind. You could cross it in seven minutes if there were a straight way across. There is not. From the rim it reads first as vegetation run riot in a place that has no business supporting it, a dense green filling brimming almost to the lip of the crater, canopy pressed against canopy, roof against roof.

And it is a town. That is the second thing you understand, and it arrives slowly.

There are streets. There are terraces cut in rising rings into the inner slope, and districts, and thresholds, and dense neighborhoods stacked three and four layers high. There are avenues kept clear between walls. There are gathering places where the ground has been pressed flat and covered in pale felted matting, and market-hollows where bright fruits are laid out in rows by color. But no hand here has ever made a tool. Nothing is cut with an edge, nothing is fired, nothing is nailed. The town is grown, chewed, woven, trained, and glued.

The houses are living gourds the size of cottages, their vines guided for generations up frames of bent cane until the fruit-body itself swells and hardens into a chamber. The walls stay alive. In the warm season they thicken almost imperceptibly, putting out a new green skin inside the old one, so that a doorway slowly narrows over the years and must be gnawed wider again by the family that lives there. Roofs are layered palm and oil-leaf, lashed not with rope but with the plant's own climbing tendrils, which continue to creep and tighten. When a roof leaf dies, another unrolls to replace it from beneath, smelling faintly of resin and hot wax.

Between the houses grow the real forest, because the town and the forest are not separate things. Barrel-trunked trees rise out of the lanes, their bark sealing whole cisterns inside their own bodies. Their leaves are thick, dark, and varnished, curled at the edges to hold a spoonful of dew through the morning. Under them: shrub layers, fern layers — though the ferns are dry to the touch, more feather than leaf — and then, at ankle height, a continuous spongy mat of moss and root-hair that covers every path and drinks anything spilled on contact. The lushness is real and it is an act of absolute thrift. There is no river here. There is no well, no pond, no rain worth naming. Outside the rim, the mountain sheds what little moisture touches it and stays bare mineral. Inside the bowl, nothing is allowed to leave.

Water, here, is never a place. It is only ever inside something.

At night the sharp crater rocks cool faster than the living bowl below them, and a thin breath of fog forms against the stone teeth and slides down. The canopy is shaped to catch it — millions of leaves tilted at the same slight angle, hair-fine channels running down petiole and trunk into hidden bladders, stone cups, root mouths. By dawn every cup is licked dry, every bladder fruit plucked before the sun can open it. The inhabitants — and there are so many — carry water in their own bodies, in cheek pouches, in the sealed fruit-skins they trade in the market-hollows, passing a green globe from mouth to mouth with the gravity of coin. A bladder-fruit dropped and split on the matting is not a mess. The mat drinks it in seconds, and later the mat's keeper will chew the swollen moss and squeeze it out again into a cup. Almost nothing the bowl has gathered, in all its generations, has escaped it. The town is a closed mouth.

It is warm in the Cup in a way the altitude should not permit. The dark young rock holds the day's sun and gives it back slowly, and between the deepest houses there are vents in the floor of the crater where the mountain exhales a dry, mineral heat, smelling of sulfur and hot dust. That warmth rises through the matting and makes the under-canopy steamy with breath — the breath of plants, of beasts, of compost — because the bowl traps its own weather the way it traps its own water. Step out through one of the three notches in the rim and the cold thin wind takes you instantly. Step back in and you are in the greenhouse again, coats unnecessary, the air at skin temperature, heavy with green and animal smells: leaf-oil, warm fur, resin, dung drying almost before it falls into odorless pellets, the sweet rot of the deep litter under everything.

And for all this abundance of life, it is silent.

Not empty-silent. Dense-silent, the way a room full of sleepers is silent. The Cup is crowded with fauna in stacked abundance — grazers the size of hares with broad padded feet moving between the houses, harvesting the street-moss and leaving it cropped like lawn; climbers with prehensile tails and furred, gripping palms that run the roof-vines and prune them as they go; gliders that launch from tree to rooftop at dusk on membranes edged in down, arriving without a sound worth the name. Moths as large as hands work the night flowers. Their wings are felted. The flowers open without fragrance, advertising instead in pale ultraviolet faces turned up to them. Burrowers turn the compost under the lanes. Tiny bright lizards police the leaf surfaces. Everything is near everything else, all day, in layered proximity — eaters and eaten, pollinators and parasites, tenants and the living walls that house them, each species doing some small maintenance on the town simply by living in it.

Nothing calls. Nothing sings. Communication here is flash and scent and pressure: a crest raised, a patch of throat fur shown and hidden, a puff of musk released into still air at a doorway, a foot pressed twice into the matting so the vibration travels to a neighbor through the ground. Partly this is the thin mountain air, which rewards quiet. Partly — and the old ones seem to know this in their bodies if not in any story — the rim of the Cup is made of resonant young glass-rock, long unworn blades of it leaning over the town, and there are places where a sharp loud noise at the wrong hour carries a faint answering tone from the stone. So the town has tuned itself down over generations. Even the children of every species are hushed almost from birth, soothed by touch rather than voice. The loudest ordinary sound in the Cup is the slow creak of a gourd-house growing, or, at midday, the soft collective ticking of leaves closing.

Because the whole place runs on that slow rhythm. At full sun the canopy shuts leaf by leaf, presenting its silvered undersides, and the town goes into a green drowse: grazers folded in shade, gliders hanging like fruit, the market-hollows deserted, only the heat vents breathing and the houses imperceptibly swelling. In the long evening everything opens again at once — flowers, doors, bladders, eyes — and for an hour the lanes are dense with silent traffic and exchange, fruit and grooming and mating and repair, all of it conducted in gesture and proximity. Then night, the fog sliding down the rocks, the leaves tilting to receive it, the town drinking in its sleep.

Nothing here changes quickly and nothing here is finished. A street is a route the moss has agreed to for now; in ten years a barrel tree will have leaned it aside. A neighborhood slowly migrates around the bowl as its houses age, harden, hollow, and are finally allowed to collapse into the compost that feeds the next row of seedlings already sprouting in the enriched dark beneath. The rim erodes so slowly that a single rock tooth, undermined, may lean for a century before it falls — and when it does, it will fall without witness if it can, into the muffling green, and be grown over, and become a cool place where dew gathers a little better than before.

From the notch in the rim at dusk, looking back one last time before the cold outside takes you, the Cup appears to be doing nothing at all. Just a small green town in a young stone bowl, high above the clouds, warm, crowded, breathing through its leaves, keeping every drop it has ever owned, in complete and deliberate quiet — growing, at a pace too slow to watch, more and more completely into itself.
