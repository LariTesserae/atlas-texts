You come to it from below, and for most of the climb there seems to be nothing to come to.

The mountain has been worn past being a mountain. That is the first thing. It is high — high enough that your breath shortens and the light turns thin and clean and merciless, high enough that clouds travel *beneath* the upper slopes in slow herds — but it has no peak. Erosion finished its work here a very long time ago and then kept going out of habit. The ridges are rounded down to shoulders. The crags are rounded down to knuckles. Every stone is smoothed on its exposed side until it has the soft, blind look of something handled for ten thousand years. What should be a summit is a shallow bowl, perhaps three hundred paces across, with a lip of stone all the way around it.

Outside the lip, the world is almost empty. Grey-pink rock. Scree that has settled and will not slide again. A few black lichens. Wind.

Inside the lip, someone built a town.

It is small enough to take in all at once — forty, fifty structures, streets, plazas, a kind of dense intention — and it is entirely, echoingly uninhabited. No roofs. No doors. No wood, no iron, no glass, no sign of any tool more complicated than stone set on stone. The walls rise two, sometimes three storeys, built of rounded field boulders fitted so patiently that in places you cannot find the seam, and everything has been left open to the sky for so long that building and geology have begun to trade places. Steps are worn into shallow cups that hold last night's frost until noon. Doorways are worn into ovals. Whole corners have slumped not into rubble but into smooth, grassed-over mounds, as if the houses were melting at geological speed.

And it is green. Impossibly green for this cold.

That is the second surprise, after the height and the emptiness. Inside the bowl, sheltered a fraction from the worst of the weather, every interior is lush. Moss does not creep here, it *piles* — deep emerald cushions a hand's breadth thick, spilling out of windows in frozen cascades, carpeting floors, upholstering the insides of walls while the outsides stay bare stone. Ferns with stiff, dark, cold-hardy fronds grow in ranks along the damp northern faces. Tiny white flowers open in the cracks of the paving for a few hours at midday and close again. Orange and sulphur lichens bloom on the chimneys. Where a roof beam must once have been, there is now only a hanging garden rooting in dust and condensation.

It is never quite wet and never quite dry. Rain rarely falls straight here; water arrives sideways, as cloud. In the late afternoon the fog comes over the eastern lip in a slow white river, pouring between the houses, beading instantly on the cold stone, running down walls in a thousand bright threads. In the morning there is frost, then a brief, glittering melt — every cupped step and hollow lintel dripping at once — then dryness again, the moss exhaling a cold green smell, the stone ticking as it warms. Shallow pools collect in the worn plazas and stand for days, black and perfectly still, filmed at the edges with pink snow-algae. The town's one real stream exists only for an hour after the melt, threading from cistern to cistern down the main street, loud and clear and then gone into the paving.

For the town is built around water the way other towns are built around a market. The houses are inverted, in a sense: their roofs, when they had them, must have funneled inward. Every structure encloses a small cold court with a condensation stone at its center, a standing slab kept permanently colder than the air, sweating drink into a basin. You live, presumably, around your well. You sleep in the damp.

But mostly, and before any of this registers properly, you hear it.

It is loud here. Astonishingly, continuously loud, in a place with almost nothing alive in it.

The bowl is an instrument and the wind is always arriving. It strikes the outer lip and pours in, and the empty town takes it up street by street. Every chimney is a flute stopped with moss to a different depth. Every roofless house is a resonant chamber. Doorways whistle at pitches determined by their width; the narrowest alleys scream; the big gate on the western side produces a note so low you feel it in your molars and in the soles of your feet. The sound does not come from one direction. It layers, it harmonizes and beats against itself, it rises to a roar when a gust crosses the plaza and drops, never to silence, but to a kind of organized moaning when it passes. You have to shout to be heard ten paces away. Your own footsteps come back to you off the far wall a moment late, doubled, as if someone unseen is keeping pace.

And the town is still tuning itself. This is the strange part, the part that becomes clearer the longer you stay. In winter the frost wedges the stones apart by hairbreadths. In spring the moss swells into the new cracks and muffles them. Wind worries a loose lintel for a decade and drops it, and a house changes key. Vegetation is not decoration here; it is the felt in the instrument, the soft material packed into stone throats to lower and darken their voices. As the gardens grow thicker through the brief summer, whole streets go gradually flat. As they die back and freeze hard, the town sharpens again. Old inhabitants — if the word means anything here — would not have needed a calendar. They could have heard the season from their beds.

Fauna fits this economy of scarcity and volume: almost none, and what there is sounds like multitudes. Once a day, perhaps, a white bird crosses the bowl far overhead and does not land. In the cisterns there are small black frogs, rarely seen, who call once at dusk and are answered by their own echoes five, six, seven times around the walls until the whole town seems to be croaking. Something lives in the moss on the high side, a compact furred thing the size of a loaf, which whistles an alarm when your shadow crosses its street and is never visible at all — only the whistle, bouncing, arriving from everywhere, retreating as you advance. You learn quickly that sound here is not evidence of presence. Presence is mostly elsewhere.

The daily cycle drives everything, and it is not gentle. Dawn is the only quiet, a frozen held breath of perhaps twenty minutes when the air in the bowl goes still and every surface is furred with white. Then light — ordinary daylight, but thinned by altitude until shadows have violet edges — strikes the western houses and the thaw begins, dripping, trickling, the small stream waking in the street. By midday the town is at its greenest and most habitable, which is still cold enough to numb your hands in shadow. By afternoon the wind-concert is at full strength and the pools in the plaza are vibrating in standing patterns, rings and lattices that shift with the chords, so that the water appears to be listening. Then the cloud-river comes over the lip, the temperature drops out from under the day, visibility shrinks to one wet wall at a time, and the town vanishes into whiteness and noise. Night locks it in ice. You can lie in any of the moss-floored houses — the cushions are deep and dry on top, saturated beneath, faintly warm compared to the air — and listen to the stone contracting, small sharp reports like distant doors closing, one by one, all night, in no pattern.

There are rules, of a sort, though no one is left to state them. You do not build above the lip, or the wind takes your voice entirely. You do not clear the moss, or your house will howl. You keep to the main streets in the afternoon unless you want your ears ringing until morning. You drink in the morning, from what the night condensed, because by evening every basin belongs to the frogs and the fog. You orient yourself not by sight — sight fails daily — but by pitch: the low gate, the screaming alley, the particular chord of your own courtyard, learned the way you learn a face.

It does not feel abandoned, exactly. Abandonment implies a suddenness. This feels *completed*. The mountain was worn down to a bowl, the bowl was filled with tuned stone, the stone was filled with moss and water and sound, and each process is still faithfully carrying on without requiring anyone to witness it. The town goes on being built by frost and unbuilt by wind. The gardens go on packing and unpacking its throats. The fog comes in over the lip each evening whether or not there is a street for it to pour down.

Standing in the central plaza at noon, shouting just to hear yourself come back, you have the sense that people were never really the point of the place. They were a temporary, soft arrangement the bowl made use of — hands to stack the stones to the right heights, ears to know when the heights were right — and that having done that work, they could leave, and the cold, green, deafening town would continue its one long performance for the clouds passing underneath it.
