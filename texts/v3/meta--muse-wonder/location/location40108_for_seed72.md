You have to go down to it.

From the plain above it doesn't look like a town at all. Just a fresh wound in pale ground: a rectangular pit cut sixty meters below the surface, still raw at the edges, with a service ramp spiralling down one wall and the tops of vents and water tanks showing below the rim. The rock is newly exposed and unweathered — cream limestone, bands of rusty clay, seams of salt that have not yet had time to dissolve or darken. In places the cut face is still scored with tooth-marks from the excavators. In others it has already begun to weep.

It was dug for a cooling reservoir. The plant came first, then the housing for the people who would run the plant, then more housing when the reservoir was postponed and postponed again. Now the town lives in the bottom of its own unfinished foundation.

The heat arrives before you do. Halfway down the ramp the air stops moving. What wind there is on the plain scrapes across the opening and misses, leaving the pit to hold its own weather. By nine in the morning it is thick, dim, and loud. By noon, for about ninety minutes, the sun finds the angle and drops straight in, and everything — corrugated roofs, concrete block walls, pipework, the shallow sumps at the bottom — flashes white and murderous. The rest of the day the town is in its own shadow, under awnings, under tarps, under the haze of its own steam, lit a sort of permanent late afternoon.

It is a real town, in miniature. Four short streets, laid out on terraces blasted flat into the slope. A market arcade. A clinic, a school with mesh on every window, a power hall, two five-storey workers' blocks painted a dust-pink that is already peeling in the damp. Apartment doors have double doors. Stoops have drains. Every building sits up on a low plinth because the ground is never quite dry.

Water is not abundant here, exactly. It is *near*. It condenses on cold pipes and drips without pause. It beads on the undersides of tanks. It seeps in dark fans through the limestone and makes the lower walls bloom with salt. It stands in the unfinished reservoir bed at the very bottom — not a lake, a chain of warm, green, knee-deep sumps linked by mud and concrete channels, fringed at their edges with reeds. Pumps work constantly to keep those sumps from joining up and taking the lowest street. You can hear them everywhere, a low industrial throb underneath everything else, along with the fans in the power hall, the compressors, the delivery trucks idling because no one turns an engine off in case it won't start again in the heat.

And over the machines, the animals.

The pit is a trap for life in the way it is a trap for heat. Once something finds its way down, it stays, breeds, and prospers. Warm standing water, food waste, shade, cracks in raw rock, endless insects — the town has accidentally built a perfect refuge in the middle of a barren plain where nothing above survives well.

In the morning, bats pour back into the cliff in a thin black smoke and vanish into fissures above the top terrace, and swifts take their place, shrieking between the blocks. Pigeons crowd every ledge not strung with wire. Sparrows live inside the market roof. In the drains, frogs the size of fists sit throat-deep and at dusk begin a chorus so dense it stops being separate calls and becomes a kind of grinding pressure. Mosquitoes rise off the sumps in visible columns where the light catches them. Dragonflies patrol the seeps. Cockroaches own the warm spaces behind electrical panels, and red ants make highways across otherwise empty lots from one scrap of shade to the next. Lizards are on every sunlit wall, geckos on every ceiling at night. Rats use the pipe runs as streets above the human streets. There are stray dogs sleeping in the cool exhaust of the clinic, slow and uninterested in anyone, and feral cats that are officially discouraged and unofficially fed, because without them the rats would be worse.

You learn the courtesies quickly. Shake your shoes. Cover food the second it is set down — not eventually, that second. Never leave a cup of water uncovered. Sweep the threshold at night or find it blocked by beetles by morning. Sleep with plugs in your ears if you can, and with the net tucked in if you can't afford the alternative, which is waking covered in bites with a gecko watching you from the wall.

Vegetation, by contrast, has mostly failed. There is algae, luxuriant in the channels. There are the reeds, and a determined stand of oleander by the clinic that nobody waters and nobody can kill. A few people try tomatoes and peppers under shadecloth on the higher terraces; the plants go leggy and pale and are eaten down to stems overnight. At the rim, planted to hold the edge, a line of young acacias leans away from the pit, sparse and thorny. From below they look like a fringe of hair around a bald spot.

Between buildings there is a lot of nothing. Wide gravel lots where another block was meant to go. A half-built cistern open to the sky, filling slowly with seepage and tadpoles. Scaffolding that has stood so long it has become structural, with swallows nesting in its joints. The town is dense with infrastructure — pipes racked overhead, cables, valves, back-up tanks, culverts — and sparse with people. Three hundred residents, maybe. Everyone knows which pump is knocking, which neighbour works nights, which stairwell currently has wasps.

Nothing changes fast here, despite the noise. The heat lags hours behind the sun; the pit is hottest at seven in the evening, when the rock gives back its day, and coolest just before dawn, though *cool* still means you sweat through a sheet. The sumps fill a centimetre a day and are pumped down a centimetre a night. A new terrace gets cut, a wall gets sealed against seepage, a market stall opens. Then the same work again. The frogs peak and subside. The bats shift from one fissure to another as it silts up. It is all gradual accumulation and gradual wearing-through.

At night the lights come on early in the dimness — yellow work lights over the power hall, blue-white bulbs over porches, the red eyes of pump indicators along the channel — and they attract a fresh shift of moths so thick they soften every beam. The machines keep going. The frogs keep going. Somewhere a child is being told, again, to shut the screen door properly, and it bangs, and for a second the geckos scatter. The air smells of hot iron, wet limestone, diesel, algae, fried onions from the top street.

Above, on the rim, the plain is empty and silent and blinding. Below, in the hole it made for itself, the town goes on being hot, damp, half-finished, and overwhelmingly lived-in — just not primarily by people.
