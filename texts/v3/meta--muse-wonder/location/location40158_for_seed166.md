It is a pit of water sunk below the level of everything around it.

From the lip, standing on wet grass and broken hardcore, you look down rather than out. The surrounding ground — flat, grey, serviceable — ends at a concrete rim perhaps thirty centimetres proud of the earth, and then drops. Four metres down, held in a rectangular basin about fifteen metres long and nine wide, is water so still that the sky in it is not a reflection so much as a second, quieter sky laid flat at the bottom of the world.

Almost everything here is submerged. Only the topmost rungs of things show: the rim, a depth-board bolted to the north wall with its white numerals faded to ghosts, the curved crown of a pipe at the far corner, two or three reed stems in the shallow overflow notch. The rest — walls, floor, machinery, the long history of the place — continues on underneath in cool, greenish clarity.

The water is cold in a way that does not vary. Twelve degrees, perhaps, in summer and winter both, fed and drawn by some distant part of the plant at exactly the same rate, so that the level never rises after rain and never falls in drought. A white mineral tide-mark runs around the concrete at the surface, and the surface returns to it faithfully, day after day, like a needle to a dial. Nothing here floods and nothing dries. Inflow and outflow have long ago agreed with each other.

Light comes straight down. There is no roof, no building over it — only open air above the pit, and a high galvanised fence far enough back to be irrelevant. At midday the basin is plainly, normally lit, and because the water is treated and almost empty of life, you can see all the way to the floor. Sunlight reaches the bottom in soft, shifting nets, then settles on silt the colour of wet ash. The concrete down there has lost its newness decades ago. Its aggregate is exposed, its corners softened, its construction joints darkened into seams. Rust has bloomed below every bolt and then stopped spreading, fixed in long amber streaks. This is not ruin; it is a finished weathering, a place that has arrived at the form it intends to keep.

What is in the water is spaced far apart, with long clear intervals between.

On the south wall, a steel ladder descends and disappears, its lower rungs furred with a thin olive film. On the east, near the floor, is the mouth of the outlet — a dark iron circle behind a grille. On the west, higher up, the inlet pipe enters and admits its water without splash or ripple, already at the basin's own temperature and pressure, as if the liquid were simply thickening out of the wall. Along the bottom, laid in parallel runs and bolted to low concrete plinths, are the black lengths of a heat-exchange array, rubber-sheathed cables, and one brass valve wheel, green at the edges, that no hand has turned in a long time. Nothing moves among them. They are less installed than settled — industrial things that have been under water long enough to belong to the geology of the pit.

And through the concrete, rather than through the air, comes the only sound: a low electrical hum, fifty cycles, transmitted from pumps and transformers somewhere beyond the fence, felt in the teeth if you rest your head against the rim. Under water it would be fuller — a steady, muffled tone with no beginning and no end. Above the surface, the air is mostly quiet. No splash, no drip that is not immediately absorbed, no birdsong over open water. Occasionally a distant fan turns somewhere in the works, even and unhurried, and then the ear gives up distinguishing it from silence.

There is no animal life here at all. That is the first thing that becomes strange, once you have looked long enough. Open water, light, shallow ledges — and yet no pond skaters dimpling the surface, no beetles rowing the margins, no snails grazing the ladder, no frog spawn in spring, no fish shadow crossing the silt. The treatment that keeps the water clear keeps it empty. Copper and cold and whatever quiet chemistry the plant maintains have made an abiotic pond: water in its mineral condition, denied a food web. Even at the surface film, nothing gathers.

Vegetation persists only in the thinnest, most tolerant forms. A translucent film of algae coats the walls down to about a metre, where the light is strongest, then thins to almost nothing. On the bottom, in the brightest patch, three widely separated clumps of stonewort stand in the silt — pale, brittle, calcified little structures more like coral skeletons than plants, unmoving in the stillness. At the overflow corner, where the concrete is broken and soil has washed in, a single sparse tuft of reeds lifts a few stems above the surface, and moss makes a dark green line exactly at the water's edge, as if underlining it. That is all. The rest is bare concrete, bare iron, bare silt.

The air in the pit, a metre above the water, is cooler than the air at ground level and smells of wet stone, faint chlorine, and warm electrical insulation from somewhere far off. Condensation beads the shaded wall in the morning and is still there at noon. Your breath, in winter, would fog and hang. The atmosphere does not circulate so much as rest, pooled in the hollow like the water itself.

The rules of the place are the rules of a system in perfect balance. The depth stays. The temperature stays. The hum stays. Silt that is disturbed — say a stone dropped from the rim — falls in a slow, straight column, spreads its small grey cloud along the bottom, and within the hour the cloud has settled and the water is clear again, the stone half-buried, already beginning to acquire its film. The reeds do not spread. The algae does not thicken. The rust does not advance. Even decay here appears to have finished its work and retired.

It is not abandoned; abandonment would change it. It is maintained, remotely and automatically, in a state indistinguishable from permanence — a small, cold, submerged room at the bottom of the industrial world, holding its clear water up to the light, empty of creatures, sparsely greened, humming very quietly to itself through the walls, going on exactly as it is.
