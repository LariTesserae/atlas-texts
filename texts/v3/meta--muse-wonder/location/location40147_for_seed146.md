You have to go down a long way before you understand that you have not entered a cave.

At first it is a cave. A throat in dry rock, too warm from the start, going down at an angle your knees learn by the second hour. No drip. No cool breath from below the way caves are supposed to breathe. The air coming up is the same temperature as your skin, then warmer, and it smells of iron dust and hot stone and something animal-sweet underneath, like a stable that has never been cleaned and never needed to be.

Your lamp makes a small, very private world. Beyond it, nothing returns. Not black in the way night is black — night has sky in it. This has pressure. The dark pushes close and stays.

After a day of descending, the floor levels. After two, the ceiling you have been following with an upturned light simply is not found again.

That is the first revision. It is not a tunnel system. It is an interior country, left behind when something vast underneath the crust drained away, not long ago, geologically speaking. It still looks made yesterday. The walls where they can still be found are split fresh, unsoftened. Obsidian and basalt break in blades and clean conchoidal fans. Stalactites that would have needed water hang only as suggestions — mineral teeth begun and abandoned, because there was never water here to finish them. Nothing has been worn round. Nothing has had time, or weather, to learn how to be old.

And then it stopped. That is the second, stranger revision. It stopped changing at all.

### A country under a roof

Walk for weeks and the temperature does not vary. Not by altitude, not by time — there is no time-mark here, no day, no season, no cooling at rest. A steady, bodily warmth, perhaps thirty-eight degrees, everywhere, in the stones, in the air, in the dust that gets into your teeth. Sweat does not evaporate well. It salts your lips and dries to a faint crust. You do not find water. Once, perhaps, in twenty days of walking, a sealed pocket in a salt pan will weep a few spoonfuls of bitter brine if you crack it, black and stinking of sulfur, and the local life will be waiting around the crack before you have finished making it, patient, already there, as if they heard the intention.

There is weather, because a space this large cannot help it — a roof perhaps three, four kilometers overhead in the middle reaches, a floor that falls and rises in basalt swells the size of hill ranges, a horizon your light never reaches. But the weather is fixed in place. Over each great pillar — and the pillars are mountains, leaning columns of fused rock joining floor to roof, some wider at the base than a town — a warm updraft rises forever in exactly the same place. Between them, still basins of heavier air sit year after year, layered, invisible boundaries you can feel on your face as a change in smell, in dust, in the prickle of heat on your eyelids. No storm crosses. No front moves. A thermal drawn on a map a hundred years ago would still be accurate to the step.

Because of that, trails do not fade. That becomes a rule you live by.

In a normally changing place, a path is a rumor. Here, a path is a fact. The soft ash of the floor — dry, grey, knee-deep in the low places, made of pulverized stone and shed skins and droppings dried to powder — holds a footprint with its edges crisp for decades. Scent holds too. Animals navigate by long chemical handrails laid down on stone, glossy with generations of bellies and feet, and those rails lead unerringly to salt licks, to mating flats, to the warm cracks, to the cairns. You can follow a trail that was laid before you were born and find it as legible at the end as at the beginning.

Dead things do not rot in the usual way. There is too little water for putrefaction to get a good hold. They dry from the outside in, tighten, darken, become leather and then a sort of warm parchment. Mostly they are eaten before that can finish — the recycling here is perfect and unhurried — but in the still-air basins you sometimes pass a perfect, curled sleeper by the path, eyes sunken to pits, tongue a strip of jerky, looking not dead so much as stored.

### What grows, that is not growing

There are no plants. Nothing reaches for light because there is essentially none to reach for. A faint ruddy glimmer comes out of certain floor cracks when they are disturbed, a mineral phosphorescence exhaling and dying, less light than the memory of red. Once your eyes have lived here a while you move mostly by heat-shimmer, by touch, by the change in the return of your own breath off near stone.

What covers the stones instead is simpler and older than plants. Felt. In the warmer fissures, bacterial mats spread in mustard, rust, and bone-white sheets, slick at the center, powdery at the edges, smelling of hot pennies. They eat rock. They eat sulfur and iron and the hydrogen that seeps in molecular patience from the basalt. They are the pasture of this place.

Above them, mistaken at first for vegetation, are the crystal gardens. Salt and gypsum and other dry minerals grow out of cracks in forms too regular to be stone and too still to be alive: branching white antlers, needles in dense defensive thickets, translucent leaves that tinkle very faintly if the permanent updraft finds them. They do not grow now. Like everything else, they were formed in the brief, violent youth of the cavern, and then held. A forest you can walk through for an hour, shoulder to shoulder with mineral trees, their tips sharp enough to open a careless forearm without you noticing until the blood has already dried to a black line.

The density is what your body notices before your mind. Not just stone crowded close — in the inhabited belts, life is layered handspan over handspan. The felt is grazed by flat, wide creatures the size of serving trays, limpet-mouthed, moving on a muscular skirt, leaving a polished lane behind them. On their backs ride smaller pickers. Above them, on stilt legs as thin as knitting needles, herds of blind sifters pick through the ash for shed scales, eggs, dead mites, mineral flakes enriched by gut passage. Their legs make no sound on the ash. Their bodies sway above your waist height exactly where your lamp is weakest.

Overhead, where you cannot see, the air is occupied. Soft balloon things ride the fixed thermals in permanent slow circles above the pillars, translucent gut-sacs pulsing, trailing feeding ribbons that comb the rising air for spores and floating young. They never land. They mate, birth, and die aloft; the dried husks spiral down over a period of days, turning, and are eaten on the way by things with wings that are not wings exactly but stiff, dry membranes stretched on finger bones, clicking — no, not clicking. Almost clicking. A dry tongue-against-teeth sound, withheld.

It is abundant here, almost crowded, and it is almost silent. That is the enduring wrong note. A forest at night with this much animal mass should be a noise. Here the multitude has learned quiet as a material. Feet are padded or pointed to go between grains. Calls, where they exist, are so low they travel through stone into the soles of your feet, or so brief they seem like a fault in your hearing. Communication is mostly touch, heat, chemical braid. Many of the larger animals have pits along their flanks that read the infrared shape of the world — they see you, in the absolute dark, as a bright, awkwardly shaped confusion, too hot in some places, covered in dead cloth in others. They part around you without startling. They have seen your shape on the trails for a long time.

The predators, because there are predators, do not chase. They wait in a state so compatible with the stillness of the place that they might be cairns. A thing like a low table of armored segments, dust-colored, breathes once a minute beside a game rail. When a sifter steps within reach, there is a movement too economical to call a strike, and then the same stillness, with one fewer set of needle legs standing in the ash. Nothing screams.

### The builders

Every few days of walking, if you follow the old rails, you find a structure.

They are small and scattered and unmistakably made: a semicircle of flat stones set on edge, waist high, opening away from a thermal boundary; a roof is unnecessary, there is no rain, so none is attempted. Inside, a floor deliberately cleared to the basalt and swept — you can see broom marks fossilized by permanence. A pile of flaked scrapers, obsidian fresh as the day it was struck, the flakes laid by size. Sometimes a grinding hollow worn into a boulder, with mineral felt-cake residue still in it. Sometimes a cairn, taller than the rest, made of stones and skulls in alternation, the skulls all of the same broad, eyeless kind, their eye pits filled carefully with red dust.

Whoever made them used simple metal only rarely — a beaten copper awl, green as a pea, is a treasure left in plain sight and not taken by the next visitor — and mostly worked stone, bone, dried gut, crystal needle. Their tools are left, not lost. Their hearths, if they are hearths, contain no ash and no charcoal, only stones that have been heated and cracked in patterns. Perhaps they cook without fire. Perhaps the hearth is for eggs. Perhaps it is not a hearth.

You will not meet the builders on any schedule. The fauna is abundant; the people — if people is the word — are a thin presence within it, moving in small bands along the scent rails, their skin dust-grey, their heat-pits their own, carrying stone lamps that do not burn but hold a lump of the red mineral that glows when handled, cupped so the light falls only on the hands and the immediate stone. They set up a wall, use it for a generation, and leave the scrapers lined up for whoever comes next, who may be themselves returning after forty years on a circuit so large it functions as a life. Because nothing changes, nothing needs defending, storing, or improving very fast. A wall built by a great-grandmother is not a ruin. It is just a wall, slightly dustier.

They do not speak aloud much, either. When they do, the sound goes into the ash and stops, as if the country had agreed long ago to keep its counsel.

### The rule underneath

The longer you stay, the more the place turns from cave, to country, to something like a held breath.

It is young and it will not age. Its sharpness is not a stage; it is a condition. The updrafts will not shift their pillars. The balloon things will circle the same warm columns their ancestors circled, in the same direction, at the same height, combed by the same rising spores. Maps do not expire here. Graves do not sink. Songs, if they are sung into stone as a vibration, could in principle be found again.

You begin to navigate not by landmarks, which in the dark are all touch and smell anyway, but by successions: after the crystal garden where the needles sound, three hours of ash smelling of iron felt, then the basin where dead things keep, then the pillar where the air climbs and carries your own shed heat away from your face in a way that feels, for a moment, like a breeze, like weather, like a world still deciding — until you remember it decided once, completely, at the beginning, and has been keeping that decision ever since, warmly, darkly, in dense and quiet multitude, under a roof no rain has ever struck.
