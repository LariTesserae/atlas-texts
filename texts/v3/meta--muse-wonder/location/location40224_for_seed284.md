There is only one place on the mountain where you can stand without being taken by the wind, and the city is built there.

From below you would never see it. The peak is young — too young, geologically, to have softened — a black spire of basalt and volcanic glass reared up in a single impatient ridge, its flanks still sharp enough to cut rope, its scree still sliding in dry, whispering sheets years after a fall. Near the very top, where the air has gone thin and dark and the sky at noon is not blue but a deep, bruised indigo, the ridge splits. A fissure. Two hundred meters long, thirty across at its widest, sunk straight down into the rock like a mouth held almost shut.

They roofed the mouth.

Steel ribs arch from lip to lip, bolted into fresh stone. Over them: layered canvas impregnated with graphite and oil, felt, sheet aluminum patched a hundred times. From a distance it is just another shadow in the crag, a blink of a signal lamp, a faint plume of grey dust venting and instantly torn away. From inside, it is a town.

You enter through the Lips, two felt airlocks in sequence at the southern, leeward end. The fans beyond them work constantly, pulling in the outside air — which is too thin to work in, too cold to breathe deeply, and utterly dry — and thickening it just enough, warming it just enough from the waste heat of the machines, that a person can go bare-headed. It is still cool. Breath shows. Fingers go numb on uninsulated metal. Everyone wears wool under everything, and gloves with the fingertips cut out for the fine work.

And it is dim, even at midday. What sun gets in comes through slit windows in the roof and through the translucent, soot-stained panels, falling in pale blades. The rest is lamplight, amber industrial bulbs strung on catenary wires, forge-glow from below. The thin country outside is blinding on snow and obsidian; in here your eyes adjust to a permanent early evening.

There is no water. Not in any way you would recognize.

No pipes run with it, no tanks slosh. Outside, moisture arrives only as rime, a white fur grown overnight on the windward rock, and in the hour before dawn the rime-crews go out on tethers to scrape it into insulated boxes before the wind strips it. Inside, that harvest is rendered, measured out by weight in sealed flasks, most of it going straight to the coolant loops and the air scrubbers. People wash in hot dust and pumice. Clothes are beaten and aired. Machines are washed in oil and ammonia vapor, in closed cabinets that hiss and go silent. Your throat is always dry here. Speaking costs something, so people speak low and briefly, close to each other's ears. The thin air wouldn't carry a shout far anyway.

That is partly why a place so turbulent is so quiet.

For it never stops moving. The roof bellows. In a gust — and above the roof the wind is a continuous, tearing katabatic river, falling off the summit, rising in thermals off the black noon rock — the whole membrane sucks in and sighs out, the ribs creaking, the lamp strings swinging in slow arcs. Everything not bolted down is chained down, and most bolted things are on skids or rails because the Yard has to be rearranged again and again: freight gondolas arriving on the great cable from below, swinging in through the northern gate on their overhead track; crates unloaded, weighed, broken; the cable-spooling plant with its cathedral-high drums slowly winding and unwinding steel rope thick as a wrist; the forge pit sunk into the fissure floor where anchor bolts, pitons, carabiners, replacement teeth for ice-engines, and new rib sections are cast and quenched in oil with a soft, furious *whump*.

Forge, warehouse, dormitory, engine shop, and market all occupy the same volume, stacked. Sleeping tiers climb the east wall in galvanized cages three and four high, curtained with blankets. Workshops wedge into the west wall, their benches cantilevered over the central aisle where the gondola track runs. Conveyor belts, padded in felt to kill the noise, carry ore samples, mail, food bricks, machine parts between levels. People move on ladders and narrow catwalks in a constant, purposeful circulation, anthill-dense, shoulder to shoulder at shift change — and yet muffled, felt-soled, gloved, their tools wrapped, their electric motors (no combustion up here, the air won't spare the oxygen) humming through the rock more than through the air.

Nothing green lives in the fissure. Outside, in the cracks that never see direct wind, there is a crust of black and rust-orange lichen, and that is all. Inside, no planters, no gardens; the light and water won't allow the extravagance. Instead, improbably, everything else is alive.

The roof trusses are a roost. Alpine choughs — glossy black, red-legged, absurdly acrobatic — nest by the dozens in the warm junctions of steel, and yellow-billed cousins crowd the vent cowls. Swifts that nested in the bare cliff long before there was a city have simply moved indoors, stitching the dim air above the Yard from dawn curfew to lamp-out, wings ticking close past your face. Pigeons, brought up as messengers and never successfully discouraged, occupy the spooling plant. Sparrows live entirely inside, generation after generation never having seen a tree, fat on crumbs and forge-flies. In the ductwork there are mice, solemn and unafraid. In the stores there are spiders in every warm corner, and silverfish, and a slow, thriving population of moths around the lamps, which the swifts harvest.

They are not pests here, by law and by custom. The birds are the ventilation made visible — where they will not fly, you do not breathe — and their nesting is worked around. A crane will sit idle for a month because a chough has built in its sheave housing. The chief engineer keeps a chalk map of active nests the way other people keep a map of live wiring. Ibex sometimes appear at the Lips at night, drawn by salt licked from the rime boxes, staring in at the lamplight. No one shoos them. Weight dropped outside is gone forever; salt dropped inside brings the mountain in to visit.

The rules accrete out of the conditions, as they must:

Tether everything, even indoors, even yourself on the catwalks when the roof is breathing hard. A dropped wrench falls through a floor grate to the sorting pit; a dropped anything outside falls four thousand meters.

Water is weighed, not poured. You sign for it.

Do not shout. The air is not made for it, and the fabric roof will take a loud noise and turn it uneasy.

Do not net a bird.

Work moves in bursts governed by the gondolas and the gusts. When the outside wind peaks, the gates seal, the fans labor audibly through the walls, and the whole city draws into itself to mend, to sort, to re-rig, to sleep in shifts in the swaying tiers while the roof drums softly overhead. When it eases — never calms, eases — the gates crack, the cold, star-dry air knifes in, the crews spill out onto the summit in masks to repair cable, clear rime traps, replace a torn roof panel, look down for a moment at a sea of cloud far below and a horizon visibly curved.

Then the membrane sighs. A lamp string swings. A swift cuts the space where your head just was. Below your boots, through the grate, the forge breathes orange, and the spooling drum turns, winding the bright line that holds this hidden, crowded, nearly waterless room at the top of the world to everything underneath it.
