It is a sea held up in the sky.

Not metaphorically. Far below, clouds move in a slow white plain and hide whatever lowlands exist. Above them the land has risen — young, black, abrupt — a whole high country of sharp volcanic ridges and tilted plateaus lifted too recently for weather to have softened it. And across the top of that country, from valley to valley, lies water.

Warm water. Clear in the shallows, green-black in the sudden depths. It fills the craters, it drowns the forests, it joins lake to lake over submerged passes until there is really only one interrupted inland ocean perched above the world, with mountaintops breaking through it as islands.

The edges are not beaches. Erosion has not had time to make beaches. Basalt fins go straight down into the lake, still faceted from their cooling, still knife-edged where they have split. Obsidian rubble lies in angular fans under the surface, unworn, each shard visible through ten feet of water as if through glass. In places pale travertine is building outward from hot springs in fresh, chalk-white terraces, soft as wet plaster at the rim, hardening behind. The stone here is either black or white or rust-red with iron. Almost nothing is grey and rounded yet.

Above the surface the air is thin and bright, the light ordinary and clean at noon, falling hard on wet rock. A step below the surface everything changes: the light turns green-gold, sound falls away, and the cold of the altitude is gone. Geothermal heat and sun-warmed shallows keep the water at blood temperature over vast areas. You can hang suspended with your face at the boundary and feel two climates at once — scalp in thin, dry wind, ears and throat in warm, motionless water.

That boundary is hard to find, because the vegetation does not respect it.

This is first of all a plant place, layered until there is almost no empty water. Underneath, on the drowned volcanic soil, grow meadows of stiff, translucent grasses whose blades trap bubbles and stand in dense, swaying ranks even though there is almost no current to sway them. Over them, a submerged forest.

The trees were growing before the plateau dammed and flooded, and many of them simply did not die. Their trunks go down into black silt and up through twenty feet of water, bark furred in red algae, branches still putting out leaves below the surface — broad, waxy, slow leaves, darker than any land leaf, that open and close over the course of days. Higher up the same trunks produce a second, different foliage in the air: smaller, paler, feathery crowns on the emergent tops. One tree, two canopies, with a bright raft of floating leaves around its waist where it crosses the light.

Between the trees hangs everything else. Curtains of vine descend from the aerial branches and continue growing underwater until they root again in the bottom. Lily forests, not lily pads but actual ascending stalks, push plate-sized leaves up step by step, so that in the deeper channels you move through storey after storey of green discs, each holding a lens of trapped air beneath it like a glass bell. Reed beds crowd the flooded saddles in such density that their stems click softly together, though there is no wind down there. Mosses sponge over the basalt. Horsetails the height of houses stand in flooded groves along the old valley floors, jointed, hollow, still dripping inside when a bubble rises through them.

It is lush, but it is not soft. The young mineral water makes many of the plants rigid with silica. Grass cuts. Reed stems are like bundles of green glass rods. The floating mats are woven so tight in places that rain pools on top of them in a second, upper lake an inch deep, warm, full of tadpole-sized plantlets, sitting on leaves sitting on the real lake twenty feet above the drowned ground.

Animals are rare here, and their rarity shapes the quiet.

No herds, no flocks, no insect hum at evening. The altitude is too high for most things, the flooding too recent, the passes up from below too sheer. Now and then a single long-legged wading bird works the travertine shallows, grey as ash, placing each foot with exaggerated care among the sharp stones, and its loneliness looks like a permanent condition. Something pale and lunged — not quite fish, not quite salamander, as long as a forearm — lives under the lily storeys and surfaces perhaps once an hour, rolls one eye above the waterline, and sinks again without a ripple worth naming. Small transparent crustaceans graze the algae, in places thickly, but they make no sound and move only when the light shifts.

Mostly the fauna is anticipated rather than present. Hollows in the silt where nothing has nested. Perfect feeding grounds no mouth has found. When a creature does call — a low, doubled note from across a flooded crater, late in the day — the whole plateau seems to attend to it, because there is nothing to compete with it.

The soundscape is muffled even above water. Dripping from the emergent trees. The very occasional tick of cooling rock at nightfall, far off in a dry ridge. Bubbles rising from a hot vent, gathering size under a leaf, detaching with a soft, delayed *bloop* that can take half a minute to travel. Rain, when it comes, does not patter so much as merge: the sky is already so humid above this warm high sea, the clouds so often level with the surface and drifting *through* the tree crowns, that rain arrives as a thickening, a wall of suspended droplets becoming too heavy to suspend, and for an hour you cannot tell where cloud ends and lake begins. You are submerged standing up, breathing wet air, looking down through wet air at clearer water below your knees.

And those are the rules here, if it has rules: distinctions persist but in the wrong places. The bottom is a forest floor you can swim over. The surface is a ceiling you can poke your head through into dry wind. Bubbles do not always rise — in the densest plant exhalations they lodge in the underwater canopy and stay for weeks, growing a skin of green, so that looking up from below you see a second, inverted meadow of silver fruit. Fruit itself often ripens underwater, heavy, orange, unpicked, splitting at last to release seeds that sink rather than float, each in its own slow parachute of mucilage, taking an afternoon to descend ten feet. Leaves fall upward from the submerged branches, coming to rest against the underside of the surface, where they rot in a bright ceiling of debris.

Movement is slow in a way that recalibrates attention. Currents travel meters per day, diverted and re-diverted by plant growth. A wave started by a rockfall at one end of a crater lake will still be arriving, diminished to a long breathing swell, the following morning. The water level rises — the whole plateau is still tilting, still damming new valleys behind fresh lava levees — but by finger-widths per season. A tree that is ankle-deep this decade will be a drowned, double-canopied thing in your grandchildren's time, and it appears to be preparing for this in advance, putting out its dark water-leaves well above the present surface, on speculation.

At the rim of the high country, the sea finds its exits. Water pours over sharp lips of basalt in waterfalls so tall they disassemble halfway down into mist, never reaching the lowlands as liquid at all, feeding the cloud plain below instead. So the lake is less drained than exhaled. What leaves as vapor returns weeks later as that vertical, silent rain. What stays grows denser: another vine across a channel, another lily storey, another season's travertine lip raising a pool by an inch.

In the late afternoon the light goes level and amber, and then the place is at its most coherent. The emergent crowns glow. The water takes the color of the sky so exactly that the basalt islands seem to float, their reflections completing them below into dark symmetrical mountains pointed at the drowned earth. Underneath, the forest is already in a green dusk of its own. Nothing hurries to feed or to hide. A bubble works loose somewhere and rises, turning, through leaf after leaf.

You could walk most of this country, but only in the manner of walking on the moon or in a dream — along the bottom, in warm dimness, pushing gently between stems that are nearly touching, in a silence so complete your own heartbeat seems to trespass. Or you could swim the passes between peaks, weeks of it, valley after flooded valley opening ahead. Either way there is always another ridge under you, another grove ahead, another terrace of white mineral steps descending into deeper, warmer, older-looking water that has in fact been there only a breath, geologically speaking. The world is still being made here, and it is being made, improbably, from the top down: first the height, then the sea upon it, then the forest learning to live inside both.
