There is a town at the bottom of a shallow quarry, and you hear it long before you can see anything unusual about it.

The road comes down from a cool, bare heath in three switchbacks. No trees up there, just wet stone and low cloud. Then the guardrail ends, the road straightens, and the entire place opens below you in one flat piece: about nine streets, maybe forty buildings, wide empty lots between them, a perimeter wall made of black slag-glass blocks. You could walk across it in twelve minutes. You could see the far wall from the near wall. It looks almost sparse, from above — a little industrial hamlet laid out with too much room between everything.

Then the sound arrives through the car floor.

It is not a noise with peaks in it. It is a level. Compressor roar from the refrigeration plant, a deep continuous thrum from the rendering boilers, reversing alarms, ventilation fans the size of houses — and under, through, and braided into all of that, animals. Gulls screaming. Jackdaws clattering. Starlings in a black, shifting uproar. Pigeons clapping up from roofs in waves. And underneath even the engines, a bass note that seems to come from the drains themselves: frogs, thousands of them, calling from the condensate sumps, their voices resonating up through concrete culverts until the pavement hums faintly under your shoes.

It never gets louder. It never gets quieter. That is the first rule you learn here. The plant has run at exactly the same load for thirty-one years, because it has to — it stabilizes pressure for a gas line and cold for a regional food store, and varying it even slightly would ripple outward for hundreds of miles. So the steam never thins, the cold never deepens, the waste stream never pauses. It is always about nine degrees in the bowl. It is always damp, but never properly wet. Condensation beads on every pipe and drips at a metronome pace into shallow, green-black puddles that have not dried since the plant opened. The light is ordinary daylight, grey and even, pooled in the quarry like something stored there.

And because nothing ever changes, it is always, permanently, the first evening of spring.

That is what the animals decided, a long time ago.

There is no vegetation. Not suppressed, not struggling — absent, in a way that takes a while to register. No grass in the cracks. No weeds along the walls. No trees in the little plazas the original planners drew with such optimism between the worker hostels. The ground is raked gravel, cracked concrete, and black glass aggregate that crunches faintly underfoot. What arrives as a seed is eaten within the hour. What manages to sprout is scalded by a steam vent, frozen by a leaking ammonia line, buried in droppings, or stripped by beaks before it has a second leaf. In the damp corners where something does grow, it is not green. It is simpler than that: mats of black and mustard fungus slicking the bases of walls, pale mineral crusts flowering across wet brick, a soft grey felt on the inside of disused ducts. The town's true groundcover is feathers and guano.

The guano has, over decades, become a geological material. It whites the roofs in drifts. It streaks every ledge, stanchion, and handrail. It has etched the galvanized steel of the cooling towers into lace at the edges and polished the loading-yard bollards smooth as ivory, from the feet of waiting birds. Rain — the occasional real rain, slanting in over the quarry lip — does not wash it away. It just re-wets it, sharpens the smell of ammonia and fishmeal and hot iron, and sends a milky film sheeting across the streets into the sumps, where the frogs are waiting, and where the midges breed in clouds so steady that the streetlights have permanent brown halos, even at noon.

The buildings are urban in intention and industrial in fact. Three-storey hostels with laundry lines strung *indoors*, across hallways, because laundry put outside comes back speckled and damp and smelling of the yards. A canteen. A clinic with double doors and an airlock, because of the midges. A row of shops, most shuttered, one open, selling earplugs, boot covers, netting, batteries, tea. Everything spaced far apart — ammonia fire regulations — so the town has avenues wide enough for six lanes carrying almost no traffic, and plazas where a single bench sits in an acre of concrete, every horizontal surface occupied, in strict rotation, by birds.

Because the second rule is the perches.

Every perch in the town is taken. Every rail, wire, pipe-run, roof ridge, antenna, and windowsill has its bird, and every bird has its exact orientation, facing into the prevailing draft from the cooling towers, beaks slightly open. Above the town, all day, is a slow aerial holding pattern — a vast, patient, circling queue of gulls and starlings and jackdaws, turning in a flattened spiral over the plant, waiting for a vacancy below. When a bird dies — and they die constantly, of age, of fights, of flying into a fan — another drops from the queue within seconds and takes the precise spot, turns, settles, opens its beak. The total never varies. Workers swear the silhouette of each roof is identical in photographs taken ten years apart, down to the last tail. They are right, and it is not a metaphor. The shape is a property of the place, like the temperature.

Below the roofs, it is as dense. Rats move along the warm pipe-bridges in the open, unhurried, glossy, too well fed on spilled grain and rendering waste to bother hiding. Cats — there are official cats, with collars and roster numbers — lie on transformer housings in the steam warmth and watch chicks walk over their paws. A pair of ravens has nested for years inside the housing of a decommissioned siren, and the town has quietly agreed never to recommission it. Bats pour at dusk from a ventilation shaft, not in a burst but in a continuous ribbon, because in the warm outflow ducts it is always roosting temperature and always almost-night. Year-round, in the insulated pipes under the cold store, sparrows are raising chicks on waste heat. Year-round the frogs call and mate and lay in water that never freezes and never warms. There is no season to end. Breeding never switches off. The chorus is not a performance. It is an metabolism.

The strangest accommodation, and by now the oldest, is the mimicry. Three decades of living inside machine noise have taught the starlings and jackdaws an entire second language. They do the reversing beep of the yard trucks perfectly. They do the two-tone warning before a pressure release. They do — this is documented, and the safety office is tired of it — a flawless imitation of the ammonia leak alarm, in the correct pitch, sustained for the correct duration. So the plant no longer uses sound for its most serious warnings. Serious things are announced by smell: a puff of wintergreen through the ventilation, a chemical sweetness on the air. The birds have not learned to make smells. Yet. Human conversation, meanwhile, happens at a shout, or in the low, flat, lip-readable murmur people develop here, leaning close under the roar. Visitors get headaches by evening. Residents get a kind of ringing silence when they travel anywhere else, and cannot sleep in the quiet, and come home.

Nobody clears nests. That was tried, in the first decade, with nets and spikes and a contractor with a falcon. The falcon stayed. It lives in Cooling Tower Two now and is, by seniority, essentially a foreman. The nets filled with nesting material within weeks — the birds used the net *as* scaffolding and built denser. The spikes became perches with better drainage. Removal, it turned out, was pointless anyway: the plant's waste makes food at a fixed rate, the sumps make insects at a fixed rate, the heat makes shelter at a fixed rate, and the animal population simply solved for those numbers and stopped. It is an equilibrium so complete it has become architectural. Maintenance crews work around nests the way you'd work around a load-bearing beam. There are pipes in this plant that nobody will ever replace, because a colony of swifts has used the lagging as a maternity ward for nineteen generations, and moving them would, in the words of the site manager, "change the sound."

Stand still in the middle of the main street at midday — cool air moving steadily between the hostels, your coat dampening at the shoulders, a fine white dust on your sleeves, the frogs making the drain covers tick faintly with vibration, the queue turning, turning, patient as weather overhead — and the deepest strangeness is not the noise or the numbers. It's the stillness underneath it all. A puddle here has a painted line at its edge marking its level, and the water is at the line. A bird leaves a wire; the wire has another bird. Steam blows sideways, shreds, and is replaced by identical steam. The shift changes; the new crew wears the same orange as the old crew, takes the same posts, shouts the same greetings into the same wall of starlings and engines. Nothing is building up. Nothing is running down. The quarry took a town, an industry, and several million animal lives, and locked them together into a single, loud, feathered, dripping machine that has found its one note —

and holds it.
