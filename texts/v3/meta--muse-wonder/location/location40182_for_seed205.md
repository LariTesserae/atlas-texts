It begins long before you can see it properly, as a sound coming over the edge of the world.

You climb to the plateau by an old, worn stair of switchbacks — not built so much as maintained, generation after generation, where a seasonal melt once cut a gully through the rimrock and roots have since taken hold of the cut and kept it from closing or widening. The lowlands fall away behind you, hazed and distant. Ahead, the land lifts once, gently, and then simply does not stop.

It is a continent raised up on its own shoulders. High enough that the air is thin and clean and the light, at noon, is ordinary daylight sharpened by altitude — white, honest, unforgiving — but at the surface the heat is lowland heat, equatorial heat, contained and returned by the living ground. By mid-morning it is already over a hundred degrees in the open. By afternoon the wind itself feels heated, as if it has passed across coals.

And yet there are no fields, no forest in any orderly sense, no bare soil to speak of. From the rim to the far, weather-bent horizon, the plateau is covered entirely by vegetation. Not dense. That is its first strangeness. Total, but sparse.

The ground is a plant.

Under your feet is a single, interwoven mat many feet thick — not soil held together by roots, but roots become soil, bark become earth. It is made of a tough, slow species the plateau peoples call the Floor: broad, overlapping root-plates and flattened stems that have spread, merged, grafted, and re-spread for longer than the cliffs have held their present shape. In places it is smooth and grey-green, polished by wind and passage. In places it hummocks into low knees and elbows, or opens a dark stomatal pit the size of a well, which sighs cool air for a minute and then, without hurry, closes again. Walking on it is like walking on the back of something that has politely decided not to notice you. It gives slightly. It is warm through the soles. At night it retains the day and you can sleep on it without a mat, if you do not mind the slow, subterranean ticking of sap.

Nothing grows from soil because there is none to reach. Everything roots into the Floor, or into each other, or hangs in the air and drinks condensation. The Floor itself drinks what little water the plateau allows: dew.

That is the governing scarcity here. For all this green mass, it is a damp country, not a wet one. No rivers run on the surface. No lakes shine in the hollows. Rain, when it comes, comes as brief, violent, localized weather that the plateau makes for itself — a tower of cloud forming over a warm stretch of mat in late afternoon, unmaking itself in twenty minutes of hammering rain that the Floor swallows before a puddle can properly form. Most mornings begin white: mist trapped between the giant plants, dew beading on every leaf-edge, running in bright lines down thorn and fiber to be caught in leaf-cups before the heat takes it. People, animals, whole ecosystems are arranged around those few hours. By ten, the world has dried again.

What keeps it alive is storage. Every plant here is a reservoir wearing leaves. Trunks are swollen. Leaves are thick, waxed, folded shut at noon. The Floor holds last month's rain somewhere down in its sponge, and if you know where to listen — literally listen, ear to the warm surface, for a hollow, eager note under the general roar — you can auger a careful hole with a hardened thorn-wood drill and draw up a skin of sour, greenish water. The hole will seal itself in a day, scarred over.

And standing out of this living plain, at distances you misjudge for hours, are the Pillars.

Each is a tree, technically. Each is also an architecture, a climate, a neighborhood, a god of limited jurisdiction. Their trunks — often forty, sixty feet across, buttressed with root-fins that merge seamlessly into the Floor — rise three, four hundred feet before they branch, and when they branch they do not so much produce a canopy as a separate suspended savanna: limbs thick as lesser trees, draped with gardens of epiphytes, hanging moss-forests, orchid mats, bromeliad ponds, whole thickets that have never known the ground. Between one Pillar and the next there may be two miles of open Floor, or ten, or thirty. From far off, on the shimmering plain, they stand like a sparse colonnade built to hold up the sky for a race of giants who never arrived.

Their spacing is the emptiness at the heart of the place. You can walk for days in full sun across the Floor with one Pillar shrinking behind you and the next not visibly growing ahead, and in that entire traverse you are never off vegetation, never out of the ecosystem — the Floor ripples with low succulent grasses that uncurl at dawn and shut to grey fists at noon, flowering weeds travel in mats, climbers run for hundreds of yards just under the surface and erupt in brief, garish blooms — and yet you are profoundly alone in space, exposed, with nothing vertical to break the horizon except heat ghosts. It makes the plateau feel simultaneously overfull and deserted. Life is everywhere underfoot and overhead. Company is rare.

Until you listen.

The sound reaches a volume past which the word "loud" stops being useful. It is not noise added to the landscape. It is one of the landscape's load-bearing structures.

Every Pillar is inhabited at every level by animals in numbers that defeat counting — insects first of all, in strata: gnats in visible columns around flowering crowns, thumb-sized beetles in iridescent convoys moving up the trunk highways, moths the size of hands sleeping under leaves by day in such densities that the underside of a limb looks furred. Then frogs, thousands of them, living their whole lives in the bromeliad cups and hollow limb-crotches of the canopy, never descending, each cup a pond, a nursery, a territory defended by a call like a struck bell. Lizards run the bark. Snakes, bright and patient, run the lizards. In the upper gardens live monkeys with dust-colored coats and infants clinging to their backs, troops that travel limb to limb and, during the long crossings between Pillars, descend reluctantly to the Floor at dawn and go on all fours in single file, silent — the only silent thing here — with lookouts running upright at front and rear.

Birds occupy the air in layers by height and hour. Swifts stitch the trunk-zone. Parrots and hornbills and a dozen raucous, crested things work the canopy fruit falls. Higher, almost out of sight, raptors ride the updrafts that boil off the hot Floor and hang for hours without a wingbeat, watching the plain for the herds.

Because there are herds. Grazers the size of cattle, long-necked, with splayed heat-shield ears and hides patterned in disruptive green-brown, follow the flowering wave. Like everything else here, they eat the plant that is also the ground — carefully, cropping the uncurled morning grasses and the protein-rich erupting blooms, never tearing the Floor itself, which is bitter, fibrous, and mildly toxic. Predators follow them: lean, long-legged cats that hunt by sound more than sight, and packs of hooting, hyena-like dogs whose chorusing at dusk can raise the pitch of a whole district.

All of them are calling. That is the point. On this plateau, sound is the main medium of sex, warning, navigation, and war, and evolution, given a hot, bright, visually confusing world of identical green, has turned the volume up and up and up. The cicada-analogues alone — pale, hand-length insects that wedge into bark resonant-chambers grown by the Pillars themselves, hollows in the wood the tree produces the way other trees produce thorns — can, in aggregate, in mating season, make the trunk hum like a struck cable. You feel a Pillar's fauna in your sternum before you reach its shade. Frogs answer. Birds answer frogs. Monkeys scream down birds. Certain canopy flowers, thick, waxy, bell-mouthed, have no scent at all: they open only when a specific frequency shakes pollen loose from their anthers, and so the frogs that pollinate them have been selected, cup by cup, for pitch. Some of the larger blooms visibly tremble minutes before an animal calls, anticipating a regular.

The peoples of the plateau speak in shouts and, among intimates and hunters, in sign: elaborate two-handed languages developed for use under the roar, where a companion six feet away may as well be across a river at noon. Children develop hoarse, carrying voices early. Old people's hearing goes in patterned ways — they lose the frog register first, keep the deep Pillar-hum longest, and are said near the end to hear only the trees.

Human presence is real, and almost visually negligible: built at 0.8 on any surveyor's scale. A seasonal camp at the foot of a Pillar is a few woven windbreaks, a platform of lashed saplings raised on a root-knee, racks for drying strips of grazer meat and sliced succulent pith. Everything is made by hand from what the plant world sheds: fiber cord, thorn needles, tools of dense heartwood and sharpened bone and occasionally a traded blade of simple hammered metal, gone green, treasured for generations. At the condensation seeps there are low rings of rimrock hauled up piecemeal over centuries, each stone a few pounds, the labor of the ring in total enormous, marking: *here*. Water under here. Do not drill elsewhere. Leave the scar clean.

And there are older, stranger remains — partial, scattered, half-swallowed by the Floor. Fallen Pillars do not rot as trees elsewhere rot. Their heartwood is mineral-heavy, almost stone, and a trunk dead for five hundred years may still lie across the plain as a long grey ridge, hollowed by beetles into a natural barracks that shepherds and herds both use for noon shade, its interior chambers frescoed by successive generations with handprints in red resin and pollen-paste. Some fallen trunks are still, obscurely, alive along one side, putting out leaves from a corpse. Nobody cuts them. The etiquette of the plateau, older than any of its current languages, is absolute on this point: you may take what is shed, given, dead-and-dry, or grown for taking. You do not wound the Floor. You do not axe a Pillar. Where foreigners once tried — there are stories, and one valley near the southern rim where the Floor is scarred black in a spiral a mile wide and nothing has rooted since — the whole district, animals and plants together, is said to have gone quiet for a season. The silence is described in the stories exactly the way other cultures describe an eclipse. As an omen. As a system noticing.

The plateau keeps its own weather because it is large enough to be a cause. In the mornings, mist rivers flow slowly between the Pillars at knee height, following invisible drainage in the Floor's surface, and the herds move in them half-visible, backs and horns emerging. By midday the Floor's transpiration — billions of stomata exhaling in the open stretches — loads the air until distant Pillars stand in blue, trembling veils of their own making. Thermals rise in pillars of their own, carrying seeds, insects, spiders on silk, fledgling birds making their first terrifying inter-tree crossing on a cushion of hot rising air they did not entirely intend. In the late afternoon, as noted, small private thunderstorms assemble over the warmest districts, rain furiously on a square mile, and dissolve, leaving the Floor steaming and every leaf-cup replenished and the frogs beside themselves. A traveler can watch three separate rains happening at once on different parts of the horizon, under a sky directly above him that has not a cloud in it.

The year turns on the flowering wave. It starts, most years, near the eastern rim after the long dry-bright season, when the first Pillar crowns flush crimson — visible for thirty miles — and the grazers turn as one animal. Then, week by week, for reasons of altitude, temperature, and some slow chemical conversation conducted root to root through the Floor itself, the bloom sweeps westward across the continent at roughly the pace of a walking herd. Orchards of canopy fruit ripen in sequence. Insect populations explode district by district and collapse behind the wave. Birds nest in the crest of it. People move with it, camp to camp, Pillar to Pillar, harvesting ahead of the herds and hunting behind them, mending the well-rings, re-weaving platforms left for the season. Behind the wave the plain looks, for a while, almost subdued: seed heads dry to pale straw above the green, the roar drops by noticeable increments, petals rain in drifts that the Floor reabsorbs. Ahead of it is only heat, patience, and the white mornings.

At night — this is when the strangeness, which all day has been merely ecological, becomes personal — the heat leaves the air but not the ground, and the sound changes register rather than diminishing. The day chorus hands off to a night chorus of different frogs, night-insects with calls like sustained violin notes, owls with voices like struck water. The Floor's pits open wider in the cool: whole fields of soft, regular breathing in the dark. Some of the canopy blooms are night-openers, pale, and they release their pollen to moths in puffs timed to the moths' wingbeat frequency, so that in a shaft of ordinary moonlight above a Pillar pool you can see little timed explosions of gold dust, one per moth, like a slow firework being repeatedly invented. Bioluminescence is rare here — the daylight species never needed it — but in the deep hollow interiors of the oldest Pillars there are fungal gardens tended by beetles, green-lit, cathedral-quiet by local standards, where the roar comes filtered through a hundred feet of wood and sounds, at last, like weather on a distant coast.

And occasionally, for minutes at a time, everything stops.

No one has a settled explanation, though everyone has an opinion polished by retelling. A great predator, maybe, moving somewhere far off, whose infrasonic tread the whole system feels through the Floor and answers with a held breath. Or the Floor itself, sending one of its slow electrical conversations through the root-graft network, during which — for reasons no pollinator can afford to interrupt — every caller instinctively waits. The silence does not spread; it simply, everywhere within a mile or ten miles, *is*, all at once, and then a single frog tests the air, and another, and the birds resume mid-phrase, and the volume climbs back to its deafening structural norm as if nothing had happened. In that brief quiet you can hear things normally impossible: your own pulse. The sap ticking in the ground. The wind worrying a dead leaf somewhere, hundreds of feet up.

You can stand then, in the middle of the night, on a warm living continent raised a mile into the sky, with a herd sighing in the mist somewhere out of sight, dew already beginning to gather on every surface including your own skin, and an unborn private thunderstorm condensing somewhere in the black above an invisible Pillar — stand in the total vegetation, the overwhelming fauna, the vast and humming emptiness between green towers — and understand that the emptiness was never empty. It was just waiting, the way the whole plateau waits, holding its water inside itself, holding its sound at full throat, for the next ordinary, deafening morning.
