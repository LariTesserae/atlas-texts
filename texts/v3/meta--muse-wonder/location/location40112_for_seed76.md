There is no rain here. There has not been, in any meaningful sense, for a very long time.

It is a high country — not mountains, exactly, though it is higher than most mountains. It is a continent lifted whole, a plateau so broad and so elevated that it lives above the weather that feeds the rest of the world. Below its edges, far below, clouds form and break and rain themselves out on lower slopes. From up here you can sometimes see their tops at the rim, a slow white sea lapping at stone. None of it arrives.

What arrives is light.

The light is the first physical fact. It is bright in a way that has nothing to do with warmth. The sky is a pale, hammered blue, almost white at noon, thin and depthless. The sun is small and hard and exact. Shadows are black cutouts with razor edges, and cold inside. Stand in full sun and your exposed skin prickles and burns while your back, in shadow, goes numb. The air itself stays cool — coat-cold, breath-visible in the morning — even at midday in midsummer. Heat does not accumulate. It strikes surfaces, glitters, and is gone into space again.

And the scale defeats correction. This is not a valley you can learn. It has its own interior distances, its own local winds that are born and die a thousand miles from any edge. The horizon is not a line so much as a gradual failure of detail: stone paler and paler, groves smaller and smaller, until land and sky trade places. On clear days — most days are clear — you can walk for a week toward a dark patch that looked an hour away and watch it refuse to grow. Thermal columns rise in the afternoons in places that have decided, for geological reasons, to be warm, and fall again at night in other places. The plateau makes its own circulation the way an ocean does, slowly, without ever becoming a storm.

Between the groves, it is empty.

The ground is old mineral, settled into its final forms. Long wind-yardangs streamlined in the prevailing direction, their tails drawn out for hundreds of yards. Basins of white and ochre salt where something once evaporated and never returned. Sheets of pumice and dark basalt polished not smooth, but *finished* — every sharpness taken off ten million years ago, every hollow holding a fine, floury dust that is the color of the rock it came from. Dry streambeds wander and braid and end, not dried up recently but fossilized, their cobbles cemented in place, a memory of water preserved in stone more faithfully than water preserves anything.

There is almost no free water anywhere on the surface. No streams, no dew, no damp soil under stones. Turn a rock and the underside is as dry as the top, only darker. Lick dust and it pulls moisture *out* of your tongue. The thin air itself is desiccated; it cracks lips, whitens skin, makes a nosebleed start without warning and stop without clotting properly.

And yet, scattered across this, the groves are lush.

You see them from far off as an improbability: a dense, dark green-black mass rising out of pale stone, too rich for the place, like a piece of jungle set down intact. Miles of bareness, then — a forest. Miles more, then another, not connected, not thinning toward each other. Each one is an island complete in itself.

Up close, the lushness resolves into something stranger and drier than it promised. The plants are full, broad-leafed, layered, dripping with growth — but nothing in them is wet. Break a leaf and it does not bleed sap. It tears like oiled leather, like thick paper, and the inside is fibrous and pale and almost warm from the sun it has been storing. The great canopy trees — pillars of corky, fissured bark, thirty feet around — are not full of water. They are full of wax and resin and trapped air. Their trunks ring faintly when struck, hollow-chambered. Their leaves are heavy paddles, silver underneath, dark above, coated in a powder that comes off on your hands and smells of hot stone and pepper.

All the water in this world is inside the living things, and it stays there.

That is the central rule. A seed begins with the water its parent gave it — sometimes a thimbleful, hoarded over decades — and it adds to it, molecule by molecule over centuries, by prying apart hydrated minerals in the rock with its roots, by condensing the faintest exhalation of its neighbors in the still night air inside the grove and taking it back before dawn. Almost none is lost. Leaves seal. Bark seals. Fallen leaves are reabsorbed by fungal mats that dry them to husk and pass everything usable upward again. The grove is a sealed vessel made of green things. Inside its canopy, the air is fractionally softer, fractionally less murderous to breathe — not humid, never humid, but *less dry*, the way the inside of a closed mouth is less dry than a desert. It is enough of a difference that your eyes stop aching.

Outside that seal, plants take other forms. The open ground is not bare of life, only sparse and low: crust lichens in sulfur yellow and arterial red spreading over basalt in perfect circles, some of them hundreds of feet across and thousands of years old, growing a hair's width a year. Stone-rosettes that open their mineral-flecked petals to the sun and close to fists at night. Long, glassy grasses that grow in the lee of rocks where the wind drops its cargo of dust, each blade a translucent tube full of air, clicking very faintly together when a gust finds them. Everything is spaced. Even in abundance there is distance — plant from plant, grove from grove — as if the land insists on emptiness between presences.

It is quiet in a way that is not just an absence. The thin air is a poor carrier. A shout flattens within fifty yards and comes apart. Birdsong would not work here, and there is almost nothing to sing. Sounds are small and close: the tick of cooling rock after sunset, a continuous geological chorus as the whole plateau contracts. The creak of canopy trees flexing. The dry rasp of leaves handled by wind, more like sand being poured than rustling. Inside a grove at noon, with the canopy sealing out the sky-wind, there can be minutes of silence so complete you hear the plants themselves — a soft popping as air-pockets in stems expand in the heat, a slow groan as a sun-tracking crown turns on its cork joint, degree by degree, to keep its leaves square to the light.

Because despite the stillness, nothing here is static. This is an active place with its processes turned inward and vertical rather than horizontal.

By day, the leaves track. Whole canopies slew imperceptibly, then visibly over an hour, a green tide turning. Photosynthesis here is greedy and opportunistic; the light is superabundant and everything else is scarce, so the plants spend light like wealth to buy the rest — splitting rock, fixing nitrogen from the thin air, pumping minerals up from fissures a hundred feet down where root-tips, white and blind and incredibly strong, are slowly widening cracks that began before any grove existed. At the plateau's surface the dust moves in long, low rivers a few inches deep, sorted by wind into ripples, feeding the root-zones. Dust devils walk the empty basins in the afternoons, tall, elegant, nearly transparent, picking up nothing much and carrying it miles to set it down again.

By night, everything reverses. The temperature falls off a cliff. Frost forms — not from water in the air, but hoar crystals of carbon dioxide and mineral salts blooming on stone and leaf-edge, white and evanescent, gone an hour after sunrise without ever being liquid. The air-pocket plants deflate slightly, sighing out. The great trees close their crowns into buds against the cold. It is then, in the dark and the contraction, that most growth happens: roots extending their fraction, trunks adding a dense, narrow ring, seeds splitting.

Fauna is rare, and almost all of it lives in or on the groves, dependent on that sealed water like passengers on a ship. There are pale, long-legged things that move through the canopy and never come down, drinking by chewing leaf-pulp and excreting pellets dry as chalk. There are burrowers in the deep dust under the trees whose eyes have reduced to light-sensitive patches, who emerge at dusk to lick salt and eat fallen lichen. Once in several days of walking the open ground, you might startle the only large animal of the flats: a solitary, hump-backed grazer the color of pumice, its body inflated with air-sacs that let it survive the altitude and the temperature swings, cropping stone-rosettes with a horny beak. It will watch you — fauna here has not learned fear in any useful hurry — then amble off at a pace that covers ground deceptively fast. Its dung is dry fiber. Its footprints fill with dust within the hour.

There are no predators worth the name, because there is not enough prey to support the habit. Death is mostly failure of seal: a tree whose bark cracks too deep in a cold snap and loses its interior in a season, standing afterward as a perfect, light, hollow shell that the wind plays through for a century before it falls. A grove can die that way, all at once, if the oldest trees go together, and then you find its ghost on the plateau: acres of standing cork pillars and leaf-leather draped and mummified, not rotting — nothing here rots properly in the open — just desiccating further into the general dryness, becoming more and more like the stone around it.

New groves begin obscenely far from old ones, where a seed, light as ash and sealed like a tiny boat, has ridden a thermal for three days and come down in a dust-hollow with a crack underneath it. Most fail. The ones that do not will spend two hundred years as a single dark bush, accumulating, before they can afford a second tree.

There are no structures, no marks, no paths except the temporary ones animals wear between a dust hollow and a grove and abandon. Nothing has ever been built here because there is nothing to build with that is not alive and hoarding itself, and nothing to build *against* except light and cold and wind, which cannot be kept out by walls. Shelter is a biological fact: the lee of a rock, the inside of a grove, the shadow of your own body.

Walk here long enough and your sense of what a landscape is *for* readjusts. It is not cycling water, as most living places do. It is cycling light through mineral, very slowly, at altitude, in silence — turning sun and stone into brief, dense, green accumulations that hold their tiny inheritance of wetness against a continental dryness, mile after empty mile, under a sky too bright to look at and too thin to carry your voice away.
