From below there is almost nothing to see.

The mountain has had so long to erode that it no longer looks like a mountain. Once, perhaps, it had a peak, sharp ridges, young black slopes. Now it is a stump, a broad molar worn down to the gum. The sides go up for thousands of feet in smooth, sun-bleached folds of rock — no crags left, no loose scree, everything rounded by wind and time into long, tired curves — and then they stop, abruptly, at a rim. Above that rim, from the valley, only a fringe shows: a dark line of trees, a scatter of roofs, smoke that does not rise so much as pool and spill.

You climb to it on a path cut for donkeys, switchback after switchback in red dust. The air gets thin and your chest starts to work. The valley shrinks. Hawks turn below you. By the last hour you are walking inside cloud-shadow even at midday, because you have come up under the lip of the crater.

And then you step over the rim, and the town opens inside the bowl.

It is small enough to cross in ten minutes, and dense enough to feel like a city. The whole floor of the old crater — a few hundred paces across, saucered, sheltered — is built over, roof touching roof, wall sharing wall, lane turning back on itself. There is no outside street. There are only passages, some roofed with thatch and fig branches, some open in narrow slits to the sky. The rim rises all around it, a hundred feet of warm, lichened stone, and it steals the sun for most of the day.

So it is dim here, and warm. Not cool, as high places should be. The bowl traps air. Heat settles in the stone and stays. At dawn the town lies in blue-gray shade while the peaks across the valley are already blazing. At noon, for perhaps an hour, light falls straight in and everything turns white and green and startling — leaves flare, limewashed walls hurt your eyes, lizards scatter — and then the rim takes it again, and the lanes go back to amber gloom, to woodsmoke haze, to the green half-light under the trees.

There are trees everywhere, improbably lush for a place with no water. No stream runs in the crater. No spring, no well that does not go dry in the hand. The rock is old pumice and ash, porous as a sponge; rain, when it comes twice a year in a brief hammering, vanishes straight down through the floor and is gone. The soil stays powdery, red, warm to the bare foot.

The plants have learned another way to drink. Great figs root in cracks in the rim and lean their crowns over the roofs, dropping shade and figs and white roots that grope for the cisterns. Prickly pear is planted as hedges and living fences, fat with stored rain from years ago. Agaves line every lane. On the shaded side of walls, where night dew condenses on cold stone, there are mats of moss, ferns in the cracks of stairs, and colonies of gray air-plants that live on mist and dust alone, their roots curled in air. At night they tick with dripping. Every roof is shaped to that sound — curved tiles, gutters of split bamboo and hammered copper, funnels of clay — all of it leading down, patiently, drop by drop, into the dark mouths of household cisterns cut deep into the rock under each house. You hear it if you wake before dawn: a slow, irregular plinking under the floors, the town quietly harvesting the sky.

What the roofs cannot catch, donkeys carry. All morning a train goes down the outer path and up again, skins sloshing, bells clanking, boys shouting them on from spring to spring halfway down the mountain where water does finally force itself out of a seam. It is too precious here to waste on anything quick. Faces are wiped, not washed. Courtyards are swept, not sluiced. Pots are scoured with ash and a handful of grit. Urine goes into jars for the tanners and the fig trees. A spilled cup is an event people remember.

The houses are built from what the mountain already gave up: undressed basalt, rounded river-stone hauled from far below generations ago, sun-dried brick the color of the dust. Nothing is sharp. Centuries of feet have bowed the stone steps into shallow dishes. Door lintels are polished where heads and hands have brushed them. Roofs are thatch over bent poles, weighted with stones against the wind that combs the rim at night. Where metal appears it is simple and prized — a copper bell, an iron hinge beaten flat, a knife, a loom's fittings — everything else is wood, clay, fiber, bone, hand-loomed cloth dyed madder and indigo hanging in the lanes and further dimming the light.

And it is loud, out of all proportion to its size. The crater is an amphitheater. Every sound is caught and handed back.

Goats bleat from roofs where they have climbed to eat the thatch. Roosters answer each other from courtyard to courtyard at no particular hour. Pigeons — hundreds of them, housed in holes left purposely in the upper walls — burst out in a clatter and settle in a cooing that never quite stops. Swifts nest by the thousand in the rim and scream around it in the dimming hours. In the fig trees the cicadas keep up a dry, electric sawing that rises until you feel it in your teeth, then cuts off all at once, leaving the bells and hammers louder. Children shout, because at this altitude even ordinary speech has to be pushed. Women call across roofs while they grind corn on stone metates — that rhythmic, gritting scrape going house to house like a slow tide. Somewhere a mason taps. Somewhere hides are being beaten. Dogs bark, donkeys bray against the rock, bees hum in the wall-hives built into the sunny side of houses, cats fight on the tiles. At noon, in the brief blaze, it all seems to pause for breath; in the warm shade, it resumes.

Animals are not kept apart here. They live in the density with everyone else. Chickens pick under market tables. Lizards own the walls, flicking between sun and shadow. Long-tailed birds steal grain from open sacks and are only half-heartedly chased. A monkey, very old, very dignified, sits tethered by the main cistern and watches the water being drawn as if he were in charge of it. At night, bats come out of the rim caves and the air over the roofs fills with soft, erratic wings, while below them oil lamps are lit — small clay dishes, wicks floating, flames sheltered by cupped hands — and the town sinks early into darkness even though the stars above the rim are fierce, white, and close enough to seem low.

Nothing here moves fast. The thin air forbids it, and the heat under the trees encourages a particular economy. Work is done sitting down, or in short spells, with long rests in doorways. Donkeys plod. Elders deliberate. Roofs are rethatched a strip at a time, across a season. A wall that falls is rebuilt with its own stones, in the same place, by grandchildren. The mountain itself seems finished changing — it has arrived at its final, rounded form, and the town has settled into it the same way.

Only the slow cycles turn: dew forming and dripping, figs ripening and dropping with soft thuds onto thatch, cisterns falling an inch a week and rising again after rain, smoke pooling in the bowl at evening and thinning before dawn. In the dim, warm heart of the high crater, surrounded by abundance and entirely without water to spare, life has made itself thick, noisy, rooted, and patient — pressed close together under leaves, listening to itself echo off ancient stone.
