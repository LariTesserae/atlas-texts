It is not found from below. You find it by sound first, and only after you have already climbed past where sound should be possible.

The mountain itself is young — obscenely young, for a mountain. Nothing up here has been rounded off yet. The ridge at 4,000 meters is a spine of unweathered black rock still splitting as it rises, edges sharp enough to cut a boot, faces that have never learned rain. There is no soil to speak of. Between the cracks a little vegetation holds on and gives up by turns: a thumbprint of white moss in a lee, three blades of a straw-colored grass bent flat by wind, lichen the color of dried blood that grows only on the underside of stones. Everything else is bare, cool stone, cool air that smells faintly of iron and snow even in midsummer.

And then, in a fold of the summit where two new slabs have been pushed up against each other and not yet sealed, there is a slit no wider than a man's shoulders. From it comes a noise that does not belong at this height.

It is deafening well before you see the water. A roar, but enclosed. A crashing, but repeating too fast. It beats up through the rock into the soles of your feet, up through your knees into your teeth, until your own breathing seems to be happening inside someone else's chest. People who come here — and very few do — stop speaking a hundred meters out, not out of reverence at first, but because speech simply stops working. You shout and the mountain takes the words out of your mouth and returns only vibration.

The slit widens downward. Handholds have been cut into it, long ago: not stairs, just shallow notches chipped with stone tools, polished dark by wet hands. A rope of twisted hide and plant fiber, replaced generation after generation, goes down into bright light.

Because it is bright down there. That is the second wrong thing.

The chamber is tiny. That has to be insisted on. Twelve, perhaps fifteen paces across if it were dry, a near-perfect bowl cupped inside the peak, open to the sky through a ragged eye far above. Normal daylight falls straight in, clean and white at noon, blue in the late afternoon, and it does not darken the way it should. It enters the water and keeps going.

For the bowl is full. Brim-full, and not with meltwater.

It is a sea, in a cup, at the top of the world.

Cold salt water, clear as glass on the rare seconds it allows itself to be clear, fills the hollow from wall to wall and is in violent, permanent motion. No stream feeds it that anyone can see. No wind reaches it except a thin shiver through the eye above. And yet waves are breaking in there, knee-high waves born in the middle of the little pool and hurled outward to shatter against the rim, a vortex opening off-center and wandering, sucking foam and grit down and then coughing it back up in a warm — no, still cold, but less cold — plume from below. The surface never settles. It heaves, it slaps, it throws spray ten feet up the walls to fall back as indoor rain. The whole volume turns over constantly, currents reversing without warning, so that a leaf or a chip of wood dropped in will be dragged under, spun sideways, shot to the far wall, and held dithering under the surface for a minute before it is spat out again.

You enter it, if you enter it, by that rope, and the moment your feet break the surface your ears tell you a lie that your body believes instantly.

The pressure is deep-sea pressure.

Three meters down in a mountain tarn should be nothing. Here your ears scream and need clearing, and clearing again. Your sinuses ache. The water holds you with a dense, oceanic weight, squeezing the ribs. A stone hammer dropped from the rim — primitive things are all that have ever been used here, antler picks, basalt mallets, hammered copper no thicker than a fingernail — falls and falls through the bright water, turning slowly, growing smaller, and does not strike bottom for an uncomfortably long time. It becomes a speck in blue-green clarity, then nothing. The bowl you can almost step across has no floor you can rely on. Sometimes a floor is there, close and pale, a fresh skin of mineral just precipitating out of the water, still soft as wet chalk. The next morning it will have been unmade by the turbulence, or folded under, or lifted in sheets and broken. New rock is being born down there in the churn, pillows and blisters of stone forming and aborting, giving the water a milky edge before they harden, crack, and sink again into that impossible depth.

This is why the place feels so empty, despite all that motion. There is almost nothing in it. No sediment bank, no forest of weed, no clutter of shells. Vast, clear water between presences. One boulder sits — or hovers, wedged — near the eastern wall, barnacle-free, too clean. A single carved platform of undressed stone juts just at the waterline on the north side, big enough for two people to crouch. Otherwise: void, filled with force. The emptiness makes the sound bigger. The bowl is a drum.

On and around that platform are the marks of everyone who decided the place needed tending.

They are scattered, and none of them have succeeded at whatever they were for. A low lean-to of poles and flat stones collapsed outside the slit, rebuilt half a dozen times in slightly different spots. Cairns — waist-high stacks of water-worn, incongruous pebbles that must have been carried up — marking a path that does not need marking. Inside, tied to iron-hard wooden pegs driven into cracks, a tripod of lashed branches still holds a copper ladle on a cord; it bangs, bangs, bangs ceaselessly against the wall in the spray, adding a bright, insane bell-note to the roar at irregular intervals. Someone carved a column of notches into the rock at the waterline, each notch a year’s level, but the lines wander up and down without trend because the sea in the cup has its own tides, fast ones. Every forty or fifty minutes the whole surface sighs down a hand’s breadth and then surges back higher, flooding the platform, sucking at the rope, though the moon may be nowhere in the sky.

No one fishes here, although there are fish. Or a fish.

Life is rare, and it appears as an exception. A smear of dark algae films the newest stone for a day and is stripped away. The white moss creeps a little nearer the splash zone each wet summer. And sometimes, when for perhaps twenty seconds the vortex stutters and the waves fall out of rhythm, translucent things uncurl in the middle depths where nothing should be able to hold position — blind, shrimp-like creatures with too many swimmerets, pale as fingernails, pulsing in place. Once, or twice in a lifetime if local stories are honest, something longer moves under them: an eel-thin, eyeless animal the length of a forearm, dark along the back, that seems less to swim than to be carried remembering how to swim. It shows itself, it is gone. Killing anything taken from the cup has always been considered a waste of effort — there is never enough to eat, and what there is tastes of deep cold and copper.

The rules, learned in the body rather than taught, are simple.

You do not stay long. The sound gets into you and begins to rearrange your pulse; after an hour your hands shake when you leave, after three you will hear the crashing in perfectly quiet rooms for a week. You do not trust the shallow appearance. You keep a line tied around your waist if you wade even to your knees, because the undertow is not a metaphor here — it takes ankles with real, practiced strength and tries the rest of you. You do not drop metal that you need again. You leave the ladle alone except to drink, and the water, for all its salt, leaves a fresh, almost sweet aftertaste, as if it has never been exposed to a shore before.

And on certain evenings, when the light through the eye turns gold and strikes the spray so that the whole chamber is full of brief, broken rainbows, the new rock below audibly grows. Not groans — ticks, pops, a soft fizzing like bone knitting. The mountain is still coming into being around its stolen ocean, sealing it in, lifting it higher year by year. Whether it trapped a piece of an ancient sea as it rose, or whether seawater is being forced up through fractures from some unmapped depth all the way to this improbable summit, or whether the pressure and the depth are simply happening here in defiance of the size of the container — none of the explanations sit still long enough to be believed.

It remains: a pocket you could hide with a village’s roofs, hidden inside a peak, empty and furiously full, lit like a courtyard, loud as a foundry, cold as a northern tide. A place where the deep has been misfiled in the sky, and is still, turbulently, trying to get its bearings.
