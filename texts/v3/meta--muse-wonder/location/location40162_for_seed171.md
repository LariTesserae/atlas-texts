The first thing is the sound. Not at the edge of it — inside it.

It is deafening the way a waterfall is deafening, or standing inside an engine room, except there is no single source to turn away from. The roar comes from the ground, from overhead, from inside the trunks. Water hissing through a billion hollow stems. Leaves striking leaves in a high, cold wind. Valves in the great roots opening and slamming shut, deep enough to feel in your molars. New wood cracking as it grows. On top of it all a low, continuous boom, almost below hearing, that rises and falls in slow waves across the country, as if the whole landmass were breathing through a reed.

You have to shout to be heard two feet away. After an hour your own voice feels irrelevant.

This is a high place, and a new one. A blister of fresh crust lifted thousands of meters above a barren lowland that is still mostly steaming basalt and ash, a continent-sized plateau still being pushed up from underneath, millimeter by millimeter. The rock here has had no time to be worn down. Where it shows at all — in a sudden fault scarp, in the bed of a new crack — it is black, glass-sharp, unweathered, with edges that cut. There are no rounded mountains, no old river valleys, no soil in any honest sense.

Because the plants got there before erosion could.

They cover everything. Not forest on land, but land made of forest. There is no ground to find. You walk, if you can walk at all, on a springy mat of interwoven roots and fallen fronds and living moss, sometimes ankle-deep in cold water, sometimes suspended ten or twenty meters above the actual stone on a lattice of trunks that have grown into each other until species stop meaning anything. Below that lattice are flooded caverns of shade where water sheets slowly over root-walls. Above it, layer upon layer: reeds packed stem-to-stem, shrubs with leaves like wet hands, fern trees, canopy giants whose crowns have fused into a single uneven roof that stretches out of sight in every direction.

There is no empty space. Every surface is occupied, and then occupied again. Trunks carry gardens. Leaves carry smaller leaves. Standing water carries floating mats that carry miniature forests no taller than your shin. Even the freshest, hottest cracks in the basalt fill within days — first with a white mineral fuzz, then green, then a dense, steaming thicket forcing its way up as the crack widens. The air itself is packed: mist, spray, drifting spores, pollen, fragments of leaf, droplets condensing on your skin and clothes faster than they can evaporate.

It is cool, always. Eight, ten degrees, even at midday. The light is dim and green-grey. The plateau makes its own clouds — a permanent low ceiling exhaled by the canopy, trapped by the altitude, lit from above but never quite admitting sun. What light reaches the floor comes filtered through a dozen layers of translucent leaf. In the lower levels it is a perpetual twilight where everything drips.

And everything is wet, but not from rain, exactly. The plateau *makes* its weather. In the morning the whole system exhales at once and fog fills the canopy spaces until you cannot see your hands. By midday the upper leaves warm a fraction, the lower roots pull hard, and water begins to move upward in quantities that should require impossible pressures — silent inside the xylem, deafening where the great trunks vent it through spray-pores to cool themselves. It condenses on the cold underside of the cloud deck and falls back as a fine, internal rain that never reaches the outside world. In the afternoon the temperature difference between the sun-warmed cloud top and the shaded interior drives slow, rotating winds across hundreds of kilometers, entirely contained within and above the vegetation. Storms are born here, travel a thousand kilometers, and die here, having never touched another landscape.

There are no animals. None. No insects in the air despite the flowers — and there are flowers, huge and small, opening and closing in coordinated pulses that sweep across entire provinces in minutes. No birds in the canopy. No worms in the mat. Decomposition, pollination, seed dispersal: all of it done by the plants themselves, or by wind, or by water, or by mechanisms that don't quite fit either category. A dead trunk does not rot and wait to be eaten. The roots of its neighbors grow into it within hours and pull it apart cell by cell, reclaiming it. Nothing is wasted because nothing else was invited to the feast.

That is the strangeness that accumulates, the longer you stay. Not that it's alien — almost everything here is recognizably leaf, bark, root, seed — but that it behaves with a coordination and an efficiency just past what biology should be able to do.

Leaves turn in unison on a windless morning, tracking a light source that hasn't risen yet. A change in chemistry on the far western escarpment — a new mineral vent breaking open — produces a change in leaf color on the eastern edge less than a second later, as if distance were a suggestion. Water flows uphill in open channels, cold to warm, against gradients, gathering in reservoirs in the canopy during droughts that exist nowhere but in the system's own forecast. Local pockets of air become suddenly rich in oxygen around a climber who is suffocating in the thin altitude, while a meter away the air is heavy with CO2 being hoarded for a growth surge. Fire will not stay lit here; the vegetation around a flame simply withdraws oxygen from that cubic meter of air until it gutters out, then floods it back again.

There are no buildings. No towers, no cables, no panels, no machines buried under the roots in any shape you'd recognize. The technology is not *in* the landscape. The landscape is the technology: a single, distributed, planetary-scale engine built out of living tissue, seeded onto raw rock and left to construct its own support systems. Every cell carries engineered structures smaller than organelles — assemblers that spin silica into leaf-armor, split minerals directly from basalt, fix nitrogen and rarer elements in proportions no natural forest could sustain, and pass information along root networks in ways that seem to ignore the time it should take to cross a continent. It is building soil where there was none, at roughly a centimeter a year, laying down its own dead as a controlled substrate. It is thickening the atmosphere above itself. It is stabilizing the uplift, holding the sharp new stone together with root tension so it cannot landslide.

In other words, it is not finished. This is not an ecosystem in balance. It is an ecosystem in *preparation*.

Walk far enough — and you cannot walk far, you push and climb and wade, you follow the rare natural tunnels where water has forced a passage through the growth — and the zones change, but they never thin. Near the rising fault lines it's all pioneers: low, fast, metallic-leaved things drinking directly from mineral seeps, their growth audible as a continuous ticking and splitting. In the older central basin they've had time to build upward, and the trunks are hundreds of meters around, not single trees but merged colonies, their interiors hollow and full of falling water. On the windward rim, everything grows flattened and interlocked into a living wall that breaks the high-altitude gales into that endless roaring music, feeding the energy back into flexing stems that generate — something. Warmth, maybe. Or charge. The leaves there spark faintly on dry nights, except there are no dry nights.

Time here is not measured in seasons so much as pulses. A growth pulse rolls across the plateau over weeks: buds swelling, sound rising to a pitch that makes your chest ache, a visible shiver in the canopy as millions of tons of new tissue extend at once. Then a drinking pulse, when the water table in the root-mat drops and the pools shrink and the mist thins and for a day or two the light gets almost bright. Then an exhaling pulse, and the rains inside begin again.

Nothing lives here yet that could appreciate it. That's deliberate, you come to understand. Animals would be a complication — grazers, predators, disease, uncontrolled consumption — in a system still laying down its foundations. First the world has to be made habitable: soil deep enough, air stable enough, water clean and cycled, poisons from the new crust locked away in wood and crystal. Then, presumably, something else can be introduced.

For now there is only this: a vast, cool, dim, water-loud continent of vegetation growing on a mountain that is still being born, too dense to cross, too loud to think in, patiently and impossibly engineering itself into a place where a future forest could one day simply be wild.
