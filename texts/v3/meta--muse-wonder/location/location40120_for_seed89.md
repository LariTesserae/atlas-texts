It begins as a crack in the mountain, almost too narrow to notice.

High enough that the outside world has thinned to its essentials — black rock, wind, a few cushion-plants pressed flat into the gravel, snow that does not so much fall as arrive sideways — there is a vertical fissure in the summit stone, perhaps two hands wide at the top. No path leads to it. No wall, no cairn, no cut step marks it. From a few paces away it is only another shadow in the broken ridge.

But the shadow breathes.

Warm air rises out of it, continuously, even when the wind on the ridge is cold enough to numb skin through cloth. The air smells dense and animal: ammonia and wet feathers, sweet rot, iron, old droppings, warm stone. And from below comes sound, not as an occasional call but as a condition of the place, a packed, many-layered noise that travels up the shaft and sets the loose grit at the rim faintly trembling.

The fissure widens as it descends. Twelve, fifteen meters down, it opens into a single chamber.

The chamber is not large. You could walk its length in forty paces, if walking were possible. It is maybe twelve meters across at its widest, with a vaulted ceiling lost entirely in darkness, walls of limestone scalloped and fluted by a very long patience of water and acid. It has the proportions of a small cathedral, and the same kind of enclosure — except a cathedral is meant to be empty enough for sound to travel. This place is full.

Darkness here is almost complete. For a few minutes near noon, when the sun lines up with the crack above, a thin, dusty blade of light cuts down and touches one wall, and in that fringe there is a sparse, bright border of life: wet moss, a film of green algae, three or four ferns with pale, unfurling tips, lichen in rust and sulfur patches on the stone just beyond the drip-line. The rest of the year, the rest of the day, nothing. The light does not reach the floor. It has never reached the floor.

Yet the chamber is warm. Not with sunlight, but with bodies, and with decay.

The ceiling belongs to bats. They hang in clusters so dense they stop looking like individuals — thousands of free-tailed bats packed shoulder to shoulder, fur to fur, a single dark, shifting pelage stretched across the rock, punctuated by ears, by folded wings, by the constant small adjustments of animals sleeping against one another. Their collective heat rises, meets the cold meltwater seeping through the limestone, and condenses. The ceiling rains slowly, all the time, drop by drop, though it almost never truly rains inside. Each drop falls through warm, scented air, lands on guano, on stone, on fur, and is audible because everything here is audible.

Just below them, in the cracks and ledges of the upper walls, echolocating swiftlets roost in a second band. Their nests are minimal things — stiff, translucent cups of saliva and feather pressed into the rock — and they crowd so closely that departing birds brush the ones still clinging. Their clicking never entirely stops. It comes in waves, a dry, rapid patter like rain on gravel, building until the whole chamber seems to be ticking, then subsiding to a murmur. The sound does what light cannot. It tells them the shape of the room. It tells you, too, if you stay still long enough: the space feels larger by ear than by hand, the echoes returning softened and delayed from walls hidden behind living bodies.

Lower down, the walls belong to insects.

Cave crickets with antennae longer than their bodies pick their way over the wet stone, pale and jointed, feeling ahead of themselves. Spiders wait in irregular webs strung between ledges, their threads beaded with condensation and gnat wings. Centipedes, some as long as a forearm, move through the lower dark with an unhurried, rippling certainty, hunting cockroaches in the crevices. The stone is seldom visible. It moves, minutely, under overlapping populations.

The floor is the densest part of all, and the slowest.

It is not really a floor. It is guano, deep enough in the center of the chamber to take a leg to the knee, accumulated over centuries into strata — black and fresh at the surface, then reddish, then gray, then pale and crumbling with age and bone. It steams faintly in the cold drips from above. It breathes out heat as it decomposes. And it is inhabited at every level: beetles burrowing and rolling it into balls, cockroaches in the tens of thousands turning it over, millipedes threading its underside, fly larvae in the wettest seams, mites in numbers beyond estimating. A low, continuous hum rises from it, part wing, part chewing, part the soft collapse of pellets falling from the ceiling. Every few seconds something lands. Every few seconds something else eats it.

At the edges of the chamber, where the guano thins against rock, water gathers — never a stream, never anything that flows properly. Just pockets. Three or four shallow basins worn glass-smooth into the limestone by centuries of dripping, each holding black, still water a few inches deep. Condensation and snowmelt keep them alive. Blind, translucent shrimp graze the guano-film on their surfaces, jerking backward from any vibration. Nothing larger lives in them. Almost everything in the chamber comes to them eventually to drink, and so their rims are polished even smoother by tongues and feet and the soft abrasion of bodies, and flecked with white droppings that the next drip will slowly dissolve.

At the one place where the falling light sometimes reaches, near the wall under the shaft, there is a flat stone, unworked, unmoved for as long as anything has used it — and choughs from the ridge outside use it. They come down at dawn with snails and hard seeds wedged in their beaks, drop them onto the stone from a height, descend, and pick at the cracked shells. A scatter of old shell fragments rings the basin beside it. It is the only technology the place possesses: a stone that is a tool because generations of birds have agreed, without building anything, that it is one.

The rules here are not the rules of the mountaintop outside. Outside, everything is distance, exposure, wind, scarcity. Inside, everything is contact. Vision is useless; stillness matters. If you move quickly, the room answers — a burst of clicks, a loosening of wings overhead, a ripple through the crickets, a hot, sour updraft from the disturbed floor, gnats finding your eyes and mouth within seconds. If you stay still long enough, the room closes back over you. Crickets' antennae explore your boots. A spider lets a thread settle across your sleeve. The bats resume the small, dense rustling that is their version of silence, which is not silent at all.

Time moves slowly, even when the noise does not. The drips fall on intervals you can count between. The guano deepens by almost nothing each year. The limestone loses itself to acid and claw and dissolving droppings over durations no single animal here will witness. Even the great daily event of the chamber — the evening departure — happens gradually, a long unspooling. First the swiftlets, spiraling up the shaft in a clicking column that lasts nearly an hour because the crack only allows so many at a time; then, as the last light leaves the ridge, the bats, pouring upward in a warm, leathery river, while below them the centipedes emerge and the crickets grow bolder and the floor's hum changes pitch, predator by predator. Hours later they return the same way, in reverse, bringing the outside world in on their bodies: cold air, insects, seeds, dust from valleys far below that almost nothing else at this altitude ever touches.

That is, in the end, how the place feeds itself. It is a warm, dark pocket held open at the top of the world, too high and too barren to support this much life on its own — so the life commutes. It hunts and grazes in the thin bright world outside, and it sleeps, breeds, dies, and rots in here, and the chamber grows imperceptibly richer and warmer and louder on the difference.

Step to the rim at dusk and the mountain seems empty: rock, wind, a few dark plants, snow beginning to tick against stone. Put your ear near the crack, and the summit is the most crowded place for a vertical mile in any direction — a single room, packed wall to wall and ceiling to floor, roaring softly in the dark.
