The first thing is the sound, and it arrives before the place does.

You come down into the basin on a road cut straight across black stone, and halfway down the slope the air changes. It gets warm against your face, wet enough to condense on your skin and on the basalt beside you, and the noise rises up out of the hollow in a solid wall. Not a noise that starts and stops. A roar, layered. Underneath it a low, continuous rushing, as if a river were falling somewhere under the ground. Above that, thousands — tens of thousands — of frogs. Above that, higher still, a grinding, shrilling insect hum from every warm wall and canal edge. It presses in your ears, in your teeth. You can feel conversations happening around you on the road, mouths moving close to ears, hands flashing, and you cannot hear a word of it.

The basin itself is new. That is obvious even through the steam.

It is a broad, shallow caldera floor, maybe eight miles across, almost perfectly flat, made of lava that has not yet learned how to be old ground. No hills have been worn down here, no river has had time to cut a valley. The rock is still black and rust-red and sharp at the edges, sheet upon sheet of basalt and pumice and glassy slag, unsoftened. Water does not run in bends yet. It spreads. It seeps up through cracks, gathers in warm, ankle-deep sheets, stands in rectangular canals the people have dug because nature had not yet dug any channels of its own.

And over it, packed nearly edge to edge, is a town.

No green softens it. The lava sterilized everything when it came, and the ground is still too hot in places, too mineral, too freshly poisoned with salts for soil to form. There are no trees, no gardens, no grass in the streets, no moss except a slick, dark film where water stands permanently. From the rim the town is all mineral color: black stone, grey stone, whitewashed lime, red tile made from baked ash, the tan of hide awnings. Even the food markets have no bright produce heaped up. They have fish. They have snails. They have pale mushrooms grown in cellars. They have dried meat brought in on pack animals from outside the basin, and grain in sacks, grey-brown and dusty.

The light hangs dim under a permanent cap of steam.

Warm water meets warm air everywhere — from the fissure at the far end of the basin where a spring throws itself into a hot sinkhole and boils back up as vapor, from the canals, from the bathhouses, from a hundred shallow geothermal pools that never cool. The vapor rises, spreads, and stops, trapped by the rim. The sun is a bright smear somewhere above it, never direct. At noon it is a heavy twilight. At morning and evening it is the same heavy twilight, only a little thinner at the edges. Lamps are lit in the houses all day.

Inside that dimness the town is dense to the point of touching.

Houses of hand-cut basalt block, two and three stories high, share walls. Their roofs are flat or shallow-pitched with stone tile, dripping constantly where condensed steam runs off. Alleys between them are sometimes no wider than your shoulders. You pass doors hung with leather curtains, windows shuttered with woven hair and bone slats, courtyards roofed over entirely to make a second, interior sky. Every wall is used. Swifts nest in holes intentionally left under the eaves, hundreds of them, coming and going in silent dark streams because their calls are lost in the larger noise. Bats roost in the bathhouse rafters. Lizards run on the sun-warmed — ground-warmed — stone even in the shade.

The canals are the streets that matter most. They run straight as rulers, block by block, black water warm to the touch, filmed with algae. And they are alive in a way that explains the sound and sustains it. Frogs sit shoulder to shoulder on the canal lips: small brown ones, large mottled bullfrogs, bright-throated toads wedged into the mortar joints. They call without pause. Not in waves, not in a night chorus that builds and fades. Continuously. The water is always the same temperature, fed from below, so there is no breeding season. There is no cold to stop them, no dry season to thin them. Tadpoles in every stage cloud the shallows at once. Fish mouth at the surface between them — carp-like, whiskered things that eat the algae mats and the insect larvae. Herons stand statue-still along the aqueducts. Ducks push through the market canals picking at snails, entirely unbothered by the crowds pushing past.

Insects fill what the frogs leave. Crickets that live in the warm cracks of houses and feed on refuse and fungal growth sing from inside the walls themselves; a house at close range seems to vibrate with them. Midges hang over standing water in visible columns despite the frogs. That is why the frogs are protected. It is not sentiment. The town's law, carved in white stone at every canal bridge, forbids killing a frog or draining a pond without the work-gang's mark. Remove them for a week and the air would be solid with biting flies. The swifts are protected the same way. So are the bats. People, animals, and buildings are not separate populations here. They are one density, layered into the same warm volume.

That density demands a different kind of human life, and the town has invented it with only simple tools.

There is almost no metal. Knives are obsidian and flaked basalt, hafted with sinew. Pots are stone and fired clay. Water is lifted by hand-cranked bucket chains, aqueducts are gravity-fed from the high spring tanks, mortar is lime and ash mixed in pits and carried in hide slings. What wood exists has been hauled in from beyond the rim and is precious — roof beams reused from house to house, doors patched for generations.

And almost no one speaks outside.

Outside speech is useless. Children learn hand-sign before they learn to talk properly; there is a whole grammar for the markets — price, weight, quality, insult, joke — flickered across a stall counter while fish slap in a basket beside you. Street signs are not written so much as carved and inlaid: a tile fish, a tile hammer, a red hand for a house where the floor is too hot to stand on. Porters wear bells only for the blind, and even then you feel the bell more than hear it. Most adults go out with plugs of waxed wool or carved bone in their ears, and their faces have the calm, inward look of people who live inside their own muffled heads for much of the day.

Indoors is where voices live. Houses are built double-walled, with a dead air space stuffed with ash and pumice between the outer basalt and the inner plaster. Courtyards are roofed and then roofed again. You duck through a leather door, then a second leather door, and the roar drops — not to silence, never to silence, you can still feel the frogs through the floor — but to a level where two people sitting knee to knee can shout companionably and be understood. Kitchens, workshops, sleeping rooms all huddle around these cores. People tell stories indoors. They argue indoors. They sing indoors, close and low, more for the vibration in the chest than for anyone across a room.

The smell indoors is wet plaster, cooking oil, fish, animal skin, and the faint sulfur that is everywhere outdoors too, in the water, on your clothes, in the back of your throat. The ground is warm through thin sandals in certain streets, uncomfortably warm in others where the new rock is thinnest over the heat below; those streets are marked, and houses there have raised stone floors on stubby pillars, with cool canal water run underneath in summer — though summer is a word imported from outside. Nothing here changes enough to need seasons named.

That is the strangest part, once you have stayed a while. Nothing changes, and it does not change at a level beneath weather.

The spring at the sinkhole has roared at the same volume within living memory and beyond it; the old people carve tally marks for years but not for rainfall or flood, because the water level in the canals is set by sluice gates and does not rise or fall. The steam cap thickens a little at night and thins a little by day, but the temperature on the caldera floor when you step out of a house is the temperature you stepped into last month. The frogs do not stop at dawn. The insects do not stop in winter. Work is measured not by light but by water clocks in the gang houses and by shifts of porters at the rim road, because appetites and sleep are the only reliable cycles left.

New construction looks exactly like old construction, partly because the tools are the same, but mostly because the stone is all the same age. There are no ruins. A house abandoned for a generation has no vines to take it, no soil to fill its rooms; it just stands, dripping, with swifts in its roof, until someone knocks it down for block. Erosion has not started its slow argument with the town. Rain — and warm rain does fall, straight down out of the steam, almost daily — runs off the roofs and the flat lava and into the canals without cutting anything. The place feels embryonic and finished at the same time: a city built on ground that was molten within ancestral memory, still settling into being land, supporting a life that has already become completely, deafeningly settled on top of it.

At the far edge of town, beyond the last bathhouse, the canals end in settling ponds where boys wade with nets, shouting silently at each other in sign. Beyond that the black floor goes on empty for a mile to the fissure itself, where the spring water pours over a lip of fresh, glassy rock and vanishes into heat with a noise so large it stops seeming like noise and becomes a kind of pressure in the landscape — the bass note under the frogs, under the insects, under everything. Herons nest on the warm ledges nearby, in the dimness, among rising vapor. Nothing grows there at all. The water flashes, the rock steams, the sound continues, exactly as loud as it was when you arrived, exactly as loud as it will be when you climb back up the rim road and, an hour later, still ringing, realize you have begun to hear birds again somewhere far above, in a colder and much quieter world.
