It is called the Pan, and you can smell it long before you can see it.

The trail up is ordinary high desert — thin air, red pumice, basalt broken into plates that ring faintly underfoot. No grass. No scrub. Even lichen gives up a few hundred feet below. The wind up here has polished everything for a very long time: stones rounded without water, cairns sunk and re-stacked and sunk again, three low windbreak walls of unmortared rock. They are the only built things. No one built well here, or stayed. Beside the last cairn there is a shallow stone bowl, empty, polished inside from hands. Beside it the cut steps begin.

The Pan is not a summit. It is what the summit is hiding.

Thirty, perhaps forty paces across. A bowl sunk into the peak, then roofed — not completely roofed, but lidded by fallen slabs and its own overhanging rim, so that the sun, which is brutal and white on the trail, never reaches the floor. You descend the chipped steps from glare into a darkness that does not lift at noon. At first it seems the bowl is empty and still.

Then your eyes adjust, and then your ears, and then you understand there is no floor.

The whole bottom of the Pan is moving.

###

Wall to wall, rim to vent, packed shell against shell, leg against antenna, is life at a density that stops looking like animals and becomes a surface. Blind cave crickets the length of a finger, their antennae three times their bodies, stand six and eight deep. Under them, slower, armored millipedes flat as coins. Under them beetles with fused, matte-black wing cases. Under them — you know only because they sometimes surface, pale and eyeless and sighing open along their sides — soft worm-things that can bear the heat coming directly out of the stone.

There is no bare ground to step on. The ground *is* the compacted molt of ten thousand generations: chitin layered and pressed and heated until it has become a kind of dark horn, springy underfoot, warm through the sole of a shoe. When you do step, the surface does not scatter. There is nowhere to scatter to. It parts in a slow, reluctant ripple, closes behind your ankle, and immediately begins to climb, seeking the moisture of your skin and breath.

Because there is almost no water here. No spring, no seep. high on the back wall, where cold mountain air meets the hot air rising from below, a stain of condensation darkens the rock an inch wide and never drips. At night it is visited in strict, silent rotation. Everything else lives on what it can make from itself, from each other, and from the thin bacterial films that crust the fumaroles — sulfur-yellow, bone-white, iron-orange mats, simpler than any plant, slick as mineral and faintly luminous only by contrast with the dark. They are the meadow. The first layers graze them. The next layers graze the grazers.

It is hot down there in a way the altitude should forbid. Outside the rim the air can cut with cold. Inside, the stone breathes heat in pulses. The central vents — three low cones of sulfur crystal and glassy black obsidian — exhale without quite hissing, and the temperature at the center of the floor would cook meat. Nothing stays at the center.

That is the rule that organises everything.

The carpet rotates. Not panicked, not fast. A vast, almost imperceptible spiral, in at the cool edges, out along a warmer current, turning, turning, so that the animals baking at the bottom of the pile are lifted, carried outward on the backs of others, cooled for a while against the wall, then drawn in again. Towers form where the current stalls — hanging clusters from the low ceiling too, bodies locked leg-to-leg like grapes, swaying to shed heat — then dissolve and reform a pace away. If you watch long enough, your own position in the spiral becomes obvious: the floor is doing a slow folk dance to keep from dying of its own abundance.

On top of the carpet ride the predators. Hand-long centipedes, the color of wet ash, surfing rather than walking, their many feet barely dimpling the backs below them. They take crickets without breaking the rotation. A ripple, a brief lifting of heads toward nothing, and then the surface smooths. The preyed-upon do not cry out. Nothing here cries out.

That is the other strangeness. For all this motion — millions of mouths, claws ticking on shell, wings that no longer fly rasping in their cases, the vents sighing, a stone sometimes loosened from the rim and falling muffled into bodies — the Pan is mostly quiet. The bodies absorb sound the way they absorb light. You hear your own pulse indecently loud. You hear the scratch of your clothing turn, below, into an answering scratch.

And periodically, without warning, it all stops.

Every animal, on floor and wall and ceiling, freezes at once. Antennae halt mid-wave. The centipedes flatten. The spiral holds its breath. Five seconds. Twenty. The silence becomes total except for the heat working in the rock. Then, as if a single instruction had been lifted, the carpet resumes exactly where it left off, the same leg completing the same step. No tremor a human can feel precedes it. No sound follows it. It happens in bright morning above and in starless night below with the same indifference, and the animals waiting at the condensation stain do not use the pause to cheat the rotation.

At the coolest edge, where the steps come down, someone long ago tried to make a second set of concentric rings out of stone, knee-height, perhaps to hold back the tide or to mark where a person could stand and not be taken into the current. The rings have failed in the way everything fails here: not smashed, but polished, subsided, overgrown with molt, full to the lip with patient crickets. The sulfur mats have grown over the lowest stones. The vents have not moved in living memory, and neither, really, has the bowl. It is a finished place, in equilibrium — mountain, heat, bacteria, hunger — wearing itself smooth one body at a time.

You do not stay long. The air is thin and sulfurous and it gives you a headache behind the eyes; the heat climbs your legs though the stone under you is alive and shifting; things are in your cuffs, gentle, tasting, looking for the salt of sweat, which here is a kind of rain. When you climb back up the steps, they go with you for a surprising distance into the light, slowing as they cool, dropping off one by one onto the red pumice.

Behind you, in the dark that the sun cannot enter, the floor closes over the place where you stood, and keeps turning.
