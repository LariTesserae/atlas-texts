There is a mountain that has forgotten it was ever tall.

From a distance it is only a table — high, broad, planed down by an amount of time that makes volcanoes and ranges look temporary. Its top is a peneplain, mile after mile of old basalt worn to low rolls and shallow pans, with no peak left to catch the eye. Nothing sharp has survived up there. The stones are rounded to the shape of river stones, though no river runs there now. The passes are rounded. Even the fractures have softened and sealed.

Under the middle of that table, the mountain is hollow.

You reach it by a descending gallery, hands on a guide-rail bolted into the wall, and the last of the daylight does not fade behind you so much as get taken. At first there is dimness, then less, then a darkness that has weight. A lantern brought in goes out without flickering. An open phone screen thins to a brown smear and dies, and for a moment your palm feels warmer and there is a low note, almost below hearing, in your teeth. The darkness here is not an absence that lights can fill. It is maintained.

The chamber beyond is perhaps ninety meters across — cathedral-scale, hamlet-scale, small enough that your own footsteps tell you where the far wall is, large enough that most of it is nothing. That is the first thing your body learns. You stand in an enormous emptiness. Warm air moves very slowly against your face. The space above you, and to the sides, and across the black pool at the center, is vacant. Whatever lives here crowds the margins and leaves the middle alone.

It is warm, in a way the high country outside has no right to be. Twenty-four degrees, maybe twenty-five, and wet enough that your skin never dries. Moisture condenses on your forearms. The rock weeps. Water finds its way through the old basalt in seams too fine to see and emerges everywhere at the walls, sheeting over travertine, ticking from the ceiling at intervals so long you count your breaths between drops, then lose count. In places the floor is bare stone, polished by dripping into shallow cups that overflow into one another. In places it gives underfoot like a soaked mattress: moss, leaf-mold, mineral crust, soil held together by root.

The built things are few and they explain, without explaining themselves, why any of this persists. Three pylons stand at irregular intervals around the central pool, none of them taller than a person, all of them leaned a degree or two off true by the patient settling of the floor. They are made of a matte ceramic-like material that feels warm and faintly alive to the touch, unmarked by the water, unscarred, though the stone plinths beneath them have been eaten to lace. A fourth pylon lies on its side in the shallows, still warm, half-buried in silt. There is a low, broken ring of the same material near the south wall, and a hut of fitted stone that has slumped into its own doorway, and lengths of rail, and flat benches worn concave where unknown sitters once waited in the dark. Occasional artifacts turn up under the moss — a cup, a sealed case, a tool with no edge and no buttons that grows slightly warmer when you hold it and slightly louder, as if agreeing.

The pylons, and the lattice buried under the soil that you can sometimes feel humming through the soles of your feet, take the sun that falls on the dead mountain above and refuse to let any of it arrive as light. They drink the whole spectrum that eyes would use, and pay it out in other currencies: heat in the rock, a slow circulation of air, minerals drawn up through hair-fine conduits in the soil, sugars delivered directly to roots in pulses that come hours apart. Anything in the chamber that tries to make light of its own — a lamp, a spark, a phosphorescent fungus — is harvested the same way, converted downward into warmth and a deep, building hum. That hum is the baseline of the place. On top of it, the world is loud.

Sound does all the work here that light does elsewhere, and it does it generously. Because the chamber is so empty, and because its walls were worn smooth a million years before anything built the pylons, every noise travels whole. A drop falling forty meters into the pool arrives as a clean, ringing *tock* that circles the room twice. The frogs at the marshy edge call in overlapping phrases, each one waiting for the echo of the last to clear before answering, so that a chorus becomes a kind of slow conversation. There are thousands of them, thumb-sized, blind, their skins cool and granular, and there are salamanders in the side pools with feathery external gills that wave when you speak near the water. Swiftlets nest in the ceiling seams and commute through the dark on streams of clicks, their wings making a dry rain sound overhead. Moths the size of your hand blunder softly against leaves and skin, drawn by scent and vibration. Under the leaf litter at the walls, the invertebrate life is so dense the ground seems to shift in place — pale centipedes, snails with soft translucent shells, beetles that drum the undersides of leaves with their abdomens in a ticking that rises and falls like distant machinery.

You notice them all at the margins. Step toward the center, onto the open stone apron around the pool, and abundance falls away. The air is empty. The water is empty — black, mineral, thigh-deep at the middle, with a bottom of silt so fine it holds a footprint for days. Almost nothing crosses the middle except the swiftlets, high up, and the slow rain.

For the vegetation the darkness is not a hardship; it is the original condition, and it has been growing into it for a very long time. What grows here grows black, or so close to black that touch has to correct the guess — deep maroon ferns shoulder-high at the walls, with fronds that unroll over weeks and feel waxed on top and suede beneath; trees that are not quite trees, their trunks fluted and soft-barked, putting out broad leaves that turn not toward light but toward the warm vents in the floor, reorienting a few degrees a day; curtains of aerial roots descending from a ceiling canopy no one has ever seen, ending at head height in damp, exploring tips. Moss covers the old stone in cushions a hand sinks into. There are flowers, folded tight, that never open to be looked at. They open by loosening, at nightless intervals, and release a scent so concentrated — rot-sweet, green, animal — that you can locate each bloom the way you would locate a voice. The moths find them in seconds. Pollen drifts down through the empty air and skins the pool.

Everything grows slowly, and everything recurs slowly. The chamber has a weather with a period of roughly a day, though no sun ever marks it. For hours the condensation gathers, the dripping thins, the frogs go quiet one territory at a time, and the hum of the lattice sinks until the room is almost still except for the swiftlets. Then the ceiling lets go. Rain comes straight down through the dark in one warm, roaring minute, rattling the fern leaves, hammering the pool, waking every calling thing at once into a volume that presses on your ears, and then it stops, and the long gathering begins again. The plants stiffen during the rain and relax after it. The pylons tick as they cool. Outside that minute, almost nothing in the chamber changes at any speed a visitor can witness. A fern frond you marked with a thread will have moved by your next visit, not during it.

The rules, once you have been there a while, feel less like rules than like the place declining to be otherwise. Do not bring light; it will be eaten, and the eating will warm you and make the dark seem darker. Walk by the rails and by the changes in flooring — flagstone, then gravel that sings underfoot, then boardwalk over the marsh — and by the territories of frog-song, which are as fixed as street names. Speak at a moderate volume or the echoes smear into mush and the swiftlets falter; shout, and leaves for meters around fold shut and stay shut for an hour. Do not expect to be watched back. Almost nothing here has eyes worth the name, and the things that hunt do it by listening to the water move around your legs.

When you leave, climbing the gallery with one hand on the rail, the first gray seep of daylight at the entrance shaft looks less like an increase in light than like the world resuming a habit this one room, high in an ancient, flattened mountain, opted out of — and kept opting out of, warmly, wetly, loudly, in the lush and crowded margins of its own great emptiness, long after whoever taught it how had gone.
