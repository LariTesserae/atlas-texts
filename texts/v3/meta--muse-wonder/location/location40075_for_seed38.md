It is a high place that has forgotten it was ever mountains.

Once — so long ago that the rock itself seems tired of remembering — there were peaks here. They have been worn down to almost nothing. What remains is a plateau, continent-wide, lifted far above the lowlands, and so thoroughly eroded that its surface rolls only in the gentlest swells, a few meters of rise and fall across a day's walk. No cliffs. No gorges. No sharp stone. Only here and there a low hump of pale quartz breaks through, polished smooth as a knuckle bone, the hardest core of some former summit. Everything else has settled into its final form.

And over that final form, plants have closed completely.

There is no soil to find, not in the ordinary sense. There is plant on plant on plant. A living crust, then a spongy layer of moss and fallen leaves still holding their shape, then wetter and blacker layers, then peat that goes down ten, twenty, thirty meters, still faintly warm from its own slow rotting. You walk on roots and stems and compressed centuries of leaves. The ground gives underfoot and slowly rises back after you pass. In places it quivers. In places it is hollow, and your step makes a soft, drum-like thud over a chamber roofed with interwoven roots.

Above you, it closes again. This is not a forest with clearings. It is a continuous mass with different densities. Low trees whose trunks fuse into one another, reed-like growths thirty meters tall braided together by vines, fern trees, moss pillars, hanging gardens that root in mid-air on other plants. Their canopies knit into a green ceiling far overhead, and beneath that ceiling a second and third ceiling, so the light that arrives at the ground is dim, green-gray, directionless. It is warm here, surprisingly warm for the altitude — the living mass holds heat the way a body does, and exhales it constantly as vapor. The air is thick, wet, close against the skin. It smells of leaf mold, rain, tannin, warm resin, and the faint iron smell of black water.

Water is never far. It is, in fact, the condition of the place.

Rain falls, but rain is hardly distinguishable from the other forms water takes: condensation dripping in slow beads from every leaf underside, mist beading on hair and bark, fog that condenses directly onto cold moss in the night and runs in threads to the ground. The plateau makes its own weather. By late morning the warm peat and leaves transpire so much moisture that clouds form *inside* the forest, between the lower and upper canopies, and drift horizontally. By afternoon there is a luminous gray suspension in which trunks appear and vanish at twenty paces. A drizzle may begin and continue for nine days, not falling so much as materializing, until every hollow is brimful.

Because the land is so old and so flat, water does not run off decisively. There are no true rivers on the top. There are chains of dark pools linked by seepage, broad sheet-marshes where water moves slower than a walking animal, boggy channels that reverse direction after a week of rain. The plant sponge holds it all. At the distant rim of the plateau, where in theory it should pour away, a dam of living rootwood a kilometer thick has grown across every outlet — not built, accumulated — and filters the outflow to a perpetual weeping. From below, the plateau edge is said to cry constantly.

The pools are black mirrors full of sky. Their water is stained almost opaque with leaf acids, and yet crowded underneath: pale blind fish nosing in peat, translucent shrimp, larvae that build cases from chewed leaf, frogs the size of thumbnails in colors of wet moss. Life is abundant here, but it has adapted to dimness, wetness, and muffling. Almost nothing calls loudly. Sound does not travel; the vegetation drinks it. You hear what is very near: a drip landing on a broad leaf with a soft *tok*, another, irregular. The faint rasp of a beetle through dry upper leaves of the peat. The submerged popping of gas bubbles rising through bog, one bubble every minute or so, from decay far below. Large animals move like weather systems — a family of squat, hairy grazers with splayed feet for the bog, pushing through fern walls, their chewing audible, their breath steaming. They do not vocalize. Birds exist in profusion but fly low and short between perches, flashing dull red or blue, communicating with wing-flicks and soft clicks rather than song. Insects make a presence less as noise than as touch: gnats in warm columns, moths as big as hands blundering gently against your face in the twilight, ants in covered highways inside hollow stems.

At night it is not much darker than by day, only a deeper green-black, and the dripping increases. Phosphorescent fungi stipple the undersides of the root-architecture, not bright, just enough to show where the ground drops into a pool. Animals come out to feed on them, and their eyes catch what little light there is, everywhere, patient and unstartled.

Here and there — days apart, if you know how to read a slight unnatural straightness in the growth — there are built things. They are few, and none of them are new. A ring of piled quartz stones, now so thoroughly grown through that the stones are held suspended in a wall of root after the lower ones have crumbled away. A rectangular hollow, plainly once a shelter, whose wooden walls have rotted out but whose shape is preserved by the plants that colonized them: a ghost house of moss with a doorway, a window, still slowly thickening. A carved basin in a quartz hump, perpetually full, now a nursery for frogs, its carved grooves softened to the texture of skin. A line of low markers crossing a marsh, followed faithfully by animal trails though whatever boundary it marked has been meaningless for a thousand years. Nothing metal. Nothing wheeled or fired or jointed with precision. Just stone put on stone, wood leaned on wood, and then the long collaboration of plants and water taking the shape and keeping it, long after the intention is gone.

The rules of the place emerge slowly, over weeks of travel:

Fire is not a possibility. Wood is either living or saturated. Death does not dry things here; it ferments them. A fallen giant does not bleach and harden, it softens standing, becomes a vertical bog, sprouts orchids and young trees from its own flanks, and subsides over decades into the floor it once rose from.

Direction is not a property of landmarks. The interior is too vast and too uniform for that. You navigate by gradients: a slight increase in a particular reed where water is fresher, the lean of a flowering vine toward a centuries-old light gap, the species of moss that means you are crossing one of the almost imperceptible watersheds. Experienced life here — animal and, presumably, the few humans who still wander it barefoot and tool-less, living in the ghost houses — reads vegetation the way others read stars.

Time is slow in proportion to scale. A storm system born over the center of the plateau may drift for a month before it reaches the rim and dissipates. A light-gap caused by a fallen canopy tree takes forty years to close, and in that brief bright interval a whole temporary ecology of sun-plants, butterflies, and nesting birds rises and completes itself and is shaded out again. Seeds that fall may wait a decade in the wet dark for the chemistry of the peat to change around them. Some of the low, gnarled trees on the quartz humps have been adding a ring of growth thinner than paper each year for longer than the stone markers have existed.

And perhaps the strangest rule, the one you feel in your knees and in your sleep: the plateau is still settling. Not eroding in any visible way, not rising — just exhaling, compacting, digesting its own past. Occasionally a whole acre of ground sighs down by a hand's breadth in a night as a buried layer collapses, and by morning the pools have rearranged themselves around the new hollow, the roots have already begun to stitch across it. The place is not being built up or torn down. It has arrived. Mountain, forest, marsh, and ruin have ground against each other for so long that they have become one material — high, warm, drowned, green, and quiet — extending to every horizon under its own low sky of leaf-filtered cloud, giving back, very slowly, the shape of whatever steps upon it.
