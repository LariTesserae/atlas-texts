You have to go down to reach it.

Not far down — thirty, forty meters below the plain — but the descent changes everything. At the surface there is only a lip of fresh black rock, cut so recently the edges are still sharp enough to catch skin, and a broad shaft going in. No weathering yet. No softening. Rain has not had time to round the stone, roots have not had time to pry it apart. The walls weep as you go down, warm water beading and running in bright threads over basalt and pale construction ceramic, and the air gets heavier, warmer, dimmer with every switchback of the stair.

At the bottom, the shaft opens into a hollow that should not be able to hold a city, and almost doesn't.

It is a borough the size of a hamlet, built inside a cathedral.

The chamber is one vast, undivided volume. Its ceiling is lost in warm haze. Its floor is sunken well below the world above, a shallow bowl of dark stone and white street-material still too new to be scuffed. Around the rim, rock rises in fresh, angular terraces. Through cracks in it, groundwater presses constantly, sheeting, dripping, ticking, finding channels the builders cut for it and channels it has invented for itself.

And in the middle of all that emptiness — streets.

A grid of them. Six, eight cross-streets of dense, deliberate urban construction, lamp-posts and transit rails and multi-storey facades, drainage grates, awnings, bridges between upper levels, service ducts, signage lit in soft amber. Buildings crowd shoulder to shoulder along the blocks, five and six stories high, made of a matte material somewhere between concrete, shell, and living bone that sweats in the humidity and quietly knits closed whenever a crack tries to form. Balconies. Recessed windows. Shopfronts at street level with transparent doors that sigh open as warm air moves past them, expecting a body.

There is no one in any of it.

That is the first rule of the place, and everything else grows around it. The city is urban in form and empty in fact. You can stand in the central intersection and see down four long streets at once and meet nothing. The buildings are shells around more emptiness — floor after floor of cool, dim rooms, unfurnished, faintly smelling of wet mineral and warm electronics, their windows glowing at a low evening level not because anyone has switched them on but because dusk is part of the cycle here, and the cycle continues. Far overhead, lightwells in the ceiling admit a filtered grey-green day from the surface, never direct, never bright. At certain hours angled collectors bend what light there is down in slow, visible shafts, and for a while the haze turns gold in columns. Then it dims again. It is always twilight at street level.

It is warm below the surface. Twenty-eight, twenty-nine degrees, close and enveloping. Condensation forms on every cooler surface: railings bead, signage blurs and clears, the lower leaves of the street trees drip without rain. The builders expected this. Indeed, the buildings drink it. Their facades are laced with capillary channels finer than hair that draw moisture out of the air and pull it, impossibly, upward against gravity, along walls, across ceilings, into roof cisterns you can hear filling behind the ceramic. Sometimes a wall will darken in a slow travelling wave as a hidden reservoir equalizes. Sometimes a dry street will begin, very gently, to run uphill with a film of water no pump could account for.

Because water is near the surface here in every sense. It comes through the rock, it falls as manufactured rain at intervals from misting lines strung between the rooftops — a warm, deliberate downpour that lasts eleven minutes and makes the whole chamber roar — and it gathers in a plaza at the lowest point of the bowl, where the streets give way to a round, open cistern.

The cistern plaza is the heart, and the loudest part.

Water pours into it from four carved spouts, white and aerated, and yet the basin never fills and never empties. Its surface holds perfectly level while somehow also draining continuously downward in the center, a slow, glassy vortex that descends into a lightless shaft without splash or sucking sound, carrying away exactly what arrives. Leaves that fall into it circle once, twice, and go down out of sight. Around its rim, broad steps go down into the shallows and stop. The sound there is constant: falling water, pumps cycling deep under the stone in a bass rhythm you feel in your molars, ventilation sighing through the street grates, the transit system running its empty route.

The transit is one silver car, windowless, driverless, big enough for twenty people, that glides on no visible rail down the main street every nine minutes. It slows at each stop. Its doors open with a chime. It waits the correct interval for passengers to board. It closes and moves on, humming, its passage stirring the hanging vines. No one has ever told it to stop doing this, so it doesn't.

Vegetation has been given the run of the infrastructure, deliberately, as if the city meant to be a garden that happened to have apartments. It is lush beyond what the dimness should support. The plants are engineered for it — broad, dark-green leaves efficient at low light, ferns that unroll from the seams between paving slabs where nutrient film is fed to them, climbing figs that have been taught the facades and grip them with pale aerial roots. Trees are planted at intersections in deep wells, species with leaves like hands and bark that stays wet and black, their canopies meeting over the narrower lanes. Mosses colonize the north sides of the ducts. Orchid-like flowers with no need for pollinators open along the balustrades, close, open again, drop their petals into the gutters where the street-wash carries them to the cistern. Fruit swells on a row of small trees by the eastern wall, ripens, softens, falls, and decays sweetly where it lands, feeding the soil beds. There are no insects on it. No birds in the canopy. No animal life at all but the life of the plants, breathing, transpiring, adding their own humidity to the air until the whole chamber smells green and mineral and faintly electric.

The absence is what makes the loudness strange. Every sound is mechanical or hydraulic or botanical — water hammer in the pipes, the tick of the buildings expanding in the warmth, the pop and creak of fast-growing stems, a construction gantry high on the unfinished ninth block extending a new beam with great hydraulic deliberation, mating it to a wall that rearranges its own surface to receive it. The city is still building itself, slowly, in its upper corners, following a plan. A facade will spend a week migrating a window two meters to the left, the material flowing in a way that makes masons' language useless. A bridge extrudes itself across an alley overnight and is wet and perfect in the morning. It is young, all of it. Nothing has been allowed to erode. Self-repair is faster than decay.

Walk far enough down any street and you reach a boundary surprisingly soon. That is the other surprise — how small it is, for all its density. Ten minutes crosses it. Beyond the last lit block the streets simply end in rising rock, or in a service door into warm dark tunnels where pipes sweat and recede. The city has edges like a room has edges. It was not built to sprawl. It was built to be complete at exactly this size, a single, sealed idea of urban life, lowered into a hollow in the earth and switched on.

And it waits, if waiting is the word for systems doing what they were made to do without reference to use. Kitchens in the empty apartments run water through their taps for thirty seconds each morning to keep the lines clear, and you can see it sheeting in the lit windows, draining away. Lifts rise and fall in their shafts on inspection cycles, opening onto empty landings. Doors unlock at seven and lock at twenty-three. Mist falls. The car arrives. The cistern takes the river and gives back silence at its center. The trees drip. The gantry builds another meter of a street no feet will wear down.

From the rim, looking back in from the access stair, it reads less like a ruin than like a held breath: a dense little metropolis in a warm, wet, dim bowl of fresh stone, almost entirely hollow, loud with its own continuing, lit for inhabitants who have not come, green with a life that needs no animal to complete it.

The loudest thing in it, in the end, is that nothing answers.
