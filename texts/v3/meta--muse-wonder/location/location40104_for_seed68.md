It is a town laid out for a much larger town, in a place that has not had time to be worn down.

The basin is low and shallow, barely a dip between fresh ridges. The ridges are not old mountains. Their cuts are still pale and sharp — where the road was blasted through, the rock shows in clean bands of grey and black, corners unsoftened, scree still angular at the base. The soil is thin and new over it. Things grow fast here, but nothing has grown for very long. Alders lean in, all about the same height. Ferns unroll in waist-high walls. Moss makes a bright, spongy skin on anything that stays still for a season. It is lush in the way a vacant lot is lush in June: eager, green, a little formless.

There is no river. That is the first rule everyone learns.

Water arrives sideways. Fog comes in cool off the lower ground in the evening and pools in the basin until the streetlights are halos and the corrugated roofs begin to tick with condensation. Rain comes in short, indifferent squalls, then stops. For an hour the gutters run and the gravel streets shine. Then the ground drinks it, the rock holds it in pockets, and everything is simply damp again — boots never quite dry, paper curling in drawers, salt clumping in the shaker, a dark tide-mark of mildew at the base of every north-facing wall.

So they built the Condenser.

It occupies the center of the hamlet the way a church would in another town, except it is larger than the church, which in fact was built small and off to one side later. The plant is a long, galvanized hall with three riveted intake towers, a forest of ductwork, and a row of industrial fans set into its eastern wall like jet engines half-buried in concrete. It draws in the foggy air, cools it, wrings it out, and sends the rest back out again, slightly drier and smelling faintly of hot metal and oil.

It is very, very loud.

Not sharp-loud, most of the time. Deep-loud. A continuous roar and hum you feel in your molars, in the soles of your feet through the mess hall floor, in window glass that buzzes sympathetically at certain frequencies. Near the fan wall you shout and still cup your ear. Further out, across town, you just learn to speak in a projected sort of way, as if everyone you talk to is a little deaf, which after a few years at the plant, they are. The company issues ear protectors in cheerful industrial colors — orange, yellow, lime — and people wear them around their necks like necklaces when they're off shift, a local fashion no one notices anymore.

Every ninety minutes the compressors cycle down for seven minutes. Those seven minutes are the town's true clock. In the sudden, ringing quiet, you can hear everything the plant has been covering: the clank of a loose chain somewhere, water dripping from every eave and duct in a complex patter, sparrows exploding out of the vents, someone's radio, children actually laughing at playground volume. Conversations all over town naturally pause and restart in that gap. Long sentences get saved for it. Then the fans sigh back up, the air itself seems to lean eastward, and voices rise again.

The town around it is urban in intention, sparse in fact. There is a real grid: paved streets with painted curbs, numbered addresses, storm drains, a squat concrete school, a company store with a neon sign, two-story dormitory blocks with communal balconies, a clinic, a power substation humming behind a chain-link fence, hydrants painted silver. But between the buildings are broad gaps — empty lots gone to blackberry and young pine, a foundation poured for a cinema that became a skate park and then a frog pond, gardens fenced with scrap pallets. You can stand on Main Street at noon in bright, normal sunlight — because by noon the fog usually burns off clean and blue — and see perhaps fourteen people, a delivery truck, a line of wet laundry, and three deer picking their way delicately between the dormitories to lick condensation off the outflow pipes.

The deer are not shy. Nothing here is shy; there hasn't been time and there aren't enough people to teach them. Fauna has poured into the damp edges the plant creates. Swallows nest by the hundreds in the warm duct joints and hawk insects in the fan wash. Crows line the intake towers at dawn and scream, competitively, over the machines — and win, briefly. Frogs colonize every accidental pocket of water: the cistern overflows, the settling tanks, a blocked culvert, the deep puddle that lives all year under Tank No. 2. On summer nights, when the fans run low, their chorus is a second industry. Salamanders, black and gold, commute solemnly along the condensation channels. Herons stalk the one artificial pond where the finished water is held before being trucked out, standing in six inches of clarity they clearly believe is theirs.

Most of the water leaves. That is the second rule. It is cleaned, mineral-balanced, sealed into tanker trucks that grind out through the rock cut twice a day, bound for drier towns downcountry. What stays for the hamlet is rationed by a gentler system than it sounds — blue tokens for drinking water, unmetered roof-catch for washing, a strict and universally obeyed prohibition on drinking the delicious, cold drip from the main ducts, because it has run over copper. Every house has its barrel. Every barrel has a lid against frogs. Every lid fails, eventually, to some frog.

Vegetation has made its own peace with the arrangement. On the intake side of the plant, where the air is drawn thin, trees are sparse and a bit stunned. On the outflow side, where spent air sighs out perpetually moist, there is a kind of accidental jungle in miniature: tree ferns the plant manager's predecessor imported and lost control of, gunnera with leaves big enough to shelter a child, moss six inches deep on the duct supports, orchids — actual orchids, escaped from someone's windowsill generations of cuttings ago — blooming in the perpetual cool humidity of a vent. Gardeners in town take cuttings from there the way you'd borrow from a library.

Newness shows everywhere else, too. The concrete sidewalks have not yet cracked except where roots are just beginning to lift them. The slag heaps behind the maintenance yard are still sharp-edged and unweathered, sprouting a first fuzz of green. The paint on the water towers is still glossy. Rust, which you would expect everywhere in this damp, seems to be losing its slow war to constant repainting and to the simple fact that nothing has been here long enough to truly rot. Change comes on the slow clocks: the reservoir filling by inches over weeks of fog, the alder grove adding a foot a year, the hill cut learning, gully by gully, how water wants to run down it. Even the machines agree with this tempo. They roar constantly in order to produce, drop by drop, a surprisingly small amount of water.

At evening the cycle closes. Heat goes out of the streets. Cool air slides back down the fresh ridges. Breath shows. The fog returns, first as a smell of wet rock and crushed fern, then as a thinning of the streetlights at the far end of the grid, then as a general, soft erasure. The plant's outline stays — bulk, ducts, towers — with red aircraft lights blinking on top, and the fans pull the white air in visibly, in slow spirals. Every surface begins again to sweat and drip. Doors swell in their frames. The deer come out onto the warm pavement. Somewhere in the seven-minute quiet, an owl calls from the uncut trees beyond the last street, very loud and very close, and for a moment the whole hamlet, people and machines together, seems to be listening.
