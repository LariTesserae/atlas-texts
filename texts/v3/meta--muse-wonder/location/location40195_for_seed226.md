At first you think the sound is the town.

You hear it long before the plateau levels out and the walls come into view — a roar, low and continuous, coming down the mountain path through the cloud. Not wind. Not exactly a river. It gets louder as the air gets thinner, until it seems to be coming out of the stone under your boots, out of the empty sky, out of your own ears ringing a little from the altitude.

Then the path turns, the cloud tears, and there it is above you, in hard bright daylight: a city.

Or the beginning of one.

It is called Brinnel, and it occupies one small, high bowl that has not yet learned to be a valley. The whole place is perhaps three hundred paces across, rimmed by pale, unweathered rock that still has the clean, shocked look of something recently made. The cliffs are not fissured and softened the way old mountains are. They are smooth, cream-colored, almost fleshy, with sharp unworn edges and long streaks where water weeps straight out of the stone and darkens it green-black. Nothing here has been eroded into gentleness. The gutters are not yet gutters. The hollows have not yet decided to be ponds. Geology is still happening, slowly, under the foundations.

And into that raw bowl, people have laid out a city far beyond their numbers.

There are avenues. Proper avenues, paved with fitted slabs, wide enough for processions that have never happened. There is a central market square with numbered stalls carved in a ring, an arcade for magistrates, a bathhouse, a library vault, cisterns, granaries, a theatre cut in concentric steps into the tuff. Houses rise three and four stories along the main streets, their facades carved with lintels, cornices, niches for lamps.

Almost all of it is empty.

A door opens onto a courtyard where no one lives. A stair climbs to rooms that have walls but no roof, bright with ferns. You cross a plaza big enough for ten thousand people and hear only your own footsteps, a rope creaking somewhere, and that roar. Only forty or fifty people live in Brinnel at any time, most of them clustered near the lower gate where the smoke from three kitchens loses itself at once in the cold. The rest of the city stands maintained, swept, repaired, and uninhabited — streets kept clear for a population imagined in the future tense.

The rock helps them and will not hurry. It is a soft volcanic tuff, pale as cut cheese when first exposed, hardening in the air. A person with a copper chisel, a wooden mallet, and patience can carve a doorway in a season, a room in a few years, a house in a working life. Everything is made that way: hand-cut, hand-hauled on sledges, lifted by rope cranes and timber scaffolding that clings to the unfinished upper stories like a second, wooden city growing off the stone one. There are no wheeled carts; nothing arrives except on backs and pack-frames up the long stair-path from below. Hammers tick irregularly all day from different quarters — *tak, tak... tak* — small sounds almost lost in the larger noise.

Because the roar is engineered, too.

Through the exact center of Brinnel, down the main avenue in an open stone channel as wide as a man is tall, runs the town's water. It falls from a spring lake above the rim, drops through a series of stepped cisterns, and tears downhill between the houses, white and muscular and astonishingly loud. Side channels branch off it like veins into courtyards, baths, gardens, workshops. Flumes sing under bridges. Overflow pipes pour in silver sheets from roof corners even when it hasn't rained. In the cistern chambers below the plaza, the sound multiplies in the dark vaults until the walls tremble faintly under your palm.

Near the main channel you have to raise your voice. Across the square you have to shout. The inhabitants mostly don't bother. Brinnel has developed an elaborate working language of gestures — broad, unembarrassed, performed across distances: *water rising, bring the mallet, stop cutting, come and look, eat now.* Children learn it before they learn to project their voices. At night, when the sluices are partly closed and the roar drops to a heavy rushing, the sudden ease of ordinary speech feels almost indecent, and people talk for hours in the courtyards just to enjoy not yelling.

Water is everywhere more quietly, too. It is just below the surface here, unmapped, still finding its routes through young rock. It condenses on cold walls at noon. It beads on the inside of roofs. It seeps up, sometimes, through a newly laid floor, in a perfect clear bead-line following a crack no one can see. For this reason no new room in Brinnel is finished in its first year. They carve it, floor it roughly, and leave it empty and doorless through a full turn of seasons, with chalk marks on the walls. Where the weeps appear, they cut drainage runnels. Where a spring decides to be born in a corner, they either move the room's purpose around it — now it is a washing place, now a fern pit, now a shrine with a perpetually full cup — or they abandon that chamber to the water entirely and start again one wall over. Some of the finest houses have a small, gleaming, unwanted stream running straight through the pantry.

The cool is constant, but the light is not dim. That is the surprise of the height. The sun stands clear and ordinary and strong overhead, hard enough to warm your shoulders through wool, while the shade of a wall cuts like winter. Breath smokes in the morning. Hands go numb in the channel water in seconds. Then you step into a sheltered courtyard at midday and the air is almost balmy, trapped and green, smelling of wet stone and crushed leaves.

Because somehow, on this raw rock, everything grows.

Cloud comes over the rim most afternoons and sits in the bowl for an hour, drenching the city without rain. It catches in nets of knotted rope strung between the unfinished towers — one of Brinnel's few inventions, those great sagging webs — and drips steadily into barrels. Moss takes hold wherever water pauses: on north walls, between paving slabs, along the library steps, in the theatre seats, softening all that new geometry with emerald and jade and black-green cushions. Tree ferns unfurl in the sunken gardens. In every inhabited courtyard there are terraced beds packed improbably tight — broad beans, potatoes with purple skins, bitter mountain herbs, marigolds, climbing peas using the scaffolding as trellis. Vines go looking for empty houses and find them, pouring in through window holes, rooting in rooms reserved for grandchildren. A granary that has never held grain is currently full, to the lintel, of hanging onions and a single nesting —

No, not nesting. Fauna is what Brinnel mostly lacks, and notices.

A pair of swifts summers in the bell tower and their return is announced. Moths, white and slow, gather around the cistern lamps. There are small deafening frogs in the lower tanks, heard far more than seen, and blind silver fish no longer than a finger in the deepest cistern, whose presence is inferred from ripples. Once in a few years a condor crosses the bowl, enormous and silent, and everyone comes out into the empty avenues to watch its shadow slide over roofs and market stalls and gardens alike. Dogs are too expensive to feed up here. Beasts of burden belong to the world below. The city makes do with people, ropes, and time.

And time is the true building material. Brinnel is rising still — not just the construction, the ground itself. Surveyors' marks cut into the gateposts no longer align after twenty years. Doors have to be trimmed at the bottom every decade as the bowl lifts, almost imperceptibly, on whatever slow pressure is still remaking this mountain. Nothing here is fast enough to witness. A crack appears in a cistern wall across a childhood. A new spring warms, cools, and dies across a marriage. The city plan, painted on plaster in the arcade — concentric streets, a second market, gardens on the rim — is not expected to be complete for four hundred years.

So they rehearse.

On the first clear day of each season they ring the market bell and open stalls they do not need, laying out stone weights, cloth, salt, tools, dried beans in beautiful orderly rows, shouting prices into the roar for an hour to a square that holds perhaps twenty buyers. They sweep the theatre. They chalk the names of future streets on boards and fix them to corners where the street is still a scaffold and a chalk line on raw tuff: *Street of the Weavers. Street of the Second Gate. Street of Rain.* Children are apprenticed not really to a trade but to a continuation — this wall will be your son's wall, this arch will be closed by someone whose grandmother you might meet.

By late afternoon the cloud comes down again. It fills the avenues first, then the square, erasing the far houses while leaving the near ones wet and startlingly close. The channel goes on roaring invisibly. Green drips from green. Somewhere a chisel starts again, *tak, tak,* patient as dripping. The empty city tightens around its few warm kitchens, its nets of water, its gardens steaming faintly in the cold, and goes on being unfinished — not ruined, not abandoned, but embryonic, loud with water and promise, waiting centuries for itself to arrive.
