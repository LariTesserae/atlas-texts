You have to be on your knees to find it, and even then you almost miss it.

Under a bank of leaf-litter, where the soil is still dark and newly split, there is a slit no wider than your hand. It is not a burrow. Nothing dug it. The earth has been pushed up from underneath in clean, sharp plates, the edges of the clay still crumbly and unweathered, and between the plates a root — white, hot-looking, not yet barked — has arched and left a mouth.

Warm air comes out of it. It smells green and iron-sweet, like a snapped stem, like rain that hasn't fallen yet.

You go down headfirst because there is no other way, and the root closes the light behind you, and then, impossibly, does not take it away.

### The Room

It is one room. That is the whole of it.

Perhaps six feet across if you measured the outside — a bulb, a tuber, a swollen seed sunk below the surface and only just germinated. But inside the measurement stops behaving. You stand up, which you should not be able to do, and your head does not touch the ceiling. You take ten steps toward the far wall, which you can see clearly, and it is still ten steps away. You take ten more and your outstretched hand brushes it suddenly, cool and damp, without the intervening distance having felt crossed.

The chamber is hollow. That is its strangeness against itself.

Everything is plant, and almost nothing is in it.

The walls, floor, ceiling — there is no reliable word for them — are one continuous living surface, the inside skin of the bulb. Pale gold-green, veined, ribbed like the inside of a melon, like the vault of a throat. It is plant as substrate, plant as architecture, plant as the ground you stand on and the air you are allowed to breathe. Outside this skin there is more of it, solid for yards in every direction, roots and storage flesh and dark compressed leaf-matter, a total vegetal mass with this one secret emptiness at its heart.

In the middle: nothing.

No undergrowth crowds you. No vines hang down. The airspace is clean, vacant, resonant. A few root-hairs, fine as fishing line, cross the void at ankle and shoulder height, so widely spaced you keep forgetting them until one touches your cheek. A few spores drift, and because there are so few of them, you notice each one individually.

Below surface, it should be black. It is not. The light is ordinary daylight, even and normal, the kind of light you'd expect at noon in a clearing. It has no source and casts no strong shadows. Later you understand — the plant is drinking light far above, through leaves you will never see from here, and conducting it down through translucent stems the way deep-sea creatures are said to dream of the sun. The walls simply exhale it. You can read by it. Your skin warms in it. When a cloud must pass overhead, up there in the world, the whole room dims for twenty seconds and brightens again.

It is warm. Not hot, but body-warm. Condensation gathers on the higher ribs and runs in beads along the veins. Now and then a drop lets go and falls up.

That is the first rule that breaks.

### Water, Sound, Growth

The damp is intermittent and personal. The walls sweat in cycles. For an hour the air is almost dry, crisp, smelling of starch and new wood. Then the ribs swell, pores open along seams you hadn't seen, and a fine mist precipitates out of nowhere — not falling, just *arriving* on surfaces, on your arms, on the floor that was dry a moment before. Tiny cup-leaves set into the wall fill with a mouthful of clear liquid each. Nothing ever pools. Nothing ever floods. It is a place made of occasional rain inside itself.

And it is loud. Impossibly, embarrassingly loud for a hidden pocket in the earth.

Every process of growth is amplified by the hollow shape. Cells expanding make soft, sequential popping sounds, like knuckles, like ice, like distant fireworks underwater. When a new root-tip pushes a fraction of an inch through the outer skin, the room reports it as a deep wooden creak, a ship's hull turning. Your own heartbeat comes back to you off the far wall a second later, bass-heavy. A single falling — rising — drop strikes a cup-leaf and booms.

Then the echo begins to arrive *before* the sound.

You tap the wall once, experimentally, and hear the tap, clear and close, and *then* feel your finger make contact. A spore bursts somewhere in the empty air with a green tick that you heard a breath earlier while you were still watching it intact. It does not happen always. That is worse. The room is young — days old, perhaps hours — and its physics have not settled. Time is still being grown, unevenly, in patches.

Growth is visible. If you watch a rib, a pale seam will lengthen by a hair, then another. A thorn near the entrance slit is still glass-sharp, translucent at the tip, not yet blunted by any erosion; yesterday it probably wasn't there. The floor under your feet gives faintly, then firms again, as if adjusting to the idea of weight.

The plant is trying out being a place.

### The One Animal

There is almost no fauna.

No ants find this. No worms cross the floor. The air that passes through so much living tissue comes out filtered, almost sterile.

Except for her.

She is a shrew-like thing, blind, naked-pink where she isn't furred in wet grey, and she lives alone in a dimple in the wall where three ribs meet. She is the only animal the bulb tolerates, or the only one who learned the slit before it sealed last spring — the stories the place tells you are not in words.

She has a tool.

It is a snapped-off thorn, its base wrapped clumsily and ingeniously with root-fiber and hardened sap, making a handle just long enough for her forepaws. With it she scrapes, taps, pries at a particular vein until it beads with milky fluid, which she laps. With it she cuts a fallen fiber into lengths and weaves them, without any plan you can decipher, into the dimple where she sleeps. Primitive, undeniable: not a structure, not a settlement, just one creature extending her reach with a made thing.

She ignores you if you keep still. If you move suddenly, the room takes your movement and returns it as a low drum-roll, and she vanishes flat into the wall dimple and does not reappear for a long time.

When she scrapes, you hear the scrape first, see the shaving peel second. She seems used to it. She pauses before the sound happens.

### Rules As They Emerge

Stay long enough and other rules declare themselves, not all at once.

Gravity holds toward the nearest wall, weakly. In the exact center of the void you feel a peculiar lightness in your teeth, a suggestion that up is negotiable. The spores fall outward. Dust from your boots drifts to the floor, reconsiders, and settles instead on the side wall at a slow diagonal.

Seeds do not behave. Nothing has flowered here, yet occasionally a hard black seed the size of a pea appears in midair at chest height — *appears*, without falling, as if remembered into existence — hangs ticking faintly, then snaps toward the ceiling and embeds itself with a rifle-crack report. The ceiling is already studded with them, in no pattern.

Your voice, if you speak, comes back harmonized. Not echoed — completed. You say half a word and the room supplies a tone underneath it, the plant's resonance finding the vowel and holding it until it becomes something like a choir of one note. People who found this place before — and the thought arrives with absolute certainty, though there has been no sign of them, no footprints in the living floor that heals itself smooth, no built thing at all — people who found it must have stopped speaking very quickly.

Because the room learns sounds too fast. It has already learned your breathing. It does it back at you, slightly louder, from behind the wall by your left ear, when you hold your breath.

It is not hostile. That is important. A bulb does not have hostility. It has processes. It is photosynthesizing, storing, splitting, preparing to fill this hollow in with new flesh as its germination completes. The sharp new seams, the white root, the unfinished light: this is a temporary organ. In a week, in a month, the walls will meet in the middle with a sigh loud enough to deafen, the one shrew will be elsewhere or incorporated, the cup-leaves will fold, and the room will be solid plant again, without scar.

For now it is an emptiness grown inside an excess of life, lit like afternoon, warm as a held hand, ringing with every tiny event.

You climb back toward the slit — which takes three steps and also a long time — and as you squeeze out into leaf-litter and comparatively silent, comparatively dark ordinary earth-smell, you hear behind you, clearly and before it happens, the soft tap of your own knee against the root on your way in.
