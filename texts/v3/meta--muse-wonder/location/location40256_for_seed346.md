It is called Ternlow, and it is a city the size of a hamlet, sitting in a hollow that has not finished becoming a place.

From a distance — insofar as there is a distance — it looks ordinary enough. Low northern light, the flat grey kind that comes through thin cloud at mid-morning and stays unchanged for hours. Black ground. A tight cluster of roofs and towers no taller than a cathedral, all of them crowded together as if for warmth. A faint plume of condensation rising and immediately flattening in the cold.

The hollow is new. Not ruined, not eroded — *unworn*. The basalt under Ternlow is still sharp at the edges, glassy and unsoftened, with no soil worth the name, only a damp film of mineral dust in the hollows. No river has found its way here yet. Water has not learned any paths. It arrives as intermittent rain that forgets to fall everywhere at once, as fog that condenses directly onto walls, as beads of moisture sweating out of cold stone in the middle of the afternoon for no meteorological reason. For most of the day the ground is just damp enough to darken, and to show your footprints behind you for a long time after they should have dried.

Beyond the last street, the ground becomes less certain. This is the first rule of Ternlow, and the one newcomers learn by standing still and looking.

The basalt thins into something like sand, then into a pale granular shimmer, then into patches that are sometimes shallow water, sometimes matte darkness, sometimes simply not there in a way your eyes slide off. Unobserved corners of the unfinished land hold several states at once, politely, without choosing. Survey drones hang over the boundary day and night, not mapping it so much as *continuing to agree on it*. Where they watch steadily, matter settles. Where attention lapses, a fence post may spend the night as a column of cold vapor and be a fence post again by breakfast, a little embarrassed, a few centimeters to the left.

The city is being built, but so is the physics underneath it. The builders — originally engineers, now mostly the descendants of engineers — describe the work as "finishing a low place." The hollow is a dip not just in elevation, but in something else: a pocket where reality was left embryonic. Ternlow's technology does the rest very slowly, a centimeter of fully reliable world per good year.

That is why everything is so dense.

Inside the rendered perimeter — roughly nine hundred meters across, if you measure on a day when measurement is cooperative — is an entire urban civilization compressed to the scale of a village green. Streets barely wide enough for two people pass between buildings that have been layered, subdivided, folded, and re-layered for four generations. Homes over workshops over archives over cisterns over homes again. Balconies bridge the alleys. Pipes run overhead like handrails, warm to the touch. Staircases serve three addresses on the way up and a fourth, sometimes, on the way down.

The building material is mostly a white ceramic grown rather than manufactured, extruded by machines that pull matter partly from local basalt and partly from adjacent probabilities of basalt. This is why spare rooms tend to arrive before they are needed and why demolished walls occasionally persist as a chill outline you can walk through on humid days, feeling the faint resistance of a doorway that decided to remain. Rooms are added by causal looms in the undercity — vast, silent frames that do not so much assemble matter as persuade it that it has already been assembled. The looms make no noise you can hear. They make a pressure behind the molars, and they make frost form in spirals on nearby surfaces even in summer.

And it is cold. Always. The kind of cold that lives at 1 or 2 degrees and refuses drama — never quite a blizzard, never a thaw. Your breath shows. Metal sweats. The rendered sun is a normal sun, the light a normal, cloud-softened daylight, but the heat that should come with it seems to be one of the things still under negotiation. The city conserves warmth the way other cities conserve water: inertial sinks in the foundations store the momentum of footsteps and door-slams and release it as low heat through the floors. Entropy wells under the market square keep the central baths steaming. Still, everyone wears layers indoors, and the city's sparse vegetation clusters wherever warmth leaks.

The greenery is pioneer stuff, engineered and stubborn. Grey-green moss in the seams of the ceramic. Lichen in orange and bone-white rosettes on the north faces of towers — not decoration; the lichen is load-bearing in a metaphysical sense, its slow chemistry anchoring causality the way roots anchor soil. Along the warm pipes grows a pale, hair-thin grass that never seeds. In the cistern courts, black algae mats breathe once a day, all at the same moment, releasing a smell of iron and rain. Nothing flowers except, rarely, the boundary moss, and when it does, the flowers are watched very carefully and then burned.

Sound does not travel well in Ternlow, partly because of the cold and the density of baffling walls, partly because beyond the perimeter there is not yet enough consistent air to carry echoes back. The city is mostly quiet. Dripping condensation. The tick of new ceramic cooling. A distant, irregular *settling* sound from the edge of the world, like a house deciding on its foundations. Voices stay close to the mouths that make them. Even the market, three hundred people trading printed food and salvaged futures on a Saturday, sounds like a library with a kettle on.

Fauna is rare and treated as news. Winter gnats live in the vent-steam and nowhere else. A species of eyeless, heat-seeking moth flutters around the looms. For eleven years there has been one fox, or possibly the same fox at different ages overlapping; it is sometimes seen entering an alley old and white-muzzled and leaving it young and red, and the residents leave out food without discussing this. Birds arrive occasionally from outside the unfinished zone, fly a single bewildered circle over the roofs, and leave in a direction that is not always the opposite of the one they came from.

Daily life has adapted to the breakdowns with the weary practicality of people who also adapt to plumbing problems. Mirrors are kept covered between uses because reflections lag — at first by a blink, later in life by whole seconds, smiling after you have stopped. Addresses are given as relationships, not numbers: "above the bakery that is sometimes a chapel, left at the stair that counts fourteen." Stairs are counted aloud, always, because a staircase left uncounted may add a step out of loneliness. Calendars are printed with two blank days a month, unlabeled, which residents use for events that occurred but cannot be placed. You do not argue with a neighbor you meet who is younger than you remember and does not know you yet. You offer them tea. You make a note of what they said, because it may be advice, or a warning, or just the city rehearsing a person it intends to have later.

Change comes slowly here, and then maintains itself retroactively. A new well is dug over ten years. A child grows up. The perimeter drones inch outward, and a new strip of black basalt clicks, almost imperceptibly, into being fully and permanently real — and within a week no one can quite remember it ever being otherwise, because the finished world edits the memory of its unfinished state as it goes. Old photographs show streets that have always existed, held by people who, at the time, had not yet decided to be born.

At night Ternlow contracts a little, the way living things do in the cold. Lights come on in the dense stacked windows, amber and white, doubled here and there by windows that belong to adjacent versions of the same room, occupied by lamplight of slightly different colors. Condensation freezes on the ceramic in fern patterns that repeat motifs from streets on the other side of town. The looms work on below, silent, persuasive. On the edge of things, the unfinished dark waits without menace, holding its water and stone and nothing in suspension, patient as geology, patient as code.

It is a small place. It contains avenues, slums, a university, three religions founded by accident, a prison that has held the same prisoner at five different ages, and more people than its map should allow. Almost everything that has ever happened there is still happening, quietly, in some layer of it.

The builders say that in a few hundred years the hollow will be complete: weather will arrive on schedule, erosion will begin its long softening work, rivers will commit, distances will stay put. They say this with genuine hope, and also with a certain grief — because by then Ternlow will be merely a cold, damp town in a low place, and not, as it is now, a city dense enough to hold a world together by refusing to spread out inside it.
