There is no ground here.

You come up to it from below, on a stair cut straight into the mountain, and at a certain height the stair stops being a stair in rock and becomes a stair in masonry. The rock ends — not gradually, not with rubble and outbuildings and a ragged edge where building gives way to stone — but all at once, under one step. After that, everything your foot touches has been placed.

It covers the whole high valley. From the pass where you enter it extends past sight, a town-sized, valley-sized roof, cool and wet and blinding under a thin sky.

At first it looks like a city of low roofs. Then it looks like a field of stone funnels. Then, as you walk out into it and your eyes adjust to the glare, neither of those is right. It is a catchment, built and built and built until there was no room left to build in.

Square basin shouldered against round basin. Conical funnel sunk between four troughs. Long, shallow pans tiled in white limestone, tilted by a finger's width toward a central hole. Domes, very low and broad, beaded with water. Channels no wider than a hand running between everything, carrying a thread of water so clear and slow you have to stare to be sure it moves. There is no gap. There is no yard, no street in the ordinary sense, no bare patch. If you want to cross it you walk on rims and dividing walls, on the narrow stone lips between one water surface and the next. In places the builders laid flat stepping-slabs across the pans, dark basalt against the pale stone, and those are the only paths.

The stone is young. That is the strange thing for a place that must have taken generations of hand labor. The edges are still knife-sharp. The chisel marks are still crisp in the limestone, parallel grooves where a stone hammer and a copper — later an iron — chisel worried it into shape. The corners of the basins have not softened. The channels have not been eaten smooth by their own water. Nothing has crumbled back toward nature. It feels recently emerged, as if it were uncovered last spring rather than finished a hundred years ago, and perhaps that is because the water here does not erode so much as deposit. Every rim is furred, very slightly, with a white mineral crust. Every funnel wears tide-rings, pale on paler. Where water stands for a long time it lays down a skin of lime so smooth it looks glazed.

And it is wet everywhere, persistently, in a way that cannot be explained only by rain.

Rain does come. At this elevation the weather arrives sideways, sudden veils of it sweeping the valley and dimming the glare for a minute, filling everything with a soft hissing. But on clear days, on windless days, the place still weeps. Water condenses on the domes in the night and runs in beads at noon. The north sides of the low dividing walls are dark with damp to the top. Funnels sweat. If you put your palm flat on a slab at midday — the sun overhead fierce enough to burn the back of your neck through the cold air — the stone under your hand is icy, and in a minute your palm is wet.

That is the rule of it, discovered slowly as you go deeper in: the whole valley is hollow.

Under the catchment is the reservoir. Not a lake in a cave, exactly, but a second, inverted city of vaults and cisterns and stone pillars, full in the late summer, echoing and emptying in the winter, always cold enough to chill the roof it supports. The cold rises. The bright, thin mountain air touches stone cooled from beneath and gives up its moisture. So the place waters itself, in part. What falls as rain runs through the holes in the pans, through clay pipe throats no wider than a wrist, sealed with pitch and braided fiber, into the dark below. What condenses on the surface does the same. What is lost rises again on warm afternoons in a faint vapor from the deeper shafts and condenses again the next night. A slow loop, patient as breathing, except nothing here breathes.

There are no plants. Not a tuft of grass in a channel, not moss in the damp shade of a wall, not even the green film you expect on standing water. The water is too mineral, too alkaline, bitter with lime and a trace of salt leached from the mortar. It leaves white on your lips and a dry feeling after drinking, as if it took more moisture than it gave. It beads on stone and leaves stone behind when it goes. In the oldest pans — oldest here meaning perhaps a decade, the deposit has built up that fast — tiny crystal gardens grow in the corners where the water lingers longest, clear and salt-white, branching like frost that never melts. They are the closest thing to a living system. They grow a fraction of a millimeter across a season, they are broken by a careless foot, they begin again. Mineral only, and slower than anything alive.

There are no animals, either. No birds cross to drink. No insects skate the pans. The brightness may be part of it — at noon this entire valley is a mirror broken into ten thousand small mirrors, each pan and trough and wet dome throwing the sun back, so that light comes not only from above but from below and from every angle, and there is almost no shadow except the thin black line inside each funnel. It hurts to look across it. You learn to look down, at your feet on the dark stepping-stones, and even there the water beside the path flashes. At this height the air is cool, still cold in the shade of your own body, and the wind that finds its way between the low walls is colder still off the water. Sunburn and shivering at once. Your clothes never quite dry. Your boots are wet by the second hour whatever the weather, from splash and condensation and the necessary stepping, now and then, into a channel because the rim you were following simply ends at a sluice gate made of a single oak plank, swollen tight in its grooves, worked by a rope and a wooden lever worn smooth by hands that are not here now.

For there is no one here, either. And that absence becomes the center of the landscape the longer you stay in it.

It was obviously maintained — has to have been, constantly. Leaves would have clogged it, but there are no trees above it to shed them. Dust does blow in, fine and grey, and someone swept it from the pans before it could muddy, someone scraped the lime crust before it could seal a drain, someone re-pitched the clay pipes and replaced the ropes and hauled, in baskets, more white stone up from quarries somewhere below the cloud line. The tools they used were simple ones; you can read them in the work. No wheel-cut stone. No metal larger than a hand tool. Great slabs levered into place on wooden rollers, set on mortar mixed in troughs that were then incorporated, permanently, into the catchment itself, becoming part of what they were built to make. Thousands upon thousands of them. A whole high valley turned, by hand, into a single instrument for gathering water.

Now only the water maintains a kind of order. The sluices that work, work because they swelled shut or were left open. The levels equalize slowly through underground connections you can hear, sometimes, if you kneel and put your ear to a drain hole: a trickling far below, a hollow plink, an occasional deep tick as cold stone contracts at dusk. That is almost all the sound there is. Mostly quiet, in a complete and padded way. No birdsong, no leaves, no voices to carry. Your own footsteps are small and wet. The wind makes no sound until it finds one of the ventilator shafts — short stone chimneys, capped with hoods, rising at intervals from the reservoir below — and then it produces, unpredictably, a low, empty note, like air across the mouth of a jar, and stops.

The districts emerge only after hours of walking this roofscape, because from any one place it all looks the same. Toward the eastern rim the funnels are deepest, great inverted pyramids of stepped stone, designed for the heavy rains that come off the peak. In the center, the pans are widest and shallowest, a blinding white plain of water an inch deep, meant to catch dew — and they do, night after night, the whole center filmed and glittering before the sun has properly risen, mist sitting on it in a sheet that burns off upward as you watch. Toward the west, where the valley narrows to its outlet, the system changes character: trough on trough, stepped down in terraces, clay channels replacing stone, a series of settling basins where the mineral water rests and clears and drops its white load, until at the very end it gathers in one long, roofed conduit and leaves. It leaves downhill, out of sight, through a stone mouth built like a doorway for no one, presumably toward fields and people somewhere far below who may or may not still be there to receive it. The flow at the mouth is modest. A stream you could step across. It is hard to reconcile with the enormity of what collects it, until you understand the time scale: this place does not work in storms, it works in seasons. A millimeter a day, across ten thousand pans. A cistern filling over the course of a summer by increments no eye can measure. A sluice opened once a year.

By late afternoon the light goes raking and the whole construction reveals itself in relief, every rim casting its thin shadow, every chisel groove visible, the wet stone going gold and then rose without ever looking warm. Condensation is already forming on the eastern sides. The crystal corners gleam. Somewhere below your feet, very far below in feeling if not in fact, water settles a fraction lower or higher in a vault no one will enter this year, and the stone above it gives up one more bead, which runs, slow as anything here, down a funnel worn by nothing, into a hole, into the dark, to wait.

You cannot camp on it. There is nowhere to lie that is not a rim between waters, nowhere to make a fire even if you had wood, which you do not — there is no wood here but the sluice planks and the levers, and they are waterlogged and white with salt. You cannot be dry, you cannot be in shade, you cannot find a place where something was not made. Those are its conditions, as plain as its weather.

When you leave, back down the stair that turns from masonry to mountain again, your legs know before your mind does that you have stepped off the artifact and onto the world. The rock there is rough, unshaped, stained with damp, and after hours on the catchment it looks almost shockingly formless. Behind you the valley goes on shining, packed rim to rim, gathering the evening out of the air, drop by drop, in silence, for whoever it was built for.
