It is a high town in wet stone country, and the first thing you understand from a distance is how new the land underneath it looks.

The plateau does not roll. It breaks. It rises in clean, pale facets from the cloud-country below, limestone and grey-blue rock still holding the sharp angles of its uplift, as if the mountain had been folded up recently and not yet had time to soften. The cliffs are not crumbled into scree slopes. They go down in straight joints and narrow, V-cut gullies. The rim is a line of unweathered teeth against the sky. Even the waterfalls keep that newness — they do not wander or braid. They fall straight from cracks in the cliff, white and thin, into basins that have not yet widened.

And yet, at this height, it is warm.

Not hot. Warm the way a greenhouse is warm at mid-morning, or a sheltered courtyard after sun has hit wet stone. The latitude does the work the altitude would undo. The air is thinner and clear, but it is constantly laden. Sun comes down in ordinary, bright daylight, no paler or stranger than anywhere else, and it lifts steam off roofs, off pavements, off the dark leaves in the gardens. From the rim road you can look across the whole settlement and see that faint exhalation everywhere, a low, slow breathing of moisture from surfaces that never fully dry.

Because water is not a season here. It is the ground condition.

It seeps from the rock in a hundred places. It beads on cliff faces that are not near any stream, darkening the limestone in long vertical stains. It stands a hand's breadth below the soil in the garden plots — dig with a wooden spade and the hole shines, then fills. It runs in the open, not wild, because the town has learned to give it a path everywhere, but it is always within earshot and almost always within sight: in stone channels cut along the streets, in clay pipes ticking under house eaves, in shallow rills that cross the plazas under flat stepping-stones, in cisterns under every courtyard, full to a black, still lip.

The town is spread widely across the upper valley to accommodate it. This is not a dense city huddled around a well. It is an urban place, with districts and markets and a clear civic order, but spaced out, low-saturation, each cluster of buildings given room for its waterworks and its green. From one neighborhood you can see three others across the terraced basin, and the open, wet meadow at the valley bottom beyond them, and the aqueduct line coming in from the eastern scarp two miles off. The scale keeps opening as you walk. A street that seemed local turns out to be a causeway. A reservoir that seemed a pond is one in a chain that steps down the whole eastern slope.

The houses are built by hand, and built low. Squared stone, hand-dressed with stone hammers and copper chisels, the tool marks still crisp on newer walls. Timber — dark, resinous wood that does not rot quickly — for lintels and roof frames. Roof tiles of fired clay, heavy with moss on the north side. Many houses stand on raised stone platforms a knee or a waist high, not for defense but to keep the floors above the water table when the ground swells. Their eaves are wide. Under the eaves, woven reed mats hang to break the drip-lines. Everything that can be made from the immediate country is: limestone, clay, reed, fiber rope, carved wood, beaten copper for gutters and hinges. Nothing rings much. Even the building yards are quiet places, men and women scraping stone with stone, the sound gritty and small and soon swallowed by wet moss banks.

Between houses, vegetation takes every margin the water offers. It is lush without being jungle. Ferns uncurl from wall joints. Moss thickens on shaded steps so that foot traffic has to keep to the center stones, which are worn dark and slick. Garden terraces hold broad-leafed food plants, their leaves always beaded, and stands of tall grain that pollinates on the wind. Reeds crowd the lower channels. In the uncultivated ground at the valley's south end, where the town gives out, the land returns to marsh-edge: sedge, standing pools, pale flowers that open in the morning and close by their own slow routine, no bees moving among them. Hand-pollination is a normal chore here, children going along crop rows with feather brushes in the still weeks. It is not remarked upon. It is simply how fruit is set, in a place that has plants in abundance and, as far as anyone's memory goes, has never had animals to do it.

That absence is the town's deepest quiet.

There is no birdsong over the roofs in the morning, because there are no birds. No insects drone in the warm grass. No dogs, no herd animals in pens, no beasts at the hand-carts that move stone and tile and harvested greens along the causeways — people pull them, in pairs, on wooden rollers. The marsh has no frogs. The cisterns have no fish, only green algae filming the stones below the waterline. Life here is people and plants and the slow mineral life of wet rock.

So the soundscape is reduced to almost nothing, and what remains is softened. Water dripping from tile to channel. Water trickling under a bridge-slab, muffled by moss. Reeds shifting when a breeze comes down from the rim, then settling. A wooden gate. A loom from an open doorway, regular and low. Human voices carry, but people do not need to raise them, and the wet surfaces drink echo. On the broad central plaza — pale stone, always damp at the joints, with a fountain basin at its center that never overflows and never drops — you can stand at noon in full sun and hear chiefly your own sandals on the stone.

Nothing here hurries to change, either. The springs run at the same rate in the morning and at dusk. The mist that gathers in the low marsh before sunrise burns off to the same height each day and gives way to the same clear, warm light. There are no great floods because rain does not need to fall for the ground to be wet; the rock itself delivers water at a steady pressure, and the town's channels, cisterns, terraces, and spillways have found the exact level where inflow and outflow agree. Gardens do not go through dramatic seasons. Leaves replace leaves. The grain is cut and the grain stands again in the same terraces, the stubble rotted quickly in the warm damp and dug back in with wooden forks.

It makes the rules of building into rules of balance. You do not dam a channel; you widen your section of it. You do not dig a deep foundation; you raise a platform and let the water pass beneath in a culvert of loose stone. Every roof must shed to a gutter, every gutter to a tank or a street-rill, every tank to an overflow that someone downstream is counting on for their garden. Houses are repainted with lime wash not for beauty, though the whitewash is bright against the green, but because the lime slows the moss. Paths on the steeper lanes are cut with shallow cross-grooves so feet and cart-rollers can grip stone that would otherwise be glass-slick by midday. New construction looks almost immediately at home: within weeks, fern spores find the mortar, the copper greens at the edges, the platform stones darken to match the older ones. Within a year only the sharpness of the corners tells you a wall is young — like the cliffs, like the gullies, like everything here that has not yet been rounded by time.

By late afternoon you might walk out past the last houses, where the causeway narrows to a footpath between irrigation basins. The whole valley opens then. Terraces step away below you, each one a mirror or a field or a roof cluster, and beyond them the marsh shines, and beyond the marsh the plateau edge drops to cloud and distant lower country you cannot see the bottom of. The aqueduct crosses your view on low stone arches, carrying spring water from the weeping cliff as it has without pause or rumor of failure. Warm air moves up from the wet fields, smelling of green leaf, clay, lime, and clean stone.

It is not an empty place, and not a wild one. It is a settled, worked, inhabited height where stone is sharp, water is constant, growth is steady, and the quiet is not waiting for anything. The town does not feel paused. It feels finished in the way a level basin of water is finished — still on the surface, complete, holding its shape.
