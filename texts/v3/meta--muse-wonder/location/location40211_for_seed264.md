You hear it long before you can see where it is coming from.

Not a single sound, but a working sound. A low, continuous roar braced underneath by rhythm — a chuff, a sigh, a heavy metallic clap, then another chuff somewhere else answering it, then another. From the low rim of the basin it sounds unmistakably industrial, as if a valley full of steam engines and pump houses were running just out of sight, all of them synchronized, all of them at full load.

When you come down into it, there is nothing built there at all.

The basin is perhaps twelve kilometers across, sunk only a little below the warm country around it, flat enough that the far side is softened by haze. No towers, no sheds, no roads cut across it. No ruins, either. Only ground, water, stone, and steam, arranged sparsely across an enormous amount of empty space.

The floor is cracked mud and mineral crust in broad polygons, each plate the size of a room, curled slightly at the edges. The colors are not earthy so much as metallurgical: rust red, sulfur yellow, black iron-stained clay, white salt, the pale gray-blue of silica laid down in thin sheets. In places the surface is smooth and hot to the touch. In places it rings hollow under your boots, as if there were pipes underneath. There are, in a sense. Not made pipes, but water-worn tubes and chambers in the rock below, scoured over so long a time that they have taken on junctions, elbows, reservoirs, and narrow throats with the regularity of plumbing.

That is the engine of the place, and it has been running so long it has worn itself perfect.

Scattered across the flats, hundreds of meters apart, are the vents. Each is a low chimney of travertine and sinter, waist-high to twice human height, weathered smooth on the windward side and crusted into cauliflower lumps where the spray falls. Each chimney is capped by a flat slab of dark basalt, too regular at first glance, until you see that the slab is simply the piece of old lava roof that never fell away. Steam pressure gathers underneath it. The slab lifts — slowly, grinding at one edge — lets out a sudden white jet and a long locomotive exhalation, then drops back with a clap that travels through the soles of your feet.

*Llift, breathe, fall. Lift, breathe, fall.*

Every vent keeps its own period. One near the eastern pools fires every nine seconds. Another, larger and partly choked with orange mineral wool, labors for almost a minute, its cap trembling and leaking threads of vapor, before it heaves up and discharges with a roar that momentarily drowns the rest of the basin. None of them speed up. None of them slow down. Watch one for an hour and the interval does not drift. Break a piece from a chimney — though the crust is harder than it looks — and within weeks the spray has replaced it in the same shape, because the water arrives at the same pressure, at the same temperature, carrying the same dissolved stone, and falls in the same wind.

The wind is part of the machine. It comes always from the northwest, warm and steady, not strong enough to bend you but strong enough to carry every plume sideways at the same angle. The steam flattens, merges, and forms a low, luminous sheet that drifts across the basin and condenses on the cooler flats at the far side. That is most of the water here. There is no river entering, no lake at the center. Only pockets: shallow pools caught between the mud polygons, warm as bathwater, intensely clear over mats of black and amber slime, their levels never visibly rising or falling. Some are rimmed in white salt like machined washers. Some bubble gently from below, each bubble the same size, breaking the surface in strings. Some are still, and return the sky exactly, and under that reflection something pale moves, rarely — a blind shrimp, translucent, feeling its way along the mineral edge. They are one of the few animals that belong here.

Life has not taken this place over; it has found a few tolerable seams in it. There are no plants at all. No grass at the margin, no reeds in the pools, no green stain even where the ground is permanently damp. What grows is simpler and slower: bacterial mats in the hot runnels, rust-colored filaments waving in water too warm for your hand, black lichen-like crusts that are not quite lichen in the shaded undersides of slabs, and mineral gardens where the spray lands — tiny sulfur flowers, silica needles, salt feathers that build up and erode away at exactly the same rate, so that they are always present and always the same size. Occasionally a bird crosses high above the basin, a curlew or a heron on some efficient line to somewhere else. They do not circle. They do not come down. Ground-nesting would mean deafness, and drinking would mean heat, and there is nothing here to eat that would repay either. At the edges, on still evenings, you might see small dark moths working the warm updrafts, rising and falling without seeming to travel. That is nearly all.

Between the vents the basin can be almost empty for ten minutes of walking at a time. Dry heat comes off the plates. The air smells of hot clay, struck matches, iron left in rain, and faintly of oil — though there is no oil, only certain gases coming up through wet stone making that deep, used, mechanical smell. Condensation beads on any cool surface you bring in: a metal canteen sweats instantly, a lens fogs, a compass needle trembles because the black sand under the crust is full of magnetite and the whole basin hums with a faint electrical unease during the steam discharges. Your hair lifts slightly near a large vent just before it fires. Experienced visitors learn to read that as a warning and step back before the plume comes.

For all its movement, nothing here is actually happening. That is the strangest part, and it takes a day or two to understand it.

The pools do not fill. The chimneys do not grow. The cracked plates do not crack further; the fine cracks fill each night with condensed steam and dissolved silica, soften, seal, and reopen in the same places as the sun warms them. A footprint pressed into the damp margin in the morning is glazed by noon, softened by evening mist, and gone — not washed dramatically away, but restored, as if the surface had remembered its required level. Fog forms before dawn in a band at the same height above the western flats, burns off from the same edge, and leaves the same beads of water on the same cold stones. Even the light behaves ordinarily above the basin — plain, warm daylight, no color in it — but the steam sheet diffuses it so completely at ground level that shadows are short-lived and edges blur, and distance becomes hard to judge. A vent that looks fifty meters away may be three hundred, merely larger, doing exactly the same thing.

The loudness is not chaos. Close to a firing chimney it is overwhelming, a physical pressure in the chest, but step behind its cap-rock in the moment after discharge and there is a pocket of sudden, ringing quiet shaped exactly like the stone. Then the next vent fires across the flat, and the next, and the rhythm takes you again. The whole basin keeps time without keeping progress. It is an equilibrium you can hear: heat in, steam out; water lost, water condensed; stone dissolved below, stone deposited above; slab rising, slab falling.

It feels less like a landscape that has machinery in it than like machinery that, given enough time and no inventor, resolved itself into landscape. A factory with the factory removed, leaving only the process, running at full capacity to produce nothing but itself.

And when you climb back to the rim in the evening, the sound does not fade the way natural sounds do. It stays level, level, level — then cuts off cleanly at the ridge line, as if a door had closed. Behind you the caps are still lifting and falling in the haze. Ahead of you the ordinary warm country is almost silent. Nothing in the basin will be different tomorrow.
