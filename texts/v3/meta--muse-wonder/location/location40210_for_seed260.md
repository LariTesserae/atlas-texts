First you hear it, and then a long time after that you arrive.

It is not loud the way a storm is loud, or an engine. At this elevation — far above the tree-line of any finished mountain, far above cloud, above weather as it is usually understood — the air is too thin to carry sound properly. And yet it does, deafeningly, through the wrong medium. The tone comes up through your feet. Through the stone hammer in your hand, through your teeth set against each other, through the fluid in your inner ear. A low, continuous grinding note, so vast you stop perceiving it as a sound after the first hour and start perceiving it as a condition, like gravity. Only when you press your forehead to someone else's forehead can you speak, bone to bone. Otherwise your voice is taken the instant it leaves you.

The locals — there were locals once, the scattered building says so — wrote on slates.

You are on a plateau that has not finished being made.

That is the simplest way to put it. The rock here is embryonic. Valleys have not learned to be valleys. There are hills still inflated, soft in the middle, like loaves that have risen but not baked. You can push a copper spike into a cliff face in the morning and by evening, weeks later by your own slow counting, the cliff has closed around it without cracking. Basalt cools here in bubbles and ropes and smooth, unscarred eggs the size of houses, none of them yet split by frost, because frost needs water and there is almost none, and none of them yet worn by wind, because erosion itself seems hesitant, inexperienced. Edges have not been invented yet. Everything is rounded, provisional, as if the geology is a first draft it intends to revise.

The sky over it is dim even at noon. The sun is a pale, small coin seen through smoked glass, not occluded so much as distant in a way that has nothing to do with distance. Light falls, but poorly. Shadows are longer than the objects that cast them, and they do not always attach. Sometimes you will see the shadow of a stone cairn lying clear on the pumice ten feet from the cairn itself, trembling in the tone, waiting for the stone to catch up. Sometimes at dusk — a dusk that takes half a day to happen, everything here is slow — the new rock gives back a faint light of its own, a bruised green phosphorescence from inside the not-yet-hard places, and then the shadows fall upward for a while.

It is cool, dryly, absolutely. Your lips crack within a day. Your breath does not fog. There is no dew, no rain, no river, not even the promise of one; the channels that might someday hold water are occupied by something else, a kind of pooled silence in the lowest ground, where the great tone for some reason cancels itself out. Those silent hollows are the most dangerous and the most barren places on the plateau. Nothing grows there. The pumice there is grey and truly dead. If you walk into one, the sudden absence in your bones is like falling.

Between the hollows, scattered across an open country you can walk for three days without crossing your own track, the vegetation is lush.

It should not be. There is no water to be lush with. But in the places where the tone collects — along fault seams, in the lee of the unfinished hills, in shallow bowls that act as ears — the growth crowds knee-deep, waist-deep, in dense, isolated jungles separated by miles of bare, new stone.

They are not plants as a wetter world would recognize them, though they borrow the shapes: ferns, mosses, low glossy trees no taller than a person, reeds. Their leaves are thick, waxy, filled not with sap but with pressurized air and a slow oil that beads black when you cut it and smells of hot copper and crushed mint. They do not drink from the soil. They drink the sound.

Hold still beside one of the fern groves and you will see it. Every frond is tuned. The largest leaves shiver at the lowest octave, almost too slow to see, drinking through their whole surface; the smaller, glassy leaflets higher up tremble faster, in harmonics. Dust and mineral grit shaken loose from the rising rock hangs in the air around them in standing patterns — rings, lattices, little Chladni mandalas suspended at leaf-height — and is slowly drawn in. That is their rain. That is their pollination, too, for there are no animals here at all. No birds startle. No insects add their own hum to the drone. No worms turn the thin, ashy soil. When the plants reproduce, a mature leaf will resonate until it exceeds what it can hold, shiver once, beautifully, and shatter into a hundred bell-clear seeds that ring as they fall and root where their note fades.

The absence of animal life is complete enough to become a presence. You keep listening for a second layer under the tone that will never arrive. You keep expecting, in the lush places, a rustle that is not caused by vibration. The groves move constantly, but they move together, all in phase, like hair underwater, and that sameness after a while is lonelier than stillness.

And the groves move across the country, over years. Because the note changes. Slowly — this whole place changes slowly, with the patience of something that has not yet learned hurry — the source below shifts, the mountain underneath the plateau is still pushing up at an inch, a handspan a year, and the resonant bowls migrate. The forests follow, dying crisply where the tone thins, sending their ringing seeds ahead into the new loud ground. You can find their old beds: perfect circles of black oil-sand and leaf-shaped hollows in the pumice, still faintly warm.

That is what the builders understood.

There are not many of them left as structures: a dozen low huts without roofs — roofs would have caught the tone and shaken themselves apart — built of unmortared bubble-stone, each with its floor deliberately dished like a drum. Cairns at the passes, not marking graves but notes; move one stone from a cairn and the note in that pass changes, just slightly, and the plants for a mile around re-tune. A forge, if you can call it that, with a simple copper anvil green with age, stone hammers, pliers beaten from a single sheet of metal, and frames of woven reed-fiber stretched with plant hide, primitive resonators meant to be stood inside when a person needed to think in quieter bone.

They wrote on slate because shouting, here, is agriculture. A loud voice in an untuned place can start a grove that was not intended. A word held too long takes root. Several of the abandoned huts are choked, burst from the inside, by a single glossy tree whose leaves are unmistakably shaped — recurved, cupped, almost like mouths — around a vowel someone spoke a generation ago and could not take back. It is still growing, very slowly. It is still, if you put your ear (useless) and then your forehead (better) to its trunk, saying it.

This is where the rules begin to fail in earnest.

Up here past the altitude where the world is supposed to be finished, cause arrives out of order when the tone is strong. You hear the ringing of a seed before the leaf shatters. You find your footprints ahead of you in the virgin ash on the far side of a grove, already softened at the edges, already with tiny black seedlings unfurling from the compression your weight has not yet made. Light comes late. The small sun rises, and perhaps twenty minutes later, sometimes an hour, the plateau decides to be lit. Memory is unreliable in a specific way: everyone who has spent a season here remembers the same hut from the inside, at the same fire that never burned because there is no wood dry enough and no need for heat in that particular memory, listening to someone explain in forehead-to-forehead speech how to leave.

You might not be able to leave by the way you came because the way you came is still being made, and it may be made differently behind you. The stair cut into the south face, three thousand shallow steps chiseled with copper tools into rock that was soft at the time, now arrives at a cliff that has reconsidered. In one of the silent hollows there is a perfect, dry lake bed of salt-white mineral, cracked in polygons, and in its center a wooden boat, primitive, caulked with plant oil, waiting with great seriousness for an element that has not been invented here yet. Its builders were not foolish. They were early.

The plateau is about the size of a small country or a large valley system, if size could be trusted; distances swell in loud ground, shrink in quiet. It takes an hour to cross a grove you could throw a stone over. The horizon is close and black-toothed with peaks that are still inflating, exhaling no steam, only a higher, almost inaudible harmonic that makes the fillings ache in teeth that have no fillings. At night — a slow, dim blue night where the stars are startlingly clear and too numerous and arranged in constellations that repeat, slightly offset, like an echo — the tone drops by perhaps a quarter-step over six hours, and all the lush places sigh down with it, leaves closing in tuned sequence from the largest to the smallest, and for a little while you can hear, under everything, the deeper process: stone being thought of. Grinding. Deciding. Becoming.

Nothing here is being destroyed. That is the strangest part for a visitor from an older world. Destruction will come later, with water and ice and time and animals to burrow and die. For now there is only the deafening, patient addition of a place to itself, and the green-black, sound-drunk life that has learned, ahead of schedule, how to live on it.

If you stay, you learn the local wisdoms quickly, written in many hands on the slate by the forge:

Walk in the loud places, rest in the moderately loud, never sleep in silence.
Keep your mouth shut in untuned ground.
If you must speak, speak in a grove that is already speaking, and say only what you are willing to harvest later.
Touch foreheads. Mean it.
And if you find a place where your shadow has arrived before you, sit down and wait politely for yourself. You are about to learn something you have, in the ringing air above this unfinished mountain, already said.
