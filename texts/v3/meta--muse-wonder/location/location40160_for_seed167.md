There is no surface here, not in any way that matters. A few body-lengths above the rooftops the water is a warm, brown-green dimness, the last place where light still remembers itself. Below that it gives up. What reaches the city is only a thinning of the dark.

The city lies in a low bowl.

It is cut into limestone and packed mud at the bottom of a warm lagoon, maybe fifteen meters down, in water so full of fine silt and drifting life that noon and midnight are nearly the same. The bowl is about a town across — a slow swim from rim to rim, if you follow the open avenues and do not get turned around in the side streets. Beyond it, the lagoon floor rises toward eelgrass flats and a distant, broken shore. Inside it, everything is built, occupied, and old.

Nothing here was made with a tool. The city was secreted, cemented, gathered, stacked, burrowed, and died into being.

Oysters made the first walls. Generation cemented to generation, shell upon dead shell, until their reefs rose in ridges and broken terraces. Sponges grew up between them in thick, breathing towers — yellow-grey, russet, dull purple in the near-dark — their chimneys open to the current, filtering the water that passes over the streets. Tube worms added their chalk pipes in dense white thickets. Crabs carried in broken shell, coral rubble, pebbles, and the clean-picked bones of fish, packing them into foundations. Octopuses dragged stones into rings and made doors that could be closed from inside.

So there are neighborhoods, after a fashion. Long, current-swept avenues where the limestone has been worn smooth as a tongue. Plazas kept clear by crabs endlessly sweeping silt away with their legs. Low houses of shell and stone with one round entrance. Vertical districts where a single sponge tower is bored and tunneled into a hundred chambers, each occupied, the residents separated only by a thickness of living wall. Old quarters where the building and the builders have both died, softened, and slumped together into mounds that younger things now live inside without knowing they are in a ruin.

Everything is rounded. The water has had a long time here.

It is warm all the way down — bath-warm, blood-warm — and it moves. Twice a day the tide turns and the whole city changes direction. In the morning the current comes cool-sweet, or as close to sweet as this tannin, plankton, mud-smelling water gets, flowing in over the lagoon rim and down the main avenues. It brings clouds. Not weather clouds, but food: plankton so dense the water grains against the skin, tiny translucent shrimp in drifting crowds, fish eggs in season, bits of detritus loosened from the flats above. The sponge towers lean imperceptibly toward it. A million small openings widen. The city inhales.

When the tide turns, it exhales back the other way, warmer and cloudier, carrying waste, larvae, shed skins, and the dead too small or too soft to be used as building material.

To live here is to know which way the streets are flowing by taste alone.

Vegetation barely holds on. There is no forest of kelp, no green meadow in this dark. At the upper rim, where the gloom is thinnest, a sparse eelgrass grows in separated blades, each one furred with algae. On the roofs there are films — brown, olive, a slick rust-red — bacterial and algal skins that make stone treacherous. White mats bloom for a few days over a dead ray or a collapsed wall, then are grazed away. Most of the city is animal and mineral: shell, mud, sponge, limestone, silt the color of tea.

But it is dense with fauna, dense in layers.

In the streets just above the rooftops, blind silver fish move in schools so coordinated they seem to be one animal changing its mind. They have almost no eyes. They feel each other, and the towers, and you, through the pressure-lines along their sides. Below them come the eel-things, ribbon-long, living in drains and under ledges, sliding out to hunt by smell. Translucent shrimp hang in the mouths of alleys by the thousands, antennae interlaced, so that a narrow street can be blocked by a living, trembling curtain that parts as you push through it.

Every crevice is tenanted. Small crabs in oyster valves that snap shut when a shadow of movement passes. Gobies in bottles that were never bottles — just shell-curved hollows of exactly the right shape. Worms in the mud with feathery crowns that vanish all at once, a whole garden disappearing in an instant, if a ray passes overhead. Sea cucumbers work the avenues at night-that-is-always-night, eating the street itself and leaving behind clean, pelleted sand. Clams under the foundations keep their siphons up like periscopes into the current.

And the octopuses are the great householders. Each keeps a den in the lower city, a stone-ringed room with a gathered floor — crab shell, bright pebble, the occasional perfect, spiraled shell saved for no edible reason. They maintain their doorways. They evict. They reach a long arm out without looking and take a passing crab with the thoughtful air of someone closing a shutter. When one dies, its house does not stay empty a full tide. By the next inflow there is a fight for it, silent, eight-armed, conducted mostly by touch in a doorway too narrow for the loser to retreat gracefully.

It is mostly quiet. Water takes sound and makes it intimate and strange — things are either very close or hardly there at all. A faint, muffled booming of surf arrives from somewhere beyond the rim, more in the chest than in the ears. Close by: the tick of crab feet on shell. The soft rasp of a snail crossing stone. A shrimp snap, now and then, sharp as a knuckle crack and immediately swallowed. Whole hours can pass with nothing louder than the working of the sponges, which is not a sound so much as a feeling that the walls are drinking.

The rules are simple because there is no choice in them.

You navigate by touch, smell, vibration, and the direction of flow. Light is a rumor from an upper world and also a danger — the few things that come down from the bright flats are hunters, herons stabbing from above at the rim, barracuda appearing like a thought at the edge of the avenues before they are gone and three fish fewer.

You do not waste protein. A dead fish is eaten, then its bones are lived in, then its bones are ground by urchins and crabs into the gravel that paves the newer streets. A collapsed house becomes a reef becomes a foundation becomes a house again. Nothing leaves the bowl unchanged.

You also do not build against the current. New sponge growth that blocks an avenue is eaten back, undercut, buried in silt within a season. The streets that remain are the ones the tide agreed to. This is why the city feels planned, though no one planned it — it has simply been pruned by water for longer than anything living in it can account for.

Now and then the warm stillness quickens. A storm passes somewhere above the lagoon, invisible except as a day of thicker dark and a rain of sediment that sets every filter-feeder coughing silt from its chimneys. The crabs work double shifts, clearing doorways. In spawning season the water turns briefly milky and frantic with eggs and larvae, and every mouth in the city is open at once, and even the predators seem, for an hour, too full to move. In the coldest month — cold being a relative term, a softening of the warmth — the eels leave the drains in numbers and knot together in the deepest plaza in a slow, blind, muscular ball, then disperse again as if nothing happened.

In the oldest part of the bowl, at the center, there is an open space where nothing is allowed to build. Crabs clear it. Fish circle it. The limestone there is carved by current into long parallel grooves, and at certain tides the flow across them makes the faintest possible tone, below hearing, in the bone. It may just be an accident of stone and water. But generations of animals have kept it open anyway, swimming around its edge rather than across it, generation after generation learning the detour from the movements of their parents.

That is how the city persists in darkness: not by map or memory, but by habits worn into bodies, and bodies worn into stone, until there is almost no difference between the place and what lives in it.
