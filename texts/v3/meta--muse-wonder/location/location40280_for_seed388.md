It is a town you could walk across in twelve minutes, if you ever walked in a straight line. You can't. 

It sits low in a warm bowl of ground where the water table is higher than most of the floors. From a distance it doesn't look like a town at all, just a dense black shoulder of roofs rising out of reed-marsh, all of them smoking, all of them wet. Then the ground firms under your feet — cinder, clinker, broken brick packed down over a hundred years — and the roofs separate into houses, sheds, boiler halls, pipe bridges, drying lofts, all of them built against each other, sharing walls, sharing gutters, leaning into the same damp heat.

There is no dry approach. The marsh comes right up to the doorsills. It comes, in places, *through* them. Shallow black water stands in the lanes and moves so slowly you only know it's moving because a leaf turns on it, very gradually, and is drawn toward a grating. Reeds push up between paving stones. Duckweed — though there are no ducks, never have been — makes a perfect, unbroken green skin on every still pool. If you step off the packed ways, your foot sinks to the ankle in warm silt that smells of iron and boiled leaves.

Everything is old, and settled into its wetness. This isn't a place flooding. This is a place that has finished flooding, generations ago, and arranged itself around the result. Brick steps are worn into shallow bowls. Iron handrails are polished bright where hands have gone for decades while the rest has gone to red scale and green rust. Stone lintels weep even when it hasn't rained. Roofs don't shed water anymore so much as conduct it, moss-thick, drip by deliberate drip, into barrels, troughs, ceramic channels that run along the eaves at head height. Nothing has a sharp edge left. The water and the work have taken them all off.

The heat comes from the ground and from the Works in the middle.

The Works is not one building. Or perhaps the whole town is one building and the Works is its heart. It's hard to tell where factory stops and housing starts. A row of narrow workers' cottages has a flue running through its back wall; their kitchens are warm in all weathers because the main boiler is on the other side of the bricks. A chapel-like pump hall — tall, arched windows gone opaque with steam — has laundry strung across its nave on lines that sway in the updraft. Apartments are built on top of the coal store. A schoolroom vibrates gently all day above the flywheels. Stairs go up outside, turn, become a pipe-walk, become someone's balcony hung with ferns, become a bridge over an open canal of steaming outflow, and end at a door painted blue that hasn't been dry since it was painted.

The light is low and greenish brown, even at noon. Warm cloud never quite lifts off the marsh, and smoke from three short chimneys doesn't rise so much as spread and join it. Over that, the plants have made another roof. Because though it is dim, it is lush in the way only shade and permanent wet can be lush. Huge soft leaves crowd every unguttered wall. Liverwort carpets the north sides of houses up to the windows. Ferns grow out of mortar joints three stories up, fed by a leaking downpipe. Vines have found the telegraph wires and the pipe insulation and thickened them into green cables. In the warmer vents by the boilers, something like a fig grows, broad-leafed and glossy, its roots gripping hot brick. Its leaves are perfect.

That is the strange thing you notice second, after the noise. All the leaves are perfect. Nothing has eaten them.

There is no animal life here. 

No midges over the marsh, though the air is warm and still and should be thick with them. No frogs in the ditches, no rats in the coal store, no pigeons in the eaves, no worms turned up when a paving slab is lifted. No spider has webbed the corners of the pump hall, though the corners are dark and undisturbed for years. Fish do not rise in the canal. The green skin on the pools is never broken from below. People live here — a few hundred, close enough in the narrow lanes that you smell soap and coal smoke and wet wool on them as they pass — but they live in a landscape that has politely, completely, excluded every other breathing and crawling thing. Food is stored in open sheds and doesn't spoil by pests, only softens with mold. The reeds stand full-seeded and un-nibbled. At night, when the Works drops to its low cycle, you can hear how total the absence is under the machines, because there is nothing small and alive to fill the gaps.

Instead, the machines fill everything.

The sound is enormous, but it is slow. That is the rhythm of the place: loudness without hurry. The main pump makes one stroke every twenty seconds, and each stroke is a deep, chest-felt *thud-hiss* that travels through the brick underfoot before it reaches your ears. Conversations take it into account without thinking; people pause on the thud, resume on the hiss, or talk in short, practised hand-signs over their shoulders while carrying things. Between strokes there's the constant voice of escaping steam, water hammer knocking patiently in the pipes overhead, a loose inspection plate somewhere that has rattled at the same tempo for ten years, and once an hour the slow grind of the sluice gear, tooth by tooth, raising a gate by inches. The flywheels in the hall turn at walking pace, but they are twelve feet across and belted in leather as thick as your arm, and their passing makes the window glass bow faintly in and out, in and out, all day.

It is industrial, dense, electrical. Thick cables in tarred bundles run under the eaves, sagging with condensation. Lamps burn day and night because the streets are roofed in too many places by walkways and washing and leaf canopy to ever be bright: amber bulbs in wire cages, hissing faintly where drips strike them, reflected in every wet surface so the town at knee-height is always lit by a second, watery town. Dynamos smell of hot oil and ozone. There is a small turbine house where the outflow drops three feet — the only fall for miles — and that three feet, gathered and geared and patient, lights the whole settlement.

Water is used, and used, and used again before it is allowed back to the marsh. It arrives brown from the reed beds in an open leat. It settles in tiled cisterns under the main street — you can go down damp steps behind the pump hall and stand in a vaulted, cathedral-quiet space, except for the dripping, and watch the town's whole weight supported on brick arches standing in black water that mirrors the lamps perfectly. It is pumped up to header tanks on the roofs, where it warms in the sun that never quite reaches the streets. It runs the boilers, cools the bearings, washes the coal, fills the kettles, comes out grey from the houses into another set of channels, is strained through beds of reed and gravel on the southern edge where the women pollinate the greenhouse vegetables by hand with little brushes — there being nothing else to do that work — and finally slips away under a low iron bridge, no warmer or dirtier, the old men will tell you, than when it came in. Only slower.

On the roofs, the condensers make a private weather. Warm vapor from the vents meets the cooler air trapped under the leaf canopy and falls again as a fine, warm rain that never reaches the marsh outside. It rains upward there too, or seems to: droplets forming on the undersides of the tanks, growing, letting go, and being caught mid-fall by an updraft and carried back to condense again. Clothes hung indoors dry only to a certain point and stay there, agreeably warm and permanently damp. Paper is kept in tin boxes. Books swell. Iron tools are wiped with grease every evening as a matter of course, like feeding something.

That's what living here mostly is: maintenance at the pace of the pumps. Wiping, greasing, clearing a grating of reed-matting, re-pointing a wall the ferns have loosened, replacing a gasket. Nothing changes quickly, but nothing is ever finished. The town sinks a finger's width a year into the marsh, and the Works pumps it, by that exact amount, back up. Old doorways have had three new thresholds added, one atop the next. A window that was at street level in a grandmother's time is now a coal hatch. No one considers this a problem. It's a calendar you can build on.

By evening — though evening is only the lamps becoming relatively brighter, and the cloud going from grey-green to black-green — the stroke of the pump slows to one every forty seconds. People move their chairs into the lanes where the water has been warmed by outflow pipes running under the stones, and sit with their feet almost in it, talking across the hiss. The leaves close nothing down. The marsh exhales. Condensation starts to run in earnest down every wall, and the whole close-built, steaming, verdant town ticks and drips and settles, stroke after slow stroke, exactly where it has settled for as long as anyone's house has stood: low, loud, wet, and entirely, inexplicably unshared with anything that creeps or flies.
