There is a plain, and then there is not.

You are walking on warm, flat ground — pale dust, sun-hard stone, no shade worth naming — and the light is ordinary daylight, strong and white, and then the earth simply stops and goes down. Not a canyon worn down over ages. This one has no softened edges. It is new.

They call it the New Low.

It is a rift valley that opened below the surface of the world and has not finished opening. Twenty, maybe thirty kilometers long now, a kilometer wide in the middle and narrowing to a hand's breadth crack at the far, growing end, and deep enough that the floor has its own mornings and evenings. The sun does not reach the bottom until late, and leaves it early, but when it does it arrives all at once, reflected off unweathered walls, and for four or five hours the below-place is as bright as anywhere above. Bright, and warm, and loud.

Nothing about it is eroded yet. That is the first strangeness your feet learn. The walls are not slopes so much as fractures: sheets of basalt and rust-red stone and black volcanic glass split clean, with edges that can still cut hide. Scree at the base is angular, interlocked, unrounded by water or wind. A stone kicked loose yesterday still shows its bright interior today. Where rain has fallen — and it falls strangely here, in brief local showers made of the place's own steam condensing on cool morning rock — it has cut only the first tentative rills, finger-width, wandering, stopping where a new crack has taken the water away underground. There is no soil. There is no green.

Vegetation has not arrived. It may not have been invited yet. What covers the damp places instead is simpler and older: microbial mats in sulfur-yellow and milky white and a bruised orange, spreading like spilled paint around warm seeps; translucent fungal threads that are perhaps not fungi at all but mineral crystal growing hair-fine in the condensation; slicks of algae-dark slime in shadowed pockets where light leaks but sun does not. They smell of iron, of hot pennies, of rotting eggs and wet clay. They are eaten, constantly.

Because if there are no plants, there is nevertheless life past bearing.

The opening of the Low exposed a world that had been living in darkness under the plain, and also made a trap and a refuge for every creature above it. The result is a density that feels less like an ecology than like an evacuation that never ends.

At dawn the bats come back. Not thousands. A continuous river of them, so thick they shade the eastern wall, pouring down the updrafts to roost in fractures that did not exist last month. Their returning sets off the crickets — palm-sized, blind, milk-pale cave crickets that were never meant for daylight and now spend the bright hours packed leg-to-leg under overhangs, clicking and shifting, dropping in panicked drifts when a shadow passes. Where a new vent has opened warm and wet, flying insects hatch in synchronized millions from mud that was dry at sunrise, rise, mate, die, and carpet the pools by afternoon, and then the swifts and swallows and improbable clouds of dragonflies come screaming down from the plain to feed, and their noise is added to the bats settling, and the frogs — eyeless some of them, pink-skinned, others ordinary green frogs fallen or washed in from above and now breeding explosively in every temporary pool — and the snakes following the frogs, and the rodents following everything that dies.

There is a food web here with no leaves in it. It runs on chemosynthesis and catastrophe: bacteria eating sulfur and iron, insects eating bacteria, everything eating insects, and a constant rain of death from above. Lizards, seeds that will not sprout in this hot mineral ground, wind-blown carrion, whole ant colonies undermined on the plain and delivered, still organized, into the depths. On the floor you walk on crunching drifts of beetle shell and shed skin. In the pools exposed when an underground river was decapitated by the split, there are fish, white and sightless, suddenly living under the sun. They turn slow circles. Herons have found them. The herons look bewildered and ecstatic.

And the place will not hold still long enough for any of this to settle into habit.

Dynamic is too calm a word. The floor heaves — measurably, a hand's span in a bad week. Plates of new stone tilt, grind, subside with a sound like a house collapsing far away. A mud pot that bubbled gently at breakfast boils dry by evening, cracks, and becomes a steam vent hissing loud enough to force conversation into shouting. A crack that was a damp seep yesterday is today a stream, tomorrow a black slot that swallows the stream whole and breathes hot air. Rockfalls are not events, they are weather. Everyone, animal and human, has learned to read the prelude: the crickets go silent first, then the dust lifts from a wall in a fine, thoughtful veil, then your teeth feel it.

So the animals move like water finding new channels. Twice, three times a day, triggered by a tremor or a vent bursting or nothing visible at all, a panic will run the length of the floor — thousands of creatures flowing around boulders, parting, rejoining, rodents and lizards and flightless insects and startled birds all in one direction for fifty meters, a hundred, then stopping as abruptly as if a switch had been thrown, and resuming eating and mating and fighting as if nothing had happened. Experienced guides watch these tides the way sailors watch currents. A still pool full of fish is a warning, weirdly; it means the ground beneath is warming. A crowd of animals gathered on high, broken ground that looks exposed is a recommendation. They know which stone sings before it falls.

The human presence is scattered and deliberately light. No one builds here as if to stay.

On ledges partway down, and on raised plates on the floor that have been stable for at least a season — which is to say, ancient by local reckoning — there are windbreaks of stacked unworked stone, low and crescent-shaped, roofed sometimes with hide on poles that can be carried away in minutes. Cairns mark paths, but everyone understands a cairn marks where a path was. Fire pits are lined with obsidian shards. The tools are stone, bone, wood, twisted fiber: chipped scrapers for butchering the windfall of animal death, bone hooks for the blind fish, woven nets that are set at dusk across bat-rivers and taken down heavy before midnight. Metal is rare and never wasted on anything that will be abandoned, which is everything.

Camps are placed by sound as much as by sight. Too near the Wet Seam — that restless middle zone where most of the pools and seeps and steaming mud collect — and the frogs and vents will keep you from sleep and the ground may be a crust over scalding water thin enough to break under a child's weight. Too near the Dry Pan at the settling western end and you will haul every drop you drink down from the rim in skins, past nests and roosts so dense the path is white with droppings and loud with protest.

People here have rules, offered without ceremony to newcomers. Never camp where the mats are black; black means the water under them kills. Never follow an animal tide, only cross it. If the birds go up all at once and do not come down, leave your food, leave your shelter, go up the nearest scree — not the nearest path, the nearest scree. Mark nothing with a cut, because cuts heal over here as the stone swells and you will trust a mark that has moved.

At the far eastern end is the Tearing. You can stand at a safe distance — a distance revised daily, scratched on a tally stick — and watch the valley invent itself. It does not look dramatic most minutes. Dust. A crack darkening. A sound from inside the earth like ice breaking on a lake no one can see. Then a block the size of a house settles a meter with a boom that goes through your sternum, bats erupt from a fissure that was not admitting bats an hour before, and for a moment the air is full of white crickets falling upward on the heat. By the next morning there will be a new pool there, or a new vent, or a new sheer wall with strata exposed in bands of red and black and cream that no eye has ever seen, already being written on by bacteria, already being colonized by things arriving on the wind and on feet, already beginning — too soon, eagerly — to be a habitat.

The New Low has not decided what it is. A lake bed waiting for a river that keeps being stolen underground. A valley that may close again, slowly, and take its brief sunlit underworld back into darkness. A wound, a nursery, a pit. In twenty years the sharp edges may soften, seeds may find purchase in dust caught in cracks, the animal flood may thin into something that can be called a population instead of a crowd. For now it is raw geology with an ecosystem happening to it at full volume, bright as day and below the world, damp in its hollows and hot in its bones, unfinished in a way that makes everything living in it live faster.

At midday, when the sun finally stands over the rift and light floods straight down, the whole floor is revealed at once — steaming, crawling, glittering with wet mineral and wings. It does not look empty for lack of plants. It looks overfull, as if the earth had been cut open and its hidden contents, startled, were still pouring into the light.
