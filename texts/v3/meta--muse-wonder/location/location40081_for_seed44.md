It is low ground that never quite dries.

From the levee road you drop down perhaps twenty feet into a shallow bowl, an old marsh oxbow that was drained once and then only half-kept drained. The rim is a grassed earth embankment, slick and dark, with a single gravel track coming down between banana stands. Below it, packed into an area you could walk across in fifteen minutes, is the town, the mill, the housing, the canals — all of it held inside vegetation so thick the settlement does not so much sit in the plants as occur inside them.

The first impression is green weight. Not a garden, not overgrowth on top of buildings, but plants as the actual substrate. Banyan-type figs, planted generations ago and trained on iron frames, make the walls. Their aerial roots have thickened into buttresses, fused into lattices, grown through corrugated iron and around concrete posts until you cannot always tell where support ends and tree begins. Bamboo culms, fifteen feet high, stand in dense trained rows for fences and scaffolding, still living at the base, cut flat at the top. Broad reed mats form awnings. Banana, elephant grass, taro, cane, and a low, spongy carpet of moss and fern fill every interval that is not a path, a canal, or a working yard.

And water is in all of it, just under the surface. The soil under the root-mat paths gives with a soft, saturated sigh. Shallow wells are only four feet deep before they brim. Canals, stone-lined and worn smooth, run in a grid under plank bridges, brown-green and slow, choked at the edges with water hyacinth. Every roof drips long after rain. Every iron pipe in the shade sweats condensation. In the morning, water weeps straight out of the fig trunks where they press against warm walls, and runs down in clean lines through the algae.

It is warm in the bowl in a way the levee road is not. The canopy and the close walls trap it. The air is normal daylight — bright enough at noon in the central yard that you squint — but filtered everywhere else through two and three layers of leaf, so most lanes live in a humid, green-tinted shade. Steam from the boiler house does not rise and disperse; it hangs, mingles with mist off the canals, and drifts at head height between the drying sheds. Clothes put out on lines between dormitory blocks never get crisp. Paper softens. Leather is oiled constantly or it blooms white with mold within a week.

The built part is unmistakably a town, and an industrial one, just compressed. At the center, on the only real patch of raised, graveled ground, is the Combine: a fiber mill and pump station and boiler house built back-to-back under one long, patched roof of rust-streaked tin. Inside, belt drives slap and turn, decorticators chew cane and reed fiber with a rhythmic, grinding roar, and two big diesel pumps keep up a bass thump you feel in the soles of your feet anywhere within a hundred yards. That is the governing sound. Added to it are saw-teeth biting bamboo, steam let off in sudden white sighs, hammers on pipe, the clatter of a small rail trolley hauling wet bales along a single loop track, and everywhere, under it, water — dripping from leaf to tin to canal, trickling in gutters cut into living wood, plinking from eaves into barrels that overflow perpetually.

It is loud, but nothing moves quickly.

That is the paradox of the place once you have stayed an hour. The machines are urgent; the town itself changes at the pace of roots. The canals silt slowly. The fig walls thicken by fractions of an inch a season. Subsidence is measured in painted marks on the pumphouse door, checked monthly. The flood pulse, when the river outside the levee rises in late summer, arrives not as a rush but as a creeping lift in all the wells at once, an extra inch in the canals at breakfast, another by the next morning, floorboards in the lowest sheds going dark with damp from beneath. The pumps work harder for weeks, the hyacinth mats rise and press against the bridges, and then, just as slowly, it all settles back.

Around the mill yard, the urban fabric is layered tight. Workers' housing is two and three stories of timber and woven panel and salvaged sheet metal, built up on pilings and old mill piers, connected by covered walkways that are also trellises. Laundries, cookhouses, a mess hall with its chimney perpetually streaming thin woodsmoke from the one permitted hearth besides the boilers, a company store, repair shops for belt leather and pump valves and machetes — each is wedged into the next, separated not by empty lots but by a breathing wall of planted cane. Lanes are named for practical things: Pump Lane, Dry Shed Cut, Second Canal. The little market square is not paved at all, but floored with a dense, springy mat of interwoven roots that has been worn polished by feet in the middle, where women sell taro, dried fish brought in from outside, kerosene, salt, machine oil, lengths of rope the mill itself has made.

Materials repeat wherever you look: dark wet teak gone almost black, concrete stained bottle-green, copper pipe verdigrised, iron kept alive with grease that collects green dust, rope that must be replaced because it rots, canvas that swells and stiffens. Nothing new stays new. The established, weathered quality is not picturesque ruin — it is maintenance as a state of equilibrium. A crew of pruners is always out, as essential as the pump engineers, standing on ladders with saws, thinning a roof of leaves so a lane gets light, cutting back a fig root that has begun to lift a walkway, but never, by unwritten and then very written rule, cutting a structural root through. You do not clear here. You negotiate. If a house needs to expand, you train a new wall for two seasons before you can hang a door in it. If the mill needs a new pipe run, it goes over the canal on a living bridge of bound bamboo, not through the bank.

Ecology has simplified itself under the noise and the wet heat. There are frogs in the canals at night, a sudden chorus after the mill shuts down for its brief midnight pause, and mosquitoes in the still air under the awnings. But larger animal life is surprisingly rare. No herds, few dogs — they do not thrive with the damp in their lungs — and only an occasional heron standing motionless in the settling pond behind the mill where the fiber wash-water spreads in shallow, reed-planted pans before it is let back to the river. Sometimes a snake will be found coiled on a warm pipe in the morning and will be carried, carefully, on a long pole to the levee and released, because killing things is less useful here than relocating them. The real fauna is the plant mass itself, digesting light, lifting water, dropping leaf litter into the canals to be dredged and composted and spread back on the levee gardens.

Smell binds it together: hot iron and diesel, wet earth, crushed green fiber sweet and slightly fermented from the soaking pits, woodsmoke, algae warming in sunlit canal reaches, and the faint, clean smell of rain even on days when it has not rained, just condensed and fallen from the canopy.

By late afternoon the light in the yard goes amber on the tin roofs while the lanes are already in shadow, and the pumps change shift with a double blast of steam that sends every loose leaf trembling. Bales of finished rope fiber, still damp, are stacked under open sheds to cure, and they give off heat of their own as they do. People move homeward across the little bridges with their trousers rolled, stepping automatically around the places where a root has made a trip-step everyone knows. The fig walls close around the sounds, containing them. From the levee at dusk, you cannot really see the town at all — only a denser, darker green in the hollow, a plume of steam lit from below, floating lights in windows behind leaves, and that steady, muffled thumping coming up through the ground, as if the marsh itself had taken on a slow, industrial heartbeat and was content to keep it.
