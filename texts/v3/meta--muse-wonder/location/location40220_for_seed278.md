It is a high country that has forgotten how to be sharp.

You come up to it — though *up* stops meaning much after a while — and the land does not rise into peaks so much as it simply stays high, for months of walking in any direction. Four, five thousand metres above whatever sea used to exist below the cloud rim, and still the horizon is not a ridge but a continuation: pale stone, low undulation, another shallow basin, another pale stone whaleback beyond it. The ancient mountains here were worn down so long ago that erosion has finished its argument. Everything is rounded, settled, balanced. Granite domes sit half-buried in their own gravel. Basalt flows have been polished to a dull sheen by wind-borne ice. Frost has cracked the plateau surface into slow polygons, ten, twenty metres across, and that cracking is still happening, at a pace you could outlive without noticing.

It is large enough to refuse weather from elsewhere. Around its edges, storms circle and break themselves trying to climb. In the interior, under a permanent high-pressure dome of its own making, the air barely moves. That is the first rule of the place: the centre is calm, not because it is sheltered, but because it is too vast and too cold for weather to cross it. Clouds form in a ring far away at the rim. Here, the sky is a thin, dark blue overhead fading to a perpetual dim grey at the edges, as if the sun is always just about to rise somewhere else. It never climbs high. For half the year it circles, low and watery. For the other half it only brightens the horizon.

And it is silent in a way that is not empty. Sound simply does not keep. The air is too thin, the ground is covered in frost-felt and fine mineral dust, and everything alive has learned to move without announcing itself. Your own breath is an intrusion. The creak of your boots is obscenely loud, then gone, swallowed after a few metres. There is no wind-song, no running water, no birdsong. When your ears adjust, you hear your heart, and the faint ticking of ice contracting under stone, and sometimes — not a sound, exactly — a pressure in your teeth.

Water is present only as dampness and delay. There are no real rivers on the plateau. In summer, if you can call the slightly less dark weeks summer, the top centimetre of permafrost loosens and black melt-pans appear in the basins: lens-shaped pools with no inlet and no outlet, mirror-still, tasting of iron and salt. Condensation beads on cold rock at dawn and freezes before it can run. Hoarfrost grows sideways on the lee of boulders. Most moisture in this place has been breathed, once. It leaves a warm body, clouds for a second, and falls as glittering crystals that do not melt. The ground stays just wet enough under the surface crust for life to persist in the cracks — a dark skin of lichen, grey-green and copper-orange, hugging stone so tightly it looks like a stain in the mineral itself; cushion mosses the size of a hand, centuries old, growing only in old footprints where the soil was pressed and warmed and fertilised; in the melt-pans, a brief, rust-red algae that blooms, feeds a hatch of glassy insects, and is gone within weeks.

There should not be enough vegetation to support what lives here. That is the second rule, and it takes a long time to understand because at first the plateau looks empty.

The animals are spaced so far apart that density feels like absence. A dark shape on the horizon. Another, three kilometres beyond it. A line of them, perhaps, along a basin rim, each separated by a distance that makes them look solitary. Then you realise the horizon itself is moving, very slowly, everywhere you look, and that the shapes are not boulders — or rather, some of the boulders you passed yesterday are not where you left them.

They are the size that makes scale unreliable. The largest — the Furnace Beasts — are thirty metres from blunt, low head to tail, six-legged, broad as a factory floor, their backs humped with overlapping plates of bone and dark, iron-rich hide. They graze with their heads down, scraping lichen and mineral crust from stone with teeth that are not quite teeth: dense alloy ridges, grown, not made, that wear into bright facets. What they ingest would starve anything else: lichen, rock dust, frozen soil, the metallic salts around the melt-pans. Inside them, it does not merely digest. It refines.

A Furnace Beast is warm in a way nothing else on the plateau is allowed to be. Heat moves under its skin in visible migrations on a cold morning, shimmering the air above its spine. Its gut is a sequence of chambers — grinding, fermenting, and then something close to smelting, where symbiotic microbes and swallowed grit and its own electrical current break ore down into usable metal and sterile, steaming waste. The metals go into bone, plate, teeth, blood. The waste comes out behind it in slow, dark falls that steam, freeze, and become, over the following decade, the richest soil on the plateau. A faint track of moss follows every herd, years behind them, like a wake.

They do not call to one another. Opening the mouth wastes heat. Instead they communicate through the ground — a patterned pressure of those enormous felt-padded feet, felt rather than heard — and through the short, whiskered organs along their jaws, which find each other in crackling, nearly invisible exchanges of static. When a herd is near, your hair lifts. Compasses lose interest in north and turn, politely, toward the animals. The ice in the melt-pans shows moving lights deep under its surface, not reflections but induced currents, the herd's collective field stirring the salty water as they pass. Their bodies, in aggregate, are a dynamo the size of a weather system. No one built this industry. It grew. It has castes and throughputs and production lines that take sixty years to complete a single circuit of the continent: the Breakers out front, smaller, tusked, loosening stone; the Furnaces processing; the lean, long-necked Seeders at the rear, sifting the warm waste with sieved mouths, distributing lichen spores and insect eggs from glands along their flanks. A factory that walks so slowly its chimneys are nostrils baffled with flesh, exhaling heat without a hiss, condensing its own smoke into frost that falls straight down.

And the Furnace Beasts are only the most obvious life. Once you learn to look, the fauna is overwhelming — not crowded, but inescapable, as if every niche that cold and dimness and thin air should have closed has been occupied by something patient and strange. Blind white burrowers move beneath the frost polygons, following the electrical roots of lichen. Gliders with wings like taut grey sails ride the almost-nonexistent thermals above sun-warmed stone, never flapping, never crying, their shadows crossing the melt-pans for minutes at a time. In the brief algae bloom, the pans fill with segmented, copper-shelled swimmers that filter the water and are eaten, in turn, by knee-high stalkers who fish without splashing, freezing one foot to the ice and waiting hours between strikes. Small herd animals — dense-furred, faceless-looking under their sensory manes — travel in the lee of the Furnaces to steal their warmth, cleaning parasites from between the great plates with tongues rasped like files. Even a carcass does not end the industry: a dead Furnace lies on the plateau for a century, looking, from the air, exactly like a wrecked and rusting installation, ribs like the frame of a hangar, turbine-curved lungs collapsed inside, its iron bones slowly harvested by beetles and burrowers and lichen until the distinction between skeleton and ore deposit is only a matter of timescale.

There are no nests here. No burrow entrances you could call architecture, no dams, no structures, no artifacts. Young are born standing on open ground in the wind-shadow of their mothers and are walking within the hour. The dead are not buried. Tools are not made. The technology of this place is entirely embodied and inherited — grown teeth, grown circuitry of nerve and metal, bacterial furnaces carried gut to gut from parent to calf. When the first surveyors saw the herds from orbit they mapped them as industrial sprawl. Only at ground level does it sort itself into breath and appetite.

Predation, too, is quiet. The largest hunters do not chase. They lie the colour of stone in the shallow basins for days, metabolism nearly stopped, feeling for foot-pressure through the frost, and rise only when a Furnace calf has strayed far enough that the herd's electrical field thins. Even then there is no roar, no scream. A struggle conducted mostly by weight, in a silence so complete that the sound of your own swallowing becomes the loudest event for kilometres.

Time here works at the pace of the herds. A migration is not seasonal; it is generational, a slow spiral from basin to basin that may take longer than a human lifetime to repeat. Vegetation follows behind at the speed of moss. Lakes form, evaporate to salt, are ground underfoot, and form again in the same footprints ten thousand years later. The dim light does not so much change through the day as it thins and thickens, and in the long polar dusk the animals' static markings glow faintly — rows of blue-white dots along flanks and throats, brightening when they exchange signals, dimming when they sleep standing in loose circles, heads inward, frost building on their outer plates until they look like part of the terrain again.

Stand among them (they will not flee; nothing here has learned to fear anything as small and brief as you, though the ground-pressure of your steps will be investigated by whiskers and tongues the size of doors) and you become aware that the emptiness was a misreading. The plateau is full. It is full at a scale and a tempo and a volume that human senses weren't built to register: biomass in the millions of tonnes, moving at centimetres per hour, conducting its business in electricity and pressure and heat, in near-darkness and near-silence, under a sky it manufactures itself.

Eventually the herd passes. This takes days. The last Seeder moves over the basin rim, the induced lights fade under the ice, your compass remembers north. The moss in the new footprints is already darkening, damp with condensed breath. Where the Furnaces fed, the stone is scraped clean and bright. Where they slept, there are shallow warm depressions in the frost, already freezing from the edges inward.

Nothing has been built. Nothing has been said. In a decade, something green will be growing in their wake, very slowly, in the cold.
