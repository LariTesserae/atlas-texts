The sound reaches you before the place does.

Not birdsong. Not insects. There are none of those here. It is a roar made entirely of plants growing too fast in warm air — leaves unfolding by the million and rasping against each other, trunks thickening and splitting their own bark, seed pods warming until they burst with cracks like distant gunfire, vines dragging themselves over vines, roots forcing down through root. From the low ridge above the valley it sounds like a waterfall. Closer, it sounds like weather trapped in a room. Inside, you will have to shout to hear yourself think, and even then the thought comes back changed, chewed green.

Below you the valley is a city.

That is the wrong word, and it is also the only one. It extends for several kilometres across a warm, shallow basin, district after district in a loose, rising sprawl, and everything in it — street, wall, tower, bridge, roof, plaza, stair — is plant. There is no stone foundation, no timber cut dead, no tool mark anywhere. It has grown into urban form the way a vine grows into the shape of a fence, except the fence was never there.

Tower-trees stand five, eight, ten storeys high in crowded terraces, their trunks fluted and buttressed like apartment blocks, balconies of broad leaf extruded at irregular intervals. Between them, hedge-walls run in true straight lines for fifty metres and then, dissatisfied, buckle and produce a corner. Root-bridges arch over sunken avenues. Canopy roofs meet overhead and are immediately pierced by younger, more ambitious shoots. Every surface is layered: bark under moss under creeper under flowering vine under a skin of seedlings that have taken root in the dust caught in the joints of the older growth. There is no exposed ground. What looks like ground is a woven mat of root and fallen leaf and compressed green matter, still warm, still faintly moving under your feet, as if the whole valley floor were breathing through its sole.

And it is new. That is the strange part under all the strangeness. For all its density, nothing here looks old in the way forests are old. The ridges at the valley's edge are sharp, recently split, pale inside where the earth tore. The plant-cliffs have not softened. Thorn-hedges end in bright, unweathered points. A tower that collapses — and they are always collapsing somewhere, with a long wooden scream and a concussion of leaves — leaves a wound of clean white wood and shocking sap that starts sprouting before the dust has settled. The city does not accumulate ruins. It composts them at speed, in public, loudly. By the time you have crossed a plaza, the fallen tower behind you is a knee-high thicket in the shape of grief.

The light is ordinary daylight, which makes it worse. Nothing hides in gloom. The sun stands clear above the basin and pours down through the canopy in green-gold shafts, and every impossible thing is brightly, plainly visible. The air is hot — not desert hot, but greenhouse hot, a wet warmth that lives at skin level — though the place is, by any measure, almost dry. Rain falls rarely and never fairly. Instead the city sweats. At dawn, condensation gathers on a billion leaves and for perhaps twenty minutes there is a private, interior drizzle beneath the canopy, fat drops ticking down from leaf to leaf into cup-shaped basins and hollow trunk-cisterns. Then the warmth takes it. By mid-morning the avenues are dusty with pollen and shredded leaf. The plants hoard what little water they collect with visible suspicion; the cup-leaves fold if a shadow falls across them too purposefully. If you are thirsty here, you learn to wake before the light and drink from whatever has not yet noticed you.

There are no animals. No flies around the fermenting fruit that drops and rots uneaten in the gutters. No birds in the tower-trees. No tooth marks, no droppings, no paths worn by anything with feet. And yet the flowers open, enormous and obscene, in the middle of thoroughfares. The deafening is how they breed. At certain hours the whole valley finds a rhythm — a slow pressure of creak and rustle and pod-burst building street by street — until the air itself begins to vibrate in your teeth and your sternum, and the pollen shakes loose in visible golden weather, drifting sideways through the avenues in clouds thick enough to cast shadows. The shadows fall upward onto the undersides of the canopy. Nobody remarks on this. There is nobody.

You enter by an avenue, because the vegetation parts in avenue shapes whether anyone walks there or not. It is paved with a flat, grey-green lichen that gives slightly, like fat. On either side, house-fronts of braided vine present doorways: single huge leaves, veined like hands, that curl open as you approach and sigh shut behind you. Inside — you will look inside, everyone does — are rooms. Root-furniture. A table grown from one continuous piece of living wood, still putting out buds along its edge. Chairs shaped with an opinion about the human spine. In one house, a staircase. In another, a bed of moss in a frame of smooth saplings, and beside it a smaller bed. For whom, the city declines to say. Nothing has ever slept here. The moss is unslept-in, immaculate, faintly warm, and in the night it grows up through the frame and over the pillow in an exact, tender imitation of a sleeper's shape, and in the morning the shape gets up on no legs and is reabsorbed by the wall.

Deeper in, the city's mimicry becomes less architectural and more personal, which is the point at which most visitors begin shouting just to prove the voice in the noise is theirs.

It builds from memory, not consciously — there is no reason to think it is conscious, and several reasons, screaming at 3 a.m. from inside a flower the size of a wardrobe, to hope it is not. But a district you pass through twice will have adjusted. The second time, the arcade of hanging roots will be the arcade from the town where you grew up, translated into leaf. A courtyard will arrange its fountain-vines — water hoarded all week released at noon in one extravagant, wasteful, hissing plume — around a space the exact shape of a room you have not entered in thirty years. The plants get the details wrong in the way dreams do. The childhood bedroom has too many doors, all of them leaves, all of them breathing. The wallpaper pattern is a repeating moth that is also a flower that is also, when the light moves, your own name in a script you almost recognise. Then a gust of growth goes through the street like a shiver and the whole block decides to be something else — a market, perhaps, stalls of fruit offered to no buyers, awnings of petal snapping in a wind that exists only at stall height — and you are politely in the way of a crowd that isn't there, jostled by pressures in the loud air where shoulders would be.

The geometry does not hold still long enough to betray you all at once. Streets run straight and arrive somewhere else. You can walk downhill in every direction from the central plaza, which is a vast open bowl of white flowering ground-cover that tracks the sun with a sound like frying. You can turn left four times around a tower and come out on the tower's roof, standing among its crown-shoots, looking down at the street where you can see — because the growth there is young and has not yet learned privacy — yourself, still walking, a few minutes out of date, shouting something up at you that the leaf-roar eats. If you wave, the other you does not wave back. The other you is looking at the roof, where no one is standing. This is considered, by the city, a normal intersection.

Growth here is not a background process. It is the traffic, the weather, and the politics. A shoot will come up through the avenue lichen while you watch, hesitate, and commit to becoming a wall. Seeds land on your shoulders and root in the warmth of your collar within the hour if you stand still listening. Vines test the air in your direction with the delicacy of fingers reading a face. Whole neighbourhoods flower at once and drop their petals in a warm, suffocating snowfall that piles in drifts against doorsills and is gone by evening, digested. Fruit splits on the branch and spills seeds that sprout in the pulp of their own parent. A tree on the corner of a street whose name you never learn ages as you pass it — bud, leaf, blossom, fruit, rot, and a bare black architecture of branches shaking their seeds down like rattles — and then, because you have passed, begins again at bud, slightly less patient this time. Its shadow, however, continues to the end of the street at the tree's own pace and turns the corner alone.

There are seasons, but they are addresses, not times. One quarter is always in first spring, shrill green and dripping with the dawn-condensation that elsewhere lasts minutes and there lasts all day, running in runnels down shining trunks into the thirsty dark between roots. The next street over is in a permanent late summer, heavy, resinous, half-asleep, its leaf-litter fermenting sweetly in the heat. Cross a bridge of braided aerial roots — it will sway toward destinations — and you are in an autumn of continuous fall, leaves detaching in their millions and drifting up as often as down, so the air is a slow turbulence of colour and the ground-cover beneath is perpetually surprised by a rain of gold from a clear sky. The residents of these seasons are flowers that imitate bells, seed-heads that tick, vines that have grown into the outlines of deer and stand motionless in garden plots until the plot is reabsorbed, and once, at eye height, unfolding from a wall, a plant that has taught itself your face badly and opens and closes its leaf-mouth around the shape of the word you were about to say.

The sound changes but never stops and never, ever drops. At night — darker here, but never dark; the flowers give off a faint stored light, a greenish afterglow, so the city glows like something remembered underwater — at night the note drops from roar to a grinding, creaking vastness, the sound of ten thousand doors in ten thousand houses slowly deciding. Pods burst less. Roots speak more. Walking, you hear the floor reorganise itself under the mat of growth, streets being quietly unbuilt and rebuilt in the dark in configurations you will fail to recognise at dawn, which comes with a city-wide intake of breath: every leaf turning at once, a sound like the sea finding out about the moon.

It would be wrong to call the place hostile. It would be wrong to call it anything that implies it has taken your measure. It is total — that is the fact you keep returning to, shouting, hoarse, delighted, afraid. Plant as street, plant as house, plant as the memory of a house, plant as weather, plant as population and the absence of population. There is no outside to it within the valley; even the dust in the air is seed, even the light arrives processed by leaf, even your own voice comes back to you an hour later from a grove that has learned it, greener, slower, saying what you said in the order the roots preferred.

If you stay too long the city, in its courtesy, begins to build you in. A doorway adjusts its height. A bed stops growing its sleeper because the position is, provisionally, filled. Shoes left on a warm step at night are found in the morning laced with white rootlets, opinionated, beginning to walk themselves in small circles. The avenue out — there is an avenue out; there are several, all of them sincere — keeps being re-routed through districts that resemble, with increasing confidence, the place you were always going to end up.

You will know it is time to leave when the crowd that isn't there starts leaving room for you in its photographs. When the flower with your face gets the mouth right.

Walk fast. Don't stop to listen. Listening is how it gets the pitch.
