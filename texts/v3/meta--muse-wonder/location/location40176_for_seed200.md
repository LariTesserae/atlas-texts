It is a low bowl that has never finished becoming land.

For miles in every direction the ground stays almost level, a broad, shallow depression sunk just below the surrounding country, smooth and uncarved. There are no cliffs, no gullies, no river valleys cutting down through it. The earth here is too young for that, and it stays young. What rock exists is still soft — pale gray and ochre mudstone that dents under a foot, crumbles between fingers like damp clay, weeps clear water from a fresh break. Rounded mounds rise a few feet and subside again without ever hardening. Nothing has had time to erode because nothing has had time to set.

Over it all, the heat is total. It does not rise in the morning or fall at night; it simply is, thick and wet as breath inside a mouth. The light, paradoxically, is dim. A permanent canopy closes overhead in three overlapping layers — broad-leafed trees with trunks slick with algae, then a lower tangle of palms and tree-ferns, then hanging curtains of vine and moss — and above even that a low, unmoving deck of cloud that never parts and never storms. What gets through is a green, filtered twilight, warm shadow without sunbeams. Shadows have no edges here. Everything is lit the way things are lit underwater.

And it is underwater, in pieces. Water is not gathered in lakes or rivers so much as it is *near* — just below the surface, just above it, in the air. The soil gives with a sucking sound and fills a footprint in seconds. Shallow sheets, inches deep and tea-brown, connect puddle to puddle across the flats. Moss holds water like a sponge held loosely shut. Leaves drip when nothing has rained. Bark sweats. At any moment the air is condensing somewhere close by, on a cold frog's back, on the underside of a leaf, on your skin, which never dries.

Because of that, the vegetation does not so much grow as crowd. It is lush without being tall-forest majestic; it is dense, layered, saturating every volume. Dark leaves the size of doors overlap to catch the dim light. Roots do not go deep into the soft ground — they spread sideways, interlacing into a living mat, and where a tree falls its trunk is covered in ferns, fungi, orchids, and seedlings before it touches the mud. There is no bare ground. There is no bare *surface*. Trunks carry gardens. Rocks — the few soft ones — carry gardens. Still water carries a film of duckweed and bright algae so continuous you could mistake it for lawn until it parts around a swimming insect.

The fauna is the garden, moving.

This is the loudest part of the place, and the loudness, like the heat, does not change. A constant chorus fills the bowl from ground to canopy: frogs in the leaf axils where rainwater pools, frogs in the sheet-water, frogs under the root mat, each calling over the next; cicadas and katydids in a metallic, grinding band of sound; waterbirds picking through the shallows with soft, splashing cries; and under it all the granular hum of insects in the air itself. The air has texture. Gnats hang in columns. Dragonflies — blue, scarlet, clear-winged — stitch through them without thinning them. Beetles cross leaves in processions. In the water, small fish, tadpoles at every stage, snails, leeches, and flatworms turn the shallows into a slow boil of life. On the mud, crabs and enormous earthworms work the surface, and the surface, looked at closely, is mostly *them*.

You are never looking at an animal here. You are looking at dozens, at different scales, occupying the same arm's length of space: ants on the leaf, a treefrog on the ant's leaf, a spider in the fork above the frog, mites on the spider, a bird watching all of it from a branch that is also home to beetle larvae boring inaudibly inside. Predation is not an event. It is the background rate. Things are being eaten at exactly the speed they are being born, hatched, budded, and grown. A carcass does not rot so much as get promptly redistributed. A fallen fruit is a brief, writhing city and then a clean husk.

That is the rule that holds the whole bowl in place: nothing is allowed to progress.

Ordinarily a new land like this would harden, drain, cut channels, cool its own microclimate with tall forest, settle into succession. Here the life prevents it. Roots and burrowing animals churn the soft mudstone back to raw mud before it can lithify. The canopy traps the heat that would otherwise drive weather; the trapped heat keeps the cloud deck fed; the cloud keeps the light dim and the rain imminent and the ground saturated. Plants grow fast in the warmth and die fast in the dimness, and their fall feeds the mud that feeds the plants. The frogs have no season because the pools in the leaves never dry. The insects have no season because the frogs never stop. There is no winter, no dry month, no dawn chorus because there is no silence for a chorus to dawn *into*. Even day and night arrive muted, a slight thinning and thickening of the green gloom, the temperature unchanged, the sound unchanged, some species trading places with others in a rotation so regular it feels like stillness.

There are stranger touches, mild but persistent once you notice them. Plants and animals borrow each other's strategies until the categories fray: leaves that close slowly around trapped insects and hold them for days; frogs nearly transparent and photosynthetic-green, sitting motionless for so long moss begins on their backs; fish that spend the whole of their lives in a single interconnected sheet of water never deeper than a hand, traveling miles without ever finding a bank. Some of the soft, new rock, broken open, shows the perfect, embryonic curl of shells that never became fossils — just impressions in mud that never became stone.

Walk far enough and the bowl repeats itself without repeating exactly: a slightly more open flat where the sheet-water widens and lilies open pale and heat-scented; a slightly denser thicket where the root-mat rises above the water and you walk, springing slightly, on woven living wood; a stand of younger trees where the cloud-light comes down a little stronger and the insect hum rises half a tone. But the conditions do not graduate toward an edge. There is no drier, cooler, quieter margin. It goes on at the same pitch until, presumably, it simply stops at higher country — a place still in its first chapter, held there, steaming and singing and teeming, by everything that lives in it refusing, collectively, to let it grow up.
