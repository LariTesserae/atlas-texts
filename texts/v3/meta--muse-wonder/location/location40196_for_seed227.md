You don't find it from a distance. The land here is too low and too flat for that — a cold, dark plain of spruce and wet stone, barely above the sea, where the trees grow close and the light comes down blue and late even at midday. You find it by walking wrong. The ground, which has been soft with moss for an hour, firms underfoot. The trees thin, though you don't notice at first why. There is no underbrush. There is nothing, for fifty paces in any direction, except low stone showing through the ground-cover like the backs of buried animals. Then the stone opens.

It is a single hollow worn into the limestone, no wider than a village yard, and so perfectly sunk that its rim is level with the roots of the surrounding trees. From five steps away you hear nothing. At the rim, your ears pop.

The sound lives inside it.

It is not a waterfall, though at first it feels like standing inside one. It is not wind in trees. It comes from below — from a fissure in the floor of the hollow, a black crack no wider than a man's shoulders and longer than a boat, polished absolutely smooth at its edges. Far underneath, out of sight, air is moving through a system of caves that eventually reaches the cliffs on the coast. The tide breathes in and out of them, twice a day, very slowly. The hill breathes in and out of them, as the cold at its surface rises and falls. Here, all of that breath is forced through one throat.

So the bowl roars. Continuously. It roars at a pitch low enough that you feel it first in your sternum, in your molars, in the loose skin at the back of your hands, and only second as something heard. On top of the low note is a tearing hiss, and on top of that, irregularly, small stones somewhere deep underground knock together and the whole floor ticks through the soles of your boots. Your own shout, produced out of pure reflex at the rim, does not exist. You feel your throat do it. That is all.

Nothing stays loose in the bottom. The floor is bare, swept and re-swept for thousands of years into a shallow dish of grey rock, almost empty of dust, grit, fallen leaves. Anything dry that falls in is worried back and forth for a while, abraded, and then lifted in the spiral of air and thrown out onto the rim, where it collects in odd, sorted drifts — pine needles here, white pebbles there, a perfect crescent of dead beetle shells. The hollow has been doing this long enough that the walls are grooved in a slow spiral, like the inside of a shell. This is not a young wound in the earth. It is an old, settled shape, in balance with what made it.

And yet the walls are lush. That is the first contradiction your eyes have to learn. Nothing grows on the floor, and everything grows on the sides. Out of the direct blast, in crevices and ledges and the lee of every bulge of rock, the stone is upholstered. Moss a hand's thickness, dark green going almost black in this light. Liverworts like wet leather. Small ferns with thick, waxy fronds that tremble constantly, all of them, all day, so that the walls appear to shiver with a green animal life. Where condensation gathers — and it gathers everywhere, because the air rising from the caves is a fraction warmer than the cold air trapped in the bowl, and it beads on every cold surface — the moss is beaded too, and grows long, dripping filaments down toward the floor that never quite reach it before the moving air dries them back.

There is no stream. There is no pool. It is only damp: stone that is always wet to the touch, wood that never fully dries, the hems of any cloth you wear darkening by the end of an hour. Now and then a true drip falls from the overhang on the north side and is instantly shredded to mist. The cold collects here and does not leave. Even in summer there is frost in the deepest shadow at noon, rime on the undersides of ledges. Your breath comes out white and is torn away sideways.

The darkness is part of the architecture. The rim overhangs. The spruces lean inward over it from above, their roots exposed, their lowest branches woven by whoever tends this place — tended it, once — into a rough, living thatch that has since grown wild. Perhaps a quarter of the sky is visible straight overhead, a cold coin of it. The rest is branch, root, and rock. At midday it is twilight in the bowl. In the morning and evening it is simply night, and the moss goes black, and you navigate by the feel of the roar getting stronger or weaker against your face.

People have been here, but only just, and never for long at a time. It is clear nobody could live in the sound. What they built, they built into the walls.

On the east side, where a ledge interrupts the spiral and makes a pocket of merely terrible noise instead of unbearable noise, there are three low shelters. They are not houses. They are curved windbreak walls of unmortared stone, roofed with split logs and turf, each one open away from the centre. Inside one there is a sleeping platform of woven hazel, rotted through. Inside another, a stack of cut moss bricks, dried hard as peat — ear-stoppers, perhaps, or insulation. There are stone troughs hammered out under the best drips, to catch condensation for drinking water, green with algae and still half full. A rope handrail, knotted from twisted bark fibre and gone soft with damp, is pegged with antler pins down the one practicable descent. Further down, someone has left a kit where it fell or was set aside: a stone hammer, a wooden mallet split along the grain, wedges of antler and dense yew, a basket whose bottom has blown away. Simple things, made by hand, maintained by hand, and abandoned the same way.

Nothing about the work looks recent, and nothing about it looks finished. A fourth wall, on the south side, was begun and given up at knee height. The stones for it are still there, in a neat pile, sorted by size. Beside them a raven sometimes sits — the only bird that comes regularly, and it never calls. Once, in the quiet pocket behind the largest shelter, a vole crossed over my boot without any hurry at all; it may have been quite deaf, or quite at home. Nothing nests in the bowl. Nothing grazes the moss. Spiders keep to the outer rim, where their webs can hold. Life here is rare, brief, and mostly visiting.

There are rules, which you learn without being told. You do not take a flame down: it lies flat, gutters blue, and goes out, and the smoke shows you exactly how the air is moving whether you want to see it or not. You do not speak, and after ten minutes you stop trying; people who came here must have spoken with their hands, or tapped messages into the rock with their knuckles, because in the quietest shelter the stone at hand height is worn smooth in a patch about the size of a spread palm. You do not stay in the centre. Your nose will ache. Your eyes will water from a wind you cannot feel as wind, only as pressure. You keep your mouth slightly open, the way divers do, so the pressure has somewhere to go.

Time moves differently because the sound does. Over hours the roar sinks and thickens as the tide turns far away, or thins almost — almost — to something you could imagine talking over, before filling out again. Over days the condensation freezes, thaws, freezes lower down. Over years, visibly, very little happens at all: a fern uncurls a fraction each week, the moss climbs a thumb's width up a wall in a decade, a new groove appears in the limestone where the old groove has deepened by the width of a hair. It is a slow place that happens to be loud. The loudness is the only fast thing in it.

When you climb out — hand over hand on the bark rope, past the trembling ferns, past the drip-troughs and the unfinished wall — the rim arrives like a shoreline. Two steps beyond the lip, the spruces close in, the moss is soft and ordinary underfoot again, and the silence is so total it rings. The sound does not follow you. It never did. It stays in its one small room in the earth, roaring at the dark and the wet stone and the empty floor, exactly as loud whether anyone is there to go deaf in it or not.
