High up, where the mountain has thinned its soil to almost nothing, there is a place you would walk past without seeing it.

From a distance it is only a shoulder of pale rock above the treeline, warmer-colored than the grey around it — cream, rust, a faint wet yellow. The wind up there is constant and thin. Vegetation holds on in the cracks in low, sparse cushions: tiny hard leaves, a few grasses gone straw-colored, plants that grow sideways rather than up. Cairns mark the way, if you know to look for them — not grand ones, just three or four flat stones stacked by hand, tipped over and restacked over the years. Near the last cairn is a single low structure, little more than a windbreak of unmortared rock with a collapsed lintel, where someone once waited out weather. A blackened fire scar. A chipped stone blade left in the ash, edge still usable.

The entrance is a vertical crack where the pale rock has grown over itself. Sideways, shoulders scraping, pack left outside. The crack bends twice, and with the second bend the world goes out.

Inside, there is no light at all. Not dimness. Not gloom. A complete, warm dark that does not change with day or season or storm outside, and has not changed, as far as anyone can tell, in living memory.

It is a single chamber, pocket-sized, perhaps four meters across if you could pace it, which you cannot. The ceiling hangs so low in places that standing means bowing, and in other places it comes down in wet stone curtains to meet the floor. You learn the room by touch. Everything is near. Everything is touching everything else.

The place is still being made.

This is not an eroded cave, carved out. It is a hollow inside a growing spring-mound, a dome of travertine building itself skin by skin from the inside. Warm water rises from deep in the mountain — not hot enough to scald, blood-warm, steady — and as it spreads and cools it leaves its minerals behind. The walls are coated in moonmilk, a soft white paste that gives under your palm like wet cheese and slowly hardens into stone. Popcorn calcite knobs crowd the surfaces, still damp, still forming. Thin stalactites descend in clusters, dense as teeth, some no longer than a finger, some brushing your hair when you duck. Below them the floor is a packed mosaic of rimstone pools, lip overlapping lip like scales, each basin brimming with clear warm water, each spilling in a thread no thicker than string into the next.

Water is everywhere and in every state. It weeps straight through the rock. It beads on the ceiling and rains down in a gentle, unceasing patter that never quite becomes a pour. It condenses out of the air itself — the air is so saturated, so close, that breathing feels like drinking through cloth, and droplets form on skin that is already wet. Your clothes do not dry here. They warm through, and stay warm and heavy. Hair clings. The dark fills your mouth with the taste of limestone, clay, and faint iron.

And yet the pools never rise. For every drip that falls, somewhere under the floor another leaves, through pores in the young stone, down and away. Inflow, condensation, outflow, mineral laid silently over mineral — a perfect, locked agreement. Outside, snow can melt or summer can dry the mountain to dust; in here the drip rate does not alter. There is no morning. The temperature does not fall at night. Time is not measured by anything changing, only by the accumulating crust on things left too long.

Sound stays very close to the body. Mostly quiet, because the wet stone and wet air swallow it. A drip lands in a near pool with a soft, round *plink*. Another lands on stone, farther off, flat. Water threads over a rim with a sound like a held breath released through teeth. Your own shifting makes small sloshings ankle-deep. Speak, and your voice comes back muffled and immediate, as if the room were lined with hide. Whoever used this place before seems to have understood that: there is a flat resting stone at the center, worn smooth, just large enough to sit on cross-legged with your feet in the warm basins, and the custom — you can feel it in the arrangement of things — is to sit, not to explore.

Because people have been here, though never many, and never for long. Set into niches in the wall, at hand-height, are three small clay cups, hand-pinched, unglazed. Or they were clay. The spring has been at them: two are filmed white, the third is half-turned entirely to stone, fused to its niche, a cup becoming geology. A string of carved wooden beads hangs from a calcite knob and has gone stiff and pale with crust. Little stone diverters — flakes wedged into seeps — nudge one prized drip toward a drinking basin. Everything made has been made simply, by hand, and everything left is being slowly unmade into rock.

Almost nothing lives here. It is too dark for green things. At the entrance bend, where you squeeze through, pale rootlets have pushed down from the sparse plants far above, blind white threads searching for exactly this water, most of them ending dead in the warm air. One blackened fern persists right at the limit of the vanished light, more memory than plant. Deeper in, the life is microbial and invisible: a slickness on submerged stone, a faint opalescent film that gathers at the edges of pools and breaks at a fingertip. Now and then — rarely enough that you may sit an hour and see nothing, though seeing is not the word — something moves the water from beneath. A cave shrimp, transparent, the size of a thumbnail, ghosting between basins. White isopods on the wet walls, motionless for long stretches. Old accounts mention a pale salamander, eyeless, that was found once in the lowest pool and never again. The chamber feels inhabited mostly by processes, not creatures.

The unusual part is not the warmth in the cold altitude, though your body registers it as a kind of mercy after the wind. It is the sense that the rock is at an early stage of itself and knows it. Put your palm flat to the wall and it does not feel finished — it feels *ongoing*, granular, tender, faintly yielding, as if stone here has not yet committed to being hard. Sit still long enough and a film of moisture and mineral begins to form on your own skin. A white dust settles on a sleeve left trailing in a seep. You understand, without anyone telling you, that if you stayed — really stayed — the place would not reject you. It would coat you, basin by basin, and incorporate you into its floor.

That is why no one stays. You sit in the absolute dark, in the dense, warm, dripping quiet, until your heartbeat becomes the loudest regular sound and begins to seem synchronized with the drips, or the drips with it. Your sense of the room's size dissolves; it could be a womb, a lung, the inside of a single tooth. Then some older instinct sends you back, hands feeling ahead over wet knobs and stone curtains, knees in the warm pools, toward the bend where the rootlets hang and the black fern waits — and, eventually, a blade of cold, thin light that hurts more than the darkness did.
