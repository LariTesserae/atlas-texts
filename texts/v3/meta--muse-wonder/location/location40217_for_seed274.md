It is a low place you come down into, and it closes around you almost at once.

From above it looks like nothing more than a tear in flat limestone country — a sink, a shallow bowl maybe eighty paces across, twenty feet below the level of the plain. The rim is broken rock and hard, sun-baked dust. Then you find the cut steps, worn bowl-shaped in the middle from feet, and you go down out of the wind and the glare and into shade, and the air changes on your skin.

Below, it is always warm. Not hot, not steaming like a spring that scalds, but bath-warm and close, the kind of warmth that comes up from water and stone and stays trapped. The air is in the low thirties, the water a little warmer than that against your ankles. It never seems to cool at night or heat at noon down here, because there is almost no noon. The walls lean, the opening to the sky narrows to an uneven strip, and a permanent mist hangs where the warm water meets that dim, filtered light. Everything is in a soft grey-green gloom, wet-looking even where it is only damp, distances dissolving after ten or fifteen feet.

The floor of the bowl is not a floor at all. It is packed — completely, impossibly packed — with stone.

Hundreds of tufa columns rise straight out of shallow water, shoulder to shoulder, elbow to elbow, from knee-high stumps to pillars twice the height of a person. They have grown up over a very long time where mineral water seeped and precipitated, and then been worn back smooth by that same water, until growing and wearing found a balance and mostly stopped. Their sides are rounded, bulged, fused together in places, separated in others by gaps just wide enough to turn sideways through. Their surfaces are cream and ochre and rust-brown, slick with a thin mineral skin, pitted with thumb-sized hollows that each hold a bead of water. No plant takes hold on them. The chemistry is wrong, the light is too low, the stone is too wet and too clean. There is only a faint, slippery film in the warmest pools, a pale scum that gathers and breaks, and white salt-like crusts at the waterlines that are not alive at all, just what the water leaves as it breathes.

And the water is everywhere. It is the second packing. Between every column is a pool, a channel, a sheet running over stone. Ankle-deep here, waist-deep where a basin has been hollowed out, never quite still though it never seems to go anywhere. It seeps from the walls in a hundred weeping threads, condenses on the undersides of overhangs and falls, one drop and then, after a long count, another, in exactly the places it has always fallen — each drip has grooved the lip it falls from and dimpled the stone it lands on over centuries. Small runnels find the old courses cut for them and follow them without widening them. Rain, when it comes hard on the plain above, can be heard as a distant, muffled rushing, but the level down here does not rise. Somewhere under the southern wall a throat in the rock takes precisely as much as the springs give. The place has settled into its account and keeps it.

So you do not walk so much as negotiate. You wade, you brace a palm against a warm, wet pillar, you step onto a submerged ledge worn glassy where others have stepped. The pillars press the sound down. There is no echo, not really — everything is too close, too damp. Drips are near and private, a soft *tick* into a pool behind your shoulder, the faint lap of your own movement coming back off stone a foot away, your breathing made humid. Otherwise it is quiet in a full way. The mist takes sound the way it takes sight. Above, insects and wind might be moving, but down here a call would not travel twenty feet before it was smothered.

Once in a while, rarely, something else moves. A small, dark freshwater snail makes a silver track below a waterline, and stays there for weeks. A heron will sometimes stand, bewildered and too large for the gaps, in the widest pool at the northern end where the light falls best — it finds almost nothing to eat and does not stay. In the shadow under the central overhang, if you are still long enough, tiny pale fish no longer than a fingernail flicker in the deepest basin and vanish into a crack. They seem less like inhabitants and more like exceptions the mineral water tolerates.

The people who used this place never tried to clear it. They could not. What they left is scattered and fitted into what was already there: three or four flat-topped stones squared off with hammerstones to make standing places above the deepest water; a low platform built of fitted blocks between two columns under the overhang, dry only by a hand's breadth and blackened in one corner where a small fire was once repeatedly made; a copper ladle, greened and thin, left on a ledge beside a basin where the water runs clearest and sweetest over the stone; wooden poles lashed across a narrow place to help you climb, the wood dark, sodden, iron-hard with mineral now. On the east wall, where the steps come down, someone chipped a series of shallow cups in descending order, each overflowing into the next, to catch drinking water before it mixed with the bitter pool water below. The work is all hand-work, patient, adapted. Nothing large can be built here. There is no room that is not already occupied by pillar and pool.

At the centre — though centre is hard to judge when you can never see across — there is a slightly larger clearing, cathedral-small: a round pool perhaps fifteen feet wide, ringed by the tallest columns, leaning together high above to leave a single, dim shaft. The water there is deepest, stillest, the warmest to enter. Condensation gathers on the stone vault over it and rains back in a slow, perpetual circle. Put a hand in on any day, in any season, and it feels the same. Stand there long enough and your clothes stop being wet or dry as separate states. Your hair curls. Paper would soften and give up. Iron left here greens, wood petrifies at the edges, skin wrinkles, but nothing rushes toward decay — the minerals seal everything almost as quickly as the damp can undo it.

It does not invite staying. It does not repel you either. It simply continues in the exact form it has found: warm, low, dim, full to capacity with wet stone, thin air, slow water, and silence. Leave for a year and return down the worn steps, and the same drip will be falling from the same lip, into the same dimple, between the same two ochre columns, while the mist closes behind you as if the gap you made passing through were already being forgotten.
