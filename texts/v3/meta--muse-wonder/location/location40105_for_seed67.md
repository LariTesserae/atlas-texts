It is all floor.

Not paved-over land, not a city built on top of a continent. There is no underneath where the making stops. If you pry up a plate in one of the service aisles — and the plates are everywhere, warm, faintly ridged so your boots will grip when they are wet — you find pipes, cable runs, a second floor twelve centimeters below, older but painted the same gray-green. Below that, ducts. Below that, another floor. Test borings have gone down four hundred meters in the central districts without reaching anything that could be called ground. They pass through strata of poured mineral resin, riveted iron, glazed ceramic tile, concrete laced with fiber, maintenance voids full of warm standing water, and then more floor. Whatever planet this was built on has been entirely superseded.

It is low. The whole constructed continent tilts perhaps three meters in two thousand kilometers, just enough to persuade water to move. The horizon is not a line of hills but a gradual failure of detail: gantry after gantry, shed roof after shed roof, cooling stack after cooling stack, until the blue of distance is made only of more of the same thing, softened by humidity.

The daylight above it is ordinary. That is one of the strangest things. Blue sky, white sun, clouds that have nothing artificial in their color. Late-morning light falls on a place to which late morning means nothing.

Because it does not change.

Temperature here is a permanent thirty-one degrees at skin height, warmer over the heat-exchange fields, a degree or two cooler in the rain belt, always returning to itself. There are districts of weather fixed in place like provinces. Over the western condensers a single vast thunderhead has stood for as long as records exist, white above, bruise-gray below, raining in a curtain so sharply bounded that you can stand with one shoulder wet and the other dry. The rain falls, runs along the collector avenues back to the condensers, rises again. Twenty kilometers east the air is permanently clear and smells of hot iron. In between is a fog province, a low, warm, luminous mist through which the shed lights glow all day, where everything drips and the animals emerge already darkened with moisture. These weathers do not travel. They are geography.

Water is near the surface everywhere, but it is never wild. It sheets ankle-deep across the washing yards in a slow, clear film. It runs in canals as straight as ruled lines, partitioned by weirs, sluices, filter grates, and measuring bridges. It falls from weeping rock-faces that are not rock at all but cooling walls built to imitate cliffs — deliberately roughened concrete sweating condensation in beads, streaked with mineral salts, where nozzle arrays keep the surface perpetually damp. It pools in shallow basins whose bottoms are tiled and whose levels never vary by more than a finger's width. Even the apparent marshes at the margins of the animal yards are engineered marsh: a warm, flooded plain of baffles and feeding trays, its mud a manufactured slurry of ground shell, ash, and bacterial paste, replenished from below.

There are no plants. None. That absence becomes more total the longer you stay.

There is no grass between the plates, no weed in the canal edge, no tree giving shade to a yard, no vine on the gantries. Seeds arrive on the wind from beyond the edge of the Works and die without exception on the heated, salted, lightly electrified floors. What covers the wet surfaces instead is simpler than vegetation: a living film, slick and iridescent, olive-black in the canals, mother-of-pearl pink on the feeder trays, a chemosynthetic skin of bacteria and fungal thread grown from chemical dosing pipes. It smells faintly of brine and warm bread. It is cropped as fast as it grows. Machines tend it; animals graze it; the dosers replace the nitrates and phosphates at exactly the rate at which mouths remove them. In places the film thickens into soft, translucent pads that quiver when water passes, but it never becomes leaf, stem, or root. The world is mineral, bacterial, animal, and made.

And the animals are overwhelming.

You notice them first as density, not as individuals. The yards extend for days by service train, each yard divided by rails, troughs, and low partitions, and in each yard the broad-backed trough cattle stand flank to flank in their thousands, pale gray-pink, nearly hairless in the warmth, their skin beading with canal water sprayed at intervals from overhead pipes. They do not jostle. They stand oriented with the flow of water, heads down to the feed film that unrolls slowly beneath them on submerged belts, breathing in a rhythm close enough to synchrony that a whole yard seems to sigh and settle, sigh and settle. There is almost no lowing. They have been made quiet over a very long refinement: throats that do not call, ears that flick at dripping water, eyes that track the moving belt.

Between their legs run the smaller orders. Scurry-hens, blind and featherless, pick spilled feed-pellets from the runnels. Long black filter-eels work the canal grates in living bundles, hundreds knotted at each sluice, sieving the bacterial film with their mouths. In the roof spaces of the long sheds — sheds that are each eleven kilometers end to end, roofed in translucent panels that turn the normal daylight amber — mite-swarms hang in the warm updrafts like dust that has learned to coordinate, and the dust is eaten, in patient aerial passes, by soft-winged scoop-bats roosting in such numbers that the undersides of the rafters appear furred and slowly breathing.

Higher still, the picking cranes. They are animals and equipment at once: tall, tendon-driven, harnessed to counterweighted gantries, their beaks fitted with steel tips, sorting carcasses and components on the transfer lines with a precision no one supervises. They do not fly away. Their ancestors may have; these step solemnly along their rails from birth to allotment, fed from hoppers, their wing-bones pinned into the harness frame, absorbed into the industry that grew up inside their behavior.

The industry is everywhere but it makes surprisingly little noise. Density would suggest a roar — the pumps, extruders, renderers, separator drums, the kilometer-long pellet presses, the automated rail lines gliding on rubber-sheathed wheels — but the air takes sound and softens it. Humidity swallows high notes. Machinery runs electric, belted, muffled, set into pits below floor level. What you hear, mostly, is water: dripping from pipe joints in a steady, unhurried patter; the hush of spray curtains; canals ticking against their ceramic banks. Over it, at the edge of perception, the accumulated breath of billions of animals, a wind that never rises and never falls. Occasionally a metal gate closes, far off. Occasionally something large shifts its weight on wet concrete. Then the quiet restores itself, complete.

Because nothing accumulates and nothing is lost, the Works can afford to be fresh. Erosion has had no purchase here. Canal corners are still sharp enough to cut a careless hand. The painted edges of the loading docks are unchipped; where hooves and wheels do wear a groove, crawler-repairers come in the nightless working hours — there is no night work schedule, the lamps simply supplement the sun to a constant working brightness in the sheds — and lay new surface in a warm ribbon that sets indistinguishable from the old. Rust blooms are scrubbed before they spread. Salt crusts are harvested from the cooling walls weekly and returned to the dosing system. The place looks recently built, improbably new, as if construction crews had left a month ago. They have not left. They were never here in that sense. Building and maintenance are the same continuous act, and its product is that there is never a visible before or after.

You can ride the central aisle train for six days at freight speed and pass one repeating composition: intake reservoirs glittering in the open; brood halls where infant animals arrive on heated conveyors in identical, silent rows; growing yards steaming after spray-down; transfer gantries; processing sheds with sealed sides and water running clear out of them; pellet plants; feed silos; and then intake reservoirs again, because the line of production curves, eventually, across continental distances, back into itself. Rendered protein becomes feed. Feed becomes animal. Animal waste is stripped for minerals and returned to the bacterial film. Dead stock enters the same closed accounting as everything else. Water evaporates, condenses, rains in its assigned district, is filtered by eels, drunk, urinated, scrubbed, and drunk again. Even the heat is reused until it is lukewarm and harmless.

No one receives the output. Or rather, the output is the place itself, maintaining its appetite. Long ago — and the records, like everything else, are kept at a constant temperature in cabinets that no longer yellow — this must have been built to supply somewhere else. Ships, perhaps. A population. A war. Whatever the destination was, the destination ended, and the Works, unable to register an ending, smoothed the change into their equilibrium. A full silo triggers diversion to rendering. Rendering triggers feed production. Feed production triggers breeding. Absence of demand reads, to the system, exactly like perfect demand. It has been running the same day ever since.

Underneath the animal floors are the nursery levels, denser than anything above: tier on tier of warm cells lit at constant noon, humidifiers sighing, newly made creatures fed by tube and belt in such uncountable succession that individual birth loses meaning. For every animal that dies in the yards above, one of precisely the same mass is already ascending in a freight lift to take its place, so that aerial survey photographs taken decades apart are identical down to the distribution of bodies in the yards — not the same bodies, but the same pattern, unbroken. From the sky the herds form static textures, like printed cloth. Only up close do you see nostrils flare, an eel uncoil, a crane's eye blink.

The rules emerge slowly. Nothing here may be first, and nothing may be last. No species can spread, because its enclosure and ration are the size of its species. No machine can fall behind, because spares are manufactured from the same stream the machines produce. No puddle can deepen, no film can starve, no population can crash or bloom. Even loneliness is hard to sustain: inspectors sent across the Works report that after the second day they stop feeling they are traveling, because each new district is not merely similar to the last but actively maintained to be the same as the last, temperature correcting, sound correcting, animal density correcting around their presence, repair-crawlers already erasing their footprints from the wet dust of the service roads — the dust being not soil, but ground plate and dried film, swept nightly back into process.

At the far edge, if you ever reach it — most lines loop long before — the floor stops at a sea-cliff of its own construction, ceramic and sheer, where treated water pours in a thousand even falls into an ocean the Works does not use. Gulls nest there in millions on the discharge ledges, the one unregistered fauna, screaming in the one place the quiet fails. Their droppings are washed off. Their eggs roll and are washed off. They eat the overspill eels and are not entered into the accounting, and so they are the wildest thing on the continent: a flaw the system tolerates because it is, at every measurement, always exactly the same size.

Stand anywhere else, at any hour, and the impression is of a world holding its breath at a single, sustainable instant. Warm wet air on your face. The smell of iron, brine, milk, hot rubber, and clean ammonia. Shallow water shining between the plates. Sheds to the horizon, breathing faintly with contained life. A crane stepping above you on its rail, careful as a thought. A yard of cattle sighing in unison at nothing.

It will look like this when you leave. It looked like this, the plaques say, when the plaques were installed, and the plaques have been replaced four thousand times, each time identically, to keep them looking new.
