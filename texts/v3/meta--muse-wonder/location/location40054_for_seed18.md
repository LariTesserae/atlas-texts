There is only one room up there, and the mountain has been capped shut beneath it.

You do not arrive on ground. You arrive on iron. The last three hundred meters of the ascent are catwalk, switchback stair, and riveted ladder bolted into a concrete plug that was poured over the summit a century ago, smoothing the peak into a level plinth. No rock shows. No soil. Retaining walls of riveted plate hold back nothing but weather. A railing, a fuel gantry, a cable head with a wheel that still turns once a day whether a car comes or not. Below the plinth the world falls away so steeply, in thin air, that looking down feels less like height than like being removed from geography altogether.

The building sits on that plinth like a small cathedral made by a boilerworks: eighteen meters long, twelve meters high at the ridge of its glass roof, walls of corrugated iron and soot-dark brick, a round tower at the far end that is not a tower but a horn mouth, flared, ten feet across, aimed out over the void. It was built to make a sound other things could navigate by. Airships, first. Then mine trains in cloud. Then nothing, really, but it has not been told to stop.

Inside, the air changes completely.

Outside it is cold-bright and starved of oxygen. Inside it is warm as a laundry, close, wet without ever quite raining, and so loud your body understands it before your ears do. The sound is not an event. It is the medium. A low diesel throb in the floor plates, a compressor sighing through copper lungs, belts slapping in an overhead gallery, and above it all a continuous tearing resonance from the horn throat, even at idle, where wind finds the pipe. When the main blast comes — and it comes on its own slow schedule, after forty or fifty minutes of pressure gathering in the riveted tanks along the north wall — the glass rattles in its frames, water shakes out of the gutters in a sudden mist, and conversation, thought, any distinction between inside your skull and outside it briefly ceases. Keepers have always communicated in hand-signs painted, helpfully, on the walls between the gauges. Visitors put the wax plugs in at the door or they learn why the tin box of them is nailed there, lidless, by the coat hooks.

And yet the middle of the room is empty.

That is the first true thing after the noise. The chamber is a hollow. A single immense volume, unpartitioned, with a swept void at its center shaped by the machine itself: the great horizontal piston, the rocking beam, the slow flywheel turning once every seven seconds with its counterweight passing close enough to the floor to polish a bright arc in the plate. Nothing is allowed to persist in that ellipsoid of movement. Nothing does. The floor there is bare iron, oil-dark, warm. All life has learned the boundary precisely, and crowds to the margins instead.

At the margins, it is a jungle.

Warmth rises from the boilers. Moisture condenses every night on the cold glass and the iron trusses and runs down in established channels, drip to rust-seam to gutter to sump, so that by dawn there is a brief interior weather — a patter of falling drops, small shining pools in bolt-heads and pipe saddles, troughs green with algae — and by midday most of it has been breathed back into the air by the plants. There is no earth anywhere, originally. What soil exists has been made here, decade by decade, from ash raked out of the firebox, from leaf fall, from bird lime, from the felted remains of filters. It collects only where it can stay: in wall pockets, in the crooks of pipes, atop the pressure tanks, along the shadowed south wall where a trough aqueduct was once meant to carry coolant and now carries ferns.

Maidenhair spills from valve housings. A fig, rooted impossibly in the ash box of a decommissioned boiler, has sent its trunk sideways along the wall and its roots into every warm seam, and puts out leaves as big as hands wherever the oil mist thins. Orchid-like things with waxy, speckled throats cling to the copperwork. Vines attempt the center constantly and are trimmed back, not by any keeper, but by the passing beam, so they have formed a living green arch that exactly describes the machine's reach — a negative of it, leaf-edge vibrating in the blast. Moss has mapped the drip lines in dark green cartography. Where the verdigris is thickest on a pipe, a different fern grows than where the iron runs red; the rust is old, arrested, in balance with lichen and oil, no longer eating the building so much as providing it a skin. Even the glass roof is half-vegetated from within, leaves pressed flat against the light, so the daylight that falls in is normal in strength but green-filtered, aqueous, as if the room were a shallow tank.

The fauna have adopted the same perimeter law, and they are everywhere the machine is not. Swifts enter through the horn mouth — they treat its idle moan as a kind of doorway music — and nest in a dense, chattering colony behind the baffle plates, where the vibration is fiercest and predators can't abide it. Alpine choughs with lacquer-red bills walk the roof trusses. Bats roost in the warm felt above the tanks. Lizards, improbable at this altitude, live out their whole accelerated lives on the sunlit boiler lagging, fat and unafraid; they were carried up in crates a generation ago and have no way down and no reason to want one. Tree frogs, also carried, sing from the sumps at night and are amplified by the horn into something the size of weather for a few seconds before the compressor drowns them. There are moths the color of rust that rest with wings open on warm iron and are, to any useful degree, deaf. There are bees who work the fig and the wallflowers that root in the brick mortar, and who navigate home, somehow, through a room where the air never stops moving.

Nothing large lives here. The volume forbids it. Abundance here is horizontal, marginal, small-bodied and many-fold, pressed into the walls while the center roars empty.

The keeper — there is one, in rotation, relieved by cable car once a season — occupies a lean-to of canvas and boiler plate in the one corner the beam cannot see, with a cot, a kettle fed from a condensation tap, and gauges that must be read by touch when the blast is at full throat because the needles blur. Their work is slow work, matched to the place. Grease this bearing. Clear this drip channel of fig root, but not too much, or the south tank will dry and its ferns will die back, as happened in '61, recorded in pencil on the wall. Wind the governor. Log the pressure in a book whose pages have gone soft as cloth. Everything changes across hours and seasons, not minutes. The fig fruits ripen almost simultaneously in the week after the longest summer blasts and split with an audible tick in the lulls. The swift colony arrives within the same three days each spring, pouring out of the horn at dawn. The interior rain falls most heavily at first light in winter, when the glass is coldest, and then there are mornings when the whole room drips and steams and the frogs are louder than the idle pipe for almost a minute.

The strangest rule is the seeding. Once a cycle, when pressure and wind align — not every blast, perhaps one in twenty, when the governor's brass balls lift to their painted red line — the horn clears fully, a sound so complete it empties the room of air-feeling and leaves your sternum buzzing in the silence after. In that moment, seeds loosen. Fern spore, fig pip, the light-winged things the wallflowers make: a whole suspended generation, shaken free at once and drawn by the blast out through the horn mouth in a visible green-gold breath that hangs over the drop before the mountain wind takes it down-valley. Nothing was planted below, either. But there is a line of improbable green on the leeward crags, descending in patches as far as anyone with binoculars has bothered to look, that flowers after storms. The machine was built to warn blind travelers away from a peak.

It has become, in its old age, the peak's way of reproducing.
