From below, it does not look built so much as accumulated.

The plateau rises two thousand meters above the dry low country, a continent-wide table of black basalt with its edge worn round by wind and old water, and across the whole top of it — to the horizon in every direction once you reach the rim — is the city. Not a city with suburbs fading out. A city that simply continues, district after district of stone, iron, tile, and warm wet concrete, until the far districts make their own haze and their own afternoon cloud.

The freight lifts climb the cliff face for almost an hour. The cables weep. Everything at this elevation weeps.

Water is not scarce here, it is just never finished with. It condenses on cool basalt in the morning and runs in beads down factory walls. It stands in shallow cisterns between blocks, warm as bathwater by noon. It seeps up through cracks in the older streets, darkening the stone in permanent maps. Roof gutters, canal gates, pump houses, reservoir towers, laundry yards, bath chimneys — the whole infrastructure exists not to bring water in but to keep it moving slowly enough that it does not take the foundations. In the lower quarters canals replace streets entirely, and flat iron barges move at walking pace, loaded with coal, salt, machine parts, and sacks of lowland grain.

There is no green.

No trees in courtyards, no weeds triumphant in the gutters, no parks. Whatever once tried to root here was eaten, trampled, poisoned by ammonia, or starved on basalt. Below the level of intention there are only simpler things: black and rust-orange bacterial mats slicking the canal edges, pale lichens stippling shaded walls, white molds blooming in cellars after rain. They smell faintly of sulfur and hot iron. Small grey goats crop the lichens from roof parapets. Midges graze the mats. Everything else eats an animal, or something an animal has left behind.

And the animals are overwhelming in a way that recalibrates the word.

Swifts nest in every chimney joint and ventilator shaft, so the industrial smoke rises through a perpetual screaming traffic. Pigeons occupy the ledges in densities that turn facades into slowly shifting mosaics of grey, white, iridescent green. Starlings lift off the warehouse roofs at dusk in flocks so large they shade whole intersections, then settle again with a sound like gravel poured over tin. Crows own the markets. Sparrows own the grain elevators. Bats pour from under the canal bridges at nightfall in rivers that take twenty minutes to pass.

At ground level: feral dogs asleep in warm doorways, cats hunting along the pump-house pipes, rats moving without hurry through drainage grates, mules in iron shoes hauling carts over stone worn into shallow troughs by a thousand years of exactly that route. Cockroaches in the bathhouses. Flies in a constant orbiting weather around every food stall, every tannery, every open cistern. House spiders so numerous in the older tenements that their webs lace the alley mouths into grey veils each morning, and the morning sweepers cut through them with long poles as a matter of routine.

The city is loud with this, underneath and above the industrial noise. Foundries thump at intervals you feel in your molars. Steam valves sigh. Electric trams grind on rails polished bright and furred at the edges with droppings. But woven continuously through the machine sound is the animal sound — cooing, shrieking, barking, braying, the hiss of a million wings adjusting on hot tile, the dawn chorus not as a pleasant event but as a physical pressure that starts before light and makes conversation briefly pointless in the rookery districts.

The buildings have adapted to being habitat first and architecture second, or perhaps the distinction was abandoned centuries ago. Rookery towers rise ten, fifteen stories, built deliberately with thousands of nesting holes, ledges, and sheltered cavities of brick — humans live in the interior rooms, birds in the skin. Factory owners keep loft-keepers the way they keep engineers. A good swift colony keeps a chimney clear; a good pigeon loft produces eggs, squab, feathers, and guano on schedule. Guano is in fact one of the great industries: scraped from roofs by crews in canvas hoods, dried on vast black terraces, sacked and sent down the lifts to the farmed lowlands that send grain back up. The city exports fertilizer, leather, tallow, bone meal, glue, lamp oil, preserved meat, and live breeding stock. It imports almost everything that ever photosynthesized.

Guano also whitens the city. Old roofs are crusted white and grey, streaked down the walls in long mineral fans. Rain softens it and the runoff runs milky into the canals, where it feeds the mats, which feed the midges, which lift in clouds when a barge passes, which brings the swallows down low over the water in cutting arcs. It is a closed, patient loop, and it smells — hot ammonia, wet feathers, coal smoke, ozone from the tram lines, boiled grain from the communal kitchens, rust, and underneath everything the faint mineral smell of warm stone that has been wet for a very long time.

The erosion is honest here. Nothing has sharp corners past a certain age. Stair treads are dished in the middle. Door lintels are grooved where generations of porters ducked with yokes. Basalt curbs are rounded, iron handrails worn thin and bright, monumental facades softened until the carved beasts on the older municipal buildings look like they are melting very slowly back into the wall. Buildings are not demolished so much as re-inhabited, re-roofed, re-skinned with nesting brick, their ground floors rising imperceptibly as street level accretes — packed guano, ash, broken tile, crushed shell, stone dust trodden hard. Dig down anywhere and you go through previous versions of the same street.

Yet for all the density of presence, change is slow. Trams inch. Barges inch. Construction takes decades; a new reservoir tower is begun by one generation of masons and first filled by their grandchildren. The great seasonal events are gradual: the swifts arriving over a period of weeks in spring, thickening the air day by day; the slow autumn departure; the winter battening when the mats go dark and the cisterns steam harder in the cooler air and animal bodies crowd into every warm vent until tenement walls are literally heated in patches by roosting birds. Laws follow the pace. You cannot re-roof during nesting. You cannot seal a facade without providing equivalent cavities. Every new factory must build its rookery ledge before its chimney. Roof rights and nest rights are litigated like water rights, in proceedings nobody expects to be quick.

By mid-afternoon the city makes its own weather. Heat rises off a hundred square kilometers of tile and iron and shallow black water; moisture rises from cisterns, canals, laundries, bathhouses, and the breath of uncountable animals; and a flat white cloud forms that belongs to the plateau alone, visible from the lowlands as a table set on top of the table. Rain, when it comes, often falls inside the city limits and not beyond the rim. It rattles the nesting ledges, washes the guano streaks fresh, fills every gutter with tea-colored runoff, and brings out for an hour an extraordinary density of life — frogs (the one amphibian, imported long ago and now ineradicable) calling from every drain, snails on the warm walls, dogs shaking themselves in doorways, birds bathing violently in rooftop puddles, steam curling from the foundry yards.

Then the sun reasserts, normal and bright. Water drips from a million ledges onto stone that was old before the dripping started. A lift groans down the cliff with sacks of fertilizer. Another groans up with grain. On a roof above the canal a keeper opens his lofts, and the pigeons go up all at once — a sound like a book the size of a building being riffled — circle twice over the steaming water, and settle again, already at home, on stone that their ancestors have been softening, whitening, and inhabiting for longer than anyone here keeps records.
