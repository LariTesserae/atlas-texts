It sits in a shallow, freshly cut basin, below the level of the country around it.

A ring levee holds the outside marsh back — a clean, steep bank of pale clay and crushed stone, still sharp-edged, not yet softened by rain or roots. From the top of it you can see the whole place at once: a hamlet, really, but laid out like a town. Streets, drains, power lines, water towers, workshops. Then, beyond the levee, flat water and reed beds go on without interruption. Inside, everything ends. Outside, nothing is built at all.

The heat arrives first, and it does not vary. Thirty-eight, thirty-nine degrees in the shade, day after day, with the air so wet that shade itself feels steamed. Your shirt sticks before you have crossed the yard. Concrete sweats. Cold pipes in the pump houses run constantly with condensation, beading and dripping into grated channels. Puddles lie in the low corners of the asphalt and never quite dry; when rain comes it is warm rain, falling straight through still air, and the puddles only join briefly before the pumps take the excess away again. Painted on the main canal wall is a white waterline, and the black water sits exactly on it. It sat there yesterday. It will sit there tomorrow.

This is a polder town, built where the marsh was drained, and its work is to keep the draining done.

The center is the pump hall, a cathedral-sized shed of galvanized steel and fresh, unweathered concrete. Inside, the light is amber and dim, cut by shafts of steam. Three enormous diesel pumps sit in a row on plinths, painted industrial green, their flywheels turning, their exhaust stacks rising through the roof. The floor vibrates through the soles of your boots. You do not speak in here so much as shout, close to an ear, and then give up and use hand signals. Ear defenders hang on hooks by the door, worn smooth. Gauges tremble at the same readings. Outlet pipes as wide as hallways dive through the levee wall, and beyond the wall you can hear the discharge falling, a permanent, manufactured waterfall.

Around the hall, spaced far apart as fire and flood rules require, are the other buildings: the power house with its cooling towers, the machine shop, the fuel depot, the water treatment sheds, long dormitory blocks, a mess hall, a clinic, an office with a flag that hangs limp and wet and never stirs. It is urban in its organization — numbered streets, hydrants, cable ducts, a small bus that runs the ring road every twenty minutes, streetlights on poles — but sparse in its occupation. There are wide gravel yards between everything, turning circles for trucks, empty loading bays, expanses of raked stone where nothing is allowed to grow. At noon the yards are blinding where the sun gets in, and empty. Everyone moves through the covered ways.

Most of the town is under some kind of roof. Corrugated awnings link the dormitories to the mess hall, shade sails are strung over the walkways, the workshops open into deep verandas. Under them the light stays low, greenish-grey, filtered through steam from the cooling towers and the perpetual haze of diesel and hot water. The towers make their own cloud, which spreads and holds above the levee, thick enough that direct sun is rare inside the basin. It traps the heat as effectively as it blocks the light. At night the streetlights come on and the cloud glows faintly orange from below, and it is not much darker than the day, and not any cooler. Shifts change, but the town does not.

Nothing here has had time to erode. The drainage canals are straight cuts with clean V-profiles, their clay sides still showing the tooth-marks of excavators. Culverts are bright. The rock armor on the levee is angular, unlichened. Where the asphalt has cracked, the crack is black and new. Even the rust is only a first staining at bolt heads and seams, an orange blush on otherwise new steel. Maintenance crews repaint, re-gravel, re-cut before anything can soften. A bank that starts to slump on Monday is regraded by Friday, back to the template.

Vegetation is kept sparse almost as a rule. Crews in boots and gloves go out with brush cutters and keep the canal edges shaved. What survives does so in straight lines and assigned places: a row of young shade trees by the dormitories, staked, their leaves dusty and curled at the edges despite daily watering; reeds in the treatment ponds, planted in grids; algae, everywhere the crews cannot reach, filming the slow ditches emerald and gold. On the gravel, a few hard weeds find the seams between concrete slabs and are sprayed before they seed. The ground beyond the pavement is too hot, too wet, too often disturbed to hold much. Step off the concrete at the edge of town and your boot sinks into black silt that smells of iron and rot, and comes up shining.

And yet the place is full of animals. The ditches the town makes, warm and permanent and full of insects, are perfect. Frogs chorus from every drain at a volume that competes with the pumps — a grinding, pulsing wall of sound after any rain, and a steady undertone even at midday. Dragonflies patrol the canals in squadrons, blue and scarlet, snapping up midges. Herons stand motionless at the outfalls where stunned fish circle, utterly habituated to the noise, and egrets nest colonially in the one dead tree left standing by the treatment ponds. Swallows have colonized the rafters of the pump hall itself, darting in and out through the louvered vents, raising chicks above the flywheels. Geckos wait by every exterior light for the moths. Rats run the cable ducts. Feral cats, sleek and numerous, sleep through the hottest hours on the cool concrete under the water tanks and hunt the rats at shift change. Mosquitoes rise in a visible smoke from the levee grass at evening, and then the bats come out from under the eaves, hundreds of them, and the foremen close the mess hall doors.

The sound is layered and total. Underneath everything, the pump note: a low diesel throb you feel in your teeth. Over it, the hiss of steam vents, the whine of the power house turbines, the clank and grind of the shop, reversing alarms on trucks, water falling and falling out of sight. Over that, living sound: cicadas in the staked trees, frogs in the drains, swallows, the dawn-and-all-day roosters someone keeps behind the dormitories despite regulations. You raise your voice in the street without noticing. Indoors, you raise it less. Silence, when a pump is taken offline for servicing, is not silence at all — it is a sudden, exposed ringing, and the remaining pumps seem to get louder to fill it, and the frogs get louder too.

People live according to the equilibrium. Laundry is hung in the drying room, never outside, because outside it will not dry, only sour. Doors are kept shut against insects and steam. Boots are left on racks, hosed off daily, and still leave silt prints. Food comes in on the morning truck and leaves as waste on the evening one. The mess hall serves the same rotation; the notice board posts the same warnings about heat exhaustion, hearing protection, not swimming in the canals — the water there is warm as bathwater, weeded, and moving just fast enough to be dangerous. New arrivals are told not to wait for the evening cool. There isn't one. Not to wait for the dry season. The levee was built through it once, they say, and the gauges didn't move.

Late at night — by the clock, since the orange gloom out the windows is much the same — you can walk the ring road and see the town hold its shape. Pumps on. Lights on. Steam rising into its own low cloud and condensing and falling somewhere out on the marsh as warm rain. Frogs calling from black water that sits exactly on its painted line. A heron asleep on a bollard. The levee a clean, fresh line between the kept place and the unkept, with nothing on either side appearing to win.
