It is a bowl that has finished becoming a bowl.

Not a valley, exactly — valleys still imply a river hurrying somewhere. This place is low, warm, and flattened by an almost geological patience, a shallow depression perhaps four hundred paces across where the land long ago gave up on height. The rim is not a cliff but a tired rise of white marl and rounded stone, worn down to soap-smooth nubs, grassed over in places, bare in others where the rain has polished it bright. You can stand on that rim at midday and see the whole thing at once: a single room of landscape, with edges.

Inside, everything is wet at skin-depth.

A sheet of water covers most of the floor, nowhere deep enough to hide your ankle, nowhere quite dry enough to trust. It moves in broad, slow skins over mud — cream-colored clay, black peat in seams, patches of sand the exact color of wet bone. After rain it is a mirror. Between rains it condenses. The rocks weep even in sun. The air itself sweats onto leaves and metal and skin, so that by mid-morning your shirt is damp whether or not you have touched anything.

And it is bright in a way that exceeds the sun.

Light pours in, doubled somehow, white-gold and shadowless at the center of the day. The water throws it back up into your face. The wet leaves throw it sideways. Even under the scattered trees — and they are scattered, deliberate-looking in their spacing, a low mangrove here, a lone umbrella-thorn there, reed clumps standing apart like green fountains with clear mud between them — the shade is luminous. Your shadow is pale, and a fraction displaced from where it ought to be, as if it were falling through a lens. You do not sunburn here. The heat arrives, tropical and close, smelling of warm mud, crushed stems, frog-water, iron. The burning does not.

That is the first hint of the structures.

There are not many. A handful of low plinths around the rim, half-sunk into the marl, so eroded-looking at a distance that you might mistake them for natural stone until you get close and find no joint, no tool mark, no edge your fingernail can catch. Their surface is a dull ceramic that is neither hot nor cold in the sun; touch one and the warmth travels oddly far up your arm, and the fine hairs there lift. Near the north edge a roof hangs where a roof should not be able to hang — a thin, curved slab the size of a market stall, its supports long since gone, if it ever had any, floating a man's height above a keeper's platform of reeds and salvaged panels. It does not sway in wind. Birds nest on top of it with complete confidence. Rain slides off its empty air a hand's breadth before it reaches the slab, and falls in a neat curtain.

Between the plinths, sometimes, the water stands up.

Not waves. Partitions. Clear vertical sheets, a centimeter thick, holding one shallow pool a finger higher than the next, so the bowl is terraced in water without earth. Fish pass through them with a visible shiver and a small change of color. Your hand passes through with a sensation like pushing through warm soap film. Dragonflies do not pass. They turn, always, precisely at the invisible line, patrol along it, and turn again.

Nobody alive built any of this with understood tools. The keeper — there is one, in a hut on stilts at the east rim, woven reed patched with that same seamless grey stuff — will tell you the bowl was already old when her grandmother's people walked in out of a drier country and found the animals unafraid. The machinery, if machinery is the word, is mostly below: a lattice in the mud, finer than root-hairs, holding this depression at the exact final point of its erosion forever. Rain is not allowed to cut. Flood is not allowed to deepen. The water table is persuaded, daily, to stay at the marsh's edge, so the bowl never becomes lake and never becomes meadow. It is an ecosystem held in its last, settled posture, and still, somehow, kept alive inside it.

Because the animal life is not sparse at all. Everything else is: trees, buildings, people, hills. The fauna has taken the emptiness and filled it without crowding it, the way stars fill a sky.

They are quiet. That is what you notice first, before the numbers.

A flock of ibis lifts from the south pool — sixty, eighty birds, white wings with black edges, all at once — and makes almost no sound. You hear a soft displaced sigh of air, and then nothing, as if the wingbeats were happening in the next room. Spoonbills sweep their bills through the shallows in slow semicircles, silent. Frogs line every reed stem and mud lip, hundreds of them, emerald, rust-red, translucent yellow, their throat sacs inflating into bright bubbles — and no chorus comes. A heron strikes, a fish flashes, a snake slides between lotus roots leaving a perfect, soundless V in the water — and the splash dies within a foot of its making, eaten out of the air.

Sound does not travel here. Shout across the bowl and your voice thins at ten paces to a texture, then a warmth on the skin, then nothing. The plinths were made, perhaps, to give somebody peace. Their field takes noise out past a conversational radius and lays it down. What is left is close sound: your own breath, wet cloth, a crab's claw clicking under your boot, water lipping your ankles. And underneath, through the soles of your feet, a vast faint ticking and stirring that your ears never quite get — the bowl is very loud, down where bone conducts it.

So the animals have learned other languages, and the result is a place in constant, brilliant, silent conversation.

The frogs flash their sacs in sequence, waves of color running around a pool like handwriting. Male fish rise under the surface film and turn their sides to the sun in semaphore, silver, copper, sudden blue. Crabs hold territories on the exposed mud and signal with enlarged white claws, semaphore flags, dozens at a time, all facing inward. At noon, when the condensers wake, the dragonflies rise off the water in thousands — a low, drifting stratum of winged glass at knee height, green and amber and violet — and the swallows come down through them, and the air is full of turns and near-misses and soft ticking impacts, and it is still quieter than a library.

There are turtles old enough that the lattice has grown up through the patterns of their shells in fine grey veins. There are otters in the deepest channel, which is still only waist-deep, rolling cubs over and over in the warm current. There are pygmy buffalo, no taller than goats, who come down at evening to stand belly-deep and let cleaner birds walk their backs. There are snails whose shells have incorporated the plinth-ceramic, opalescent and unbreakable, grazing algae off the invisible partitions. Waterbirds of twenty species nest in the reed clumps in a density that should mean constant territorial war and instead proceeds like a well-run market, everyone shouting silently in color and posture, beaks opening on calls you will never hear.

They do not fear people. That may be the field, too, or simply ten thousand years of nobody being permitted to hunt — weapons brought inside the rim lose their edges, their tension, their charge, in a matter of hours, unmade politely at a scale too fine to see. A dropped knife comes up out of the mud in a week as a neat row of bright grains. The hut's salvaged panels are ones the bowl, for whatever reason, has allowed to remain.

The day has a mechanism and the animals know its hours.

At dawn the water exhales; mist stands waist-high inside the rim and nowhere outside it, cut off at the boundary as if by a wall, and the birds feed walking in and out of whiteness. By mid-morning the mist is drawn up, visibly, in slow columns toward no visible intake, and condenses somewhere above the floating roof, and falls as a warm local rain that takes twenty minutes to cross the bowl, a single grey curtain moving east to west while sun shines on both sides of it. The frogs answer that with color. The lotus — lush, indecently lush, pink and white flowers big as hands rising out of water that should be too shallow for them — open for the rain and close after it. In the late afternoon the partitions lower a fraction, pools equalize, and the fish move through in great coordinated drifts from one end of the bowl to the other, and every heron and kingfisher and otter takes up its customary station without a sound to negotiate it. It works the way a tide works, except the moon has nothing to do with it. Something underneath is keeping time.

Go down into it and the rules become physical. Walk slowly; the mud accepts you but the surface film resists fast movement, so running is nearly impossible and wholly ridiculous — the water thickens against a lunging foot, parts for a patient one. Speak softly if you want to be heard. Watch where the dragonflies turn, if you want to find the walls. Do not expect a river to leave by any visible outlet; the bowl loses its water upward, into its own bright air, and gains it back the same way, a closed breathing.

At the center, where the sheet-water is clearest, the mud gives way to a disc of that seamless grey, thirty paces wide, flush with the ground, covered most days by an inch of water and a skin of green algae and the footprints of everything. Stand there at noon and the doubled light comes up through the water around your ankles and the displaced shadows of birds cross above and below you at once, and the silent frogs pulse around the edges of the disc in rings, and — through your feet, unmistakably — the buried lattice answers something in the sky with a single, patient adjustment, and every partition in the bowl ripples, once, in sequence.

An ancient, flattened, finished place, held by an unfinished technology at the exact moment before stillness — and swarming, flashing, breeding, hunting, and migrating inside that moment, endlessly, at the top of its voice, in a language that makes no sound at all.
