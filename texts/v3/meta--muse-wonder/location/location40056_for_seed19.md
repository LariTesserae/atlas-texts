It begins as a climb that stops making sense as a climb.

For the first two days you are below it, on ordinary scree, on switchbacks cut into a mountain that has long since lost any sharpness. There is no peak above you. There hasn't been for a long time. The range was worn down so thoroughly, over so much time, that what remains is not a summit but a lifting — a whole country raised into the sky and then polished flat. Stone under stone. Slabs of grey schist and brown gneiss fitted together like a pavement, the cracks between them filled with pale dust. When the wind finds a loose edge it does not howl. It makes a thin, apologetic ticking, and then stops.

The air gets less and less usable. Cool, not bitterly cold — in sun you can open your jacket, in shade your fingers go stupid — but thin enough that your sleep breaks into gasps, and normal daylight starts doing abnormal work. The sky is a clean, unremarkable blue at noon and yet the brightest stars are already visible if you look away from the sun. Your shadow is crisp. Your breathing is not.

At the top the ground levels so abruptly it feels artificial.

The Table is perhaps forty kilometers across. You can see most of it from any low rise, because there are no real rises left. No rivers cut it. No valleys fold it. Erosion has taken everything to its final opinion: a vast, open, gently dished plain of rock at an altitude where rock should be torn by ice and storm, lying there instead as if set down and forgotten. Shallow hollows hold salt in white, feathered crusts. Long grooves, no deeper than a finger, run for kilometers in parallel, following some grain in the stone older than animal life. In places the rock is darkened and smoothed almost to glass. In other places it is pitted with millions of tiny, overlapping crescents — lick-marks, scrape-marks, tooth-marks — repeated until geology and appetite are indistinguishable.

At first, from the rim, you think it is empty.

Then you think the far half of it is covered in low, dark vegetation.

Then, as your eyes adjust to the scale, you understand that the plain is not covered. It is occupied.

They stand from horizon to horizon.

The grazers — the shepherds who once came up here called them *ums*, a word that is mostly breath — are barrel-bodied animals about as high as your waist, though their backs are so thickly felted they seem higher. Head down, usually. Six short legs, or sometimes five visibly planted and one folded up into the belly felt, which makes counting them a private source of unease if you stare too long. Their coats are not fur so much as a continuous grey-brown mat, dense as carpet, dusted with rock flour until each animal carries the exact color of the ground it stands on. A curved horn, blunt and black, comes down on either side of a broad, blind-looking face. The eyes, when you find them in the mat, are pale and wet and open.

They stand nose to tail. Flank to flank. In lines that follow the grooves in the stone, in arcs around the salt hollows, in solid phalanxes where a thousand backs make a second, breathing plateau a meter above the first. There is no edge to the herd. There are only thinner places, where you might walk by turning sideways. For most of the Table, you do not walk on rock at all for very long. You walk in lanes between bodies, brushing felt on both sides, ducking under chins, stepping over legs that have been in the same position so long that lichen has grown across the hoof and onto the stone in a single unbroken crust.

And they do not move.

That is the first wrongness, and all the others follow from it.

Living things fill the world here — not just the grazers. Kestrel-like birds hang in the normal daylight above the plain, wings set, dozens of them, hundreds, at different heights, facing into a wind that is not blowing. Small black flies make a motionless halo around every dung pile, though the dung is dry as cork and may be centuries old. Under turned stones there are beetles with their legs mid-stride. In the felt of each grazer is an entire undergrowth of life: ticks like polished seeds, moths folded in the warm dark, mites trailing paths through the fibers, tiny white spiders holding webs that catch nothing but dust.

Yet the whole enormous assemblage makes almost no sound.

Part of it is the felt. It drinks noise. Stand among the ums and your own footsteps go dull, your jacket stops rustling, your heartbeat feels as if it is happening in another room. Shout — visitors always try, once — and the sound comes out honest and immediately tired, falling to nothing within a few meters, eaten by ten thousand soundless coats. The grazers themselves breathe, technically. If you lay a hand on a flank you may, after a minute, feel the faintest inward gathering, a sigh beginning somewhere deep. If you stay for an hour, it may not yet have finished. Their calls, the old bone whistles left in the huts suggest, are pitched below hearing and spaced so far apart that a single greeting might take a season.

They are not dead. That would be simpler, and in places you wish for it.

Because some of them are dead, and there is no reliable way to tell at a glance. In this dry, cool, thin air, with no water to feed rot and no scavengers large enough to make a difference, the dead do not oblige by decaying. They stand. Their felt keeps its color. Their eyes dry to milky stones but stay in place. A dead um may hold up its living neighbor for a hundred years. A living one may be so still, so cold along the legs where the blood visits rarely, that you will apologize to it for treating it as furniture and receive, perhaps a day later, the delayed blink that proves you were overheard.

Water is a rumor the animals keep in their bodies. It never rains on the Table. The salt hollows have never been lakes in human memory; the white crusts are minerals sweated up from the stone and left when some older, wetter world evaporated for good. There are well-pits among the scattered buildings — squat circles of stacked rock, a primitive windbreak here, a roofless hut there, a cairn with a bone scraper and a stone lichen-knife laid neatly on top — and every well goes down through cracked slab to dry dust. If you want to drink, tradition says, you wait until the coldest hour before dawn and press your mouth to the stone beneath a living grazer's chin, where decades of slow breath have raised a dark patch of damp no larger than a coin. It tastes of salt and iron and old wool. It is never quite enough, and yet the ums go on not drinking, licking mineral crust, cropping lichen down to the pigment, chewing mouthfuls of their own shed felt and each other's dead hair, recycling the same scant moisture through bodies that have learned to spend almost nothing.

Vegetation persists only where the animals permit it by accident. Black and ochre lichens make maps across the slabs. In the shelter of a hoofprint a cushion plant the size of a thumbnail may put out leaves like grains of green rice, and one waxy flower, and hold that flower open for years. Nothing here hurries to seed. Nothing here hurries at all.

That is the rule you feel in your own joints after the second day.

It is not that time has stopped. It moves on the Table the way the herd moves: by a step, considered across a generation. A dropped stone tool does not bounce properly; it seems to consult the ground before settling. Dust you kick up hangs, not dramatically, not magically — just a little too long, as if falling were a habit it is losing. A cut does not bleed well and does not heal. A fire laid in one of the stone hearths with dung and felt-parings will catch, burn low and clean in the ordinary sun, and still be at the same red eye of itself when you come back in the evening. Your nails stop troubling you. Hunger arrives and then forgets why it came. The birds overhead are not frozen; if you mark the position of a wingtip against a rock and return the next summer, it will have stooped perhaps a hand's breadth toward the plain.

Distance misbehaves politely. The Table is higher in the middle, everyone agrees, but the middle is reached by walking gently downhill for hours until your altimeter and your aching ears insist you have ascended another thousand meters. Animals counted in a transect will not recount to the same number; not because they have moved, but because some of them seem to be shared between positions, the same torn ear and crescent scar appearing on a body to your left and then, after you have passed fifty others, again on your right — not a similar animal, you would swear, the same one, still mid-blink from when you startled it before. Their shadows make this worse. In the morning the herd casts a second herd, black and flattened, all heads turned toward grazing that the real animals are not doing. Sometimes a shadow stoops. Sometimes one lies down. The body above it stands on.

The primitive things people left here suggest they understood, and stayed anyway for a while. Low loom-frames of lashed bone wedged between boulders, with felt thread still strung. Sleeping hollows scooped in the lee of dead ums and lined with woven hair. Markers — one white stone, one black stone — set beside particular grazers, perhaps to measure a migration that proceeds at the speed of fingernails growing through stone. In one hut, roofed at last by the collapsed body of an animal that fell half-in and never rotted, there is a tally wall. Thousands of notches. Then, near the end, the notches get farther apart, wider, clumsier, as if the hand that made them had to remember the intention between one year and the next. The last notch is unfinished. The knife is under it, where it was set down.

No one warns you not to touch the warm ones. You learn by the feeling. Most of the herd is ambient, cool as the rock. Now and then, threading the lanes, you brush a flank and find heat — deep, mammalian, startling heat — and a smell of living skin rises out of the felt, and every motionless fly around that animal is suddenly, retroactively, in exactly the place a fly would have moved to. Those are the ones who are, on the herd's timescale, awake. You step back. A head may begin, over the course of your visit, to turn.

Nothing will chase you here. Nothing will flee. Predators came up in the old migrations — there are long, low, mummified shapes among the ums with teeth still bared around throats they have not finished biting since before the huts were built — and they joined the general agreement. Overhead the hawks descend at the rate of weather. Under the felt the spiders wait for flies that arrive like statues falling through honey. It is not a peaceful place, exactly. It is a place where violence, tenderness, hunger, birth, and death have all been stretched so thin that they no longer interrupt the silence.

Late in the day the light stays normal — that may be the strangest part. No amber apocalypse, no violet sky. Just afternoon on a high, dry plain, cooling toward evening, with the sun doing exactly what a sun does, while below it an impossible multitude stands in its warmth and does not graze, does not call, does not fall, mile after mile after mile, all the way to a horizon that your legs and your instruments keep insisting is above you.

If you stay long enough, your own shadow will start getting ahead of you.
