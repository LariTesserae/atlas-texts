You come to it from above, and from above there is almost nothing to see.

The land around it is a flat, ordinary plain — thin grass, pale dirt, a few low scrubby trees bent the same way. It is warm there, and quiet, and the light is hard. Then the ground simply stops. Not a cliff worn by weather, not a canyon cut by a river. A scoop. A dent in the world, eight or nine kilometers across, as if something enormous pressed its thumb into soft earth and lifted it away again before the earth had time to harden.

The pit is still soft. That is the first thing you understand on the rim. Erosion has not begun here. There are no gullies, no scree slopes, no boulders tumbled and cracked and sorted by water. The walls go down a hundred and fifty meters in one smooth, unbroken curve of raw, yellow-white clay and crumbly new stone, still showing the layered smear of its making. It should be falling apart. It isn't. It is held, everywhere, by plants.

From the rim, the bottom is not shadowed, as a pit should be. It is brighter than the plain.

The pale, fresh walls act as a bowl for the sun. Light pours down at noon and it does not leave again — it bounces from clay to waxy leaf to clay, up under canopies, in under overhangs, until even the undersides of leaves are lit. There are almost no true shadows down there, only a greener, thicker kind of brightness. You can see the far wall, a hazy white arc beyond a sea of green, and the heat shimmering off it makes the whole basin look slightly submerged.

The descent is not a path so much as a place where the vegetation is less impossible. Moss first, ankle-deep and springy, stitching the raw earth together with a million fine roots. Then ferns that grow straight out of the vertical wall, their fronds horizontal, dripping with condensation that never quite becomes a drip. Then small trees clinging sideways, their trunks immediately curving up, their roots fanned out over the clay like hands holding on. The air gets hotter with every ten meters down, and thicker, and greener in smell — hot leaf, wet soil, crushed stem, a sweet and slightly peppery rot that never deepens. It is damp, but not wet. Your skin is filmed with sweat and mist at once. Your clothes stick. The ground under your boots never turns to mud and never dries.

By the bottom, there is no ground.

There is plant as substrate, plant as floor, plant as architecture. A root-mat two meters thick in places, interlocked trunks and vines and fallen logs that have not so much rotted as been incorporated, still solid under a skin of living bark. You walk on it and it gives a little, like a raft. Between your steps, glossy beetle-less leaves fill every gap. Look down through a rare break and there is another layer of growth below in dim green light, and another below that, rooted not in soil but in the compressed tangle of the plants that came before. The original clay floor of the pit is somewhere, theoretical. Nothing reaches it except the deepest taproots of the oldest trees.

Those trees are not tall by rainforest standards — the pit rim cuts the wind, and the reflected light comes from all sides, so they grow outward more than up. They become broad, parasol-crowned things, their branches plaited by vines, each one supporting an entire second ecosystem in its crown: orchids with fleshy, water-storing leaves, bromeliads holding dark cups of water at every fork, ferns, mosses, young palms sprouting from humus collected in a hollow limb. A tree here is an address. A single canopy can be wet on its east side from condensation, steaming faintly on its west side where the wall-light strikes it, flowering and fruiting and dropping spent leaves all at once, in different sectors, all the time.

And it is all the time. That is the rule that asserts itself slowly.

Nothing down here is in the process of becoming something else. The basin is in a furious, perfect equilibrium. A leaf falls, and in the hot damp it softens to exactly the texture of the mat — no further. A fruit ripens and hangs ripe, unpicked, unrotted, until its seeds have already sprouted inside it and pierced its own skin. Saplings stand knee-high beside saplings that have clearly stood knee-high for years, held by shade and competition at a permanent threshold. The sun seems to wheel overhead, but the temperature on the floor never negotiates; ninety-eight, a hundred degrees, a bath of air that does not cool at the edges of the day. The condensation gathers every leaf to the same beaded brightness, evaporates at the same rate it gathers, so that the forest is always just about to rain and never does. What little rain does arrive from above arrives as a fine, warm mist that is absorbed before it can gather and run. There are no streams. There has never been enough running water to carve so much as a finger-width channel. Water lives in pockets — in leaf tanks, in hollow canes, in shallow, root-rimmed hollows on the mat where it lies an inch deep, tea-colored, utterly still, and does not sink or spread.

Because of that stillness, it is quiet in a way that is not empty. Sound is taken by leaf and moss within ten meters. There is a constant, very low hum — insects, plant tissue expanding in heat, distant water shifting in stems — so low you stop hearing it as a sound and hear it as pressure. Beyond that, rare events carry an unnatural importance. The sudden tick of a seed case splitting. Somewhere far off, not often, a single large bird — white, long-necked, heron-like, though there is no lake for it — beats up from one crown to another and is gone. Tiny frogs live invisibly in the bromeliad cups, and on some days one will call, a clear glass note, and be answered by one other, much farther away, and then not again. Animal life is a rumor here. No trails, no dung, no gnawed bark in any quantity. The biomass is plant thinking about plants. If you see a lizard, flat and emerald on a sunlit trunk, you will watch it for minutes because nothing else is moving in that way.

The basin varies, across its kilometers, in mood rather than in kind. Toward the north wall, where the pale clay reflects most fiercely, there is a glare-meadow of waist-high ferns and stiff, succulent rosettes, almost too bright to look across, the air there sharp with heated sap. Toward the center, the groves close in, trunks thicker, the root-mat darker and spongier, the air smelling of mushrooms that never open. Toward the south, broad-leafed plants with leaves a meter across make a layered ceiling at head height; you walk stooped in green tunnels between their stems, brushing water from their tips onto your shoulders, emerging into sudden clearings where the light falls straight and white and the heat is like standing in front of an opened oven.

It is in the clearings that people, at some point, have been.

Not a village — the structures are scattered too far apart for that, one to a clearing, sometimes two in sight of each other's smoke if either ever had a fire. Low domes of bent saplings lashed with vine and thatched so thickly with palm leaf that they have begun to root at the edges and become half-living. A sleeping platform woven between three trunks, its leaf mattress long since dried to a crisp, springy thatch. A circle of eight uncut, unmarked stones, waist-high, inside which the root-mat has been pressed flat and bare and kept — impossibly, in this growth — clear. No tools. No metal, no pottery shards, no bones, no cold hearths that you can find. Whatever was built here was built from what grows here, by hand and tooth and stone weight, and then left for the plants to make a decision about. The plants, characteristically, have held them exactly as they were abandoned: not overgrown, not collapsed, roofs beaded with the same perpetual condensation as the leaves around them.

You get the sense they are not ruins. They are just occasionally unoccupied. The basin does not have seasons for returning, or migrations, or reasons to leave that would change its balance. Anyone who comes down must come down the moss wall on their belly, gripping roots, and anyone who leaves must climb back into a world that is, by comparison, cooler and dimmer and much drier. Up on the plain the wind moves the grass and time moves with it. Down here the light stays, the heat stays, the green stays pressed up close on all sides of you, layer after layer, until after a few hours your own movements begin to match it — slower, smaller, unwilling to tear or break more than necessary, drinking by squeezing a fleshy stem, resting not in shade but in a slightly less brilliant green.

Nothing is waiting to happen in the pit. It has finished happening, at the very moment of its beginning, and it will keep finishing, leaf for identical leaf, for as long as the walls hold their pale curve to the sun.
