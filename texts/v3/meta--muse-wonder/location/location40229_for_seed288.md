From above, there is almost nothing to see.

The plain is flat the way very old land becomes flat, when everything that could be worn down has been. Pale loess, cracked clay, salt dust in the low places. No river, no lake, no dark line of a wet channel. The surface soil rings hard underfoot and lifts in a fine, dry powder. A few thorn bushes sit far apart, grey and half-bare. The wind moves over it unimpeded and makes very little sound.

The town is underneath that.

You find it first by hearing it, then by the holes.

Scattered across several kilometres of plain are broad, rectangular pits, each ten to fifteen metres deep, sunk straight into the earth. From a distance their rims are only a darker shadow, a fig tree crown rising just to ground level, a plume of woodsmoke flattening in the wind. Up close the sound comes out of them all at once — braying, bleating, cooing, shouting, wings, bells of beaten tin on goat collars, children calling to each other, sparrows in a continuous tearing chatter — magnified by the earthen walls until it is not so much loud as physical. The pits are natural amplifiers. So are the tunnels between them. On the plain you can stand between two inhabited pits and hear almost nothing. Lean over a rim and the din hits you like weather.

This is how people live here: not on the land, but in it.

A ramp cut into one wall spirals down, its steps dished in the middle by centuries of feet, donkey hooves, and dragged loads. The temperature falls as you descend. First out of the wind, then out of the full sun, then into the true coolness of the loess, which holds the temperature of deep earth all year. At midday in summer the pit floor may receive three or four hours of direct light. The rest of the day is shade, green shade, wall shade, the dimness of rooms cut sideways into the pit walls. At the bottom it is always morning or evening.

There is no building up. Every home is a cave room opening onto a shared courtyard pit. The walls are the loess itself, yellow-brown, compact, flecked with old root traces and small stones, cut smooth and then rounded again by age, touch, and dry crumbling. Wood is used sparingly — a lintel over a doorway, a beam where a wall has begun to sag, a gate made of lashed branches. Where families have lived longest, thresholds have worn into polished hollows. Handholds beside steep stairs have been darkened by palms. Older pits, abandoned, do not remain as ruins in any sharp sense. Their corners slump. Their cave mouths sigh inward. Rain, on the rare years when it comes in any quantity, does not so much damage them as melt them gently back toward a saucer shape. Then goats graze in them, fig roots find the old hearths, and swallows take the remaining ledges.

And yet in these dry holes, the vegetation is lush.

Each courtyard has its tree. Usually a fig, sometimes a mulberry, pomegranate, or an apricot, planted not in the centre but where the sun patch rests longest. Under and around it, in beds banked with manure and household ash, grow broad beans, onions, melons on vines, herbs, peppers, squash whose leaves overlap like hands. Grape vines climb strings to the rim and spill a metre or two out onto the plain before the wind and dryness prune them back. Reed screens shade seedlings. The green is concentrated, almost startling after the pallor above — wet-looking, though nothing here is wet. The surface of every garden bed is dry dust. Dig a hand's breadth down and the loess is darker, faintly cool, holding moisture from winters long past in its fine pores. Tree roots go down much farther than the pits themselves, following old moisture seams toward a deep, brackish stillness that never rises, never flows, and is reached by only the oldest wells: a bucket lowered twenty metres, brought up not full of clear water but enough.

No open water exists. There are no fountains, no irrigation channels, no tanks standing uncovered to the sky. What people drink is drawn sparingly and kept in sealed jars in the cave rooms. What plants drink is dew, condensation on cool stone in the early morning, the damp exhalation of animals and earth in enclosed courtyards, household water used three times and finally poured at roots, and the deep memory of rain in the loess. In the driest months the gardens do not look thirsty so much as deliberate. Leaves are thick. Growth is slow. A fig tree may be a family's oldest member, tended for generations, its trunk emerging from a courtyard its planter never saw mature.

Animals are everywhere, and they are the real population.

Pigeons keep to the pit walls in the hundreds, nesting in cut holes, bursting upward in grey flocks that clap their wings against the loess and turn the shaft of sunlight opaque with dust. House sparrows own the vines. Swallows nest under every lintel and stitch the air above the courtyards at dusk. Chickens work the manure heaps. In side caves at pit level — stables with the same cool dimness as the houses — goats, sheep, donkeys, and a few mules stand in deep litter, shifting, stamping, coughing, braying without warning at a volume that in these echoing shafts makes conversation impossible for half a minute. Dogs sleep on the ramps where they can watch both levels. Cats hunt the grain caves. Lizards flatten themselves on the one warm band of wall. Bats pour out of unlit connecting tunnels after dark and are replaced, as tenants, by owls.

Abandoned pits belong entirely to them. Rooks and jackdaws colonise the rim trees. A pit that has lost its people first gains pigeons, then a fox den in a fallen room, then a bramble of wild caper and untended pomegranate splitting with fruit nobody picks, eaten by wasps and starlings in a screaming, stained harvest.

The townplan, if it can be called that, is sparse and wide. Pits are separated by fifty, a hundred, sometimes three hundred metres of undisturbed roof-earth, which is also field, grazing, threshing floor, and road. Below, some are connected by passages wide enough for a laden donkey, their ceilings low, their air smelling of dung, stored onions, cool mineral soil, and woodsmoke that has nowhere to go. The largest pit is the market: not built, just enlarged over centuries, its walls terraced with cave shops and store-rooms, its floor beaten to a pale, resonating drum of earth. On market mornings the sound there is genuinely deafening. Everyone shouts. Everyone is, by middle age, a little deaf. As a result people here have elaborate languages of gesture, touch, and facial expression, and a social rule so old it is never stated: nothing said in a pit is private, because the walls give it to everyone. Secrets are kept above ground, in the silence, or not kept at all.

Technologically there is nearly nothing that could not be replaced by hand, animal, plant, or stone. Baskets, rope, fired pots, woven wool, wooden ploughs dragged across the thin fields around the rims, grain ground between stones worn into perfect, shallow bowls. No wires cross the courtyards. No engine has ever been down the ramps. Light after dark is a small oil flame set deep in a wall niche, mostly unnecessary; people arrange their lives around the slow movement of the sun patch across their pit floor, working where it is not, resting where the cool is deepest.

Nothing here changes quickly. A new pit is a generational project, dug in dry seasons, basketload by basketload, the spoil spread so thin across the plain that the wind eventually takes it. A cave room is enlarged by a hand's width a year. Erosion does the rest, softening, settling, flattening — the town is in its final form in the same way the plain is, not finished but past the age of sharpness. Even sound, for all its violence, is cyclical and slow underneath: dawn chorus, feeding clamour, the cicada-like rasp of sparrow flocks at noon from the trees in the pits that catch the sun, the evening return of swallows and bats, then a night of goat movements, owl calls, and dogs answering each other from pit to pit across the dark, hollow earth.

Stand on the rim at dusk and you can hear the whole settlement without seeing it: a buried, green, animal city roaring softly in its holes, while around you the waterless plain extends empty to the horizon, saying nothing at all.
