It is below, and it has always been below.

You reach it by a stair that stops pretending to be dry about halfway down. First the steps are damp, then beaded, then under a skin of water that thickens as you descend — ankle, knee, waist — until the last flight is simply moving downwards through warm green dimness, hand over hand on a rail furred with algae. There is no surface to break. The water just takes your weight and the sounds of the upper world go out of you, all at once, like a lamp turned down.

It is a town, in the floor of a basin.

Not large. If the water were clear you could stand in its central square and see the rock walls on every side, a flooded quarry or crater three hundred paces across, its rim far above where the light comes from. But the water is never clear. It is full — of warmth, of silt, of drifting spores and minute white particles that hang without falling — and you cannot see across it. You learn its size by traveling it, street by packed street.

There are no open spaces down here. The place is saturated.

Buildings shoulder each other without alleys between them, three and four stories of brick and riveted iron and poured stone, their lower floors long since surrendered and packed solid with silt and root. Above that they lean together, braced with pipes, buttressed with old boilers, roofed over in places by later construction until many streets are tunnels. Every gap that architecture left, something else has taken.

Eelgrass grows in long dark ribbons from the paving of the square. Kelp forests have established themselves along the warmer avenues, anchored to lampposts. The walls wear a continuous pelt: green algae an inch thick, then feather-moss, then trailing vines with pale, inflated leaves that do not need sunlight in any hurry. Warm-water lilies have sent roots down forty feet from the bright world above; they arrive as white cords and explode at the level of the rooftops into root-mats where small blind flowers open underwater, cream and scentless. On the factory roofs there are actual groves, slow mangrove-things crouched in the current, their knees in copper runoff, filtering it.

And factories there are — that is why the town is kept drowned.

The great works occupy the north arc of the basin, a single interconnected plant grown rather than built: pressure halls, settling tanks, turbine pits, forge chambers whose round hatch-doors are six feet thick. Certain things can only be made under this much water, at this steady pressure and temperature. The presses close once an hour. You feel them more than hear them — a deep, soft concussion that travels through paving and bone, pushing a warm pulse out along every street. The conveyors creep. The turbines in their cages turn with monumental slowness, dragging chains of buckets up through the murk, spilling bubbles.

Everything industrial here is old, scaled, and still running, but nothing moves fast enough to tear the water. A crane arm takes an afternoon to cross the square. A freight sled, loaded with ingots green already with oxidation, is hauled by cable from the works to the freight lock, a journey of eighty yards that is scheduled by the day, not the hour. The power cables that web from building to building are swollen with insulation and crusted over with limestone and tube-worm casings, and they hum so faintly you notice it only when you touch one and your teeth ache.

The houses, if they can be called that, are in the interstices of the works: converted vats and boiler shells stacked and cut with portholes, joined by flexible corridors of oiled canvas and brass. Their windows glow dimly, amber and verdigris, not enough to light the water but enough to mark a habitation through the gloom. Above each cluster, trapped under inverted iron domes bolted to ceilings or under the concave undersides of bridges, are bells of air — not for breathing, mostly, but for dry work, for paper, for certain curing processes. Plants love the undersides of those domes and grow downward from them in wet curtains, reaching toward the water they already live in.

Light comes principally from above, though you would not call it day. At noon it is a diffuse green column over the square, moted and shifting, in which the root-cords from the lilies hang like rigging. At the edges of town it dies quickly. The rest is lamplight: caged bulbs along the handrails, chemical lanterns in the kelp that pulse very slowly, the dull red mouths of the forge chambers opening and closing on their hourly cycle. It is a perpetual early evening in warm fog.

It is quiet in a way that has texture. Bubbles rise from seams and vents in long, unhurried strings, ticking against overhangs. Vegetation brushes brickwork. Now and then a building settles — not collapsing, just exhaling, groaning through its timbers as it has done for a hundred years. The whole basin is eroded into equilibrium. Stone edges are rounded, iron has softened to a topography of rust and mineral scale, street signs have become suggestions under encrustation. Nothing is new and nothing is ruinous. Maintenance, human or mechanical, happens at the speed of coral: a fresh weld beaded over last month, a patch of wall re-bolted and already greening.

Animals are almost absent, and that absence is palpable among so much plant life. No schools, no shoals. A single long eel makes a circuit of the turbine pits and is known by the scar on its flank. Somewhere in the settling tanks a catfish of improbable age and size disturbs the silt once a week. Small translucent shrimp work the root-mats, barely visible. Otherwise the town moves without being watched.

The rules down here have to do with displacement. You do not walk; your feet find the rails set into the streets and you pull yourself along, or you drift just above the eelgrass and let the slow thermal current that circles the basin — in at the factory outflow, out through the southern lock, once around every six hours — carry you between districts. Doors are hatches and must be dogged against the pressure of plants growing into their seals. Fires exist only in the sealed forge hearts and in the air bells, where they burn blue and small. Anything dropped is not lost; it sinks ten feet and is incorporated, first by silt, then by roots, then by the town's salvagers with their long slow rakes.

After a while the strangest thing is not that it is underwater, but how complete it is. A laundry line strung between two vat-houses, hung with weighted cloths that sway without drying. A chapel in a former pumping station, its pews full of water-flowers, its bell sending out a muffled, shapeless note on the hour that you feel in your sternum. A market in the square where goods — tools, ingots, preserved foods, spools of cable, cuttings of the more useful vines — are stacked in crates that never need to be hurried anywhere, exchanging hands in gestures slowed by drag into something ceremonial.

The water is always at blood warmth near the works, cooler toward the walls where springs seep in. To stay is to accept that nothing will conclude today. The press will close again. The roots will have grown another fraction. The light will thin to its nighttime brown, the lanterns will take over, and the town — packed wall to wall with iron and leaf and warm dark water, bounded by rock, humming almost below hearing — will continue its one long, patient stroke.
