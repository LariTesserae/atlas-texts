It sits below the plain, in a bowl you would not notice until you were standing on its rim.

The descent is only a few minutes — raw, low ground, still new. The rim is a lip of broken black stone that has not yet been rounded by anything, each flake edged like a fresh knapped tool, ankle-twisting and pale on top with dust. Inside, the floor has sunk and settled into a shallow dish perhaps two hundred paces across. Beyond that rim there is nothing. Inside, there is a town.

At first that is the only word for it.

Sixty, maybe seventy houses. Lanes. Cross-lanes. A small plaza slightly off-centre, ringed by taller walls. Corner stones. Thresholds. A long communal wall running windward, with buttresses. Some houses have roofs of leaning slabs, some of woven, desiccated stems lashed into mats and weighted with pebbles, some are open troughs to the sky. There are compounds inside compounds — a courtyard house around an empty court, a row of three joined dwellings sharing a spine wall, a little round building with a conical top that ought to be a shrine or a granary. Everything is arranged. Nothing is random at the scale of a street.

There are no people. There never were, in any way you can prove.

No cut marks on the stone. No mortar, no nails, no glass, no hearths, no char, no middens, no paths worn between door and well. The houses are built of the same sharp rubble as the rim — plum-black basalt, iron-red scoria, and a salt-pale clay pressed into rough bricks that were never fired, only dried into hardness in the cold. The walls are thick and hollow-sounding and oddly warm in patches, as if a hand had rested there hours ago. Many of the lower stones are still half-buried, rooted. If you follow a wall down with your fingers, it does not end at ground level. It continues.

It is cool here even at the brightest hour, and the brightest hour is not bright. Light comes in dim and flattened, filtered through a permanent skin of fine dust suspended above the bowl, and cut again by the rim for most of the day. Colours go grey-blue and rust. Shadows do not sharpen; they pool. It is the light of late evening held for eight hours, then a deeper blue dusk, then a darkness so dry it feels granular on the eyes.

And it is silent — not quiet, silent. Your boots make no proper crunch. The pumice dust and porous stone drink sound at once. A dropped pebble lands with a felted *thud* that ends before it begins. A flap of roof matting shivers in a gust and makes no flap at all. Speech feels intrusive and slightly muffled, as if spoken into wool. The only sound you can be sure of is your own breath going steadily drier.

There is almost no water. Not hidden water, not seasonal water. No well, no cistern that ever held, no stain of flood on any wall, no mud-cracking that implies mud. The plaza has at its centre a beautifully made circular basin, its inner stones fitted tight as teeth, and its bottom is bone pale and dusty and has never been wet. Spill a mouthful from a flask onto the lane and it does not darken and spread. It vanishes, pulled down instantly between grains, and the spot looks exactly as before. Your lips crack by midday. Your tongue learns the taste of iron and cold stone. Nothing here drinks in a way you recognise.

What lives here lives by not drinking. In the lee of every third or fourth wall, where the wind is broken and the shadow lasts longest, the sparse vegetation has claimed its narrow strip. Low rosettes of ash-grey leaves, thick as leather and edged with dry white spines. Stems like frayed rope. A lichen that grows in mustard-coloured rings on the north faces of houses, expanding almost visibly when the temperature falls. They do not root deep. Their roots pry into basalt and unmake it grain by grain, gleaning mineral moisture locked in the rock itself. At dusk — though dusk is a change of temperature more than light — the rosettes fold inward with slow, deliberate movement, sealing themselves for the colder night. At the false dawn they open again, blind and patient. In a week a wall that was bare may wear a fringe of them; in another week they have moved, leaving only paler stone.

Fauna is rarer still, and mostly evidence. A line of three-toed prints crossing a lane and ending at a wall, as if the maker walked into it. A single grey moth in a roofless house, wings powdered the exact colour of the dust, opening and closing without any urgency. Once, at the edge of the plaza, a lizard the length of a hand, translucent at the throat, watching from a crack — gone the next time you look, without a scurry you could hear. Things live here on lichen, on spores, on each other, in very low numbers and at very long intervals. The houses are not empty because their tenants have fled. Each one appears to be occupied by emptiness itself: interiors bare, floors swept clean by air currents, no nest, no droppings. Just a slightly different quality of stillness inside the walls than outside them.

For something so silent and empty, it is furiously active.

Stand and watch — truly watch, for an hour — and the town stops pretending to be ruins. A lane of dust begins to slide, a sheet no thicker than paper moving sideways in a wind you barely feel on your skin. The rosettes turn, all of them, a fraction toward a heat source that is not the sun. Frost forms in the blue hour not as dew but as white hair growing directly out of the shaded stone; by midmorning it does not melt, it sublimates, lifting away as invisible vapour, leaving the stone drier than before. Under your palm a wall thrums with thermal contraction — not a sound, a *behaviour*, stones settling a millimetre against each other as they cool. Another wall, across the plaza, has gained height since yesterday: one new course of rubble along its top, stones still dusty from below ground, not yet bleached by the dim light.

That is the rule of the place, learned slowly. The ground heaves in the cold. Every night the young, restless basalt below the bowl pushes up. Stones rise through the dust like slow teeth and are sorted by freeze and slope and wind into lines, and the lines become walls, and the walls, pushed further, lean together and are roofed by whatever the wind delivers — slabs, stems, mats of dead vegetation blown in from the rim. The plaza is clear because that is where the heave is weakest. The lanes are clear because that is where it runs. It is not built by hands. It is patterned ground that has, by accident or by some longer logic, arrived at the shape of a settlement. Geometry doing what only towns were supposed to do.

And it repairs itself with townlike stubbornness. Knock a stone from a wall and by the next cold cycle two more have risen near the gap. Make a small cairn in a lane for a marker and within days it will be elongated, incorporated, given direction. Maps go soft and wrong in a month: a house at the eastern edge slumps and sighs down into gravel, and simultaneously a new enclosure shoulders up near the centre, fresh-edged and roofless, waiting for lichen. The total number of houses stays nearly the same. The town migrates imperceptibly downhill, eastward, shedding its rimward buildings like a slow skin.

If you spill water, a wall will begin where it fell.

If you stay long enough, your own leavings begin to be organised — boot prints erased not by wind but by dust welling up from underneath, a dropped strap dragged a thumb's width toward the nearest lane alignment overnight.

Beyond the rim, the plain resumes: sharp, cool, empty stone for as far as you can walk, a few rope-stemmed plants, a rare track, no streets. The strangeness has a border and it keeps to it. Only here, in this small, sunken, barely-lit bowl where the ground is still being born, has the world briefly convinced itself to be urban — street after silent street, house after empty house, growing, folding, flowering, subsiding, and growing again, with no one to live in it and no need of anyone at all.
