The town is in a lake that should not be there.

High in a black-rock caldera, where the wind comes thin and cool off snow patches that never quite melt, a tarn fills the crater almost to its lip. It is small enough to walk around in an hour — above water. Below water it has streets.

The first thing is the sound. Before you see the settlement you hear it, carrying strangely far in the mountain air: the white roar of the inlet fall on the north rim, frog-song by the thousand from the surface mats, a deep, irregular drumming, and now and then a clear bell-note that seems to come out of the lake itself. Underwater sound does not fade here. It accumulates.

The water is cold, clear at the edges and green-gold in the middle, lit almost as brightly below as above. Sun falls through in slanted columns. It strikes stone, and it strikes leaf — there is very little distinction. The floor of the crater is not really floor. It is root.

Centuries ago there was a dry hamlet in this hollow: low stone houses, a circular market, terraced plots, a causeway running gate to gate. Then the outlet choked — beaver work at first, then sedge, then the great rhizome mats — and the meltwater stayed. It rose a fingertip a generation. No one event drowned the town. The people simply kept raising their thresholds, then their floors, then learned to live with the lake in their kitchens. The old stone grid is still down there, worn smooth, softened with algae, doorways grown through with white roots. It gave the new town its plan.

That town is now mostly plant.

The houses are spaced far apart, with broad water between them like unbuilt boulevards. Each one is a living mound: a tangle of giant horsetail and club-reed grown up around an older stone core, the stems as thick as a man's thigh, hollow, jointed, tied while young with twisted root-fibre until they bow into walls and a low vault. Lily rhizomes, cut with stone adzes and bone saws, serve as beams — though nothing is cut if it can be persuaded to bend instead. Roofs are not roofs. They are canopies of overlapping water-lily leaves, each leaf two or three paces across, their upturned rims breaking the surface. From the rim of the caldera the whole settlement looks like a grove of pads and reed-clumps floating on dark water. Only the worn paths of bubbles, rising in steady silver threads from between houses, give away the order below.

For the water here is breathable, almost — or made so. The plant-mass oxygenates it all day. In sunlight the moss carpets and pondweed forests pearl with bubbles; the lower stories, the fish-stores and root cellars and sleeping nooks in the old stone rooms, are workable for long dives by people born to it, their skin used to the cold. And everywhere the town keeps its air-bells.

They are the true rooms. A bell is a single enormous leaf, or woven cluster of stems, trained downward at the rim to hold a pocket of trapped air the size of a cottage chamber, half under the surface. You swim up into one and your head breaks into warm, plant-smelling air while your body stays in the lake. Voices in a bell are startlingly loud — the curved leaf throws sound back, the water walls it in. Families gather there in the evenings, heads in a circle, bodies trailing below like tadpoles, talking, shelling water-chestnuts, mending nets of woven fibre. Children sleep in hammocks slung in the bells, just at the waterline, rocked almost imperceptibly. Market business is done in the largest bell over the old market circle, and it is a din: haggling, wooden clappers struck to call a shoal in, hand-drums of stretched fish skin, everyone shouting pleasantly in the resonance.

There is a surprising amount of infrastructure for so sparse and watery a place. Fish-weirs of wicker funnel trout into living pens under houses. Submerged walkways — the old causeway and its branches — run house to house in pale, scoured lines, so you can walk along the bottom in a slow, dreamlike stride, or swim a hand's breadth above them following the worn posts. Terraced gardens step down the crater's inner slope, each terrace a basket of root-felt holding snail ponds, arrowhead, wild rice in the shallows, cress in the cold inflow. Current is managed the way other towns manage roads: a sluice of lashed logs at the outlet, cleared by hand every morning, must never be widened. To drain the lake, even a little, would kill the houses roots-first. To cut a main stem is to knock a wall out of someone's home. Repairs are plantings. A new house is begun for a child and is ready, roughly, when she is grown.

The fauna have accepted the town as a feature of the lake, or perhaps the town has been accepted as a feature of the fauna. Trout patrol the streets in loose, glittering processions and shelter from ospreys in the under-house pens — enough are taken, enough escape, the arrangement is old. Newts live in the warm bell-margins. Freshwater mussels crowd the causeway stones, filtering the green light. Diving ducks commute straight through drawing-rooms. In late summer the surface between the lily roofs is loud with dragonflies, damselflies, frogs sitting on pads like citizens on porches, and the reed-beds at the inlet shake with nesting coots. Leeches are the tax. Herons are the watchmen, standing motionless on the shallower ruins for hours, and people consult where a heron chooses to stand before setting a new trap.

It changes very slowly. A leaf-canopy thickens over a decade. The lake gains perhaps a thumb's width in a lifetime, and another course of living stems is trained up to meet it. Storms up here are brief and violent — hail hammering the pads, the bells ringing all at once — but under two fathoms the water barely stirs, and the old stones hold.

At dusk the fall keeps roaring, the frogs take over from the birds, and the bubbles keep rising along the submerged streets, catching the last light. From the crater rim it looks deserted: just cool water, total green, a few reed chimneys trailing woodsmoke that flattens and mixes with mist. Put your face under, though, and the town opens — sparse, ordered, noisy, alive in every wall.
