There is almost no relief to it.

You come into the hollow across land that has forgotten how to slope — an old, low floodplain worn down over so long that ditch, bank, root-plate and fallen trunk have all softened into the same long undulations. Nothing cuts. Nothing protrudes. Even the path in is not a cut so much as a place where the leaf-mould has been pressed thinner by feet.

And then the trees close around you, and the place becomes a room.

It is small, in the way a cathedral is small compared to the sky outside it: perhaps sixty paces across, bounded completely by a ring of ancient beeches whose trunks have swollen together at their bases and separated again above, grey bark almost entirely hidden under moss, ivy, polypody fern, liverwort in the damp creases. Their crowns meet and interlock twenty metres up. The light that reaches the floor is ordinary daylight, green-filtered but not dim — you could read in it at noon, you could see colour properly — except that it arrives in slow, shifting coins and shafts, and never with any heat in it.

It is always cool here. Ten, twelve degrees, even in summer. The canopy keeps the sun at arm's length, the ground keeps giving back its stored damp, and the air sits in the bowl of the trunks the way cold air sits in a cellar, barely moving at ankle height, smelling of wet bark, leaf rot, fungus, and the faint iron tang of standing water.

Because there is water, but only ever in pockets. Water: 0.9 would be too grand a word for a stream. There is no stream. There are oval black pools caught in root-hollows, a few centimetres deep, tea-dark and perfectly still, replenished by condensation that beads all morning on the undersides of leaves and falls, irregularly, audibly, long after any rain has stopped. Bootprints fill and hold. A hollow in a nurse-log holds. The rest of the floor is simply damp through and through — leaf-mould over root-mat over more leaf-mould, metres of plant matter laid down and rotted and laid down again, until if you tried to dig for soil you would still be digging through vegetation, living, dead, and in-between, long past your wrist. You cannot touch mineral earth here. There is no stone to touch. The substrate *is* plant: root under moss under fern under fallen leaf under seedling beech, growing out of a prostrate giant that fell before anyone living was born and is now just a long green ridge, soft as a mattress, sprouting toadstools and young holly from its spine.

The centre, though, is empty.

That is the strange proportion of it. For all that totality of growth at every edge and under every footfall, the middle of the grove is a clearing with nothing in it — a lens of bare, compressed leaf-litter, thirty paces wide, no sapling, no bramble, no shrine, no stump. Just air, in a tall column, between widely spaced presences. The beeches hold the walls. The canopy holds the roof. The floor holds its breath. Deer come in at night and browse any shoot that tries the centre and lie there in the day, flattening it further; the shade does the rest. You can stand in the middle and feel oddly exposed, as if on a stage, or on the floor of a drained millpond.

And it is loud. Astonishingly, unceasingly loud for an empty place.

The hollowness works like a drum. A rookery has occupied the upper crowns for longer than the parish records go back — the same nests, repaired into great black baskets, generation upon generation — and their arguing, cawing, bill-snapping chorus drops straight down the empty shaft and doubles off the trunks. Woodpeckers work the dead limbs and sound, in that acoustic, exactly like rivet guns. Jays scream. In the root pools, in season, frogs set up a pulsing, mechanical sawing that goes on for hours. Woodlice, beetles, slugs, shrews, deer mice move through the litter at the edges in numbers you mostly hear rather than see: rustling, snapping, scratching, the soft thud of a falling beechmast. An owl lives in the split trunk on the north side. Bats come out over the clearing at dusk and take midges in the last of the light.

Then, at noon, the engine starts.

It sits at the lowest edge of the hollow, where the ring of roots dips, and it is the only made thing there: a cast-iron drainage pump, industrial-green once, now mostly moss and rust, bolted to a low pad that the roots are slowly lifting and splitting. No shed, no fence, no building of any kind — just the pump housing, a diesel block, a flywheel, a fuel tank on iron legs sunk into the mat, and two pipes, one vanishing down among the roots into black water, one running out flat under the leaves toward the fields beyond. A forester comes in twice a year with a can and a wrench, cuts the ivy back from the vents and the exhaust, and leaves. The rest of the time it runs on its timer, as it has run for decades, chugging up to speed with a smell of hot oil cutting through the leaf-smell, settling into a slow, heavy, inescapable beat that you feel in the soles of your boots and in the surface of the root-pools, which tremble in concentric rings.

That is why the place never floods, and why it never dries. The pump keeps the water exactly at dampness — draws the pockets down in wet winters, rests in droughts — and the grove has settled around that rhythm the way it settled around the rookery: the frogs time nothing to it, the rooks shout over it, a heron stalks the edge of it entirely unbothered. Engine, bird, insect and drip braid into one continuous factory-floor roar, and in the empty centre you have to raise your voice to be heard by someone standing quite close.

Nothing here changes, underneath the obvious turning of the year. Leaves fall and the floor rises a fraction and rots down a fraction. A limb comes down every few winters and becomes a ridge and becomes soil-that-is-not-soil. A beech dies standing and is replaced, over fifty years, by a beech that was waiting in its shade. The rookery thins and thickens back. The pump is repaired with parts from its own dead predecessor, which lies half-sunk beside it, indistinguishable now from another fallen trunk except for the shape of a gear still showing through the moss like a fossil. The land has reached its final form — flattened, shaded, water-held, plant-covered — and all the processes in it are cycles returning to the same point: drip, rot, sprout, browse, chug, caw.

Stand in the middle long enough and your clothes go cool and faintly damp without ever getting wet. A drop falls from somewhere high and lands, too loudly, beside you. The flywheel turns. A rook crosses the column of air overhead, black against ordinary bright sky, calling once.

There is nothing else to arrive. This is the whole place, and it is complete.
