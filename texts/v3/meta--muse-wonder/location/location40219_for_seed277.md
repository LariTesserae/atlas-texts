There is no dawn here in any useful sense.

The land is a high plateau so vast that the horizon is not a line but a fading. You can walk for a month across its central plain and still be in the same weather system, still at the same altitude, still hearing the same almost-nothing. Above the surrounding lowlands — far below, hidden under a permanent sea of cloud — the air should be thin and freezing. On the plateau it is warm, close, and dark. A thick, low blanket of steam-cloud traps the heat coming up from the ground and from everything moving slowly across it, and lets almost no starlight through. What light there is comes in a wet grey diffusion at midday, and from seams, vents, and living things at night.

The rock is old. Basalt, mostly, weathered into broad tables and shallow basins, wind-polished until it has the soft sheen of river stone. In places it is red with iron, in places glass-black, in places crusted white and yellow around places where the earth exhales. There are mesas, but they are worn down to suggestions. There are gullies, but none of them are new. Everything has reached the shape it intends to keep. Even fresh breaks — and there are fresh breaks — round off within a season under the constant damp and the scouring grit-wind.

Water does not gather here, it visits. There are no true rivers on the high surface. Rain falls out of the interior clouds the plateau makes for itself, warm and fine, and is gone into cracks before it can run. In the mornings of the coldest cycle, every stone sweats. Condensation beads on metal and hide and leaf, drips, beads again. The reliable water is in pockets: black crater pools heated from below to bath temperature, lens-shaped and mineral-sour; shallow pans where steam condenses on cold basalt overhangs and rains endlessly in a space no wider than a room. You learn to listen for that private rain. Otherwise your clothes are never quite wet and never quite dry, your boots are always dark at the seams, and the air smells of wet stone even in a drought.

Vegetation holds to those pockets and to warmth. It is sparse, low, and mostly not green. Heat-mats spread over warm ground like spilled ink — dense, leathery tangles in oxblood and charcoal that photosynthesize almost nothing and instead drink infrared and chemical seep. Where a vent breathes steadily they grow thicker, fringed with blind ferns that unroll toward heat the way sunflowers turn. In mineral pans, pale stalks grow in rings, hollow, beaded with condensed water, and click very faintly as they cool. Nothing tall grows. There is nothing to reach for overhead, and the wind would punish it, and the grazers would find it. Life here keeps its head down.

And yet the plateau is abundantly alive. The sparseness is an illusion of distance. Because the country is continental in scale, its populations are enormous; they are just spread across so much warm darkness that an hour can pass with nothing but your own breath and the drip of a pan, and then a herd comes.

They come first as heat. The big grazers — shoulder-high, hairless, their skin patterned with heat-pits like a second set of eyes — move in loose files of hundreds, feeling the ground temperature through their feet, following the shifting warm seams where the mats grow thickest. Smaller things travel with them: darting, long-eared scavengers that live almost entirely in the herds' body-heat; white moths the size of hands that navigate by the electrical weather and settle, pulsing, on warm hides. Blind burrowers turn the soil under the mats. In the crater pools, eyeless fish crowd so thick the water seems to thicken, rising at night to skim insect falls from the steam.

Predators are silent by necessity. The plateau's air is thick and sound dies quickly in it, muffled by damp and distance. Hunting is done by heat and by vibration. Long, low cats with naked tails stalk without a footfall you can hear from ten paces. Swarms of leathery fliers drop out of the cloud ceiling without a cry. Anything that made noise regularly would announce itself to things that hunt by subtler means, and would also disturb the other inhabitants — the ones that were not born here.

There are no buildings on the plateau. No roads, no rails, no fences, no ruins that stay ruined. And there are factories everywhere.

They move.

Crawlers the size of hills, some solitary, some in slow companies of twenty or thirty, cross the plateau on routes that take generations to complete. They have no lights. Their hulls are basalt-dusted, barnacled with heat-mats, scarred and re-scarred. A crawler feeds by lowering its fore-body and planing the surface away — rock, soil, mat, shallow pool and all — taking the bite into itself on conveyors you can feel through the soles of your feet long before you see the machine. Inside, and the inside is the entire point, it sorts, crushes, smelts, refines. Its belly glows in seams, dull orange, like magma seen through cracked earth. It lays nothing down behind it but a trail of finely chewed, mineral-sorted tailings, still warm for days.

They are industrial in a way that makes the word feel too small and too human. Smelters, centrifuges, electrostatic separators, synthesis vats — all of it ambulant, self-repairing, self-powered from geothermal taps they drill as they go and seal as they leave. Their drives are electric and almost silent. A crawler at full work, processing a hillside an hour at a time, makes less audible sound than a campfire. What it makes instead is a pressure in your molars, an infrasonic working rhythm, and a standing charge that lifts the hair on your arms a half-mile away. Loose iron dust aligns along the ground near a passing company. Compasses have never meant anything up here.

Who built them, and for what refined product, and where the product goes — the plateau gives no answer, because the crawlers keep it inside themselves, compacted into dense, dark ingots in internal holds, and because their makers left no depots for them to return to. Perhaps the holds are emptied somewhere off the edge of the high country, once a century. Perhaps they are never emptied anymore. Perhaps the refining has become the purpose, the way grazing became a purpose. It has been going on long enough for evolution to have opinions about it.

The animals have opinions. Cleaner swarms live in the crawlers' intake baffles, eating the mats and parasites that clog them, flushed but unharmed by periodic steam. Grazers follow fresh tailing trails, where crushed mineral and residual heat make the heat-mats bloom in a sudden brief richness — the only true pasture this country manufactures. The big pool-fish spawn in crawler-drill holes that fill with condensed steam. Predators have learned the charge-signatures that mean a crawler is about to vent, and wait at the margins for panicked prey. Some crawlers, older ones, run idiosyncratically warm on one flank, and whole traveling ecosystems have arranged themselves on that asymmetry for decades. An old crawler is less a vehicle than a moving province, with its own microclimate condensing and raining gently along its lee side.

The weather, too, belongs partly to them. The plateau makes its own regardless — vast slow cells of rising warm air that turn under the cloud ceiling, drop their moisture as warm drizzle a hundred miles away, and never involve the lowlands at all — but a company of crawlers on the move generates a following storm, a warm electric murk that trails them for days. Lightning moves inside it without thunder that carries. Rain falls into updrafts over the hottest vents and is torn back into steam before it lands, falling and unfalling in the same column. Standing stones of old basalt hum faintly in wet weather, induced by the charge in the air.

The rules of the place settle into you if you travel it.

You do not navigate by sight. You navigate by temperature underfoot, by the taste of iron, by the direction damp is coming from, by the pressure-rhythm of distant work.

You do not camp by water alone, because water here is always also heat, and everything else has had the same idea.

You do not shelter behind a crawler without understanding which cycle it is in. The warm lee that kept you alive all night is the vent side by morning.

And you do not mistake the quiet for emptiness, or the darkness for rest. At what passes for midnight, when the cloud thins enough for a few hard stars to stand in it, the plateau is at its most active: herds moving, hunters reading the warm earth, mats opening their black fronds, fish rising, and the great dark factories planing another slow mouthful out of the continent, refining it in silence, leaving behind them a warm and fertile scar that the living country is already, patiently, moving in to inhabit.
