It begins far below the place itself, in the thinning.

You climb for three days through country that keeps simplifying. First the pines go, stooped and black. Then the shrubs. Then grass that is not really grass, only a brown stubble clinging between stones. After that there is just stone and a sky that gets darker the higher you go, not brighter. At noon it is indigo overhead. At noon you can see faint stars, if you shade your eyes, though the light on the ground never gets stronger than the hour before dawn.

This is the high table. Not a peak — the peaks wore away a long time ago. What is left at 6,000 meters and more is an old, rounded plateau, twenty kilometers across, worn smooth and then shattered on the surface by cold. Schist and blue-grey slate and granite gone mealy with frost. Everything split along its grain into plates. You walk on broken pottery for days. Each step shifts a shard, and the shard rings, softly, under your boot.

That is the first lie the place tells you. Up here the air is too thin to carry sound well. Your breath comes short and white and does not go far. And yet the table is loud.

Not windy-loud. The air itself is almost still. Flags would hang. Your hair does not move. Breath-frost hangs in front of your face for a long time before it dissipates, as if reluctant.

But there is a roar.

It is low and high at once, a chord pressed into the teeth and the sternum and the small bones behind the jaw. It does not rise or fall. It does not gust. Morning, if morning is the right word for a light that never changes, it is there at the same volume. In the dim perpetual twilight, it is there. It has the quality of a waterfall heard from inside the rock behind it, and of a bell that was struck so long ago it forgot to stop.

It comes, as far as anyone can tell, from the ground.

The plateau is a shallow bowl, very slight, almost imperceptible except that water — what little water there is — finds its way inward, not outward. And all its surfaces curve, subtly. The scree plates tilt inward. The long undulations of bedrock tilt inward. It is a dish made to collect. Not water. Sound. An ear turned up to the sky, hollow underneath. Old travelers used to knock on certain outcrops and hear a booming emptiness beneath, a second, larger space under the stone where the roar lives and circulates without ever escaping.

You cannot talk here in any normal way. A shout at ten paces arrives shredded, folded back on itself. Not echoed — *returned*. You shout your name and what comes back out of the bowl, a breath before your lips have quite closed, is your name pronounced correctly but in a voice that has learned it by listening to you your whole life. Whispers do worse. Whispering is absorbed entirely. You feel the other person’s mouth move against your ear and hear only the roar making room for it, the way water makes room for a stone.

So the few people who cross here have always crossed in silence, packed wool and greased fur into their ears, and spoken by touch: a hand flat on a flat rock, tapping. The rock carries it cleanly. The rock is honest. The air is not.

The cold is a constant, too. Not a killing spike, not a storm cold. Minus ten, minus thirteen, forever. It never warms at midday because midday does not arrive. The sun is a small, white, heatless coin that crawls along behind the southern rim and never clears it, throwing the whole table into permanent shadow lit from the edge. Shadows therefore fall long and northward and do not swing. You can walk beside your own shadow for a whole day's march and it will keep pace, unchanged in length, like a companion animal.

Nothing here is in a hurry to become something else. Lichen — black, orange gone grey, a pale sulphur crust only millimeters thick — paints the lee side of boulders in patterns that have taken centuries to get that far and have now, apparently, stopped. Pools in the hollows neither rise nor shrink. They are not fed by rain. It almost never rains; when moisture comes it comes as rime, as a dampness beading directly out of the vibrating air onto cold stone in the loudest parts of the bowl, so that rocks sweat in the cold and your sleeves grow dark at the cuff for no reason you can feel. The pools are black, lens-thin, skin of ice at the edge and open, improbably unfrozen water at the center where the vibration keeps it just disturbed enough. Put your hand in and it is colder than ice and leaves the skin feeling scalded.

Condensation does odd things in that sound. Breath does not disperse properly. Exhale on a still crossing and the cloud stays, holds its shape a little too long, a soft white lungful hovering at head height. The trails across the plateau are marked not just by footprints in the frost — the frost never melts to erase them, so decades of crossings show as a braided dark line — but by a faint, intermittent fog at face level, breath after breath left behind, a dotted line of everyone who has panted their way across. Some of them are very old. Some of them hold, for a second, if you are tired and altitude-sick and not to be trusted, the curve of a mouth.

Vegetation wants the quiet, such as there is. Nothing grows where the roar strikes full. Where a cairn or a windbreak throws a shadow — not a light-shadow, a *sound-shadow* — behind it there will be a cushion of something stubborn: moss campion gone almost white, tiny five-petaled flowers the color of bone, a grass that grows in a tight spiral flat to the stone. It grows in commas, in the punctuation behind stones. Everywhere else the plates are bare.

Animals are rarer still, and they have adapted by refusing the air. Snow finches nest in the chinks of the cairns and feed their young beak-to-beak without a single call; the chicks gape in silence, throats pulsing. Once or twice a season a lammergeier crosses the rim, enormous and utterly mute here though it will scream once it drops to the valleys, and its shadow moves across the scree. It does not flap. It does not need to. Things fall strangely in the bowl. A dropped stone tool goes down too slowly and a little sideways, as if considering. The bird seems to know how to lean on that consideration. Ibex come up in the poor years, gaunt, deaf from birth it appears, their hooves wrapped by nature in soft felted pads. They stand for hours, not grazing — there is almost nothing to graze — just standing with their heads low, feeling the ground through their feet. Then they leave.

The built things are few and far between and all made of the place itself, since there is nothing to bring up here but stone. Dry-laid windbreaks, waist high, curved like a cupped hand, with no roof and never meant to have one. Inside them the roar lessens by a fraction, enough to sleep if your ears are plugged and your face is covered, enough to dream without words. Cairns of stacked plates, some taller than a person, some fallen in a way that suggests they did not fall but were laid down. A single hut near the eastern crossing with thick walls and no door — you enter over the wall by a step of stone, because a doorway, apparently, lets in something its builders didn't want. Its floor is worn in two hollows where generations of sleepers have lain with their ears to the ground.

No metal survives a telling up here. No one carries instruments. This is before tools, or after the need for them. Even a knife feels like an impertinence.

The further in you go, the more exact the wrongness becomes. Distances stop cooperating. A cairn that looks a hundred meters off will take until your legs shake to reach; the next, which looked a day away behind it, is suddenly under your hand when you look up, so close you smell the cold lichen on it. It is not mist doing it — the air is brutally clear, you can see the far rim picked out plate by plate. It is that seeing does not measure here. Only walking measures, and walking lies in the opposite direction.

Time does something similar, which is to say, it declines. Hunger comes and then forgets to grow. A cut stops bleeding at a bright, wet stage and stays that way, neither clotting nor flowing, until you descend. Fire, on the rare occasions anyone coaxes one from brought fuel in a windbreak, burns with a perfectly steady flame, upright in the still air, and produces a smoke that rises a body's height and then stops and spreads out flat, as if it had hit a ceiling none of you can see.

And in the true center of the bowl, where the inward tilt of everything finally resolves, there is a ring of seven standing slabs, unworked except for having been stood up. The roar is not louder there. That is the worst part. Inside the ring it stops.

Not fades. Stops, at the threshold, as cleanly as stepping indoors and closing a door, except there is no door and the roar continues visibly — you can see the scree dust outside the ring trembling on the plates, you can see your companion outside shouting and the breath bursting from him. In the center is a silence so complete you hear your blood and then, after a moment, you stop hearing even that properly, because your ears keep reaching for the sound that has held them for days and finding nothing to hold.

The pool in the middle of the ring does not reflect the sky. Everyone agrees on this afterward, though they disagree on what it does reflect. A brighter sky. A greener slope. The same ring with no one in it. The same ring with everyone in it lying down. It reflects, slowly, as if the water were very deep and the light had a long way to travel up. It is the only thing here that changes, and it may only be change seen too slowly for the place to have noticed and frozen it.

You do not stay in the ring long. No rule is written, nothing is carved — there is no carving anywhere on the table, not a mark, which after days of cairns and windbreaks begins to feel like a decision. But the body decides. The silence becomes a pressure opposite to the roar’s pressure. People leave it walking backward. They put the wool back in their ears with relief, like coming up into air.

Outside the ring again, the roar receives you without comment, exactly as loud as it was, exactly in tune. It will not have altered when the next crossing comes, in a year or in fifty. The finches will be gaping silently in the cairns. The breath-fog will hang at face height along the trail. The sun will be behind the rim, the shadows long and northward, the pools beaded with their impossible damp, the plates ringing faintly under each step and adding that ring, somehow without swelling, to the chord that never ends —

the mountain holding a single note, in cold and dimness, at the top of the world, long after it has forgotten what struck it.
