It is a bowl high enough that your breath never quite warms.

You come into it over a lip of new rock — black, glassy at the edges, still sharp enough to cut rope and boot leather. The mountain has not finished happening here. Nothing has been worn round. The rim is a serration of unweathered spines and split slabs, and below it the ground drops in one steep, green amphitheater to a flat no larger than a village green.

Sunlight does not properly enter. The bowl faces away from the sun, and the rim steals what is left. For perhaps an hour at midday a pale column leans in and touches the far wall, but mostly the light is a cool, even dimness, as if the whole place were under deep water or inside a cathedral whose windows have been covered with moss. You can see well enough. Colors are just slowed down: greens going almost black, stone going blue, white feathers luminous in the gloom.

And it is green beyond reason for so high and dry a place.

There is no river. No spring in the ordinary sense. No lake holding the sky. What water there is arrives sideways, at night, as fog.

After dark a cold mist pours over the eastern rim and falls, literally falls, in slow curtains down the inner wall. It pools. It condenses on every cold surface. By morning the leaves are beaded, the roofs drip in a patient percussion that is already over by the time the light strengthens, and the stone channels cut into every street run bright for half an hour and then are only damp. Then thirst for the rest of the day.

So the plants have learned to drink from air. Enormous rhubarb-like leaves, furred underneath, tilt to catch the fog and funnel it to their hearts. Ferns unroll in black thickets taller than a person, their fronds beaded at every tip. Moss does not carpet the ground so much as deepen it — knee-deep in the unwalked places, swallowing fallen stone, muffling everything. There are hanging gardens on the bowl wall where roots have found cracks in the young rock, and small, waxy white flowers that only open when the mist is actually touching them. They close, with a faint collective movement, when it lifts.

In the bottom of the bowl, someone has built a city in miniature.

Not a camp. A city. With districts and intentions.

Terraced streets climb the lower slopes in switchbacks, paved with fitted stone and edged with dew-gutters. There are gatehouses, market squares, a council hall with a slate roof, cistern courts, granaries, bathhouses fed by carved runnels, high walkways and rope bridges between one terrace and the next, alleys that turn twice for no reason except that the ground moved after they were laid. Houses crowd wall-to-wall in one quarter with upper stories overhanging the lane, and stand utterly alone in another, each in its own empty court.

The building is layered and stubbornly organized and entirely without metal to speak of. Door hinges are wood. Nails are pegs. Roofs are lashed, tools are polished stone and bone and the occasional treasured blade of hammered copper gone green. Long frames like loom-sails stand above the houses — woven nets of plant fiber and hair, turned by hand each evening to face the fog. In the morning people wring them into troughs like washing.

The people are sparse. You might cross three squares and see one person, wrapped in undyed wool, carrying a wooden bucket without a splash or a clink, because the bucket is lined with felt. A second, older, scraping moss from a gutter with a stone flake. A child, maybe, sitting perfectly still on a wall.

The animals are not sparse.

The animals are overwhelming, and that is the first true adjustment your mind has to make: the city is inhabited, densely, continuously, urgently inhabited — just not primarily by humans.

Every roof is a rookery. Not birds on a roof, but roofs built under layers of nests, season upon season, so the slate is only a rumor under guano and woven grass and shed fur. The big, ash-grey mountain rooks live there, and they walk the chimneys like citizens. Swifts occupy the eaves in thousands, packed in the dim like a second, breathing thatch. By day they hang motionless. What should be a deafening colony makes almost no sound at all.

Hairy moths the size of two hands cling to the lit side of nothing — to doorposts, wool blankets hung to dampen, the undersides of bridges. Frogs, improbably, live in every cistern and leaf-heart, cool-skinned, speckled green and black, inflating and deflating without croaking. Beetles move in slow processions along the dew-gutters, drinking in turn, antennae first, never jostling. In the granary district, fat tailless rodents with blunt faces use the side channels built, seemingly, exactly for them. They pause at intersections. They wait. One side empties before the other proceeds.

Higher up the terraces the houses get lower doors and wider thresholds, and the beds inside are stone hollows lined with bracken. This is where the long-legged, goat-like cliff grazers come down at fog-fall to give birth, picking their way through streets they clearly know, their hooves wrapped — uncannily — in pads of shed winter coat they grow for this purpose and lose afterward. A mother will stand for an hour in a market square, steaming faintly in the cold, while her newborn learns its legs on the paving. People give them the square. People always give them the square.

And there are owls, white-faced, nearly wingless by daylight because they keep their great wings folded and walk, solemn and upright, from shadow to shadow under the walkways. There are blind pink lizards in the bathhouse walls. There are bats in the council hall, where they hang above the long empty table and shift, occasionally, like a ceiling thinking. There are tiny, translucent spiders who rebuild the fog-nets the humans break, faster than the humans can break them, so that some nets now belong to no one and are harvested by both.

It is quiet in a way that makes your own body seem badly designed. Your breath is loud. Your joints are loud. The rule here — unwritten, because even carving might carry — is understood by every species present: do not startle the slope.

The young rock is still creeping. The whole bowl is slumping on its own talus at the speed of a fingernail growing, which means a house is not quite where it was last year and a street can develop, over a wet month, a fresh crack with ferns already ambitious in it. A sharp report, a shout, a dropped beam can bring down a fin of stone from the rim. So the rooks do not caw. The grazers do not bleat, even in labor; they exhale, hard, through their noses. The frogs conduct their whole courtship in pulses visible in their throats. The owls hunt by listening for things that have failed to learn silence. The humans speak, when they must, with their mouths near ears, or in a soft hand-language that the older rooks, disquietingly, appear to follow — heads cocked, one eye on the fingers.

Yet for all its quiet, the place is furiously in motion.

Fog-fall is a feeding frenzy conducted at a whisper. Flowers snap open, moths unfold and drink, swiftlets pour from the eaves in a single held breath of wings that makes the air thrum against your teeth but produces no cry. At the leaf-hearts, lines form: beetle, frog, spider, bird, each taking the stored dew in some ancient rotation that breaks down only when a newcomer arrives and has to be taught, usually by being bodily, gently, repeatedly moved to the back.

Predation is constant and nearly invisible. A stillness that was a moth is gone. A procession of beetles is two shorter. An owl is walking where it wasn't, swallowing. By dawn there are neat piles of wings under the walkways, sorted, it seems, by color — though whether that is the owls, the children, or the wind in the alleys no one will tell you aloud.

Generations move through faster than the streets can be repaired. The fern thickets can colonize a collapsed house between one fog season and the next. The grazers arrive, birth, and are gone upslope within weeks, leaving the low-doored houses to the rodents, who leave them to the lizards when the cold deepens. The human families practice a slow, contrary migration of their own: building new houses a few paces upslope as the old ones creep down, abandoning the lowest terraces year by year to a dense, contented tenancy of animals who prefer the older, damper walls. At the very bottom of the bowl the oldest quarter is human only in architecture. Doors stand open on rooms full of nests, burrows, hanging bats, black moss. A former market cross is white with rook lime and mineral drip. It is still swept. Someone sweeps the empty, animal city every morning, silently, with a broom of fern.

Perhaps that is what the place is: not a human town with too many animals in it, but a set of arrangements — streets, nets, cisterns, thresholds, silences — maintained by whoever currently has hands, for the benefit of whoever arrives next on wings or hooves to use them.

Stay through a fog-fall and you will stop counting species. You will stop, in fact, counting individuals, because at some point the bowl stops reading as a population and starts reading as a single fabric: leaf, feather, stone, fur, woven net, beaded water, all of it taking its turn at the damp in near-darkness, all of it moving, feeding, birthing, dying, rebuilding, while the young mountain shifts almost imperceptibly under everything, and the houses, full and empty alike, continue their slow, shared journey down.
