It begins long before you reach the city, with the ground giving up on being ground.

The train climbs for hours out of a valley that no longer exists on any new map, through switchbacks cut into hills so old and worn down there is nothing sharp left to cut. No peaks. No cliffs worth the name. Just vast, rounded swells of stone at altitude, flattened by an amount of time that makes human history feel like a weather event, and over everything — over stone, over rails, over the sleepers, over the lower slopes and the high plain they finally open onto — the Mat.

That is what they call it. It is not grass, though in places it is low and springy and grey-green like alpine turf. It is not forest, though in places it rears up in slow, jointed trunks thirty feet high, hollow, ribbed, knocking together in the wind. It is one continuous vegetation, and there is no place it is not.

At this height — thin air, cool even at noon, a brightness of light that makes every color look freshly washed — bare earth is a rumor. The plateau rolls away to a pale horizon, town-sized, region-sized, and the surface is leaf, root, lichen, moss, stem. In the hollows it is spongy and dark and holds water like a squeezed cloth. On the rises it is dry, curled, silvered with frost that does not quite melt. It sighs underfoot. Where the train track has been laid, men with saws have to ride ahead of the locomotive in an open car, cutting the track clear again from the morning's growth, throwing back armfuls of pale fiber that are already twitching toward the rails.

Because it grows back. Always. And it grows back faster in silence.

That is why Ternwick is loud.

You hear the city before you see its buildings. First the fog-fans on the rim, enormous industrial rotors turning day and night in frames of riveted iron and resin-bone, pulling the cloud that snags each evening on the plateau edge through acres of cold mesh, wringing damp out of mere air. There is no river up here. There has not been one for thousands of years. Water comes as condensation, as dew beading on leaf-surfaces at dawn, as the Mat's own sap pressed out in the Squeeze Works and stored, greenish and faintly sweet, in tanks that sweat perpetually in the cool. Then the pruning engines take up the sound, and the loom-sheds, and the bells.

Every street has a bell, or a klaxon, or a boy with a hammer and a length of pipe whose whole job is to keep a rhythm going. The factories have it easiest: the fiber looms in the Loom Quarter never stop, clacking and thumping through shifts, weaving root-cloth tough enough for roofs and hulls and winter coats. The sawmills shriek where they take the Mat's thicker trunks, which bleed a milky latex and give off, when cut, a smell like hot copper and crushed leaves. Chimneys stand in a ragged line along the northern district, but even their brick is furred, capped, infiltrated; green works up every mortar joint so fast the stokers paint the lower courses with tar and lime, and still by week's end a chimney will have put out exploratory leaves at its rim, testing the smoke.

Between all this industry, there is an astonishing amount of empty space.

Ternwick is urban the way an archipelago is land. Districts are dense — five-storey tenements with walls of woven living hedge over iron frames, balconies stitched with flowering bindweed, roofs of turf so thick that goats would be useful if there were goats — and then abruptly the district ends, not with a wall but with a hundred yards of low Mat, humming, undisturbed except for a raised causeway of compressed root-fiber and a line of noisemakers on poles. Then another district, a half mile off, in its own shallow bowl: the Condenser Rows, long terraces of glass and mesh. The Market Crown, where buildings have grown so entangled with an older thicket that it is impossible to say which beams are timber and which are still alive and putting out shoots through the floorboards. The administrative hill, if you can call anything here a hill, where surveyors work.

Surveyors are important, because the city moves.

Nothing is built on stone. No one has seen the stone under Ternwick in living memory; boreholes go down forty feet through root and humus and compacted centuries of leaf-fall and come up still chewing vegetation. So houses are staked, not founded — pinned with long iron augurs into the great root-crowns, lashed, flexible. And the Mat grows, perhaps an inch a year in this cold, perhaps three, unevenly, swelling in one quarter and subsiding in another, and the whole district rides that slow vegetable tide. A street that ran due east ten years ago now runs east-northeast and is fourteen yards south of where the map had it. Addresses are given with dates. Surveyors go out with theodolites and with listening rods, ear to the Mat, because you can hear the main roots groaning under strain weeks before a subsidence, a low note in the general loudness of the place, like a ship's hull deciding something.

Animals have mostly declined to participate. Above the treeline and into this — there is almost nothing with blood. A few rooks keep to the chimneys, black against the bright sky, and make a racket that fits right in. Glass houses at the pollen works maintain moths by the thousand, the only reliable pollinators for the seed-crops, and they are tended like machinery and counted like currency. Sometimes a hare is seen at the plateau edge, its fur stained green all summer from sleeping in the Mat, and the sighting makes the local paper. What the Mat thinks of animals is unclear. Things that fall asleep out on it, unprotected, have been found hours later neatly netted over with new tendrils, not crushed, not eaten exactly, just incorporated. It is patient about it.

That patience is the oldest thing here. The scientists who come up on the train, short of breath for their first week, take core samples and get quieter and quieter as the samples get older. The Mat in its present form is not a forest that colonized an eroded plateau. It appears to be what erosion left *for*. Millions of years of it, one clonal, continent-minded organism simplifying a mountain range down to a smooth table it could lie across in the sun. The city is a recent itch on it.

And the Mat, lately, has started doing imitations.

Everyone knows the ordinary weirdness and prices it in. That a path cut on Monday will, if not walked and shouted along, be ankle-deep by Friday. That sounds carry wrong across the low ground — you will hear the loom-sheds' evening whistle clearly from a mile off on a still day, and then hear it again, fainter, the next morning at the same hour, though the sheds were down for repairs; the Mat holds sound in its hollow stems and gives it back when the temperature changes. That in spore season, which comes on damp cool nights after the fans have been running hard, you should keep to the lit causeways, because the fine gold dust off the high trunks makes distances unreliable, and people have walked an hour toward a district whose lights never got any closer, and come out at dawn on the same causeway, having aged, by their own accounts, a very ordinary night and also, somehow, most of a disappointing decade.

But the imitations are newer. A tenement burns in the Loom Quarter — a tar fire, put out in an hour — and three weeks later, out on the empty Mat beyond the pruning line, tendrils have raised the same building. Not a building. Its shape. Doorways, the pitch of the roof, the exact lopsided chimney, done in woven green at quarter scale, hollow, and when the wind goes through its window-holes it makes the small domestic noises of a house: a creak, something like crockery. Demolished warehouses reappear as thickets. Last year the Mat grew a train on a disused spur, root over root over root, with wheels of knotted wood that will not turn and a funnel putting out, once, a puff of spores in the late afternoon.

No one cuts the imitations down. Partly superstition, partly the surveyors' new theory that the Mat is not copying the city so much as remembering it forward — that given enough decades of looms and bells and fog-fans, the organism has learned the city as one of its own seasonal shapes, like flowering. That Ternwick is becoming something the Mat *does*, annually, with or without the people.

So the noise is maintained, even by those who laugh at the theory. Especially at night, when the cool comes down fast and the whole plateau smells of rain that is not falling, of iron, of sap, and the clouds sit below the rim in a white sea all around the island of the world. The fans slow but do not stop. The bells in the empty streets between districts keep their uneven, human tempos. In the houses staked to the roots, you can feel the building make its tiny nocturnal adjustment, a settling, a growth-spurt somewhere below the floor, and hear, through the sole of the floor, the vast slow body underneath doing whatever it does in the dark — drinking dew, knitting over the day's cuts, practicing, perhaps, the shape of a chimney.

People sleep well up here, mostly. They are used to being a loud, brief, well-lit infestation on something ancient that has begun, politely, to learn them by heart.
