It is a town, unmistakably, but a small one held high and held in place — sixty or seventy roofs on a saddle of grey rock, and nothing beyond them. You can walk it end to end in seven minutes if you do not stop. If you do stop, which the place seems to invite, it takes much longer, because there is so much empty stone to cross between one door and the next.

The town was laid out, not grown by accident. There is a square at the center, paved with broad flagstones worn concave by feet. Around it, in an order that still makes sense after a very long time, stand the communal buildings: a bakehouse with a low, wide chimney, a granary raised on stone piers, a meeting hall with a slate roof and small windows, a covered cistern-house where the air inside is always cold and damp. Lanes run out from the square in four directions, also paved, also worn. Houses follow them, terraced into the slope so that the roof of a lower house is level with the doorstep of the one above it. The construction is layered in that practical way — older, rougher stone at the bottom of walls, newer, squarer stone above it, wooden lintels replaced and replaced again over doorways, slate laid over slate where a roof has been mended.

But it is not crowded, for all its organization. Between houses are yards, walled gardens of almost bare earth, empty plots kept swept, stretches of open paving where nothing is built at all. A house, then twenty paces of quiet, then another house. The town breathes through those gaps. From any doorway you can see out between the buildings to the drop beyond, to pale grass and rock going down for a long way, and sometimes, in the morning, to cloud lying below the saddle so that the town seems briefly detached from the valley.

The elevation explains the rest. The air is cool even under a normal, clear sun. Light here at midday is ordinary daylight, but sharper, whiter at the edges, throwing hard shadows off eaves and wall corners. Shade is genuinely cold. You keep a wool layer on until noon and put it on again before supper. Chimneys smoke thinly in the morning — woodsmoke, damp wool, crushed herbs, hot stone when rain has passed — those are the smells, faint and quickly dispersed.

There is no river. There is no spring anyone would call generous. Water arrives intermittently and is treated accordingly. Rain comes in brief, slanting episodes, rattling on slate, running in bright threads down the lanes along shallow channels cut for exactly that purpose, and disappearing into grates. At night, condensation does the quieter work. Roof-slopes and a set of tilted stone slabs behind the cistern-house bead with dew in the cool air; by dawn there is a slow, irregular dripping everywhere, into barrels, into stone troughs, into the covered cisterns that set the true size of the town. Every house has its catchment. Every house knows what it holds. That, more than the low outer wall, is the boundary. No new house can be raised unless its roof can feed its people, and so the number of roofs has not changed in living memory, or in the memory passed down before that.

Vegetation submits to the same economy. Thin soil, stone, cool wind. Tufts of wiry grass force themselves between flags and are left alone unless they block a drain. Moss collects only on north faces and in the permanently damp corners by cistern mouths. In the walled gardens people grow hard, low things — onions, beans in a sheltered row, bitter greens, herbs with small leaves that smell stronger for growing slowly. There are no trees in the town proper, only two stunted pines by the lower gate, bent in the same direction, old enough that no one claims to know whether they are still growing. Lichen does better than anything green: pale grey and dull orange rosettes on roofs and walls, spreading at a pace that makes the buildings look settled into their own age rather than decaying. Nothing here is crumbling quickly. Erosion has already done its early work and arrived at an agreement. Steps are dished, thresholds are smooth, corners are rounded, and then they stay that way, year after year, only polished further.

The technology matches the stone: hand tools, simple iron, wood cut and fitted by eye. A smith works two lanes from the square, not often, mending hinges, sharpening chisels, shoeing the few pack animals. You hear him as a clear, spaced ringing that carries and then stops. Looms clack behind shutters. Stone is still split with wedges the way the first walls were built. Repairs are constant but invisible in principle — a slate replaced with a slate of the same thickness, a beam swapped for one adzed to the same profile. The town is maintained into sameness.

Animals are correspondingly rare. Swifts nest under a few eaves in the warm season and are gone the rest of the year. A raven passes over the square most days, alone. Lizards appear on sun-warmed walls at noon and vanish into mortar cracks if a shadow crosses them. Goats are kept, but downslope in a stone pen below the saddle, and their bells are only sometimes audible, small and distant, when the wind is right. Inside the walls, an occasional cat, a few patient dogs asleep in doorways out of the through-draft. For long stretches there is no animal sound at all.

That quiet is the dominant condition. Mostly it is not silence, exactly, but a low, spaced arrangement of sounds over stone: the morning drip into cisterns, a shutter unlatched, footsteps on flags, a mallet tapping somewhere, voices in the square that do not carry far because people here do not need to raise them. No machinery, no traffic, no river-noise to fill the background. Wind moves around corners and over slate with a soft scouring sound, then drops. Midday can be so still that you hear cloth being shaken out two lanes away.

Nothing is poised to transform. The population replaces itself. Children learn the same catchment calculations, the same roof-mending, the same order of work before the damp season — clear the channels, check the slabs, lime the cistern, stack wood under cover. A house empties and another family moves in. A wall is rebuilt on its own foundation line. The seasons do pass, cooler and less cool, wetter and drier, swifts arriving and leaving, but the pattern they pass through does not widen. The town is not frozen, it is balanced: water in against water used, stone out of the hill against stone returned to the wall, people born against people buried in the shallow, stony ground by the pines.

Standing in the square in late afternoon, with the light normal and level and the air already cooling, the sum of it is not grandeur and not desolation. It is proportion. A complete, urban little world — square, granary, bakehouse, lanes, drains, rules — built sparsely on bare high ground, with empty stone between its parts and damp gathering quietly overnight to keep it alive another day, exactly as it did the day before.
