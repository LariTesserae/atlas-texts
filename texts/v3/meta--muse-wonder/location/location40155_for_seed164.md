It begins as a cliff that will not end.

You come up to it from below — from sea-level weather, from ordinary rain — and the rock keeps rising past the clouds and then keeps rising after the clouds have thinned away. It is not a mountain. Mountains have slopes and summits and a logic of up. This is a slab of continent that has been lifted whole, four kilometers into cold air, and left there, so recently, in geological terms, that it still has the sharpness of the break.

The edge is black basalt, glassy in places, splintered in others, unsoftened. No scree has had time to accumulate. No river has had time to cut a proper valley. Water does not descend the face in falls so much as weep out of it at a thousand levels — thin bright threads emerging from cracks that were sealed last week and will be sealed again, mineral water condensing inside the stone and forced out by the weight of the plateau itself. In the morning sun — and the light up here is ordinary, startlingly ordinary, a clean white daylight only a fraction brighter for the thin air — the whole escarpment shines as if it has just rained, though it is always just about to rain and it always just has.

Turn away from the edge, inland, and the scale stops being comprehensible in the usual way.

The plateau continues. Not for fifty kilometers. For thousands. It is large enough to have mislaid its own horizon. The center is a weather system unto itself, no longer influenced in any useful way by the ocean below. Winds that climb the southern cliff arrive, spend weeks crossing, and arrive at the northern cliff as something else entirely, if they arrive at all. Distances up here stop being distances and become climates.

And everywhere, improbably, in the cold, it is lush.

Not forest. There are no trees, and there never have been. There is no animal life at all — no insects, no birds, no worms in the soil, nothing that has ever bitten or burrowed or pollinated. What covers the fresh rock is a world of plants that evolved, or were left, to do without.

Moss a meter thick in the hollows, so dense it holds the shape of a footprint for a season. Liverworts spreading in iridescent sheets over standing water. Ferns no taller than your knee, their fronds perfect and unscarred, every leaflet intact for its entire life, because nothing eats them. In the wetter places, columns of bundled moss rise like soft green chimneys, hollow inside, dripping constantly, slowly accreting upward until they reach a height — always the same height — and stop, shedding from the base exactly as fast as they grow from the rim. Black-leaved rosettes in the exposed places, nearly black to drink the thin sunlight, their cells full of a clear antifreeze sap that tastes, if the old survey notes are to be believed, of bitter sugar. At night they close. At dawn they open to the precise same angle.

The ground is mostly marsh-edge. Not deep marsh, not dry land, but a continuous in-between maintained for a continent: black soil through which water moves a finger's width below the surface, shallow pools linked by slow seepage, stone that sweats by noon even when no rain has fallen. The air itself condenses on anything cold — which, up here, is most things. Railings grow beards of droplets. Leaves carry a permanent load of dew equal to their own weight; it runs off in slow pulses and is immediately replaced.

Then, after a hundred kilometers of this — moss, pool, fern, sharp uneaten green, utter biological silence — there is a city street.

It is paved. It has curbs, drainage grates, lamp posts, numbered signs. It runs perfectly straight to the horizon in both directions, crosses a bridge over a seeping black creek, passes a row of apartment blocks four stories high with lit windows and balconied planters already full of ferns, and continues out the other side into moss again, empty for another hundred kilometers.

This is not a ruin. This is the city, all of it, distributed.

The plateau is one urban system with a continental footprint and almost nothing in it. Density stats would lie about it in either direction: sum its built area and you have a metropolis; stand anywhere in it and you are almost certainly alone in a wet green plain with a single factory tower, or a switching yard, or a block of silent housing, somewhere at the limit of visibility. Between presences there is vast, deliberate space.

The nodes are industrial. Condenser plants that harvest the plateau's own fog on arrays of cold fins and send the water whispering down into cisterns. Turbine halls venting white vapor that immediately becomes more fog. Smelter stacks built of fresh riveted steel, unrusted — the cold, thin, dustless air up here does not weather things at any honest rate, and what does wear is replaced, part for identical part, by autonomous cranes working from template. Electrified freight lines run on embankments through the marsh, single track, perfectly level, the rails perpetually wet and shining. Power lines march plant to plant, sagging between pylons in catenaries that repeat, without variation, for thousands of identical spans.

And it is loud. That is the second thing you notice after the silence of the plants: there is no quiet anywhere.

Partly it is the machines — turbines holding a note just below pain, pumps, the slow percussion of presses in open-sided sheds, the shriek of a freight train braking somewhere beyond sight. Partly it is wind forced through the fresh, sharp topography, and through the pipework and cooling towers and empty apartment stairwells, producing whole chords that hang in the air. Partly it is water, falling from condenser fins in metallic rain that never stops, drumming on roofs built for it.

But chiefly it is strange because the loudness does not diminish. The plateau is capped, most days, by a level sheet of fog at a fixed height, hard as a ceiling to the eye, and that ceiling ducts sound sideways. The roar of a plant is the same at five kilometers as at fifty. Stand in the remotest moss waste, no structure in sight but a single pipeline on posts vanishing both ways, and the air is still full of machinery — a uniform, directionless industrial thunder, loud enough to raise your voice over, identical in character everywhere. The plateau has, in effect, one continuous interior, acoustically, though visually it is mostly emptiness.

There are standing storms. Because the wind never varies in direction or force at the large scale, certain clouds have been in certain places, as far as anyone can establish, forever. A dark anvil hangs permanently over the smelter district at the center-south, raining on the same ten square kilometers of fern marsh, raining out exactly the water its own stacks evaporated that morning. There is a band of perpetual drizzle a third of the way in from the eastern cliff, a waterfall with no top, slanting slightly — always slightly — toward the plateau's center, as if rain up here falls not quite down. The mass of the slab is enough, at this scale, to tug gently on things. Plumb lines disagree with the horizon by just enough to make the outer districts feel subtly tilted when you try to sleep in the empty, furnished, never-occupied apartments the city builds and maintains and cleans and heats for no one.

No one has ever lived here. The builders left before occupancy, or the city is the builder — the distinction stopped being useful when the replacement cranes began replacing the replacement cranes to the same specification. It lays streets because streets are in the plan. It plants, waters, and lights balconies because balconies are in the plan. Machine tenders move through the plant halls on rails, oiling bearings that would otherwise seize, clearing fern spores from condenser fins — the spores are the city's one recurring problem, the plants having learned to use the cooling towers the way cliff plants use updrafts, colonizing every tray and gutter with patient green. The tenders scrape them clear. The spores settle again. This has been in equilibrium longer than records run.

Nothing changes. That is, finally, the rule of the place, underneath the wet and the green and the noise. Not that nothing moves — water circulates, trains run on schedule through moss plains with empty freight cars, day follows day at this latitude under this sun. But each day is the day before, down to the height of the fog ceiling and the pitch of the turbines. A moss column grows and sheds and holds its height. A storm rains and evaporates and holds its place. A factory produces machine parts, ships half of them down the cliff elevators at the southern edge to a destination nobody observes, uses the other half to repair itself, and produces the same parts tomorrow. Leaves open at the angle they closed at. Ice rimes the shaded side of every pylon to the same thickness by dawn and does not melt by dusk, except for a margin that retreats and advances, retreats and advances, across the same hand's width of steel.

Walk inland for a month and the experience does not develop; it deepens without progressing. More moss plain, more standing water under thin ice regardless of season — there are no seasons, only the fixed cold and the fixed light. Another housing block, lit, dry inside against the wet world, smelling of hot dust and machine oil, its radiators ticking for empty rooms. Another condenser plant thundering inside its fog cap, harvesting weather it made itself. Another perfect fern, uneaten, unvisited, dropping spores into the wind that the towers will catch.

At the plateau's heart, where the survey markers gave up and simply read CENTER, the fog lies on the ground as a still, white, waist-deep sea that extends in every direction to a horizon of more fog. The sound is loudest here, sourceless and total. The moss grows up through the white in black-green islands. Nothing has ever crossed it on foot and reported anything else in the middle.

There is nothing else in the middle. There is the same thing, at the same pitch, held.

The plateau keeps its water, keeps its temperature, keeps its noise, the way a struck bell far too large to hear the strike of keeps its tone. You do not feel that it is waiting, exactly. Waiting implies an expectation of change.

You feel, after enough weeks of wet green light and unvarying thunder, that change was never one of its materials — only basalt, and water, and moss, and steel, and sound, laid down fresh and vast and cold, and locked, beautifully and terribly, into balance.
