At the top of the mountain there is a hollow that should be empty.

It is a young hollow — a cirque torn open not long ago, geologically speaking, by ice pulling away all at once. The rim is still sharp. Grey rock teeth stand unweathered around it, unsoftened by rain, their fractures white and fresh, scree still slipping a few stones at a time down into the bowl below. The wind comes over those teeth with nothing to stop it. The air is thin, iron-cold, and for most of the day the sun never quite clears the eastern ridge. What light there is arrives blue and sideways, filtered, dimmed again before it reaches the floor.

And yet the floor is a town.

Not a town built in the hollow. A town the hollow grew.

It is small — you could walk its full diameter in ten minutes if the streets were straight, which they are not — and it is completely packed. Every surface that is not a narrow path is living. The ground itself is not soil so much as a root-mat, yards thick in places, woven so tight it holds like a mattress over the broken stone beneath. Moss fills the weave. Cushion plants swell between the roots in hard green domes. Low, black-needled trees, bent almost horizontal by centuries of wind before they learned to shelter each other, have been trained — or have trained themselves — into rows.

Their trunks arch and meet. Their branches braid. Where two crowns touch they graft, bark flowing together, until what was a grove is a roofed street, what was a forked limb is a doorway. Houses here are not cut timber. They are enclosures inside a single plant's embrace: a hollow trunk widened year by year, a willow-like lattice grown in a spiral and then allowed to thicken until the gaps are walls. Roofs shed water leaf by leaf, still attached, still drinking light. If you put your hand to an interior wall in the morning you will feel it cold and faintly damp, and by afternoon, faintly warmer, as if the building had a slow circulation.

There is no metal in the town. No fired clay, no sawn board, no glass. Nothing that was not grown, bent, woven while green, or hollowed by rot encouraged in one precise place. The aqueduct is a line of giant hollow reeds, jointed, living at the root, carrying meltwater in a slow green pulse from the upper spring to the cisterns. The cisterns are cupped leaves the size of rooms, their rims rolled and stiffened, perpetually beaded with condensation. Bridges are root bundles that have been coaxed across the stream and now continue to thicken underfoot. Stairs are not built up but grown out, flat-topped swellings in a trunk, worn smooth and dark by feet.

And it is organized as a town is organized, with an unsettling completeness. There are districts.

Down by the water, where the stream spreads into a black marshy fan before it finds its exit crack in the rock, the reed-houses stand on their own roots in the shallows, and everything drips. Algae films the paths. Frothy moss hangs. This is the wet quarter, smelling of cold mud and crushed green stems.

Uphill from it is the dense market knot — really a widening where five root-streets collide under the oldest tree, whose canopy is less a tree than a ceiling held up by a forest of its own descending branches. Benches have grown there in rings. Niches have grown in the trunks at hand-height, and in them are stored seeds in their pods, dried fungi, bundles of fiber, salt-white lichen cakes. No one shouts their wares. The canopy eats sound. Voices stay low without anyone deciding to lower them, and the loudest regular noises are water falling from leaf to leaf and the soft tick of frost letting go.

Higher still, on the sunward slope where the light lingers longest, are the close-packed sleeping terraces. Row above row of small domed cells, each fronted with a woven screen of living vine that can be encouraged to close. At night the vines do close, a few inches of new tendril growth sealing the seams against the frost that comes down from the rim like something poured. In the morning they must be talked back open — not cut, if it can be helped, but unwound by hand while they are still stiff with cold. A house left alone for a week will heal its own doorway shut. A street left untended for a month will narrow by a foot on each side. Addresses here are temporary. Families keep their homes by touch, by daily pruning with fingernails and teeth and smooth stones, by sleeping against a wall so it learns the shape and holds it.

That is the main labor of the place, and it never stops. The town is active even when it looks still. In the brief warm hour — warm meaning only that water moves instead of locking — you can hear it if you press your ear to a trunk: a creaking, a faint hydraulic sigh. Shoots visibly quest across a path between morning and evening. White root hairs explore cracks in the rim rock. The stream rises an inch with melt, sinks an inch with night. Flowers the color of old bruises open only in the dimness, shut before true light touches them, and open again. The whole bowl breathes on a frost cycle: inhale of mist at dusk as warm plant air meets the descending cold, exhale at midday in a slow steaming from ten thousand leaves.

Because it is so wet, and so cold, the moisture has nowhere to go. It condenses on everything. It beads on needles and runs down bark in black lines. It soaks the outer moss until each footstep on the root-mat squeezes up a mouthful of clear water around your shoe, then the mat drinks it back. Breath hangs. Hair frosts at the edges of a hood. Inside the houses it rains upward, in a sense — warm air from bodies and compost pits in the floors hits the cold living roof and comes back down as a fine, patient drip, caught by leaf-gutters and led away. Nothing is ever dry. It is only, at best, less wet in one place than another.

Fauna, in all this green density, is strangely rare. You would expect the thicket to be loud with insects. It is not. The cold forbids it, and the plants are defended — bitter sap, resin, antifreeze sugars that taste of turpentine and salt. Now and then a snow-finch picks through the upper canopy and is gone. Under bark there are small pale beetles, blind, moving slowly. In the marsh, something amphibious turns over once in a while and does not surface. Mostly, the town's inhabitants move through an animal silence, and they seem to prefer it that way. Even their own sounds are muffled by the substrate: no footfall rings, no door can slam a door that grows.

The people — for there are people, dark-clothed in felted fiber that is itself half moss, their hands stained permanently green-black to the wrist — live less like builders and more like gardeners of a place that is trying, constantly, to become a forest again and forget it was ever a town. They carry no tools beyond stone and bone and the knowledge of which stem bends and which stem snaps, which fungus to introduce to soften a wall, which bitter leaf to pack against a shoot to discourage it. They eat what the town sheds and what can be gathered between its intentions: berries that ripen black and hot-tasting despite the cold, inner bark, the hearts of the reed stems, lichen scraped from the north faces of the rim where the town runs out and the true mountain begins again.

At the edge, that transition is abrupt. You climb the last terrace, duck under the last grafted arch where icicles grow downward from living wood, push through a curtain of hanging moss that has frozen into individual green threads — and you are outside. Bare fresh rock. Wind that scrapes. A view down thousands of feet of unweathered slope to valleys that look soft and distant and implausibly warm.

Behind you the town fills its bowl completely, rim to rim, street under canopy under terrace, a single dark green organism divided into rooms. Smoke never rises from it. No light shows at night except, here and there, a phosphorescent fungus tended in a wall niche, giving just enough glow to find a doorway by.

And in the deep night cold, when the frost takes the whole canopy at once and every leaf goes rigid, the town makes its only collective sound: a faint, scattered crackling, like a hundred small joints settling, as the water in its veins turns and holds, and does not burst, and waits for the dim morning to be released again.
