It is a town inside a bowl, high up where the air stays cool even at noon, and it is never dry and never quiet.

You come to it from below. For most of the last hour the path is a stair cut into wet rock — not grand, just worn, each step dished in the middle by generations of feet and hooves. Water weeps out of the cliff beside you in a hundred places. It darkens the stone, it beads in the moss, it runs in bright threads across the steps and makes the slate shine. Ferns grow from every crack at ankle, knee, and shoulder height. Higher up the growth thickens until the stair is tunneled: roots overhead, hanging moss, wet leaves brushing your face. You can hear the place long before you can see it — a roar that sorts itself, as you climb, into water falling, ducks and geese, wooden clappers, voices, goat bells, frogs.

Then a gate. Not a built gate so much as a gap in a living wall.

The bowl is perhaps four hundred paces across, a high hanging hollow scooped out of the mountain flank. Its rim is stone on three sides and an enormous hedge on the fourth, where the ground falls away again. The town fills it almost completely. That is the first understanding: there is no separation here between settlement and vegetation and animal life. There is only density, layer upon layer, all of it moving.

A spring rises in the upper end of the bowl, under an overhang of black rock that is always sweating. The water comes out clear and achingly cold and immediately divides. Stone channels, some of them very old and softened to the shape of troughs, carry it down through the town in a braided network. It runs along the edges of lanes, under houses, through courtyards, across the small market square in open runnels that children step over without looking. It turns three wooden waterwheels. It fills troughs, barrels, frog ponds, duck runs, and washing stones. At the lower end it gathers again and pours out through a notch in the rim as a white fall that disappears into cloud and forest far below. On a bright morning you can stand in that notch and see weather moving underneath you, rain falling from clouds that are level with your boots.

The houses crowd around that water. They are two and three stories high, built shoulder to shoulder on stone footings slick with algae. Above the stone, everything is timber, wattle, woven reed, bark shingle, and living wood. Willows and hazels have been planted at corners and trained into walls; their trunks have thickened into structural posts, their branches woven and re-woven until a house can lean on its own hedge. Roofs are thatch weighted with turf, and the turf is grazed. A roof is also a small meadow: grass, clover, buttercups, self-seeded onions, a tethered goat standing placidly above a kitchen chimney pulling at the growth before it gets heavy enough to slide.

There is no street that is only a street. A lane might be, at ground level, packed earth and flat stones set in a mat of roots, with a water channel on one side and pig sties on the other. Above it, balconies reach toward each other until they almost touch, hung with drying wool, strips of bark cloth, strings of mushrooms, cages of songbirds. Above that, vine bridges and laundry lines and the lower branches of town trees cross the gap. The trees are everywhere — rowans, alders, old apples gone gnarled, a few huge beeches in the center whose trunks pass up through holes deliberately left in roofs. Their canopy does not close out the daylight; it breaks it. The light in the bowl is ordinary, clear mountain daylight, but filtered green and scattered by leaves and mist so that at any given moment half the town is in sun and half in cool shade, and the patches keep moving.

And it is always, briefly, raining, or about to rain, or just finished. Moisture condenses on cold stone in the shade. It drips from eaves onto barrels that are already full. Wood swells and creaks. Rope darkens. Wool smells. Bread has to be baked in closed ovens and eaten quickly before it softens. Iron is rare — a few bronze bells, copper pots gone green, steel only in treasured knives — because everything rusts and rots so fast that people trust stone weights, wooden gears, bone needles, flint, and good knots more than metal. What they make, they make constantly: re-thatching, re-weaving a wall where the living withies have burst their bindings, re-cutting a channel choked overnight with fallen leaves and frogspawn.

Because the plants do not stop. In this cool wet hollow, growth is the substrate of life. Moss colonizes a shoe left by a door. A fence left untrimmed for a month becomes impenetrable. The market square is paved, or was paved; between the stones is a springy turf that has to be trampled and grazed and scythed in rotation or the square becomes a meadow. The old terraces outside the hedge, where barley and beans and cabbages are grown in black soil held by stone walls, look settled and ancient — they are, hundreds of years in their present shape — but their edges blur daily with runners and suckers coming down from the forest. Living here is not building once. It is negotiating, every morning, with a place that is trying to become a thicket with people inside it.

The animals have won that negotiation more thoroughly than the people. They are not kept apart. Ducks patrol the water channels in single file, eating algae and snails, and a lane will wait for them. Geese occupy the market steps and act as its government, hissing traders into different positions. Chickens are underfoot in every kitchen, in the weaving sheds, on the woodpiles, dusting themselves in ash by the ovens. Pigeons and swallows nest in every eave — the undersides of roofs are studded with mud cups — and at dawn and dusk the whole bowl erupts as they change places, thousands of wings making a sound like water being poured. House martins take insects over the spring pool. Bats take them at night from the same places.

Goats climb stairs, roofs, and the rocky rim. Sheep are driven through at midday, bells clanging, to graze the hedge down from inside. Pigs live in stone pens that are also retaining walls, warm and steaming, rooting through market waste. Donkeys carry wood and grain up the stair in an almost continuous line, braying at the gate. Dogs — dozens, all sizes, all employed — run messages in their own way from house to house, following children, herding ducks whether ducks asked to be herded or not. Frogs sing from every channel and cistern after rain until the sound is a physical pressure. Bees live in skeps hung under balconies, and on warm still afternoons the air over the square hums and drifts with them. Even indoors there are presences: spiders in the rafters fat on flies, mice in the thatch, a house toad by the door kept deliberately and fed.

It should be chaos, and moment to moment it is. That is the town's real weather. A sudden shower at two in the afternoon sends the whole market flipping — awnings of oiled cloth unrolling from balconies with a snap, goods snatched under roofs, sheep run for the lower sheds, the channels rising three inches in ten minutes and carrying a flotilla of leaves, cups, feathers, and lost toys down through town while boys chase alongside shouting. The waterwheels speed up and the millstones under them change note. Someone rings the big bronze bell by the spring, not in alarm but in timing: clear the lower bridge, open the second sluice. Everyone knows the sequence. Then the sun comes out, steam rises from roofs and backs and hot stone, the swallows come back, trade resumes in slightly different positions, and a team is already out with wooden rakes clearing the main channel.

Wind gets caught in the bowl and circles it. It will strip blossom off the apple trees and drop it in drifts in the lanes, rattle every loose shingle, set a hundred small wooden clappers and hollow-bamboo chimes banging on porches, then drop completely, leaving the frog chorus exposed again. A herd coming in late can seal a street solid for a quarter of an hour, during which second-story life simply takes over: people cross on balcony planks, pass baskets hand to hand over the backs of sheep, conduct arguments window to window. Nothing waits for quiet. Work is done inside the noise — weaving, grinding, bargaining, mending, teaching — voices raised to a level that would be shouting anywhere else and here is just conversation.

There are rules, of course, worn as smooth as the steps. You do not cut a house-tree without the whole lane agreeing and a replacement already knee-high. You do not let your channel silt: the household below you has the right to come up and clear it through your yard if you fail. No one builds over the main water. Goats may graze any roof but not the mill roof. The gate in the hedge is shut at dusk, not against people but against the casual departure of stock, and opened at first light by whoever's dogs woke first, which is usually everyone.

At night the turbulence does not stop, it changes register. The wheels go on turning, slower. The fall at the notch roars steadily. Owls hunt from the rim trees, frogs take the channels, pigs shift and grunt in the wall-pens, a baby wakes in one house and is answered, briefly, by babies in three others, their cries crossing the lanes under the leaf canopy. Mist comes up from the wet ground and down from the rim until the lamps — oil in clay dishes, set in niches — are just amber points in moving white. The roofs drip. The living walls creak as they cool.

By morning the bowl will have rearranged itself a little. A vine will have crossed a window. A duck will have nested in a woodpile. A channel will be running clearer, a step will be greener, a market stall will be two feet to the left of where it was, under a roof-goat, beside the geese, in the sun that has moved. The place has been here a very long time. It is not pristine and it is not ruined; it is in balance the way a river rapid is in balance — old, established, exactly itself, and never for one minute still.
