It sits below the country around it.

From the plain you would almost miss it. The land out there is cool, open grass and thin soil, wind-scoured, nearly flat to the horizon, with only a few low thorn bushes and stands of pale, wiry grass where water gathers after rain. Then the track dips toward a rim of fresh earth — heaped spoil, still loose and unweathered — and suddenly the ground opens.

A broad bowl has been cut down into the pale rock, perhaps thirty feet below the level of the plain, perhaps more in places where they are still digging. It is not a cave. The sky is directly overhead, ordinary daylight pours in, blue in summer and flat grey-white most of the year. For the first hours of the morning the eastern wall still holds the town in blue shadow, and for the last hours the west does the same, but at midday the sun reaches straight to the bottom and warms the stone. Even then it stays cool down there. Cold air settles into the pit and holds. Breath shows early. Hands stay cold in the shade of a wall long after the roofs are warm.

And it is packed, from wall to wall.

There is no wasted ground. The town fills the bowl in tight terraces, street below street, each level cut back into the rock so the houses can lean their backs against solid earth. Houses share walls. Their fronts are stone cut from this very place — creamy limestone and grey flinty rock, the blocks still sharp-edged because nothing here has had time to erode. The corners have not rounded. The cut banks have not slumped into soil. Everything shows tool marks. Pick stripes, wedge holes, the fan-shaped scars of a hand adze in unseasoned oak.

The lanes are only as wide as they need to be. Two people can pass if they turn sideways between baskets. A hand-cart takes the whole width, and everyone else presses into doorways to let it go by. The lanes are not paved so much as trampled — damp clay trodden hard over broken stone, darkened where water always runs. Above them, roofs crowd so close that a cat could cross half a street without touching ground, ridge to ridge of thatch, turf, and split shingles. Gutters made of hollowed wood jut and drip.

It is an urban place, but made with primitive hands. No cranes except a tripod of poles lashed with rope and a wooden pulley. No iron to speak of beyond a few precious bronze picks, stone hammers, wooden mallets, bone needles, rope, baskets. Mortar is mixed in pits with bare feet. Timber arrives green and warps as it dries in place, so doors are re-hung every season. Scaffolding is lashed, not nailed, and creaks and sways when men climb it with loads of earth on their backs.

They are still building it, everywhere, all the time. That is the motion under the quiet.

For it is remarkably quiet for a town this dense and this busy. Sound does not travel well here. The earthen walls drink it. There is no ringing of much metal, no wheeled traffic on stone, no large animals bellowing. What you hear, close up, is a soft, constant industry: the dull *tok, tok* of a mallet on a chisel, paced slow to keep the tool from shattering; the scrape of a basket dragged up a ramp; clay slapping; rope straining; the tread of a water-wheel that lifts seep-water in wooden buckets, *squeak... pour... squeak... pour*. Voices are low without anyone seeming to enforce it, because in these narrow cuts a normal voice comes back off the opposite wall into your neighbour's window, and everyone's windows are always within a few feet. At night, after a wooden clapper is struck twice on the rim, even that lowers.

Water is not abundant, but it is always present in the wrong places. It does not run in a river here. Rain comes intermittently off the plain, sudden cool showers that sheet off the roofs and turn the lanes to instant runnels. Between rains, water comes as condensation. Warm daytime air meeting cold cut rock leaves the lower walls beaded by morning. Alley stones sweat. Storage pits dug under floors — and every house has one, because there is no room to store anything sideways — have to be lined with clay and bailed.

In the centre, at the lowest point they have made so far, is the cistern: a circular pit lined with tight stone and puddled clay, covered for most of its width with planks to keep children and dust out. Around it the ground is permanently damp black earth, smelling of wet rock and woodsmoke that never quite lifts out of the bowl. All the roof gutters are angled, however crookedly, to eventually feed it. All the street drains, little hand-cut channels in the centre of each lane with a flat stone laid over them where carts pass, are kept obsessively clear. That is the first law of living below the rim: you do not block water, and you do not dig sideways under your neighbour without telling him. The rock is sound, but it is new — no one yet knows which beds will split in frost — and a shared wall is a shared risk.

Vegetation is sparse, and mostly opportunistic. Nothing old grows down here. Moss finds the north-facing gutters and stays bright green. A fringe of small ferns has found the seep line halfway down the western cut where water weeps from a bedding plane, drop by drop, darkening the pale rock to tea-brown. On roofs where turf was used, grass comes up thin and yellow-green for a season and dies back. What people actually plant they plant in baskets and in tiny, fiercely tended courtyard plots no bigger than a table: onions, leeks, bitter herbs that tolerate shade and damp, a single bean pole tied to a roof beam. Timber and thatch and food mostly come down ramps from the plain above, carried in, bundle by bundle, which is another reason everything is built small and low and shoulder-to-shoulder — every beam had to be lowered on ropes.

Fauna is rare and mostly at the edges. Swallows have discovered the fresh cliffs faster than anyone expected; their mud nests already line the upper cut where the rock overhangs a hand's breadth, and at dusk they cut back and forth across the open sky of the bowl in complete silence until one calls, thin and startling. Sparrows work the spill from baskets. Down in the damp corners there are slow black beetles, woodlice under planks, a pale lizard that lives by the cistern and is left alone because it eats flies. There are very few dogs, and only a handful of hens kept in wicker pens on roofs; they are quiet too, damp-feathered, scratching at grit. Rats have not yet found the place in numbers. Neither have trees.

The feel on the skin is of cool damp even in working hours. Laundry strung across lanes — and every lane is webbed with it, shirts and woven blankets and lengths of undyed cloth — never dries crisp. It dries *less wet*, and is brought in smelling of smoke and stone. Wool is essential, felted cloaks, felt caps. Fires are small, in stone hearths set deep into back walls, because smoke pooling in the bowl will choke the lower streets if everyone burns at once. So cooking is staggered by street, morning fires above, evening fires below, and the smell of the town changes by hours: wet clay, then porridge and onion, then green wood, then cold ash.

Yet for all the stillness of its air, the place is in constant motion. A new terrace is being cut at the south end even now; you can watch the line advance a few inches a day, baskets going up empty and coming down full, men rotating at the face in short shifts because the pick work is hard on the wrists. A house front is being rebuilt two lanes over after its green timbers twisted. Children whose only job is running — running with a cup of water to a mortar pit, running with a message about a drain blocked by silt, running up the long zigzag ramp to the rim to see if a party with thatch is coming in — thread through the standing adults like minnows. Weather does its own work from above, shower and clearance and shower again, filling and emptying the cistern channels, re-wetting the clay floors.

People who live here stoop slightly even when they don't need to, out of habit from low lintels built to save timber. They keep their tools wrapped in oiled cloth against the damp. They know whose roof drips onto whose path and have worked out, without writing, who sweeps which stretch of channel on which day. If you stand long enough on the rim at midday, looking down at the dense spread of roofs steaming faintly after rain, the raw white cuts surrounding it, the spoil heaps still loose and unvegetated on the plain, it does not look finished or ruined. It looks embryonic — a settlement still being excavated into existence, sharper and newer with every basket of stone removed, the erosion and softening and eventual greening all still years ahead of it.
