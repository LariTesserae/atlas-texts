It begins with a stair going down where no stair should be necessary — a galvanized switchback bolted into the side of a fresh pit, each landing already filmed with white mineral scale, each handrail too hot to hold for long without shifting your grip. The pit is not old. Its walls are black and sharp, split basalt and broken concrete in clean, unworn faces, as if the ground opened recently and has not yet learned how to soften itself. No rubble slope has formed. No dust has dulled the edges.

At the bottom is a chamber, no larger than a cathedral turned inward.

Perhaps forty meters across, eighteen high, domed above by a ceiling part natural stone, part sprayed concrete ribbed with steel. The air inside is dense enough to feel like a substance. It presses on the skin, fills the mouth with iron and wet stone, makes breathing a slow decision. It is hot — not sun-hot, but deep, enclosed, engine-hot. Heat comes up through the floor in weak gradients, gathers under the dome, finds the cooler pipes near the ceiling and turns to condensation there. So it rains, but only in places, and only in intervals. A slow bead forms on a copper line, swells for minutes, falls at last onto a leaf with a soft, padded tick. In another corner, for a little while, a fine local shower. Then it stops.

There is almost no light. A slit far above, choked with grating and vegetation from the surface, admits only a diluted grey smear that never reaches the floor. The rest comes from pilot lamps — small amber and red eyes on valve housings, a caged bulb over a door, the pale green face of a gauge behind fogged glass. Between them, darkness pools thickly. Your eyes adjust and keep adjusting; things do not so much appear as slowly separate from black.

The floor is mostly emptiness.

That is the strangeness of the density here. Wet black stone, cracked in fresh polygonal plates, stretches bare for meters at a time, shining faintly where condensation has settled. White and ochre mineral crusts bloom around vents. A narrow catwalk crosses on low steel legs. And spaced far apart, with room enough around each to walk a full circle, stand the plants.

They are lush beyond reason for a place without sun. Seven, perhaps eight mature specimens — not trees exactly, not palms, something bred or altered for this work. Their trunks are short, swollen, dark as wet bark, and from each crown unfold leaves three meters long, broad, rubbery, nearly black-green, veined in dull silver. No leaf is bitten or torn. Their edges are perfect. Fronds hang heavy with moisture, cupping small warm pools in their folds. Smaller ferns have started in the cracks at their bases, brilliant green, fleshy, unfurling so slowly that the motion is a matter of days. Roots do not search downward so much as sideways, following the warm pipes half-buried in the stone, gripping them, whitening where mineral water crusts them.

This is a condenser orchard. Or it was engineered to be one.

Pipes enter through the east wall in a bundle thicker than a doorway — insulated mains, copper returns beaded green, a pressure line painted a red that the heat is lifting in small blisters. They run above the plants, branch, descend, disappear again into the floor. Under the stone, muffled to almost nothing, is the rest of the plant: boilers, a turbine hall, scaled machinery whose presence you feel more in your teeth and sternum than hear. A small control shed stands alone on the far side, corrugated, its window black, a single plastic chair outside it slowly warping in the heat. Inside the open door: a clipboard under a film of water, a row of hand-brushes for pollination laid out in size order, a kettle, valve tags wired in a bunch. No other buildings. A stencil on the wall, softened by steam, reads NO. 3 — LOW CONDENSER, and below it, in smaller letters, NO OPEN DISCHARGE.

The silence is the most complete feature. No insects. No birds in the dome, no rodents in the pipes, no frogs though the damp and heat would seem to invite them. The leaves show no eggs underneath. The warm pools cupped in the fronds hold no larvae. Even the usual subterranean dripping is muted, absorbed by leaf and sponge-moss before it can strike stone. What sound exists arrives slowly: the tick of expanding metal, a single drop, the faintest electrical hum from a junction box, and every three quarters of an hour or so, a cycle.

The cycle has no alarm. The pilot lamps brighten by a fraction. Somewhere beyond the wall a pump takes a long, patient breath and warm water moves in the overhead lines. Condensation thickens all at once across the dome. For two, three minutes a soft rain crosses the chamber in a drifting band, plant to plant, darkening the already dark leaves, beading on the catwalk, raising a smell of hot stone and green sap so rich it is almost sweet. The great leaves tip, one by one, and spill their held pools to their roots in a series of muted pours. A vent in the floor exhales. Then the pump subsides, the lamps dim, and the chamber returns to its long stillness, as if nothing had happened except that everything is slightly wetter and slightly hotter than before.

At the north end, where a new fracture has split the wall sometime this season — its faces still glassy, its points still able to cut — young plants are starting without any pipe to guide them. A fern no bigger than a hand. A pale shoot curling not up toward the grey slit, but down and in, toward a seam that breathes heat from the rock. In here, light is the scarce accident. Warmth and mineral water are the reliable seasons, and the vegetation has learned to orient accordingly.

Stay long enough and the rules make themselves known in the body: keep to the catwalk when the floor vents sigh; do not touch the bright crusts, which are thinner than they look over scalding pockets; do not expect anything to happen quickly. A leaf takes a week to open. Rain is manufactured by the building's own exhalation. The garden neither needs nor permits animals.

It just stands there in the hot dark below the world, sparse and overflowing, drinking steam, spilling it back, while the machinery turns it over and over on a cycle too slow to watch — a small, sealed weather, still in the first sharp youth of becoming something else.
