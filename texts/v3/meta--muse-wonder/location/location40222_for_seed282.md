It is below everything, and it is not finished yet.

You get to it by a hatch that was not meant to be a door — a square of checkerplate in the floor of a sub-basement service corridor, between a boiler that no longer runs and a wall of shut-off valves painted in four generations of industrial green. The ladder down is six rungs, galvanized, new enough that the edges are still sharp and the protective oil is still on it. It takes your gloves. Below that, the cold takes your breath and turns it immediately into weather.

The room is small enough to learn by turning around. Three paces wide, four long if you don't count the equipment, and low enough that a tall person learns to keep their elbows in. There is no natural substrate anywhere. No earth floor, no rock wall to suggest it was dug. The pit was lined, then lined again. Floor is steel grating over a shallow sump pan. Walls are insulated sandwich panels, half of them still wearing their blue plastic film. Ceiling is not a ceiling so much as the underside of the building made honest: joists, ducting, cable trays, lagged pipes, all of it manufactured, all of it bolted to something else that was bolted down first. Even the air smells made — ozone, hot dust from a motor that isn't hot, the sweet chemical tang of refrigerant, wet concrete still curing somewhere behind the panels despite the cold slowing it almost to a stop.

It is just above freezing, and just below it, depending on where you stand. Temperature isn't a condition here, it's a tide. 

The compressors in the far corner — two squat, enameled machines on rubber feet, plumbed in copper — run for eleven minutes and rest for nine. When they run, the room goes down degree by degree and the damp in the air gives up and becomes frost. When they rest, it sighs. That is the whole climate.

Water here is  intermittent and entirely harvested. Nothing flows in from outside. Moisture enters on clothes, on breath, on the brief opening of the hatch, and the room is designed to steal it back. Condensation beads on the coldest pipes first, thickens, crawls — strangely, not always down. Along the charged collector wires above the trays it travels up and sideways in bright beads toward the drip points, as if gravity were a suggestion pipes make and electricity can overrule. It ticks into aluminum gutters. It freezes mid-tick into clear needles. In the defrost part of the cycle it lets go all at once: a soft, irregular rain inside a room that has never seen sky, pattering onto grating, into the sump, to be pumped, filtered through three canisters of ceramic media, and offered back again.

Frost is the fastest-growing thing down here. It feathers across coil fins in white ferns. It furres the gauge faces so you have to breathe on them — adding more water to the system — to read the pressure. It outlines every screw head on the unfinished wall in white, making the construction legible. The other growth is slower, and deliberately sparse.

Along the left wall, racked from knee to shoulder in near-touching tiers, are trays. Not many. The density of the place is in machinery, not life. In manufactured soil — fired clay pellets, shredded mineral wool, no dirt at all — are plugs of things bred to tolerate this exact insult: dwarf winter rye no taller than a finger, its blades blue-green and stiff; mats of moss in shallow pans, darkening only when the misters run; a single tray of spruce seedlings, each one a green asterisk, growing so slowly they seem like models of trees rather than trees. They do not lean to the dim light. The light is dim anyway, amber work-lamps caged under the joists and green status LEDs reflecting in wet metal, barely enough to work by and kept low to keep heat down. The plants lean, almost imperceptibly, toward the compressors, toward the waste heat that pulses there during the off-cycle. Heat, not light, is the rare season.

There is no animal life. The filters and the cold and the sealed hatch have seen to that. No flies in the trays, no spiders in the cable corners, no mice despite being under a factory. When pollination or agitation is needed the trays themselves vibrate — a plate under each shelf hums for four seconds at an interval, shaking loose a faint dust. The sterility makes the machinery sound more alive by contrast. The pumps breathe. The relays click like small jaws. A fan stirs, stops, stirs again, testing.

Mostly it is quiet. The insulation that keeps the cold in keeps the factory out. Above, somewhere, production continues at industrial scale — you can feel it through the ladder when you put a palm to it, a distant, rhythmic thud transmitted by steel, but you cannot hear it properly. In the room itself sounds are close and small and therefore enormous: a droplet hitting the sump pan, the creak of ice expanding on a joint, the tick of a contracting duct, your own jacket rustling. During the compressor run there is a low hum that you feel in your teeth. During the rest there is a silence so complete that the next drip seems scheduled.

And it is still being built. That is what separates it from a cold store or a grow room, which would be finished things put to use. This place has an embryonic quality, half its systems running a prototype of themselves. One short wall is not a wall yet, only studs and a skin of insulation board scored with pencil lines and measurements in marker. Plastic sheeting stapled over it billows faintly with pressure changes. Behind it, at intervals that are not quite regular, a small automated riveter on a rail moves an inch, pauses for a long while, and fires — *thock* — setting one fastener in the frame of a second, smaller chamber beyond. Then it waits, as if listening for a response, or for its motor to warm. Coils of unused cable hang in loops like stored intentions. A stack of floor panels leans wrapped. A clipboard in a zip bag records temperatures by hand, in handwriting that has gotten shakier, the last entry not yet filled.

The rules manifest themselves without being posted. You do not touch bare metal with bare skin. You do not stay through more than two full cycles without going up to warm your hands, because your own moisture will fog the sensors and feed the frost in the wrong places. You do not open the hatch while the compressors run; the pressure differential will hold it and then release it badly. You speak little, or the humidity needle lifts. The room tolerates a person the way it tolerates everything — as a temporary source of heat and water to be corrected for.

After a while the strangeness stops being the cold or the underground or the plants in their ceramic dirt. It is that the room seems less like a place that was installed than like a place in the process of learning how to be a place at all. It manufactures its rain, then drinks it. It makes frost faster than the moss can make a spore. It is assembling, very slowly and mostly when no one is watching, the next version of itself behind the plastic. Nothing has eroded here. Nothing has had time to wear down, soften, stain, or settle. Corners are still corners. Every surface is new, damp, and becoming colder.

When the cycle turns again, the lamps dim a fraction as the compressors kick, the frost on the coils visibly thickens at the edges, and the little rye blades stiffen with a sound below hearing.
