It is flat at an altitude where flatness should not exist.

You climb to it for days — through gullies, through scree that gets older and rounder the higher you go — expecting a summit, a ridge, some final sharpness. Instead the slope eases, rounds over like the top of a loaf, and opens. And keeps opening. A plain in the sky, three days' walk across in any direction, worn down so long ago that the mountains forget they were mountains at all.

This is an end-state landscape. The rock has been eroding for longer than there have been forests to cover it. There are no cliffs up here, no fresh stone, no angles. Boulders sit half-sunk in the ground, smooth as river stones though no river put them there. Broad pavements of red-brown laterite show where the soil has thinned, polished by wind and rain into a dull sheen. Shallow pans dimple the surface, a hand's breadth deep and fifty paces wide, where water gathers and leaves. Everything has settled into the shape it will keep.

And yet, at this height — thin air that catches in the back of your throat, lips cracking by noon, water taking forever to not-quite boil — it is warm. Tropical-warm. The sun comes down almost vertically at midday, without mercy or angle, and the dark ground drinks it and gives it back up through the soles of your feet. You walk in a T-shirt with your lungs aching. Your shadow is a small, dense pool beneath you. The light is ordinary daylight, but clarified; colors do not fade with distance the way they should. A red rock two kilometres away is as red as the one by your boot.

### The Mat

There is no bare earth here, not really. Even the stone pavements have a skin.

The entire plateau is covered by a single, continuous, living mat: tussock grasses braided through cushion-moss, through creeping rosettes the size of dinner plates, through a spongy turf of a thousand small interlocking plants that has no gaps. You do not walk *on* ground so much as *on* plants. It compresses underfoot, springy and damp, and slowly rises again behind you, erasing your print. Squeeze a handful and water runs between your fingers, brown and cold. Kneel, and your knees come up wet.

This is the water system. There are almost no streams — the mat holds moisture like a sponge laid over rock, releasing it into hollows. After rain, the pans fill ankle-deep and mirror the sky for a day. Between rains, there is condensation: mist that arrives not from above but up the sides of the plateau, spilling over the rim in the late afternoon; dew that beads so heavy at dawn that the giant plants drip. Every surface is intermittently wet. Nothing is ever entirely dry, and nothing is ever truly flooded.

Rising out of the mat — and this is where the emptiness begins — are the giants.

Every twenty, thirty, fifty paces stands a single great rosette on a thick, fibrous trunk: some no taller than you, some four metres high, their trunks wrapped in a skirt of their own dead leaves, soft and rustling and the colour of old thatch. At the top, a crown of huge, felty leaves, each one longer than your arm, blue-green, furred silver underneath to hold warmth and shed frost-bright dew. They are spaced like chess pieces left mid-game across an enormous board. Between them: nothing. No canopy, no understorey, no trees. Just the low mat, the open sky, and the next giant, and the next, shrinking but never quite vanishing at the horizon.

Volume-wise, the place is hollow. You can shout and there is nothing close enough to give you an echo. You can see a herder's smoke two hours' walk away. But surface-wise, you never step off life. Plant is the substrate, the architecture, the insulation, the reservoir.

In the cup of each crown, where the leaves meet, rainwater collects in a dark little well. And in almost every well, something lives its whole life without ever touching the ground.

### The Noise

The first thing you disbelieve is the sound.

By ten in the morning, when the sun has warmed the mat through, it starts: a stridulation from the grass itself, grasshoppers and crickets and a squat, thumb-sized cicada-thing that lives down among the roots, all tuning up at once. By noon it is not a chorus so much as a condition of the air. A continuous, metallic, deafening shimmer, loud enough that you have to raise your voice to the person beside you, loud enough that after an hour your ears ring in the gaps of it. The mat vibrates, faintly, under your palms. There is so much insect in the turf that walking sends up a living spray at every step — hoppers, moths, iridescent beetles, bees working the tiny cushion-flowers — and where you disturb them, sunbirds and swifts come arrowing in, stitching the air just above the ground.

And the insects are only the day shift.

Toward evening the pans and the crown-wells wake up. The frogs here are tiny, translucent-gold, no bigger than a thumbnail. There are numbers of them that bypass any sensible estimate. They live in the rosette pools, in the pans, in the wet axils of fallen leaves. When the mist comes over the rim they begin, and for two hours after sunset the plateau produces a sound so dense it feels architectural: bells, clicks, whistles, trills layered ten deep, navigable. You can close your eyes and *hear* the map of water — bright near a pan, thin on the drier rises. Travellers do exactly this. On overcast nights, with no stars and no landmarks, you walk toward frog-sound and away from silence, because silence means dry rock and no shelter.

Overhead, seasonally, comes the third noise: wings. Vast, loose flocks of seed-eating birds move across the plateau following the grass harvest, and migrating storks ride the thermals that rise off the hot mat at noon in slow, silent spirals — silent themselves, but the insects rising in that same warm air are not, and the swifts screaming through the columns of them are not. On some days the sky is the busiest part of the landscape. Below, in the mat, giant mole-rats work unseen, eating roots, shouldering up fresh mounds of red earth that the plants recolonise within weeks. You hear them sometimes at night, gnawing, through the sole of the ground you sleep on.

The fauna is not diverse in the textbook sense. It is *overwhelming* in biomass and presence. It gets into your food, your ears, your sleeves. Nothing up here is a large predator. There is almost nothing large at all except the plants. It is a world run by small warm and cold bodies in astronomical numbers, eating plant, eating each other, converting sunlight into noise at a furious rate.

### People, Thinly

Human marks exist, but you have to be looking.

Every half-day or so: a cairn. Not tall — nothing up here is built tall — just a man-height stack of those loaf-shaped stones, lichen-spotted, with a few older ones slumped and already half-swallowed by the mat growing up through them. They mark the lines where, on mist days, you can walk cairn to cairn.

Every day or so: a shelter. A semicircle of stone, shoulder-high, windbreak-fashion, roofed once with giant leaves lashed to a frame of dead rosette-trunks. A fire-pit with a few cracked, fire-blackened stones and a hand-axe of polished basalt left beside it. A low hut made almost entirely of plant material — woven grass walls, a thatch of those great felty leaves, overlapping like scales, waterproof as felt. Some huts are lived in seasonally by herders who bring up small, shaggy, sure-footed stock to graze the mat in the warm months, and who navigate by frog-song and cairn. Most are empty, in varying stages of being eaten. The mat climbs walls. Moss furs the thatch. A hut abandoned for five years is a green mound with a doorway.

The technology is hand-technology, and almost all of it is the plateau reprocessed: stone knives for cutting leaf-fibre, woven nets for scooping frogs and insects (both eaten, both abundant), leaf rain-capes, baskets of braided tussock, digging sticks tipped with fire-hardened wood for mole-rats. Metal is rare and treasured and comes from below — a single iron blade will be resharpened until it is a sliver. Nothing is built to last against the damp and the growth, and nobody seems to expect it to. Shelters are made knowing the mat will take them. Cairns are restacked by whoever passes next. It is an honest arrangement.

The one exception: at the centre of the plateau, where a slightly deeper pan holds water longest, there is a rough circle of standing stones, each perhaps twice your height, sunk deep, tilted at companionable odd angles, their bases lost in turf. They are far older than the huts, older perhaps than the herding. The local story is not that people put them there, exactly, but that people *found the stones standing up* under the mat when they cleared it, and cleared it again each generation. Whether or not that's true, the mat is visibly trying to re-absorb them, one slow green centimetre at a time.

### The Rules It Keeps

The days have a rhythm as regular as breathing.

Dawn: mist in the hollows, everything dripping, frogs falling silent one species at a time. Cold in the lungs, warm where the first sun hits your face.

Mid-morning: mist burns off from the middle outward. The insect roar builds. Thermals begin. Dust — red, fine, ancient — lifts in small, wandering devils that last twenty seconds and die.

Early afternoon: cumulus builds *below* the rim. You stand in sunshine watching a sea of cloud assemble at your feet, ringing the plateau. Then it comes up and over, fast, waist-high, then head-high, and the world is ten paces wide and grey and dripping. Rain, often, hammering straight down for twenty minutes, drumming on the giant leaves so loudly it temporarily beats even the insects, filling every crown-well, every pan, running in sheets off the stone pavements.

After: steam. Light coming back slanted and gold. Every insect in the world apparently reborn simultaneously. Frog overture tuning up. The mat, for an hour, is a shallow, shining lake pretending to be a meadow — you wade, everywhere, ankle-deep through plants and water inseparably mixed.

Night: clear and huge. Because the air is thin and there is nothing tall to interrupt it, the sky feels less like a ceiling than a depth. The giants close slightly, their great leaves folding up around the crown-wells — which keeps the frogs' little ponds from cooling too fast, and means the night sound comes to you muffled, intimate, as if each plant were humming to itself in its sleep.

Fire is difficult and precious. The living mat will not burn. Only the dead-leaf skirts of the giants will, and they smoulder more than flame, sweet-smelling and smoky. Cooking is slow. Warmth at night comes mostly from piled cut leaves and shared shelter. This is not a cold place, but it is a damp one, and damp at altitude finds its way into everything that is not alive and growing. Belongings rot. Wounds heal slowly. Leather goes green. The things that thrive here are the things that are either stone, or continuously growing fast enough to outrun decay.

Which is, in the end, the governing fact: the plateau has been stable, flat, warm, and plant-covered for so long that almost everything on it has stopped competing for space on the ground and started competing for *time* — who can emerge, sing, breed, and be eaten in the brief hot hours; who can close, hold water, and wait out the mist. It feels ancient and frantic at once. Empty when you look. Deafening when you listen. Soft underfoot everywhere, with nothing, anywhere, to hold onto above your knees — just the slow giants spaced across their green, wet ocean, holding their tiny frog-filled skies, waiting out another afternoon storm.
