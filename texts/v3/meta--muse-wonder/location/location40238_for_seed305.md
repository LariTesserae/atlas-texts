It is bright enough to hurt, and cold enough to kill, and those two facts never reconcile.

The sun here gives no warmth. It hangs enormous and white over a high continental plateau — not blue, not gold, but a flat, surgical white, as if the light has been stripped of everything except visibility. At noon you can see a hundred miles of broken ridge with painful clarity, every shard of black stone throwing a blue shadow, and still feel your breath freeze in a fringe of needles around your mouth. The light pours down. The heat does not follow it.

This is the White Foundry. No one built it. No one has ever built anything here. There are no roads, no walls, no chimneys, no cut stone, and yet from the moment you crest the outer escarpment you hear industry on a continental scale: clanking, sighing, grinding, the deep rhythmic thud of pistons, the shriek of steam escaping under pressure, a vast electrified hum that gets into your teeth.

It is the animals doing it. It is the plants. It is the ground itself, learning how to be a machine because the alternative is to freeze in broad daylight.

The plateau is young. It was shoved up recently, geologically speaking — a whole seabed and the volcanic country beneath it lifted two, three, four kilometers into thin air before erosion had time to soften anything. So the mountains are not rounded. They are torn. They rise in unweathered blades and coxcombs of basalt and pale limestone and glassy obsidian, their flanks still striped with fresh fault-lines the color of bone. Valleys are V-shaped slots, not yet widened. Rivers have barely begun; there is not enough water for them. Instead, in the bottom of every gorge, a chain of steaming pockets gathers — meltwater that condenses on black rock at midday, beads, runs a few meters, and locks again into clear ice lenses by the time the shadow of the ridge falls across it. Scree slopes shift under their own newness. Every few days, somewhere out of sight, a cliff calves with a sound like a dropped cathedral and sends up a plume of mineral dust that hangs for a week in the still, frigid air.

And everything — everything — is covered.

There is no bare ground. That is the first disorientation after the light. You expect a cold, high, dry place to be empty, spacious, austere. This place is packed to suffocation. The sharp rock is upholstered, joint to joint, in vegetation that has solved cold by never lifting itself into the air. Forests here are eighteen inches tall. They are forests all the same.

There are mat-pines, which are not pines, spreading in overlapping scales of bottle-green and arterial red, each scale a thick, clear bladder. Inside the bladder, in trapped and magnified sunlight, it is almost warm. You can press a gloved hand to one and feel a faint, living tepidity, and see the tiny black leaves inside turn, very slowly, to follow a sun they feel but cannot be warmed by. Under the bladders, condensation gathers perpetually. It rains downward, inside the plant, onto its own roots. Whole hillsides tick with that interior rain.

Between them grow feather-lichens in dense, upright copses, copper and violet and sulphur-yellow, frilled like coral, stiff with the iron and manganese they pull straight out of the young rock. They ring faintly when the wind brushes them. There are millions of acres of them. There are glass grasses that grow only in the lee of hot stones, and furnace mosses that keep a core temperature just above freezing by slowly, slowly burning sugars they spend the six-month day accumulating. If you cut one open — you shouldn't; everything will try to colonize the cut, including you — it steams.

The lushness is low, dense, and aggressive. It fills cracks as they open. It grows over fallen animals before they finish dying. It grows in the footprints of the great beasts, in the warm, mineral-rich slurry they leave. In the brief summer thaw, when dampness rises off the mat in a knee-high fog, the whole plateau becomes briefly, improbably tropical at ankle height and lethally arctic at head height. You walk with your boots in a warm wet jungle and your face in a freezer.

Because heat, here, is not given. It is manufactured.

The fauna have become industrial in order to live. Evolution, given a bright cold desert full of unweathered metal ores and violent updrafts, arrived independently at the turbine, the smelter, the battery, and the steam engine, over and over again.

The most common sound by day is the filtering choir. Along every ridge stand rows of gape-mouths — squat, barrel-bodied grazers, shoulder-high, plated in overlapping keratin vanes. They face into the wind with their heads split wide, and inside their throats a cartilaginous fan turns in the constant katabatic flow pouring off the peaks. It turns all their lives. It grinds airborne spores, ice-crystals, feather-lichen seeds, and the dense aerial plankton into paste, and the friction of it, geared down through a sternum like a crankshaft, works their blood around a body that would otherwise stop. When ten thousand of them are lined along a ridge, which they usually are, their fans tick and whirr in near-unison and the ridge hums like a power station. Every so often one clears its throat with a brassy, honking blast and a jet of white steam that condenses instantly into falling snow. Its own snow. It lands on its back and melts there.

Lower down, in the packed valleys, move the smelters.

They are slow beyond animal reckoning. A smelter is the size of a house, six-legged, its spine a long, arched retort of bone and deposited silica, its gut a literal furnace lined with bacterial cultures that can hold a dull red heat. It eats feather-lichen and iron-rich rock together, crushing them between millstone molars in a four-hour chewing cycle, and burns the one to refine the other. Pure metal collects in its lower chambers, cooled by circulated meltwater, and is periodically voided in heavy, ringing ingots of almost pure copper and nickel that fall from its flanks and stand steaming in the mat. The plants grow toward them at once; the ingots are heat-reservoirs that stay warm for days and shed a slow rain of condensation. Young smelters cluster around old ingots to be born beside them. Old smelters, dying, are mined by herds of smaller things while their furnace is still lit inside them. Their piston-hearts can be heard from miles away in the dense cold air: a thud. A long pause while pressures build. A hiss of valves in the neck. A thud again.

Nothing about them is fast. A herd may take a season to cross a valley you can see across. But because there are so many, because the valleys are choked with them flank to flank, hide to hide, the effect is ceaseless: a traffic jam of foundries, a river of industry flowing at glacial speed, bells and vents and grinding teeth overlapping into one continuous factory roar.

And above them, the air is not empty either.

Cold air should be thin at this altitude. The exhalations of so much life make it locally thick — warm, wet, loaded. Rising columns over smelter herds and furnace-moss fields carry whole ecosystems aloft: clouds of spark-moths with mica wings, each trailing a tiny electrostatic discharge; ballooning seed-spiders trailing conductive silk; blind, translucent kites the size of sails that live their entire lives in a thermal, turning slow propellers of ribbed membrane, generating enough current along their trailing nerves to keep their cores unfrozen and to stun the moth-clouds they trawl through. On bright days the sky darkens in strata. Wing above wing above wing, so dense that their shadows make a second, flickering twilight on the mat below and their collective wingbeat is a weather system — a low, constant thunder that never resolves into a single beat.

At night — and night, when it eventually comes, lasts for months — the light does not so much go out as change management. The plateau makes its own. Electric arcs crawl between lichen forests that have stored the day's charge in metallic fronds. Smelters glow at every joint. The moths become rivers of blue sparks flowing downhill along magnetic lines. The whole continent, seen from its own peaks, is a dark factory floor lit by furnace doors opening and closing in the bodies of animals.

Water exists here only as a temporary agreement. It condenses on cold stone at dawn and is immediately fought over. It pools inside bladder-plants. It runs, briefly and secretly, in heated channels under the mat, maintained by root systems that keep it just liquid. It erupts, perhaps twice a year, where a new fracture taps a pocket of geothermal steam, and then a hillside will be, for an afternoon, a true wetland — screaming with sudden frogs, with things hatching that have waited a decade as dry spores, with grazers crowding in to drink with their whole faces — before the vent seals with ice and the wetland is a meadow of trapped bubbles under a clear sheet, then a lichen forest again. Damp is the permanent condition. Wet is an event.

That is the rule that everything here obeys, and you learn it in your own body: *do not stop making heat, and do not ever be alone in the cold.* Solitary things freeze with their surfaces perfectly preserved. Things in crowds survive. So the smelters press together. So the gape-mouths line up vane to vane until their individual fans blur into a single wall of motion. So seedlings germinate preferentially inside footprints, inside dung, inside corpses, inside the cooling slag of their parents. So even the predators — and there are predators, glass-toothed things that skate on frozen condensation and strike at the warm seams between plates — even they hunt in coordinated, murmuring packs that share body-heat in rotating cores, their outer members developing frost-feathers that insulate the ones within. The density is not an abundance laid over the landscape. It is the landscape's central technology.

New cracks are opening all the time as the plateau continues to rise, a centimeter a year, sighing and booming in the night as stresses let go. Each crack exhales one hot, mineral breath and then begins, immediately, to be filled: first by steam, then by moss, then by insects arriving on the updraft, then by a queue of grazers waiting, patient as statues, for the lichen to thicken enough to eat. The mountain range is still being built, and it is being upholstered at the same speed.

Stand on any summit at noon, blinded, freezing, deafened by a million slow engines, and look out. To the horizon in every direction, ridge after serrated ridge, all of it ticking, steaming, ringing, grazing, refining, sparking — no smoke that was not first breath, no metal that was not first eaten, no light that is warm except the light that living things have trapped and taught to work. The vastness is not empty scale. It is scale that has been completely occupied, weather made by herds, geology interrupted by gardens, a continent-sized machine that was never assembled and will never be finished, running patiently, deafeningly, in the bright cold, at a walking pace no faster than ice.
