It starts as a sound behind the compressors.

Not the compressors themselves — those are steady, industrial, a low iron roar you feel in the molars — but something under them. A rustling that is not quite mechanical. A scratching, chirring, papery rain that rises and falls even when the line above has stopped.

You have to know where the hatch is. It is set flush in the factory floor at the lowest point of the building, behind a row of heat exchangers, painted the same grey as everything else, its handle filled with mineral dust. Lift it, and the smell comes first: cool stone, hot oil gone cold, ammonia dried to powder, the sweet reek of molted skins. No damp. Nothing wet has pooled here in years.

Below is a pit. Or, if you are small, a country.

It is perhaps three meters long and two across, and barely tall enough for a person to crouch in — and no person does. The factory was not built around it on purpose. The factory found it. Under the slab is an old solution hollow in the dolomite, a pothole scoured by meltwater before there were buildings, before there were fields here, worn for so long that erosion has finished its work. The floor is not rough. It is final. Stone planed to a dull, undulating flatness, edges softened to nothing, every crack rounded and then sealed by its own dust. Nothing here will get sharper, or deeper, or more dramatic. It will only get smoother.

The temperature drops as you descend the three iron rungs. Upstairs the plant is warm with motors. Down here it holds a permanent cellar cool, twelve degrees or so, breathing slowly through a ventilation slot that connects, eventually, to the outside air. Light comes the same way — ordinary daylight thinned through a grated shaft, and the yellow spill of a caged work-bulb left burning on the wall — enough to see color properly. Rust red. Limestone white. The black gloss of beetle wing-cases. The pale, almost translucent gold of things that have nevertheless never lived in true darkness.

There is no soil. There are no plants. Not a blade, not moss in the joints, not even the green film you expect in forgotten places, because there is no water to sustain it. A pipe that once wept into the sump was capped a decade ago. What falls now is dry: limestone dust sifted from the plant’s crushers, iron filings, flakes of paint, shreds of cardboard and paper label, dead moths drawn to the bulb, skin and hair from the boots that very rarely pass overhead. That fall is the weather. When the shift is running and the floor vibrates, it snows, very slowly, in grey.

And everything — everything — is animal.

The walls move. That is the first true impression, and it does not fade. Cave crickets crowd the upper stone by the hundreds, long antennae overlapping like a woven fringe, bodies hunched and camel-backed, the color of wet ash gone dry. At the slightest tremor they do not scatter so much as ripple, a single surface rearranging itself. Beneath them, in the middle band of the walls, cellar spiders have built so densely that their webs have merged into one continuous, trembling sheet, and in each cell a spider waits, legs folded, vibrating faintly when a cricket blunders close. Lower still, where the stone meets the floor, cockroaches move in loose, following herds — not swarming at random but trailing one another along old pheromone roads that shine faintly in the dust, pausing to groom, to feed, to mate, to eat their own dead before the dead have cooled.

The floor is a layered traffic. Earwigs in the seams. Silverfish flashing between flakes of insulation, quick as dropped mercury. Carpet beetle larvae, small and banded and indefatigable, working through a long-abandoned wool glove as if it were a forest. Centipedes — the big, red-headed kind, longer than a hand — patrol with an entirely different authority, and where one passes, the crickets make a lane. A shrew owns the far corner. You rarely see it whole, only the pointed, twitching snout testing the air from under a stack of firebricks, the blur as it takes a beetle and is gone again, its heart going so fast it seems to make its own small sound. Mice have worn their own highways in the dust along the north edge, polished runs between an overturned paint can, a split conduit, and the rusted lunch pail someone left here in another era and never came back for. Above, wedged where a disused pipe enters the ceiling, something larger shifts and sighs in its sleep — a single feral pigeon, sometimes two, heads tucked, their droppings long since dried to white mineral crust that the larvae mine grain by grain.

It is loud in a way that does not register until you are inside it. Stridulation from a thousand cricket legs. The dry hiss of cockroach wings. Webs ticking. Larvae chewing — actually audible, a faint, continuous rasp, when the machines above pause. Gnawing from the paint can. The shrew’s high, furious squeaks when a centipede crosses its threshold. The pigeon bursting awake at a dropped wrench upstairs, wings clapping once against stone, sending down a vortex of dust and feathers and startled insects, which sends up a fresh wave of chirring, which settles, eventually, back into the general din. The hollow is a perfect small amphitheater. Stone reflects everything and forgives nothing.

There is no standing water to drink, so the inhabitants drink each other. This is the rule that organizes the place. Moisture enters only locked inside bodies — a moth that grew fat somewhere green and far away, a mouse that licked condensation from a pipe three levels up, a worker’s sandwich crust dropped through the grate. It is passed, with ruthless efficiency, from gut to gut. Predators are reservoirs. The soft-bodied are currency. Cannibalism is not an aberration here; it is the rainy season. A cricket that dies at dawn is a husk by noon. A molted skin is eaten by its own former owner, who is eaten in turn with the skin still in its stomach. Even the spiders drink at their webs, taking back the silk when it tears, recycling protein that has been through the hollow a dozen times.

The scattered human things have long since stopped being human things. The toolbox, its lid rusted open, is a nursery cave, warm with beetle larvae. The junction boxes along the east wall vent a faint electrical heat and the mice press their bellies to them in winter. The paint can is a fortress that changes ownership by the hour — earwigs by day, a centipede coiled like a sleeping king by night. Someone once chalked a depth arrow and the initials J.M. on the wall, and spiders have webbed precisely along the chalk lines, as if following instructions.

Above it all the plant maintains its own indifferent seasons. The great air handler cycles and a wind pours through the vent shaft, bending the web-sheet as one sail, carrying in a few lost flies and a cold breath of outside. The handler stops and the air goes still and close, and mating begins in earnest, antennae everywhere testing the humid animal air. A forklift passes overhead and the whole population freezes for half a second — predator, prey, scavenger united in that ancient, inherited calculation — then erupts again. A bulb is replaced, eventually, by somebody who does not look down long enough to see what he is standing in, and for an hour there is a glorious, fatal summer around the new glass.

You could close the hatch and the place would not diminish. That is its completeness. It does not need the factory except as a roof and a slow rain of crumbs; it did not need the factory’s builders except to be accidentally preserved. It existed as smooth stone for millennia with nothing in it but wind. Now, in perhaps a cubic meter of cool, dry, ordinary light, it holds more lives per hand’s-breadth than a meadow — none of them plants, all of them eating, fleeing, courting, molting, dying, and being promptly, thoroughly eaten, in a noise like rain on a tin roof that never quite stops.

Put your palm flat on the floor before you climb out. The stone is cool and utterly worn and it vibrates, very faintly, not only with the machines.

With feet. Thousands of them, going about their business directly underneath yours.
