It is dark in a way that has nothing to do with nightfall.

You are already above weather. Above most air, in fact. The plateau comes up out of a sea of cloud that never breaks, a high, wind-planed country of black basalt and old ice, and for two or three months at a time the sun does not clear its rim. What light there is comes thin and indirect — starlight hardened by the thin atmosphere, a pale wash reflected up from the cloud deck far below, an aurora sometimes dragged across the zenith like breath on glass. It is enough to see shapes. Not enough to see color properly. Everything spends most of its life as silhouette and texture.

And it is cold with the finality of a place that has never been warm. Not storm-cold. Still-cold. The wind that built this place did its work long ago; now it only polishes. Terraces step down in broad, settled ledges, frost-shattered but ancient, the edges softened. Stretches of bare stone run for a kilometer without interruption, grooved by ice, patched with a skin of clear rime. Between them — nothing. No trees standing, no ruins, no road, no cairn. The emptiness is the main fact. You can walk for an hour and pass nothing taller than your ankle.

Then the ground drops, not far, ten or twenty meters into a sheltered gully — and the world inverts.

Lush is the only word. Despite the altitude, despite the dark and the cold that will split exposed skin in minutes, the gullies are choked. Black-green mats spill over the rock, yard after yard of low, dense growth pressed flat against stone to stay out of the upper wind. There are hanging gardens on the undersides of overhangs, where updrafts carry moisture and hold it clinging. There are ferns with leaves like inflated bladders, translucent and taut, that store the day’s meager gases and sigh them out at night. Moss forests no taller than a hand, but layered — canopy, understory, floor — all in miniature, beaded perpetually with condensation.

Water here is never a river, never a lake. Water is an event. It arrives as frost forming directly out of clear air onto leaves. It arrives as a brief, fine rain that falls in one gully and not the next. It collects in stone cups, in the hollow centers of rosette plants, in shallow peat pockets that freeze at the edges and stay liquid, black and tannin-dark, at the center where the earth’s residual heat and the density of living things keep it just above ice. Everything drinks quickly. Everything is built to catch a droplet before it becomes vapor again in the dry, high air.

That is why the plants have mouths.

Not metaphorically. The larger growths in the deep cuts — slow things, years to make a single ring of them, trunks like braided cable, bark metallic with iron — open seams along their length when the air changes pressure. Inside is a wet, warm darkness. Insects go in and do not always come out. Small blind voles, furred to the tips of their feet, nest in the root-chambers where it is ten degrees warmer than the surface and rich with a smell like hot copper and leaf-mold.

The fauna is abundant the way weather is abundant here: not evenly, but overwhelmingly when it comes. Most hours, you will see nothing move. Then a herd comes over a terrace in absolute quiet — long-legged, barrel-chested grazers bred by altitude itself, lungs so large their ribs show like hoops, blood nearly black with oxygen-carrying richness. They travel single file, heads down, scraping lichen with broad, horny tongues, their breath making brief clouds that the plants lean toward. Behind them, a living rain of moths, palm-sized, winged in dust that does not reflect light but absorbs it, making them visible only as moving holes in the stars. They pollinate in darkness, navigating by scent and by the faint electrical itch that all the larger plants give off when they are ready. Higher still, invisible until they stoop, are the plateau’s predators: gliding things that have traded true flight for falling with intent across the drop-offs, silent because there is barely enough air to carry a cry, and because a cry would be wasted anyway.

Which brings the sound — or its absence. Up here sound starves. The air is too thin to be a good servant. A shout flattens within meters. Stone does not echo; frost eats it. The grazers’ hooves on ice, the drip from a thawing lip of moss, the tick and creak of contracting rock — these are the loudest ordinary things, and even they seem to happen slightly inside your own head. Walking, you become aware of your pulse, your breath, the crepitation of your own joints.

And then you become aware of a vibration, not heard but felt — in the fillings of your teeth, in the soles of your boots, a low industrial regularity that has no source in any natural thing.

They come over the rim at intervals no clock keeps.

The machines are the size of houses, and there are no houses here. No depots, no rails, no landings, no structures of any kind for them to return to. They simply circulate. Crawlers, mostly, broad-treaded and low-slung, their hulls scoured to bare alloy on the windward side and furred with living growth on the lee. Some walk on articulated legs that place each foot with terrible gentleness among the stone cups so as not to crush them. Some are airborne in the way only a thing built for thin air can be — vast, slow dirigibles whose envelopes are patched with grown membrane, whose propellers turn once, pause, turn again, conserving.

They are industrial in every particular: heat-exchangers, condenser vanes, ore-sorters, drilling heads folded like the arms of sleeping insects, smelting chambers that glow not with visible light but with a heat-shimmer you feel on your face in the dark. They intake the high atmosphere and the surface frost and the black peat and they refine — water out of air, metals out of lichen-rich soil, gases out of plant-waste — and they excrete, as they go, a warm mineral mist from their trailing vents.

That mist is the climate.

Where a crawler passes, condensation blooms for hours afterward in its track. The bladder-ferns inflate. The moss forests stand up, suddenly, obscenely green in infrared. The grazers alter course days in advance to intercept a machine’s predicted route, because the grazing after one has passed is ten times richer. The moths gather around the condenser vanes in their thousands, drinking the pure water that beads there, and the gliders gather around the moths. Even the machines themselves are part of the grazing: between runs they go torpid on the high terraces, and the voles clean their intakes, and specialized birds with beaks like wrenches pick parasites of ice and rust from their joints, and a fine red lichen spreads across any hull that slows too long — which the next torpor’s attendants eat, extracting the iron, seeding the soil when they die.

No one collects what they refine. That is the strangest part, and after a while up here it stops seeming strange. The water is given to the air. The ingots, when a crawler’s hold is full, are simply set down, very precisely, on the bare terraces: neat bars of nickel and copper and something pale that does not corrode. Within a season the wind has frosted them. Within a decade the lichen has found the seams. Within a century they have become low, metallic boulders, indistinguishable from the other weathered stone, and the peat under them is the richest on the plateau. The machines do not build because building is not in their loop. They were made, presumably, to extract and to sustain the extraction — and the extraction fell away, and the sustaining remained: endless, mobile, purposeless industry as an ecological force, like a river that happens to be made of engines.

So the whole place is in motion, though it so rarely makes a noise. Machines circle, herds circle after them, predators circle after the herds, plants bloom and close and digest and seed in the windows between passes, frost forms and sublimes and forms again, the peat pockets freeze inward and thaw outward once per machine-cycle rather than once per day. Even the stone participates: uplift out of the deep earth is still, very slowly, raising this plateau, while ice and machines and iron-lichen between them wear it down at an almost identical rate, so that the terraces you stand on are neither young nor disappearing — they are maintained. A landscape kept, inadvertently, in balance by things that do not know they are keeping it.

The rule of the place, if you stay long enough to learn it, is that nothing up here survives on its own. The plants would freeze hard as glass without the waste heat and water. The animals would starve without the plants’ machine-fed flush, and suffocate the plants without their breath and dung and pollinating dark wings. The machines would clog and ice and rust to death in a single winter without the small creatures that clean them and the lichens that keep their hulls chemically awake. Separately, every one of them is impossible at this altitude, in this cold, in this long starlit dark.

Together, they make a valley-sized engine out of flesh and alloy and moss, running almost silently on top of the world, in the empty spaces between its passes — waiting, with all its abundant, interlocked life, for the next faint vibration to come up through the stone.
