At first it does not look high. It looks low and enclosed and wet, like the bottom of somewhere.

You come up the last of the cable — not riding, walking beside it where the service path has been cut into the ridge — and the ridge suddenly stops being a ridge. It opens. Or rather, it cups. A bowl no wider than a cathedral nave, forty paces from lip to lip, scooped out of the very top of the mountain. Below the rim, on every side, the world falls away for thousands of meters in blue, empty air. Above the rim, nothing. You are higher than the clouds that are moving underneath you. And inside the cup, improbably, it is green and steaming and loud.

The mountain is young here. That is the first thing your hands learn. The rock that forms the rim is not rounded or lichened smooth. It is black basalt in fresh teeth, sixty, maybe seventy years out of the earth, still in the act of being a mountain. It breaks in plates and blades, edges that will cut a boot sole, shards that ring faintly when you knock them. In places the stone is still glassy. In places it has split overnight from frost and the split is pale inside, unweathered, like a broken bone. Nothing up here has had time to be worn down.

The bowl was not worn either. It was blasted and bolted. That is where the water comes through.

A notch in the southern rim funnels the wind. All day the cloud-sea that hides the valleys pushes against the peak and, finding this one low place, pours through it. Not fast. Slow, patient, immensely heavy air sliding through a gap. The station was built to intercept it.

Three condenser towers stand in a row across the notch, not tall — this whole place resists tall things — but dense, riveted, industrial in the close, working sense. Iron frames wrapped in fine steel mesh, mesh wrapped again in coarser netting. Fan housings the size of millstones. Copper pipe, gone green at the joints. A low powerhouse half-sunk into the basalt, its roof a sheet of corrugated iron weighted with stones, its walls blackened.

The fans turn slowly. Once, twice per breath. You would think that would be quiet. It is not. The scale of the air they are moving makes a continuous roar, a tearing, rushing sound as cloud is dragged through mesh and duct and down into the cold chambers. Under it there is a deeper note from the turbines in the powerhouse floor, and under that the constant chatter and tick of water. Drips become runs, runs become a single iron flume, the flume drops six meters through the turbine and vanishes into a cistern under the grating. You have to shout to be heard standing next to another person. The sound does not echo so much as it stays, trapped by the rim, turning over on itself until your ribs learn it.

And because of it — because of the condensing, the leaking heat, the water that never quite leaves — the bowl is lush.

This should be bare rock and thin moss at this altitude. Outside the rim, it is. A few meters beyond the towers, the basalt is naked except for orange and grey lichens holding on in the lee. Inside, the air is kept warm. Not tropical, but warm for a summit: shirtsleeve warm at noon, smelling of hot oil and wet earth and ozone from the busbars. Steam from the powerhouse vents mixes with real cloud in the notch until you cannot tell which is which. Every surface condenses. The mesh weeps steadily. The pipes sweat. The rock faces on the north side cry in thin, continuous sheets down into beds of moss a hand's thickness deep.

So things grow, pressed together, layered. Moss over stone, liverwort over moss, small hard ferns forcing out of cracks where warm wastewater leaks. Someone, decades ago, must have brought up soil in sacks on the cable, and brought up plants that had no business here: broad-leafed groundcover from the low valleys, a kind of mint that has gone entirely feral, even a stunted fig that lives against the powerhouse wall where the exhaust pipe runs. Its leaves are huge and always wet. They drip onto each other. Algae paints the inside of the open settling tank a startling orange-green. Where a valve leaks — and several valves always leak — there is a perfect vertical garden on the iron, roots finding the rust itself.

Everything is near-touching. You walk a path of steel grating laid over stone, and to your left a tank sweats an inch from your shoulder, to your right fern fronds brush your hip, overhead a low pressure line ticks with heat, underfoot through the grating you can see the dark cistern water moving, very slow, black and full of reflected sky. Vines have learned the cable stays. Moss has learned the fan guards, and has to be scraped, daily when anyone is here, or it would choke them in a week.

There is not much built, in the architectural sense. One hut, stone at the bottom and iron at the top, where the maintenance crew sleeps two to a bunk when they are up for the rotation. A privy that vents heroically into the drop. A store of oil drums, lashed down. Painted markers, white and red, hammered into the basalt to show where the buried pipes run and where not to step because the rock is hollowed underneath. Everything else is machine: pumps, filters in banked rows, a diesel generator that shakes the grating through your boots even at idle, switch cabinets humming and clicking to themselves, gauges with clouded glass, a long tool bench open to the air and rusting and oiled at the same time.

Fauna has mostly declined the invitation. The thin air and the noise keep it away. A pair of choughs nests somewhere in the rim-teeth and commutes, screaming, over the bowl in the brief clear hour before sunset, red bills bright against the cloud. Large pale moths find the powerhouse lamps at night and batter themselves, improbably far from any tree. Once in a while a single bee, blown up on the same wind that feeds the condensers, blunders around the fig in a panic until it dies. Mostly the living things are the plants, and the people passing through, and the microbial orange slime that blooms and collapses in the tanks with the seasons.

The rules here are not posted, exactly, but they accumulate in you as you stay.

Stay on the grating. The moss looks solid and is not; under it is either sharp new rock or a pipe.

Do not turn anything off. Not the fans, not the circulation pump that pushes warm water through the pipes under the beds. The lushness is not natural to this altitude, it is a side effect on life support. In still air at this height it can go below freezing in twenty minutes even in summer. The plants, the fig, even the moss in its deepest layers, would blacken if the engines stopped for a night. The station keeps itself alive by wasting heat.

Drink only from the blue tap. Every other water in the bowl has passed through something — mesh, copper, engine jacket, tank. The blue tap is the finished product, and it tastes of stone and almost nothing. It is what all this is for. From the cistern, a single fat pipe leaves the bowl through the eastern rim and begins its impossible descent, bracketed to cliffs all the way to the farms you cannot see below the cloud. The whole small, loud, green place exists to squeeze drinking water out of passing weather and send it down.

And go slowly. That is the other rule, imposed by air and by machinery both. At this height your lungs take a shallow share of every breath. The fans take theirs slowly. The cloud comes slowly. Even the water, for all its noise, moves through filters and settling chambers at a walking pace. Nothing here can be hurried without breaking. The daily cycle is a long inhalation in the morning when the cloud-sea rises and the towers vanish in white and the drip-rate doubles and doubles again, a holding at midday when the sun — normal, hard, high-altitude sun — burns the bowl clear for an hour and every wet leaf steams and the basalt ticks as it expands, and a long exhalation in the evening when the pumps pulse harder to clear the cistern before night.

At night, if you sleep in the hut, you hear it change. The fans slow further. The fig leaves tick with dripping condensation. The choughs are silent in the rocks. The generator settles to a heartbeat that comes up through the floor and through the stone itself, as if the young mountain had learned a second, iron pulse, and was trying it out.
