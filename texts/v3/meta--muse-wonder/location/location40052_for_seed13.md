There is a city in a high, closed valley called Ilvarra, and the first strange thing about it is that you can see all of it at once.

You come over the pass before you know you have reached it — a sharp notch cut between two young ridges, the rock still black and angular, barely softened by lichen — and the whole basin opens below. It is not large enough to be a country. It is the size of a town, or one long afternoon's walk end to end, but it has the completeness of a country anyway: its own ring of peaks, its own weather held inside it, its own hundred small waters, its own way of keeping time, if keeping time is the right phrase for a place that refuses to change.

It is always late morning in Ilvarra.

Not morning-like. Not a light that suggests morning. Late morning itself, permanently: a clear, ordinary, blue daylight falling at the same angle it has always fallen, the shadows of the towers lying in the same short wedges across the same stones, bright enough to work by, bright enough to show dust motes in a doorway, never brightening toward noon and never thinning toward evening. At high elevation the light should feel thin and pitiless. Here it is unexpectedly kind. The air is cool — cool enough for wool sleeves, for breath to show faintly when you speak in shade, for dew to keep its beads on north-facing walls — but the sun on your forearm is pleasantly warm, and that balance, like everything else, does not drift.

Because nothing here drifts.

The peas are always at the same height in the terrace gardens. Certain trees in the orchard terraces always have blossom on their east branch, green fruit on the south, ripe fruit ready for picking on the west — all at once, always. When a fruit is taken, the awareness that another will be in its place does not even rise to the level of hope; it is furniture-fact, like a chair being where a chair is. The scree slopes below the outer cliffs, made of fresh, knife-edged fragments where the young mountains broke, never slide. Rain, in the usual sense, does not happen. Instead moisture condenses out of the cool air at a steady, unhurried rate: on slate roofs, on the undersides of rope bridges, on the broad leaves of the terrace plants, on the carved stone gutters that run beside every street. Every roof drips from the same corner into the same barrel, and every barrel stands perpetually three quarters full. Nobody in Ilvarra has ever seen one overflow, or empty.

It is damp without being wet. That is the governing quality of the water here. There is no great river. The valley floor is a chain of still ponds, each one walled in dressed stone, each one exactly reflecting its fragment of sky, linked by narrow channels no wider than a handspan where water moves with a kind of formal slowness. Frogs live in the reed edges, small green ones with gold throats, and they are silent most of the time, which adds to their numbers when you do notice them: twenty, thirty, sitting along a pond rim like a decorative border that occasionally blinks. Dragonflies hang over the channels. Under woven frames on the upper streets — long nets of plant-fiber rope strung between poles, hand-knotted, repaired by hand — mist gathers and runs down in threads into clay pipes. Those are the cloud-fields, and tending them is a respected trade, though the yield never improves and never fails.

Ilvarra is a true city, for all that. It is not a scatter of huts that got ambitious. It has districts, and the districts have purposes, and the purposes are enforced less by law than by the fact that alternatives seem physically implausible.

There is the lower quarter, where the ground is cut into green terraces: beans, squash, herbs, low fruit trees trained flat against sun-warmed walls. Then the residential terraces, climbing in broad steps, houses of white quartzite and dark basalt, each one spaced well apart from the next, with moss gardens and fenced yards between them, so that the city fills the valley yet never feels crowded: urban in organization, sparse in body. Above that, the workshops — weavers, potters, masons, rope-makers, woodworkers — arranged along a single long stepped avenue. Above them the granaries and the cloud-fields and the watch platforms, which nobody watches from, because there has never been anything to watch for: no army has climbed the pass, no fire has spread, no flood has imagined itself. The platforms are used for hanging laundry and for children to eat plums and look at the whole design of their lives spread out under them.

The buildings are layered, as old cities are, but not overgrown. A house sits on the foundation wall of an earlier house, which has become a terrace, which has become a garden wall, which has become the back of a cistern. You can read the strata in one glance down a street. And yet no wall is crumbling, no stair is worn concave. The stone is sharp-cornered, freshly dressed-looking, as if the masons had just set down their chisels. They are, in a sense, always just setting down their chisels. Ilvarra maintains itself the way a held note maintains itself: not by stillness inside the note, but by an exact and continuous replacement. Every family knows which roof slates are theirs to check, which section of channel, which bridge rope. Work is constant, unhurried, and finishes exactly where it began.

Their technology stops at a very particular line, and stops there cleanly. They have good hand-tools: iron chisels and adzes and saws, copper vessels, bronze fittings where bronze is useful, looms, potter's wheels turned by foot, grain mills turned by the broad, patient mountain goats that wander the city as if mildly employed by it. They have pulleys, levers, aqueducts, kilns, well-designed drainage. They have no engines, no clocks, nothing that ticks or combusts or accumulates force. Nothing needs to. A waterwheel on the main channel turns at one speed and has presumably always turned at one speed, lifting its little clay cups and pouring them out into a trough for the dye-works, *plip, plip, plip*, a sound so regular that locals hear it the way you hear your own pulse — which is to say, only when they listen for it.

Mostly, the city is quiet. This is partly altitude and partly design and partly custom. The streets are surfaced in stone but grown over in the joins with dense green moss, so hooves and sandals and cartwheels — the carts have solid wooden wheels, padded with felt — arrive muffled. There are no metal bells. Signals are given with wooden clappers, soft as knuckles on a door. People speak low outdoors, not from reverence but because the young cliffs around the basin are so sheer that a raised voice comes back off them too clearly, and Ilvarrans consider an echo a kind of rudeness, a voice imposing itself twice. The houses have thick walls and interior rooms with heavy woven shutters where true darkness is kept, like water in a jar, for sleeping. Night is not a time in Ilvarra. Night is a substance you go indoors to use. Families sleep in shifts. Children are rocked in shuttered cradles. The swifts that nest by the thousand under the eaves tuck themselves into that manufactured dark in rotations, so there are always some asleep in the shadowed stonework and some stitching silently through the unmoving daylight, eating insects that are also always there.

Because the fauna, for a place so hushed, is abundant to the point of comedy. You simply have to adjust to how much of it is quiet. Moths the size of saucers rest on warm walls, patterned like lichen, opening and closing their wings slowly. Bees work the terrace flowers in a low continuous hum that reads more as texture than sound. Marmots live in the spaces between foundation stones and emerge to sit upright in the sun like small, furred citizens waiting for a meeting that will never be called. Goats — white, black, amber-eyed, bells replaced by little wooden toggles that knock softly — use the flat roofs as a second street system, and householders leave the roof-gates open for them by ancient agreement, because the goats keep the roof-moss trimmed and the roof-moss, in turn, keeps the houses cool and quiet and damp-proof. Lizards run the warm south walls. A colony of pale owls lives in the granary towers and hunts, in the permanent daylight, animals that have evolved to be hunted by owls in permanent daylight, mostly the large, slow, delicious-looking moths. No population booms. No population crashes. The pigeons are always fat. The pond-frogs are always exactly as numerous as pond-frogs should be, plus perhaps one.

The vegetation is lusher than altitude and coolness should allow, and it knows its boundaries with unsettling precision. Below the houses: fern banks in the shaded channels, strawberries running along path edges, stands of blue-flowered herbs, dense cushion-moss in greens so saturated they look wet even when dry to the touch. Above the houses, where the soil thins: dark, stunted conifers, sharp as the rock they grow from, their needles permanent and resinous. Vines have been trained over the pergolas of the workshop avenue for as long as records go back — and the records, kept on baked clay tablets in the granary archive, go back in an orderly hand for centuries, describing, year after year, a city in excellent condition, harvests adequate, bridges sound, cloud-fields yielding as expected. A vine in Ilvarra does not overtake its pergola. A garden does not go weedy at the edges. The meadow between the residential terraces and the pond-chain ends in a line as clean as a hem, grass on one side, paving on the other, and if you sit and watch that line for an hour in the unchanging light, with the dripping from the roofs going *plip, plip* into the three-quarter barrels and a goat crossing a rooftop in the middle distance, it will become apparent that the line is not maintained by anyone you can see. It is simply where the meadow is, in the way that the ponds are where the water is and the morning is where the light is.

Visitors — there are few; the pass is steep, the scree unhelpfully sharp, and there is very little to trade that Ilvarra does not already have in adequate, unvarying supply — visitors tend to become agitated around what would be, at home, dusk. The body waits for the light to do something. The light declines to. People weep, sometimes, from pure physiological bewilderment, sitting on the shuttered porches while Ilvarrans bring them soup and show them, kindly, how to close the heavy curtains and make a night with their hands.

And then, after a while, the agitation passes. That is perhaps the deepest rule of the place: nothing in you is permitted to accumulate either. You eat when the household eats, from stores that are always partly used and always replenished. You sleep in the good manufactured dark and wake into the same late morning, the same cool air, the same smell of wet stone and resin and crushed herbs, the same swift-shadow crossing the same wall. You learn the wooden signals. You stop raising your voice. Your coat stays damp at the cuffs. A marmot begins to recognize you, or you begin to believe it does, and in Ilvarra there may not be much difference. The city does not grow, does not shrink, does not age, does not surprise. It simply continues being exactly, luxuriantly, quietly itself — a town held in perfect balance on top of the young mountains, in a bowl of green terraces and still water and white stone, under a light that has finished arriving and sees no reason to leave.
