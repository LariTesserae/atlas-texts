From the salt flat above, there is nothing to see.

Just a white, cracked plain at the bottom of the basin, below the level of where a sea would be, shimmering in heat that rises in visible sheets. Then a fault in the salt, no wider than a doorway, exhaling. The air that comes out is hotter than the desert, and it smells of baked clay, resin, and old smoke. If you step into the crack and down the worn stair — thirty-one steps, each dished in the middle by feet that are no longer here — the light fails very quickly behind you, and the sound begins.

That is the first surprise. This hidden place is loud.

The city fits inside a single hollow. Perhaps eighty meters across, sixty deep, an inverted bell of rock and hardened earth. It is a pocket, sealed from the plain by its own roof of stone, lit only by a crooked chimney-slit far above where the sun enters as a dull, amber diffusion, never a direct beam. Everything below lives in a permanent, hot twilight, dim as the inside of a kiln after the fire has been left to think.

And yet it was built as a city, complete.

Terraces are cut into the circular wall in five rising tiers, connected by switchback stairs, rope-bridge anchors, and narrow lanes that follow the curve. Houses crowd the perimeter — low, domed, made of sun-fired brick, river stone that must have been carried down by hand, and a black, glassy slag used sparingly for lintels. There are districts, legible even under ruin: a potters' quarter where wheels still sit in rows under collapsed awnings, their clay long since fired by ambient heat alone; a weavers' quarter with stone looms threaded with nothing; a market ring with stalls of stacked plank and reed matting, each counter grooved where knives and scales were set down for centuries. Aqueducts arc confidently from tier to tier. Chanels, cisterns, great bellied jars set into floors. All of them are dry to the dust at the bottom. Not damp. Not stained with recent use. The wells at the lowest tier have been capped with slabs incised with a simple repeated mark: a circle, crossed out.

No water ever reaches here now. The rock sweats only mineral vapor from fissures in the floor. It precipitates in faint crusts — sulfur yellow at the edges, salt white where it cools, a rust-red dust that collects in corners despite the motion of the air.

In the center, there is nothing.

That is the city's second rule. For all its layered construction, all its dense invention around the walls, the middle is a vast, empty plaza of cracked clay, smooth as a threshing floor. Nothing is built there. Nothing grows there. The heat at its center is concentrated by the shape of the hollow, pooling on the stone until small, clear mirages break and reform above it all day. Pebbles tick as they expand. The emptiness is not abandonment — the plaza was swept, edged with a low curb of polished stones, kept clear on purpose. The city turns its back to its own center, houses facing the wall, doorways opening onto the terraces. Only their backs, and their hanging gardens, face the void.

Because the vegetation has chosen the walls.

It is lush here in a way that should require rain. Vines have filled every unoccupied window, looping through roof beams and down the lanes in heavy, slack curtains. Fern-like growths spill from terrace planters in fans three meters wide. Trees — if they can be called trees — grow horizontally from cracks above the third tier, their trunks flattened against the rock for support, their crowns spreading like umbrellas to catch what light they can. The leaves are thick, dry, and startlingly green-black, with a waxy, ceramic clatter when they strike one another. Cut one, and it does not bleed sap. Inside is a fibrous, oily pulp, hot to the touch, smelling faintly of cooked nuts and pepper. The roots do not seek moisture. They seek warmth. They follow the hot seams in the stone, they crowd around chimney vents, they have pried apart bricks to get closer to the fissures where mineral air rises fastest. In the dimness they grow away from the light shaft and toward the floor.

There is no animal life to eat them. No insects stipple the leaves. No birds nest in the eaves, no lizards warm themselves on the steps, no droppings whiten the ledges. The plants are unbitten, unpollinated, perfect and faintly obscene in their intactness. They reproduce by heat alone — resinous pods swell through the long hot hours, tightening in the rising air, until the pressure splits them with a sharp report. Seeds, light as ash and edged with dry membranes, are thrown upward on the thermal column that perpetually rises over the empty plaza, then drift sideways and lodge in a crack, a planter, an abandoned room. In the stillest part of the cycle a hundred pods can go off in a minute, a ragged volley echoing around the bell of the city until you feel it in your teeth.

That column is the engine for everything else. This place is never still.

By what passes for morning, when the slit brightens from brown to dull gold, the hollow begins to breathe in earnest. Hot air lifts off the plaza, pulls cooler — only relatively cooler — air down the entrance stair, and wakes the city’s instruments. The builders were primitive in the best and strictest sense: stone, wood, clay, rope, simple bronze. They had no engines, so they tuned the city itself. Fluted pipes of fired clay are built into the terrace walls at different lengths, open-mouthed to the draft. As the column strengthens they begin to sound, not in melody exactly, but in chords that shift as doors warp open and shut on their own, covering and uncovering smaller vents. Hanging bridges sway and creak on their ropes. Unlatched shutters applaud. Chains lift a fraction on their pulleys and settle again. Somewhere high on the fourth tier a great seed-drum, a hollow log bound in reed, has rolled against a house wall and booms irregularly whenever the wind takes the vine that holds it.

The erosion here is not decay from storm or flood. It is settlement into a final shape. Brick edges have been rounded by hot wind carrying fine grit, year after year. Stairs shine where plant oils have polished them. Wood has not rotted — there is nothing here to rot it — it has simply dried into a substance like horn, splintering in long, clean sheets. Roofs have sagged but not fallen, held up by the very vines that burden them. New leaves push stones a millimeter wider apart. A root finds a mortar line and follows it for ten meters, flowering inside a bedroom. The city and its garden are slowly exchanging places, without violence.

Down on the lowest tier, behind the capped wells, a single long room has been left intentionally empty of plants. Its doorway is still cleared, its floor still gritty but bare. Inside, in stone bins, are seeds sorted by size into compartments, each labeled with a pitted clay tablet. A cold kiln, a stack of hand tools, stone blades worn to crescents, a bundle of pruning hooks. A storehouse against a future that may not need water at all. The tablets farther back change their marks. Early ones show rain, a jar, a sprout. Later ones show a vent, a sprout; a sun crossed out; a leaf turned downward, toward the earth. They were learning the new rules before they left, or before they stopped being separate from this place in some way that left no bones behind.

Late in the cycle, when the slit darkens, the process reverses in miniature. The plaza gives back its heat more slowly than the walls. Air sinks. Pipes fall silent one register at a time, from the shortest to the longest, so the city sighs downward. Leaves that opened flat to the draft fold into tight, overlapping scales with a sound like distant rain that will never fall. Pods cease firing. Bricks tick as they contract, bridges settle, and for a short interval you can hear the smallest things: dust sifting from a ceiling, a seed coming to rest on a stone counter, your own breath going dry in your throat.

Then the heat gathers again under the roof, patient and unspent, and the column begins, almost imperceptibly, to turn.
