It is not the peaks that make it high.

You climb for almost two days to get there — through pine forest that gets shorter and thicker as you go, through switchbacks cut into slopes that get smoother and rounder the higher they rise — and then, just when you expect the road to sharpen into something alpine, it stops climbing altogether. It levels. It keeps leveling. The world opens out into a vast, worn-down table of stone so old that whatever mountains once stood here have forgotten how to be mountains.

This is the Merrow Planum. Four thousand meters up, and almost perfectly flat.

Not featureless. Ancient. Every outcrop is bowed and polished, every boulder sunk halfway into the earth like a knuckle. There are no cliffs on the plateau itself, no scree, no fresh rockfall. Rain and wind and ice worked here for so long that they ran out of sharp edges to take. What is left is a peneplain of dark gneiss and peat, dimpled with thousands of shallow hollows, each one holding a little black water after the mist.

And the mist is the climate.

It is cool here even at noon — breath visible, fingers stiff inside gloves — and dim under a sky that is rarely clear blue. Light arrives diffused, silvered, low-angled. For half the day the plateau sits just below the cloud deck, and for the other half it sits *in* it. A white river will be visible hours away, crawling across the flat from the west, and then it is upon the town all at once: streets vanish after thirty meters, roofs drip, stone sweats, the air turns to fine rain without ever quite falling.

That is when everything wakes up.

The town — also called Merrow, fifty thousand people spread across perhaps twenty kilometers of plateau — is built to be rained on from *inside* a cloud. It is dense in a way that has nothing to do with height. Houses of rounded black stone press shoulder to shoulder, three and four stories, their upper floors corbelled out over lanes too narrow for carts. Workshops take the ground floors, dwellings above them, cisterns below them, and gardens on top of them, so that a single building contains four climates stacked. Covered walkways stitch roof to roof. Copper gutters, glass condenser fins, tilted slate planes, and broad sheets of oiled canvas angle off every facade, all of them beading, running, ticking with collected water.

There is no river to supply Merrow. There are only pockets, seeps, bog pools, and what can be combed from the air. So surface area is wealth. Old families own the great north-facing condenser walls down by the mills, a hundred meters of finned metal that weep steadily into pipes whenever the cloud comes through. Newer cooperatives string nets between chimneys. Children are sent up before dawn to wipe the roof-slates with sponges and wring them into buckets, a chore as ordinary here as sweeping.

Because of the cold, the vegetation does not grow tall, but it grows everywhere, lush to the point of insistence. Cushion moss a foot thick swallows walls to the second story and is never stripped — it insulates, it holds the stone together, it slows the runoff. Ferns unroll from gutter joints overnight. White and orange lichen forests creep across the ancient boulders at the town's edge. In the hollows outside the lanes, bog cotton, sundews, dwarf willow, and black sedge make a wet meadow that is always moving: pools rise an inch in an hour of cloud, spill in bright threads to the next hollow, drain again when the wind finds the plateau and the sun comes through pale and watery. In late spring the moss sporulates all at once after a mist and the air over the roofs turns faintly green-gold, spores drifting like slow smoke between the chimneys.

Under Merrow, doing the patient work that erosion started, is industry.

The old mountains washed themselves down into these basins. For millions of years their metal came to rest in the peat and silt of the plateau, concentrated grain by grain. The factories of Merrow — low, long, electrified sheds of stone and soot-dark brick down in the western district — are sieving that inheritance. Bog mud arrives on narrow-gauge electric trams, is washed, shaken, magnetically sorted, roasted in furnaces that glow amber behind small windows. Tin, copper, a little cobalt, a surprising amount of iron. The machines are powerful but strangely quiet; everything electric, everything belted and housed in moss-thatched sheds because wet air carries sound badly and the town learned early not to add to it. What you hear instead is process at a smaller scale: dripping eaves, the tick of cooling metal, trams humming on wet rails, peat bubbling softly in the worked cuttings outside town, doors closing against the damp.

And between factory shifts, the town runs on other cycles just as active. Condenser sluices are opened and shut with the cloud tides. Markets set up under canvas only in the two clear hours around mid-afternoon, then pack away before the evening inundation. Laundry is hung in heated drying-lofts that vent sweet, steamy air into the lanes. The big slow windmills on the eastern rim — where the plateau finally breaks, far out of sight of most streets, into a dizzying fall to the lowlands — turn only when the cloud river is moving, which is how people tell the weather without looking up.

Fauna manages to be both adapted and scarce. There are frogs in every cistern, small and black and almost silent except after the heaviest mists, when they call once, all together. There are town dogs, thick-coated and indoors by preference. Beyond that, sightings are events. A plateau hare, white well into summer. A crane that has mistaken a bog pool for a lake on its migration and has to wait, furious and enormous in the fog, for clearance to leave. The children ring handbells when lammergeiers ride the rim wind in from below, not to scare them but to announce them: something truly wild has bothered to come this high.

The strangeness of Merrow is not dramatic. It accumulates. Plants here grow not toward the sun — the light is too scattered for that loyalty — but toward the nearest condenser fin, where the drip is reliable, so window boxes lean at odd, thirsty angles away from the sky. On completely flat ground, with a cloud below the rim, the town can become an island at dawn: streets bright-ish and dripping, horizon a sea of white, no earth visible in any direction. Sound behaves badly in saturated air; a factory whistle on the far side of town will arrive intimate and close, while the person speaking beside you on an open roof can sound muffled, as if underwater. And the ancient boulders — people build around them rather than move them, not from superstition, exactly, but from experience. Moss-covered, rounded, lichen-crusted, they hold the day's cold long after the air warms. Put your palm to one at sunset and it is still noon-cool, still wet, faintly metallic to the touch.

The rules grew out of all of this without ever needing to be written very formally. You do not seal a wall completely; it must be allowed to drink and dry or it will rot from inside. You do not take moss from a neighbor's north face. Your right to water is measured not by your well — no one has a well in stone this shallow and impermeable — but by the square meters you keep clean, angled, and ready for the cloud. Noise after the evening mist is a kind of theft, because quiet is how you hear the gutters fill, the sluices start, a frog chorus warn of a pool about to overtop.

By night the factories dim to a few sulfurous windows, the trams stop, and Merrow waits in the cold dark for the next white river to find it. The roofs are already cold. The fins are already angled. In the hollows beyond the last lane, the black pools are rising by fractions, and the moss is drinking, and the old, flat stone at the top of the world is doing what it has done longer than the mountains lasted: holding still while everything wet and living moves across it.
