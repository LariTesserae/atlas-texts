It is not visible from the plateau.

The plateau itself is the first fact, and it goes on too long to be interesting at first glance. High, warm, worn almost completely flat — an old mountain range that has finished becoming something else. What were peaks are now broad, sun-baked swells of pale limestone and pink grit, rounded to the contour of a shoulder, a knee. The stone is split into plates the size of tables, their edges softened, their surfaces pitted with a million shallow cups. Wind has polished them. Rain, when it used to fall more often, polished them differently. Now they just heat under a normal white sun and tick as they cool.

There is very little up here. Scrub in cracks. Ants. A dry smell of hot rock and resin. Your breath is short; the elevation is in your ears and in the quickness of your pulse, even though there is no climb left to speak of. Everything is eroded down to its final opinion.

Then, at no marked place, the ground opens.

Not a canyon, not a valley. A pocket. A single sunken bowl about forty meters across and twelve deep, as if a thumb pressed into the plateau and left. From ten steps away you cannot see it. From the rim, the whole interior drops into view at once, and the sound arrives before you have time to interpret what you are seeing.

It is loud inside. Not loud the way the plateau is loud with wind. Dense loud. Enclosed loud.

You cross something at the rim — there is no wall, no fence, but your ears pop and a faint pressure brushes the skin of your face, and suddenly the outside wind is gone and all of that noise is around you. A shimmer hangs there, barely visible when the light catches dust in it: a containment meniscus, still working. It keeps warm, damp air in and dry plateau air out. It also, in some way current instruments only describe by apologizing, keeps sound in, folding it back on itself in phased layers. In the hollow you can hear your own footstep meet you a fraction of an instant before you feel it through your boot. It is not quite an echo. It is more like the place rehearses you.

Three pylons make the field. That much is clear.

They are scattered around the bowl — small, by the standards of what they do. One upright near the north wall, one tilted thirty degrees into soft soil, one fallen and half-grown-over by orchid roots. Each is a dull black mast no taller than a person, faceted, cold to the touch even at noon. No cables, no panels, no hum you can locate in your body. At intervals they produce the pylons' only visible activity: the air above their tips thickens, beads, and pulls moisture straight out of the sky in trembling, suspended droplets. The droplets do not fall so much as decide to. At night, witnesses say, some of them fall upward again.

There is no river here. There is no spring. Water is at  a dampness — in soil that darkens your palm, in stone cups that hold a mouthful after the morning pulse, in broad leaves cupped to catch drip, in condensation that runs in runnels down the shaded wall and vanishes before it reaches the bottom. At dawn the pylons pulse six, seven times, and mist precipitates in sheets inside the field, rain that never belonged to a cloud. By nine it is gone from the air and stored in plants and hollows and animal bodies. By noon the ground is dry on top and wet two fingers down. That has been enough, for a very long time, to make this one pocket lush while the whole plateau above starves politely in the sun.

The vegetation does not carpet the bowl. It stands apart, each plant given its own territory by the drip geometry. A single fig tree with leaves like green hands, its roots splayed over limestone in search of seams. Tree ferns spaced like umbrellas left open after a party, each in the center of its own dark circle of leaf mold. Moss in cushions only where the wall weeps. Orchids clamped to the fallen pylon, white and speckled-throated, quivering not with wind — there is none — but with insects. Between plants is bare warm stone and clear, empty air. Sparse and lush at once: nothing crowded, everything that has managed to be here overfed.

And then the animals resolve, and sparse stops applying to anything.

They are in the walls first — swifts, hundreds of them, nesting in the eroded solution holes of the limestone, pouring out in a single shrieking braid, circling inside the field without crossing it, snapping up insects in passes so close the wingbeats slap the air by your face. They are in the leaf mold — thumbnail frogs, copper-colored, throat sacs ballooning, calling from an inch of damp litter as if each were the size of a dog. They are in the sunlit stone — cicadas, not heard so much as endured, a metallic, rising pressure that makes conversation at midday an act conducted by gesture and lip-reading. Under the ferns: millipedes thick as ropes. Over the stone cups: dragonflies with oil-slick wings, hunting, and being hunted in turn by a breed of translucent lizard that launches itself, hangs — a beat too long — on the acoustic standing wave near the pylons, and lands with its mouth already full.

This is not a place where fauna is present. It is a place where fauna is the medium. You move through territories of sound the way you would wade through water of different temperatures. Near the fig, frog chorus. Near the fallen pylon, a clicking, castanet stratum of beetles that stridulate in synchrony with the field pulse, and have clearly been doing so for enough generations that their shells have grown resonant chambers, little tuned boxes. High on the wall, swift screams doppler in the folded air and arrive from the wrong direction. Predators have learned the misdirection. So, mostly, has prey. So have the moths that fly in deliberate silence, wings furred to swallow noise, the only quiet things here.

There are human marks, but few, and none of them recent in spirit. A low shelter tucked against the south wall, built of plateau stone and a transparent material that has not yellowed or cracked in what looks like decades of sun; inside, a cot frame, a sealed crate sweating cold, three listening horns grown from a matte ceramic that is warm like skin. A path of flat stones laid between drip zones, kept clear — not by anyone now, but because the frogs dislike crossing warm rock in daylight and the millipedes keep to the mold. Markers hammered into the rim bear no letters, just depths and dates worn nearly smooth. Whoever maintained the pylons either left knowing they would not need maintaining, or did not leave at all in any way that required a grave inside the field.

The rules of the hollow impose themselves quickly.

Speak quietly, or not at all during the noon crest; the field returns loud sounds amplified and slightly displaced, and the swifts burst from the wall at sudden volume, and for a minute afterwards nothing can hear anything hunt or hide. Stay on the stone path — not for your safety, exactly, but because the soil between plants is a living crust of eggs, fungal threads, and frog burrows, and a footprint stays indented and flooded with beetles for days. Don't drink from the stone cups after a pulse. The water is clean for the first hour. Then the nanomachinery that keeps it from evaporating in the warm thin air begins, for reasons of its own, to reorganize it, and it takes on a faint blue charge and a taste of metal and mint, and animals that drink late water walk in attentive, listening circles for an afternoon.

None of this stays still. That is the last thing the place teaches you. At intervals the whole system — pylons, mist, chorus, hunt — surges together: a pulse of condensation, a visible dimming of droplets out of clear air, every frog and cicada locking to the same rising tempo, swifts pouring from the wall in one body, dragonflies rising, lizards launching into that impossible hang. For thirty seconds the bowl is a single engine made of unrelated lives that the old technology accidentally taught to keep time together. Then it loosens, staggers, finds its separate rhythms again.

Above, the plateau continues flat and ancient and nearly silent, the wind combing grit over stone that finished changing eons ago.

Below, hidden in it, one warm room of the world goes on being improbably wet, improbably green, and too loud to think in — maintained by machines that no one fully understands, around which an entire ecology has grown like a pearl around the original, patient grain.
