It is a high valley that was not carved so much as it was laid down, still soft at the edges, and then covered over completely before the world could finish it.

You come up to it — for it is high, well above the tree line where trees in the ordinary sense should have given up — and the first thing you notice is that there is no ground. Not loose earth, not stone, not even moss in the usual forgiving way moss covers things. Everywhere your foot would fall there is a woven floor. Roots flattened and interlaced like the planks of a boardwalk, then grafted where they cross, then sealed over with a pale, living bark that is warm to the touch. In the hollows it holds water. Shallow, tea-clear pools collect in the slight sags between root-beams, and duckweed makes a perfect, unbroken green skin across them, so that from a distance the floor seems solid until a ripple opens and closes under nothing.

Beyond the floor, widely, widely spaced across an open bowl perhaps four miles across, are the structures.

There are not many. That is the second surprise. For a place so totally made, it is empty. A tower here. A long, arched hall there, half a mile from the next. A bridge spanning a marshy channel to arrive at a platform with nothing on it. Great terraced ramps rising in slow ziggurats and ending in flat gardens. And between them, enormous plazas of that same root-pavement, swept clean by water seeping up through the weave, reflecting the normal, bright daylight that falls straight into the valley and seems to have nowhere to go.

Everything is built, and nothing has been built with a tool.

The towers are not trunks, exactly, though they began as trunks. They have been coaxed — or they have coaxed themselves — into hollow columns, buttressed with flying ribs of living wood that arc out and re-enter the wall twenty feet higher, fused seamless. Leaves, broad as doors, are overlapped in shingles up their sides, each leaf's stem drilled through by a vine tendril and knotted on the inside during growth, so the whole surface sheds rain outward in a thousand small spouts. Inside, the walls weep continuously. Condensation forms in the warm air that pools at this height — warm despite the altitude, warm as a greenhouse held in a cup of mountains — and runs down in sheets, gathers in carved-out, grown-out gutters, and is carried away in aqueducts made of half-pipe stems still green and pulsing faintly.

There are stairs. That is where it begins to turn strange.

Wide stairs, with risers exactly even, made of compressed fiber that has grown in right angles. Stairs that switch back up the inside of an empty silo the size of a cathedral. Doorways arched and then reinforced with a second, smaller arch growing inside the first. Windows, round and square, with leaf-shutters that never move. A long colonnade along the wet southern edge of the valley where each column is a separate plant, but each has grown a capital, fluted, and together they support a roof of interwoven canopy that drips in a steady curtain along its eave.

No feet use the stairs. Fauna here is rare to the point of rumor: a single, oversized white moth working the trumpet-flowers that grow from the eaves; in the marsh channels, a frog so well camouflaged as a curled leaf that you only know it by the sudden stop in its one-note call. Once, perhaps, a cloud of midges over a pool. Nothing that needs a door.

And yet the building has not stopped being precise. If you put your hand on a joint, there is no seam. Vine has become peg, peg has become beam; two species meet and their bark flows together into a single scarless surface. Benches grow from walls at sitting height. Tables — flat-topped stumps polished by water — stand in the centers of round rooms. In one long hall, rows of such benches face a raised dais overgrown at its edges with ferns, as if for listening. In another, narrower building, partitions divide the interior into identical cells, each with a woven hammock of aerial roots suspended between its walls, each hammock holding a perfect, dry hollow in the shape of a sleeping body that has never slept there.

The sound fills the vacancy.

It is loud in a way that makes you raise your voice without realizing it. The hollow towers are pipes. Wind, even the mild, warm thermals rising off the root-floor at midday, finds the window holes and the open tops and sets up chords. Not gusts — a continuous, multi-toned humming, low in the big halls, higher and reedier in the clustered flutes of bamboo-like growths that line the water channels. Water adds its own layer: dripping from every eave in an uncountable patter, running in the stem-aqueducts with a gurgle that echoes inside the hollow supports, falling at the far end of the valley in a broad, shallow sheet from a raised cistern-garden to a marsh below, making a soft roar that never rises or falls.

When you speak, the buildings answer a half second later, not with your words but with the nearest pipe shifting emphasis, as if your voice has briefly stopped one hole.

Nothing changes. That is the deepest rule, and you feel it after an hour more than at first glance. The pools do not rise though water pours into them without pause; the extra must seep down through the living floor at exactly the same rate. The leaf-shutters do not open with the sun. The trumpet flowers on the eaves stay at the same half-open bell, neither blooming nor wilting, and the moth returns to the same one at intervals so regular you could set your breathing by it. Even growth has reached some negotiated settlement: a tendril that extends during the warm afternoon is pruned back at night by its own curling death at the tip, a leaf that unfolds is matched by another that is reabsorbed on the shaded side of the same tower. The whole valley hums in equilibrium.

And yet, at its rim, where the root-pavement thins and you can finally see beyond the made world, the geology is embarrassingly young. Black rock, glassy and unweathered, rises in smooth humps not yet cracked by frost. No soil has accumulated on it. No lichen. The edges of the valley are raw, embryonic, as if the land was extruded last spring and the plants arrived immediately after with plans already drawn. There is no old part of this place. The towers show no rot, no scar where a branch failed. The marsh sedges stand in planted rows, each row a different species, sorting the water by root depth as it passes toward the center. The oldest thing you can find is the polish on the stair treads, and even that is not wear — it is a different kind of bark, grown smooth.

At the far north end, beyond the last tower, is the structure everything else seems to be oriented toward, though no road connects them more directly than any other. It is a vast, low amphitheater woven entirely of roots rising in concentric rings, open to the sky, its seats filled with still water an inch deep that reflects the clouds without rippling except where drips from an overhanging canopy strike it. At its center is a single clear space. Nothing grows there. The root-floor bends around it, leaving — for the only place in the valley — a circle of the young black rock exposed, warm and steaming faintly where rain hits it.

Standing there, the pipe-chords align. The sound is loudest and cleanest there, a steady chord with a fifth hidden inside it, and the water dripping from the canopy falls all around the circle but never inside it.

The plants have built a town at altitude, in warmth that shouldn't be there, out of themselves, for an arrival that is not late, because lateness would imply time is passing. It is simply, perfectly, perpetually ready.
