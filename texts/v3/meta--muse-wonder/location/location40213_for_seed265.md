There is no light here, and it is not the kind of darkness that follows a day.

It does not lift. It does not thin toward an edge. There are no stars above the basin, no moon, no grey interval before a morning. Lamps brought in from elsewhere come on — you can feel the switch click, feel the filament grow warm in your palm through the glass — and produce heat, and produce nothing else. A spark from a cut cable falls an inch and is taken. Even the faint chemical shine that things ought to make in deep water, in rot, does not make it. The dark is not an absence laid over the country. It is the medium the country is suspended in, warmer and thicker than air, and it absorbs sight the way wet clay absorbs a footstep.

So you do not enter it by looking. You enter it by weight.

The ground is low, newly low. A broad basin, tens of miles across if you walk it in causeway miles, sunk just below the level of the water that surrounds it and the water that wants it back. It has not been land for long. The mud is still sharp. Channels have cut themselves in straight, unsoftened lines through blue-grey clay and have not yet learned to meander. Shell beds lie exposed in perfect, brittle layers, the shells unbroken, the edges of broken concrete and slag still keen enough to cut a boot. Nothing has been rounded by weather. It has only been wetted, again and again.

And it is warm, always. A marsh warmth that gets into your clothes and stays. The air is at skin temperature and saturated; it condenses on any surface cooler than a body — on iron railings, on stone, on the backs of animals that have rested too long. Rocks introduced for the levees weep constantly. The mud underfoot is warm. Shallow water standing in wheel ruts, in footprints, in the vast sheet-floods between ridges, is bath-warm, and when you step out of it your feet steam in the dark by touch, by smell.

Water is not in one place here. It is near the surface everywhere. Press down on apparently dry clay and it darkens and shines — though shining is the wrong word — it slicks. Dig a hand's depth and the hole fills. Rain is not remembered as an event so much as a state the air almost achieves every few hours, a condensing that gathers on cane leaves and cable insulation and falls in slow, heavy drops with long silences between them. Canals cross the basin, and drainage ditches, and flooded quarries where the water is so still that walking into it slowly you cannot tell, at first, where the mud slope stops being ground and starts being bottom.

The causeways are the only certainty. They are made of crushed slag, furnace waste, broken brick, shell. They run, slightly raised, from pump-house to pump-house across the whole region, and they are dense with presence even where they look — where they would look — empty. The air above them is layered with midges that never disperse, with large soft moths whose wings are wet and make no powder, with bats that fly low and slow and constantly adjust, their cries so high and so absorbed by the damp that you feel them more as a pressure in the sinuses than a sound. Under the slag edge, in the warm mud, is another layer: crabs moving sideways in their thousands, blind white shrimp in the ditch water, eels knotting and unknotting, snails the size of fists grazing mineral crusts. Step off the causeway and your boot finds life before it finds bottom. Three inches down are worms and burrowing toads. Ankle deep are fish that have learned to breathe the mud itself. They thump, softly, against your shin, curious about the heat of you.

Fauna is abundant precisely because nothing here hunts by sight. Predation is by warmth, by vibration, by chemistry. Everything has grown extravagant ears, whiskers, naked sensing skin. The herons — if they are herons — stand taller than a person in the flooded fields and sweep long bills through the water in metronome arcs for hours. They have no eyes to speak of, just smooth feathered heads. Rats swim the canals in family lines, nose to tail. Vast, slow mollusks attach to the submerged sides of industrial machinery and filter the outflow, growing shells banded with copper and zinc. In the deepest dredge pits something larger turns over once in a while, not breaching, just displacing enough warm water that a wave, an hour later, laps thoughtfully at a levee ten minutes' walk away. It has never been in a hurry. Nothing here needs to be.

Vegetation, which cannot cheat the dark as animals can, is sparse and strange. No woods, no grassland. At long intervals a stand of black cane, its joints sweating sugar, rooted in ground heated by buried pipes. Patches of leathery fungus spreading over slag heaps, feeding not on light but on sulfur and the waste heat of transformers. A pale root-mat that floats on certain ponds, flowerless, and contracts faintly when touched, like skin. Where the industrial outflow is richest, a brief, dense bloom of red-black algae will fur the water for a season, then die back to nothing for years. Most of the land is bare: mud, clay, shingle, industrial grit — somehow more fertile for animals for being bare.

Because the industry arrived before the darkness was complete, or perhaps it arrived because of it. Scattered, never clustered into a town, the works continue.

There is a pumping station every few miles, a low concrete building with a single enormous flywheel. It turns once, perhaps, while you eat a meal beside it. You feel its stroke in the causeway, a patient thud transmitted through the slag, and then a long sigh as water is lifted — or lowered — somewhere underground. There are transformer yards fenced with chain-link gone soft with condensation; the current runs, you can feel it in your teeth when you are close, a faint sour hum in the jaw, but the insulators make no corona, the warning lamps make no glow. There are conveyor gantries stretching out over flooded pits, buckets descending on chains at a rate you could outstare for an afternoon and not confirm they have moved. A gas plant inland, where the ground is a little higher and the clay still shows the fresh scrape of excavation, produces something that leaves in sealed tankers on a rail spur. The tankers have drivers. The drivers navigate by counting rail-joints and by the changing smell of the ditches.

The machines are mostly quiet, which is the uncanny part. An industrial landscape ought to roar. Here the wet dark eats sound the way it eats light, but at shorter range. A diesel engine idling twenty paces away is a warm pulse in the air and a tremor underfoot, not a noise. Voices do not carry. You can be in a crowd of frogs — and in the warm season, though season is a guess, every ditch is solid with them — and hear only the nearest, bellowing softly into the mud, while your legs register the rest as a continuous fine vibration. Echoes, when they happen, are untrustworthy. Sometimes the slap of your own boot in a puddle returns to you a fraction of a second before you step.

That is one of the rules you learn.

Other rules accumulate slowly, because change here is slow and so is understanding. Water that is perfectly still will bear weight if you move slowly enough; panic and hurry break whatever agreement it makes with you, and then it is deeper than any ground has a right to be — puddles that take a dropped tool forever, canals whose bottoms have receded since the survey. Reflections exist, in a physical sense: if you kneel and put your face near a still pool, the skin of your face feels a difference, a cooler answering presence, and the animals go quiet. Old workers say the pools keep an image of the country as it was when there was light, and that the things in that image occasionally look back with postures slightly different from your own. You will never see it. You will feel the hair lift on your neck all the same.

Distance stretches in the deepest part of the basin, near the central lagoon where three dredges have been working the same black sediment for longer than anyone can account for. Walk toward their felt thud for an hour and the thud is no nearer; your counted steps reset after rain; a causeway you have walked all your working life adds a new pump-house one year, unweathered, its concrete still curing in the warmth, its flywheel already turning, its outflow already populated with shrimp. No one remembers building it. The paperwork, in a dry cabinet in the gas plant office, is perfectly in order, signed in a hand no one recognizes, dated next spring.

Cold places move through the dark without wind. Workers call them shadows, for lack of another word: regions, body-sized or house-sized, where the warm damp abruptly thins and the smell of brine sharpens and every animal nearby goes still. They drift slower than walking pace, they pause against the sides of running machinery as if listening, and they leave a faint scour in the mud that weeds never colonize. Do not stand in one longer than a breath. It is not dangerous in any way anyone can prove. Your chest simply forgets, for a while afterward, whether it has just inhaled.

If you stay, your body adjusts in its own slow time. Your eyes stop aching from trying. Your skin learns the map of heat: the warmer thread of a buried cable under the causeway, the cooler braid of a freshwater seep crossing it, the radiant wall of a boiler house a full field away, the small, mobile warmths of rats crossing ahead of you in single file. You learn to tell cane from fungus from sleeping bird by smell. You learn that the basin is not silent, only close; put an ear to the ground and the whole region is underway — water moving through clay pores, worms moving through water, current moving through wire, the lagoon's great sleeper turning over again in its pit, beginning a wave that will reach you, gently, before you die of old age, and will still be traveling onward when the next pump-house is remembered into being beside a road you thought you knew.

You do not leave by deciding on a direction. You leave, if you do, by following warmth upward, hand over hand along a levee that gets fractionally drier, until one night the condensation stops forming on your face and the frogs behind you fall, layer by layer, out of feeling — and the dark, for the first time, feels like only dark.
