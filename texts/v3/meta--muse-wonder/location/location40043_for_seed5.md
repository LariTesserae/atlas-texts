From above, there is almost nothing to see.

The land is low and white and very bright — a broad fjord-head plain of frost-shattered stone, old beach ridges, and snow that does not so much fall as remain. The light comes off it in every direction. There are no trees tall enough to make a landmark, no smoke, no road. A few dark boulders lean together where the plain dips, with reindeer moss in their seams and a narrow slot between them, shoulder-wide, where the snow is always trampled to grey ice.

You have to duck to enter it. The passage goes down, not far, by steps cut directly into stone and hollowed deeply in the middle from centuries of feet. It makes two turns. The brightness does not diminish. It changes.

Then the bowl opens.

It is perhaps four hundred feet across, no more — a sunken, circular hollow scooped below the plain, terraced from rim to floor in five and six and in places seven levels. And it is full. Not full the way a field is full, but full the way a beehive is full, or a basket packed for winter: there is no unoccupied surface in it.

A city has been built into every inch of the depression, and then a garden has been built over the city, and then the city has been built again through the garden, until the distinction is mostly a matter of which layer you are touching.

The houses are low, stone at the base and dark timber above, roofed in turf so thick that in this perpetual bright cold the roofs are green-gold with grass, cloudberry, dwarf birch, and mats of flowering moss. The turf does not stop at the eaves. It runs down trellises, over lintels, around window frames. Walls that would otherwise be bare are hung with felted wool panels, and the felt is colonized by lichen in pale rosettes — grey, sulfur, bone-white. Between houses, where in another town there might be an alley, there is a stair, a woodpile, a stacked row of hand-thrown pots full of horsetails, a trough, a loom frame, a sleeping dog, and a trellis of peas, all somehow occupying what appears to be the same narrow space at once.

No street is wide enough for more than two people to pass without turning sideways. Most are also gardens. The paving stones are separated by seams of moss so deep and resilient that a dropped tool does not clatter, only settles. Overhead, rope bridges cross at the second and third levels, their planks muffled with hide. Hand-cranked hoists rise alongside terraces: stone counterweights, wooden pulleys, baskets of turnips or wet laundry or cut turf going up and down in a slow, continuous circulation. Nothing is motorized. Nothing needs to be. Iron appears sparingly — a hinge, a saw blade, kettle hooks, nails blackened with age — and everything else is bone, antler, stone, clay, wood worn to the color and smoothness of soap.

It should not be lush here. The air is genuinely cold; it stings the inside of the nose, makes a thin frost of your breath, keeps your fingers clumsy unless you tuck them into your sleeves. Outside the bowl the vegetation is what the plain can manage: lichen, a few inches of willow in the lee of rocks. But the bowl is a trap for light. Its upper rim is ringed with standing slabs of clear river ice, replaced by hand each generation, set at an angle in stone sockets, and the inner walls are limewashed and pale. Sunshine — and in this season the sun never fully leaves, only circles and circles — strikes the ice and the white walls, bounces down, bounces across, arrives from so many directions that there are almost no true shadows. Ferns grow underneath the terraces, lit from below by reflection. Bushes do not lean toward the sun; having no single sun to lean toward, they grow into dense, even spheres. The whole hollow is luminous and shadowless, bright as the inside of a shell.

That reflected light is enough, just enough. Not warmth, exactly — nobody here sits in shirtsleeves, and the stone under your palm stays cold — but a degree or two lifted above the killing point, held there without fluctuation. Too little and the gardens would blacken. Too much and the ice rim would begin to weep and slump. It has found its balance a long time ago and kept it. The ice neither advances nor retreats. The frost melts off a leaf at midday and re-forms along its edge while you watch, and melts again. Nothing here is in a hurry to become a different season.

Water is correspondingly modest and everywhere. There is no river, no well at the center, no fountain. Instead: damp. Condensation gathers on the cold outer faces of stones and is led away by grooves cut fingertip-wide into the masonry, into clay pipes, into covered wooden troughs. Roof turf holds the occasional rain like a sponge and gives it back slowly. Lids of beaten bronze without clappers — bell-shaped, green with verdigris — hang under the ice slabs to catch drip and funnel it silently down twine into jars. Every terrace has its cistern, dark and cold, with small pale fish turning in it and water boatmen skating the surface. Your boots come away darkened from the paths. The lower walls are always faintly wet to the touch. In the troughs and pots, frogs the color of wet leaves sit with only their eyes above the water, and never call.

That may be the strangest part for a newcomer, stranger than the light: the quiet.

It is a populated city. There are markets, workshops, children, arguments in doorways, goats on the roofs in felt hoof-covers picking their way through the cloudberry. There are handlooms going, stones being ground, dough being slapped down, carts being loaded. And almost none of it makes a sound that travels. The moss and turf and felt absorb it. The bowl's shape folds sound inward and smothers it. The inhabitants reinforce the effect as a matter of custom so old it presents itself as physics: felt soles indoors and out, wooden rather than metal vessels, doors hung on leather, bargaining in the market done by an arrangement of fingers and small white stones laid on a cloth. People speak, when they speak, close to one another and low, not conspiratorially but the way you speak next to a sleeping person. Dogs are bred small and phlegmatic and rarely bark. The ptarmigan that nest in niches left deliberately in the house walls — round, dark openings at regular intervals, like a second, smaller architecture laid over the human one — stare out with round eyes and say nothing. White moths work the flowers in daylight. Caterpillars in green and umber process along the stems. Bumblebees, enormous and furred against the cold, blunder from blossom to blossom; heard at all, heard only because one has passed within inches of your ear.

Everything alive is close to everything else, and seems to have negotiated its position long ago. Sparrows and finches nest in the eaves; their place is the eaves. Swallows have the undersides of the bridges. Beetles have the compost vaults beneath the lowest terrace, which warm the roots of the terrace gardens above them with their slow digestion. The goats have the roofs. Cats have the granary ledges, where the stored barley and dried fish are kept in stone bins against the winter that, locally, never quite arrives. Lemmings have the gaps in the retaining walls and emerge in the bright night to sit in rows, grooming, unalarmed. Under the ferns at the bowl's floor, snails with striped shells leave the paving permanently, faintly shining.

The center of the city is not a temple or a hall. It is a market court barely thirty feet wide, paved in a spiral of flat stones worn into dishes by use, and at its middle stands a single thick lens of ice in an antler frame, taller than a person. It does not seem to be worshipped. It is used: on the brightest circlings of the sun it concentrates the already-concentrated light onto a black stone table where hands are warmed, resin is softened, needles are heated for mending sails and harness, water for tea is brought — slowly, silently — to the edge of steaming. The rest of the time it simply stands there, glowing, sweating a bead of water down its face once in a while, neither growing nor diminishing.

No building in the bowl is entirely new, and the people will tell you, in their close, low voices, that none ever has been. To build a room you dismantle a room. To plant a bed you move, not remove, what was growing there. Timbers carry the notches of previous uses; stones arrive in a wall already smoothed by a previous wall. The population, the gardens, the herds, the stock of timber and turf, appear to hold at a fixed level — not frozen, because hands are always moving and leaves are always opening, but returning always to the same density, the same fullness, the way a pond's surface returns to level.

By the time you have walked the terraces twice you have lost any clear sense of the hour. The light has not changed. Your shadow has never properly formed, only a soft grey brightening under your feet. Up the hollowed steps again, through the slot in the boulders, the plain receives you back into its simpler glare and its wind, which is the first real sound you have heard in some time.

Behind you, below the level of the land, invisible until you are nearly on top of it, the little city goes on being bright and cold and green and silent — occupied to its last ledge, worn smooth by its own long continuance, holding its improbable balance without event, as if it had been noon there for several hundred years and saw no reason for that to stop.
