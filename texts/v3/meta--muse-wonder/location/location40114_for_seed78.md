It is dark first, and it stays dark.

Not the dark of a forest at night, where your eyes eventually learn the trees. This is high dark, thin dark. The sun, when it comes at all, comes sideways for less than an hour — a cold, iron-colored smear dragged along the horizon, never lifting clear of the peaks — and then it is gone again for a very long time. For most of the living year there is only starlight, auroral shiver, and the faint phosphor that sweats out of the ground itself.

And the ground itself is alive.

From horizon to horizon, which here means from one unweathered mountain wall to the next across a plateau so wide it makes its own weather, there is no bare earth. There is no soil that was not first made by the Plant. You stand on it. You have always stood on it. It is a root-mat, a felt, a braided hide of black and bottle-green and bruise-purple vegetation, meters thick in the hollows, thin and translucent over the sharpest stone where it is still learning to hold. Its surface is springy, faintly warm under the frost, dusted with rime that crackles underfoot. Cut it — and people do cut it, with flaked obsidian saws and bone knives — and it bleeds a clear, amber sap that freezes almost at once into brittle honey. By morning the cut has knitted.

The mountains underneath are young. Embarrassingly young, geologically. They went up fast and have not yet been forgiven for it. Their ridges are knives of black glassy rock, their scree slopes are loose and unlichened except where the Plant has thrown roots across them like stitching across a wound. Earthquakes still travel through them a few times a season, a low shrug that sets the whole mat rippling. New crags split open steaming in the cold, and within weeks the Plant is in the crack, root-hairs white as hoarfrost, prying, drinking mineral heat. Nothing here has learned to be round yet.

That is the paradox of the place, and you feel it in your body before you understand it: it is totally vegetated, and it is almost completely empty.

Because the Plant does not only make the floor. At intervals — a kilometer apart, three kilometers, sometimes a lonely half-day’s walk — it makes a Trunk.

It rises out of the mat without branches, without taper at first, a column of ribbed, fibrous, dark-red plant matter wider than a village, and it goes up. And up. Through the wind shear. Through the permanent river of cloud the plateau exhales for itself. The top is usually invisible, lost in blackness and distance, but on the clearest star-nights you can sense the Canopy up there the way you sense a ceiling in a dark cathedral, by the way sound changes, by the occasional fall of a leaf turning and turning for ten minutes through still air before it lands, huge, leathery, curled like a boat, loud as a tent collapsing.

Between the Trunks there is nothing. No middle forest. No scrub. Just the living floor, the wind, and vast cubic miles of cold empty air. You can stand in that openness and scream and hear your own voice thinned by altitude, and then, seconds later, returned to you by a Trunk a mile away.

That is why it is so loud here, despite the emptiness.

The Trunks are hollow for most of their height, resonant as organ pipes. Wind at altitude pours across their broken crowns and torn seed-mouths and makes tones — not one tone, but chords that change with temperature, with ice-loading, with the slow contraction of the Plant at night. A deep bass that you feel in your molars for days. A sudden fluting shriek when a storm comes up from below the plateau edge. The mat itself groans and ticks as it grows, root pushing against root, and in flowering season the seed pods, which hang under the Canopy in clusters the size of houses, split with concussions that roll across the plateau like distant artillery. Fauna answer them. Everything here has learned to be heard over the Plant.

Water is not absent, but it never gets to be a river. The Plant is jealous of it. Rain comes — short, slanting, freezing bursts generated when the mat’s slow exhalation hits the cold air trapped between Trunks — and it vanishes into the felt before it can run. It reappears as condensation streaming down Trunk ribs in black sheets and freezing into fluted ice, as pocket-lakes cupped in giant fallen leaves, as bogs where the mat has sagged over a hollow and made a warm, steaming, faintly glowing marsh in the middle of the frost. Travelers drink by pressing their mouths to a cut root, or by waiting under a drip-line at dawn when the Trunks weep. Your clothes are always damp. Your boots never quite dry. Ice grows inside your hood from your own breath, and when you enter the mat-tunnels, it melts and rains on you again.

For there is a second country under the apparent emptiness.

Push through the surface felt — there are places where grazing animals have worn it thin, places where people maintain hatch-like openings with woven fiber doors — and the mat opens into burrowed warmth. Fermentation heat. A smell of wet earth, iron, crushed green stems, animal musk. Here, in the dark between root and rock, life is abundant to the point of crowding. Pale blind beetles the size of dogs move in slow trains, cropping fungal gardens that grow on the Plant’s shed skin. Shaggy, low-slung grazers with ice-caked muzzles and ears like leaves push through, eating the mat from inside and leaving tunnels that others enlarge. Bats — though they look more like feathered frogs — drop from Trunk hollows at the long dusk to hunt the moth-storms that rise off the bogs. Above them, rarely seen but constantly heard, are the canopy things, whose calls fall down the Trunk wells days after they were made, stored and re-radiated by the wood until predator and prey are hopelessly confused. The Plant itself seems to keep sounds and play them back when the wind is right. Herds stampede from a lion-roar a week old. Hunters learn to listen for the echo’s edge, not the call.

On the surface, the fauna is larger and stranger for having so much empty room. Long-legged, black-coated runners travel between Trunks in herds of thousands, their breath making a low fog that hugs the mat, their feet drumming the hollow ground so that you can navigate by the vibration in your knees. They are following the growth pulses. Because this place is not still, ever.

The Plant grows in waves. A pulse begins somewhere — an earthquake, a warm vent opening, a pod-fall fertilizing a square mile of mat with steaming pulp — and a green-black swelling travels outward across the plateau over weeks, roots thickening, the floor rising a hand’s breadth, new Trunk-buds nosing up shiny and blind and then rocketing skyward when they find the wind. Old Trunks shed their outer skin in sheets that sail and crash. Bogs migrate. Tunnels collapse and are re-chewed. A landmark you left in autumn will be unrecognizable, overgrown or swallowed, by deep winter. Maps are sung here, and revised every time they are sung, because a stanza that lasts too long becomes a lie.

The people — scattered, in the way everything above the mat is scattered — live with primitive, exacting craft. A settlement is rarely more than one or two structures: a low hut of stone footings and Trunk-fiber walls, roofed with a single cured leaf weighted with rocks; a windbreak of woven root; a fire-pit lined with obsidian where they burn dried mat, which smokes sweet and low and does not flame much in the thin air. Their tools are stone, bone, frozen sap, plant thorn. Their wealth is rope, and tallow, and knowledge of which roots will bear weight and which bogs breathe poison after a quake. They harvest without quite farming, they hunt without quite following, they cut doorways into living Trunks and move in for a generation until the Plant decides to close the chamber and they move on, leaving a bead necklace or a carved sign in the healed scar.

Their rules are practical, and therefore treated as sacred:

Never travel the open between Trunks when the high wind starts to sing in earnest; a falling skin-sheet or seed pod will arrive without any sound of its own in that roar, and there is nowhere to hide on a living floor.

Never sleep un-tethered on the mat during a growth pulse; you will wake inside it.

Never trust a voice the first time you hear it. Wait for the Plant to repeat it. The second time, it is only the Plant. The third time, something real is calling under the copy.

Keep a light small. In this darkness, eyes — yours, theirs, the things in the Trunk wells — are tuned to almost nothing. A big fire is not comfort. It is an announcement.

And during the brief, grey noon that comes at the height of summer, when the sun finally shows a sliver of itself and the whole plateau steams, the Canopy, miles above and never seen clearly, opens something. A rain of fine, luminous pollen comes down through the empty air for hours, coating the mat, the herds, the huts, the inside of your mouth with a taste like cold metal and crushed herbs. Then the Trunks inhale — that is the only word for the reversal of wind, the way your ears pop, the way loose fiber streams upward — and the long dark closes again, and the growth begins anew.

You could walk for a lifetime here, from uplifted ridge to bog to Trunk-city and back, and never leave the body of a single organism, and never, for more than an hour at a time, be within sight of another human being. It is a continent that is also a plant, a forest with almost nothing in it, a cold high desert made entirely of life — loud, damp, dark, restless, still being built underneath you by roots and by mountains alike.
