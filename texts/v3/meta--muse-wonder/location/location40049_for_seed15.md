You have to duck to get in.

The door is not really a door — just a low opening in the floor of the wash-house above, with a wooden hatch thrown back against the wall, and nine steep steps cut down into darkness. Or not darkness, exactly. Halfway down, the sound reaches you first, and then the light does, bouncing up the stairwell and making you squint.

It's one small room below the ground, no bigger than a cellar pantry, and almost everything in it is water.

The chamber was finished only this spring. You can still tell, everywhere. The walls are hand-laid brick, uneven courses you can trace with a fingertip, thickly coated in fresh lime plaster that hasn't softened at the edges yet. The floor, the vaulted ceiling no higher than a tall man could touch, the broad steps descending into the tank itself — all of it is made. No earth shows, no rock. Terracotta tiles line the plunge, pale, unglazed, fired with thumbprints still faint in a few, and their corners are uncomfortably sharp if you brush a knee or an elbow. The mortar between them is bright white and still leaching a little, so the water has a faint milky cast for the first hour after filling, and smells clean and mineral and slightly alkaline, like wet chalk warmed in the sun.

The tank takes up four-fifths of the room. There is a narrow ledge around three sides just wide enough for bare feet, a low wooden stool in one corner kept there for scrubbing, a single clay ewer. That's all. The emptiness makes it seem larger and makes the sound much larger too.

Warm water comes in constantly through a clay pipe set into the east wall at head height, fed from the black-painted cistern on the roof above. It has been up there baking since dawn. By midday the inflow is bath-hot, and it does not trickle — it pours, a thumb-thick fall from a simple bronze spout hammered flat at the mouth, smacking the surface without pause. In this tiled vault the splash becomes a roar. Drips from the ceiling answer it, the overflow gurgles through a second, lower pipe in the opposite wall, the drain below your feet sighs as air moves in the channel, and together they fill the little room so completely that you would have to raise your voice to be heard by someone standing on the ledge beside you. The air moves with it too: steam lifted off the surface, caught in the light, condensing on the cooler upper vault and raining back down in fat, irregular drops.

That light is the strangest thing, down here, below the street and below the waterline of the tank. A square shaft was built straight up through the wash-house roof, lined inside with the same white limewash and polished tin reflectors hammered by hand and angled by eye. At noon — and this place is really only itself at noon — the sun falls in one solid column. It hits the water and shatters. The whole submerged room turns brilliant, turquoise-white, almost shadowless. Tile grout, the rippled ceiling reflection, the wavering bands on the far wall, all of it trembles. Underwater your hands look bleached and magnified. If you stay still the glare rises off the bottom in visible heat-shimmer.

Nothing lives here yet, and the builders seem to have arranged it that way, whether they meant to or not. The lime is too fresh for insects to settle. The water is kept too warm and too constantly replaced for anything to take hold. After the morning scrubbing with a stiff brush and a wooden paddle, the tank is scoured clean. What vegetation there is is just beginning at the margins, in the places hands can't quite reach: three small maidenhair ferns have seeded themselves where the inflow pipe sweats through the plaster, their fronds no bigger than coins; a faint green threading of algae has started in the shadiest bottom corner, so thin you have to look from an angle to see it; one reed, inexplicably, lifted from the filter bed outside and left unpulled on the ledge, roots in a crack, leaning toward the light shaft.

The rest of the system is still working itself out, as new built things do. The water level rises and sighs down an inch when the overflow clears. The wooden hatch warps a fraction in the humidity each day and has to be kicked, not lifted, at one corner. The plaster must stay wet while it cures or it will crack, so the tank cannot be left empty — the rule the mason left behind, repeated like a warning: keep it flooded, keep it warm, keep the spout running. If you shut the wooden sluice-gate above with its rope pulley and let the room cool, you will know by morning; the tiles will tick as they contract, the surface will go dull and film over, the fern corner will steam no longer. For now it only knows this one active state: fill, heat, overflow, condense, drip, fill again, the sun's hard square traveling an inch an hour across the sharp new tiles until it climbs the far wall and goes.

You can stand in the middle, chest-deep, and turn slowly without touching anything. There is nowhere else to go and nothing else to meet. Just the pour, the brightness doubled by water, the heat coming through the soles of the submerged steps, and the small, newly-made hollow holding it all together under the ordinary floor above.
