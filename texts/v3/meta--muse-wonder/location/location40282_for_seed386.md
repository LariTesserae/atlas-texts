It is above the clouds, and it should be cold up here, but it isn't.

You come over the rim from the outside, where the wind is thin and dry and cuts at your ears, and you step down ten meters into another climate entirely. The air gets heavy. It gets hot — skin-hot, lung-hot, 38 degrees and holding — and it stays. The caldera holds it like a cup holds broth. Below the rim the rest of the world is a white sea of cloud tops stretching to the horizon. Inside, the valley makes its own weather, traps its own steam, and never quite lets it go.

The caldera is old. Nothing here is sharp anymore. The rim is not a knife-edge of fresh volcanic rock but a broad, rounded wall, worn down and grown over, fifteen kilometers from one side to the other. You can see the whole system when the mist thins — the ring wall, the sloping apron, the flat boggy floor — but you can't take it in as one view. It keeps opening into side-valleys, hanging marshes, terraced pools cut into its own flank.

Everything is wet at the summit. There is no snow, no ice, not even a cold stream. The highest ground is a peat bog.

Black peat, meters thick, saturated, moves underfoot like a slow mattress. Water squeezes up around your boots with every step, warm water the color of tea, smelling of iron and leaf mold. It weeps out of the moss itself. Condensation forms on leaves that are already dripping, on rock that is already running. In the morning the entire forest precipitates without rain. In the afternoon it rains anyway, a straight-down downpour that turns every path to a channel in minutes, then stops as abruptly as a valve shutting. By evening the steam is back, rising off the peat, off the pools, off your own clothes.

The materials here are softened by that long wet heat. Basalt rounded to cobbles and covered in liverwort. Limestone pitted into lace. Fallen trunks that don't rot so much as melt, going in a season from wood to a green sponge held together by mycelium and roots. Where the mineral water surfaces, it lays down its own crusts: milky travertine lips around pools, rust-red iron pans, sudden delicate fans of sulfur, yellow as egg yolk, around vents that sigh in the ground. You can place your palm near one — never on it — and feel the rock faintly trembling.

Because underneath, the place is working.

That is the first sound, though it is mostly a feeling. A low, industrial rhythm under the quiet. The valley floor breathes on a cycle too slow to be a pulse and too regular to be weather. Pressure builds in the hot aquifer, lifts a whole meadow of bog by a hand's breadth, forces water up through a hundred springs at once; then somewhere deep a channel opens, the meadow settles, the springs slacken, a line of vents along the eastern terrace lets off steam together with a soft, collective exhale. Geysers here do not erupt so much as operate. They pump. Rock flutes — hollow columns of travertine grown up around old vents — whistle at set pressures, each at a different note, stopping and starting as cleanly as machinery. On still days the static builds in the trapped air until lichen crackles and the fine hairs on your arms lift and blue sparks run, soundless, along wet quartz seams in the cliff. No storm needed. The ground generates.

It's a factory that has been in production long before anything human learned the word. Intake at the rim, where fog pours in cold from outside and warms as it falls. Processing in the canopy and the pools. Output over the south lip, where the single river the valley allows itself gathers all that excess and pours out through a notch, falling three thousand meters toward the cloud sea and largely evaporating on the way down, feeding the very clouds that feed the rim. Nothing is wasted for long.

The vegetation has learned to live inside the works. Lush is not dense enough a word for stacking. There are four, sometimes five layers between ground and sky, and another below the ground in the root-mat. Low, broad trees with buttresses like fins, their bark permanently dark with wet. Above them a second canopy of tree ferns, fronds ten meters across, dripping at every tip. Above that the emergents, huge straight trunks that disappear through a roof of epiphytes into fifty meters of warm haze. Every branch carries a hanging garden: orchids, bromeliads full of warm water and frogs, mosses, small palms rooting in air, pitcher plants the size of buckets that steam gently and digest insects fooled by the mineral warmth. Vines do not climb so much as circulate, going up one tree, across a gap, down another, plumbing the system together.

In the pools the layering continues. The hottest — too hot to touch at the center, bath-hot at the travertine edge — are empty except for mats of emerald and orange algae that wave in the upwelling. The next terrace down holds tadpoles, water-beetles, blind pale shrimp grazing the mineral crust. The next holds fish, small and iridescent, and the long-legged birds that stalk them. Temperature is topography here. Species sort themselves by degrees, migrating a few meters upslope to warm themselves in the morning, down-pool to cool at noon, following the valley's thermostat through the day.

The fauna is abundant but discreet, because in this heat nobody wastes a call. The mornings are mostly dripping and ticking — frogs making a sound like a fingernail on glass, insects clicking, a hornbill's wings thumping through wet air. Midday is near silence, a held breath under the canopy while deer the size of large dogs, their coats perpetually damp and algae-tinted, pick their way through the bog pools and blow steam adapted breaths through enlarged, folded nostrils. They graze the hot meadows in loose groups, ears constantly flicking at flies. Something hunts them — you know from prints in the peat, broad paw, no claw, filling instantly with water, and from the way the group freezes together, heads up, for a full silent minute. You rarely see the hunter. High up, monkeys move through the emergent layer in a slow procession, stopping to drink from bromeliads without descending. At dusk the bats come out of the travertine caves along the west wall in such numbers they look like smoke reversing, pouring upward to feed above the canopy where the industrial air-lift of warm vapor carries insects aloft all night.

For there is another layer of production above even that. On most afternoons, if you look straight up through a break in the trees, you will see them drifting at the very top of the trapped air: long, slow condenser barges, unmanned, dark-hulled, almost silent. They never land. Nothing built ever touches this ground — no road, no tower, no clearing, no scar. They hang from the sky on the valley's own updraft and lower tether lines and scoop nets into the steam column, harvesting clean water, heat, the electric charge that would otherwise spend itself in spark and lichen. Their engines are a faint hum felt in your teeth more than heard, gone when the canopy closes again. By night they are just three red lights moving very slowly against the stars, and then not even that.

At night the valley does not cool. It condenses. Mist forms first in the pools, then in the hollows, then between the trees, until the forest is standing in its own luminous ground-cloud, lit from beneath here and there by warm water reflecting moonlight. The rock flutes sigh through their night pressures. The bog settles and rises. A frog ticks. Somewhere a tree, saturated beyond holding, lets go a whole limb of water in a sudden shower onto leaves below, and everything glitters and is still again.

Dawn comes not as cold, but as thinning. The mist lifts by being used — drunk by moss, drawn up by the opening canopy, pulled into the barges, burned off by a sun that hits the summit hours before it touches the lowlands hidden below the clouds. The peat steams. The first pool ticks over into its pumping cycle. The deer are already standing belly-deep, breathing with the meadow.
