It is called the Kettle, and you find it only because you step through it.

You are high — high enough that the trail up has thinned to a cut in pale, new rock, and the wind has been taking your breath in short, unsatisfying pulls for an hour. Clouds are below you, not above. They move slowly through the valleys like something poured. The stone here hasn't learned to be round yet. The ridge is a fresh fracture, uplifted in a single lifetime, all knife-edges and unweathered faces and scree that shifts if you look at it too hard. Nothing has had time to soften.

Then, between two black basalt teeth, the air changes.

Not gradually. In one step your face is hot. In the next, you are cold to the bone in thin mountain wind. Inside — and it is unmistakably an *inside*, though there is no roof, no wall you can see — it is steaming summer.

The Kettle is a bowl about two hundred paces across, sunk into the summit. You could walk it end to end in four minutes if you walked straight, which you never quite do. Its rim is a perfect, uneasy circle of sharp rock. Its floor is open, sparsely planted, mostly empty ground steaming gently in the heat.

It should not be warm. At this height it should be sleet and lichen. Inside it is easily forty degrees, wet heat that presses on your shirt, fills your lungs, makes the light normal but thick, like light through bathwater. The heat does not come from the sun alone. It hums up through the soles of your boots.

Seven pylons stand at irregular intervals around the rim.

They are not tall — most are waist-high, one reaches your shoulder — and they are not all standing in the same century. They are made of a matte grey material that is neither stone nor metal and gets neither hot nor wet. Water beads on the rock beside them and slides. Water beads *above* them and hangs. Scattered droplets hold in the air around their tips, trembling, refusing to fall until some invisible threshold is passed and they all let go at once in a soft, localized rain straight down.

They make no sound. That is the first wrong thing.

The whole Kettle is quiet in a way that has texture. Outside, wind screams over the ridge. Inside, you can hear your own swallowing. Water is everywhere — weeping from the inner walls in a dozen bright threads, pooling in shallow pans with white and copper-green rims, running in black runnels between stones — but it makes almost no noise. Drips fall mute. Small streams slide without chuckling. Even the steam that lifts constantly off the warm floor rises without a hiss. The pylons drink the sound the way they drink the cold.

The water does a short, frantic cycle here and never leaves. It heats in the dark pools on the floor — pools so hot at their centers you cannot keep a hand in, so cool at their mineral edges you can drink — evaporates in visible sheets, condenses on the underside of nothing about twenty meters up, and rains back down in patches. It is always just-after-rain in the Kettle. The stone is always dark with wet. Moss is always swollen. Your skin, within minutes, is slick and then, impossibly, raining with itself as your sweat lifts and briefly becomes weather.

Vegetation takes advantage without crowding. The bowl is lush but not dense; each plant has been given a clearing.

A single broadleaf tree, a kind of impossible high-altitude fig, stands near the western pool, its trunk wrapped in orchids. Its leaves are huge, waxy, and constantly dripping, and they turn very slowly through the day to follow not the sun, but the nearest pylon. Between it and the next tree — a fern-tree as tall as a house, fronds unfurling and furling in fast-forward as the micro-rain passes over it — there are thirty meters of bare, steaming grit, mineral crust, and low ferns that fold shut when your shadow falls on them.

Ferns colonize every weep in the rock. Moss makes green velvet in the cracks. On the north wall a curtain of hanging roots has grown down without ever reaching soil; they end in bulbous, translucent tips that swell with condensed water and occasionally contract, with a faint muscular pulse, and spray a mist over the stones below. Nothing here seems to trust the ground for drinking.

There is very little animal life, and what there is has learned the quiet.

Once, in an hour, a lizard the color of wet basalt moves from one hot stone to another, pausing mid-stride for ten minutes with one foot raised. Once, a pair of white moths the size of your hands drift through the steam, wings never quite flapping, riding the updraft in long, looping spirals. They are always a pair. The researchers who left notes carved into a tile by the shelter claim there have only ever been two, for thirty years. Under the surface of the largest pool, something pale and eyeless sometimes turns, slow as a thought, and the mineral crust reforms unbroken over where it passed.

Human marks are sparse and uncertain of their purpose.

Near the southern rim there is a shelter — low, open on one side, roofed with self-mending tiles that knit a fresh edge every time the afternoon rain finds a crack. Inside: a sleeping platform, a water still that still fills itself, three ceramic cups fused together by heat into a single object. Outside it, a path of flat stones leads to the central pool, but the stones quietly rearrange themselves when no one is watching. You will set out following them and arrive from a direction you did not expect, always a little closer to a pylon than you intended.

Small shrines or instruments — it is hard to tell — have been left at the pool edges: bowls that collect rain that falls *up* from the hot water for a few seconds before gravity reasserts itself, a hoop of that same grey material half-buried in grit through which steam passes and comes out cold and tasting faintly of iron, a stack of marker stones that casts a shadow pointing downslope at noon, upslope at three.

Inside the Kettle, shadows are nearly right. That's worse than if they were completely wrong. Yours bends a fraction toward the center of the bowl, as if light were being gently lensed. Dust motes drift sideways. When a droplet falls beside a pylon, it falls in a very slight arc.

Late in the day the place shows why it is active, not stable.

First the pools begin to tick — not audibly, you feel it in your teeth — then all the hanging water lets go at once. Rain falls inside while, through the invisible boundary, you can see dry, freezing sunlight on the outer ridge. The fern-tree shudders open. The fig's orchids close into fists. The pylons, for the only time, show a seam of light where they meet the rock.

Then the purge: a great, slow exhalation pushes the accumulated heat up and out through the center, a column of steam that punches a clean, round hole in the low cloud below the mountain and is gone. For eleven minutes afterwards the Kettle is merely warm. The weeping walls pause. You can hear, very faintly, from very far below, birds.

Then the weeping starts again, one thread at a time. A droplet gathers above the nearest pylon and does not fall. The tree turns a leaf.

The system, whatever it was built to test — a climate in miniature, a terraforming seed, a way to hold a single summer room open at the top of the world — is still running its experiment without anyone left to record the results. It keeps the bowl young by constantly washing it new. It keeps it empty by making it just too hot, too wet, too quiet to linger after dark, when the cycle is said to run faster and the rain falls harder and sometimes, briefly, from the ground toward the sky.

You leave the way you came, stepping from steam into wind in a single pace, your clothes instantly riming with frost where they were wet. Behind you there is no dome to see. Only sharp black rock, seven grey stones, and a faint plume rising where no volcano should be, in a place no larger than a hamlet, keeping its own weather like a held breath.
