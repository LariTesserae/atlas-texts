It is below everything else, and you hear it long before you see it.

From the street above it is only a rim of wet stone, a railing, a hot breath coming up between buildings. The rest of the city is at normal height — sidewalks, storefronts, wires. Then there is a cut in it, about two hundred meters long and maybe eighty wide, sunk fifteen meters down into the ground, and that cut is full.

Full is the precise word. There is no empty volume left in it.

You go down by a switchback stair of iron grating built against the retaining wall. The wall is old brick below and poured concrete above, repaired in at least four different periods, and in the dimness it is hard to tell where the wall ends and the growth begins. Moss has taken the mortar. Ferns have taken the cracks above the moss. Fig roots have come down from the rim in thick, flat ropes and spread across the brick like slow lightning, and behind the roots, algae has painted everything that stays damp a deep, bottle green. The stair shakes faintly, not from your weight. Everything down here shakes faintly, all the time.

Halfway down, the sound stops being background.

The pit holds sound the way it holds heat. Pump houses at the far end keep up a low, chest-thumping beat — three big reciprocating pumps, steam-driven, older than anyone working here. Over that is the hiss of condensate lines, the clatter of a line of looms in the long shed on the east terrace, the tick and slap of belt drives, water falling from level to level in open flumes, fans turning in the glass roofs, pipes knocking when the pressure changes. It is not one loud noise but twenty, layered, reflected off wet stone and wet leaves and the curved walls of the basin until the air itself seems to be made of it. People down here do not really talk. They shout close to an ear, or they use the hand-signs painted on placards by every doorway: *steam on, line moving, vat hot, duck.*

And it is warm, in the way a laundry is warm, or a greenhouse after watering. Not sun-warm. Loam-and-boiler warm.

The basin was a reservoir first — that is why it is sunk, why the walls are so thick, why the lowest floor is still a grid of shallow canals. Then it became a washworks and a steam plant for the district above, then a retting and fiber works, because the one thing it has never lacked is hot water near the surface. Now it is all of those at once, packed into terraces, and grown over by the very plants it processes.

The bottom level is water managed into rectangles. Stone-edged tanks, waist-deep, filled with cloudy green water and flax stems sunk to rot soft. Taro in the warmer, cleaner channels. Duckweed making a solid, matte skin over the settling ponds until a skimmer bar pushes through and folds it back like a blanket. Water hyacinth held in booms. Everywhere the water moves, but slowly — it is lifted by the pumps at one end, run by gravity through flumes and filters and vats and growing beds, and by the time it reaches the other end again hours have passed. The cycle is patient. The noise makes it feel frantic, but nothing down here actually moves fast.

Above the tanks, on iron gantries and stone terraces that were once housing and are now mostly work floors, is the town part. It is urban in miniature: a dense warren of sheds, store-rooms, drying lofts, a canteen, a tool library, a chapel-sized pump cathedral where the three pumps stand in a row on a tiled floor, their flywheels turning behind railings polished by hands. Narrow lanes — some paved, some duckboards over a channel — run between buildings that have been added onto upward and sideways until they meet. A balcony is also a pipe bridge. A roof is also a trellis is also a drying rack for fiber. A wall built of stacked terracotta pipe ends is now load-bearing mostly because monstera roots have grown through it and locked it together.

There is no façade that is just a façade. If a surface is vertical and not getting regularly steam-cleaned, it is planted, or colonized. Philodendron up the standpipes. Orchid relatives — bred sterile, propagated by cutting — tied in coconut husk to the warm vents. Bamboo grown in dense clumps at the terrace edges and cut constantly for scaffolding, matting, ties; it comes back in weeks. In the glass nave in the middle, where the roof is arched iron and clouded panes, the light that gets through the canopy above and the algae on the glass arrives green and dim and even, as if underwater, and the rows underneath are not flowers but young plants in trays, ferns in hanging baskets tier on tier, seedling tanks steaming gently. Condensation forms on the underside of the glass, gathers, and rains back down in irregular drips all day long, so even under a roof it is raining.

The light generally is low. The pit walls shade it, the steam hazes it, the canopy of fig and banana and trained vines that workers keep woven over lines across the top shades it further. At noon it is twilight-green. At the working level the electric bulbs take over — caged lamps on enamel shades, strung along every lane, glowing amber through mist. They are on all day. Their light catches on wet leaves and makes a thousand small highlights and no real brightness.

There is no animal life. That is deliberate, and maintained. The weekly steam-through that keeps the vats and lanes from rotting also drives out or kills anything that tries to settle. The hot effluent is treated with lime. The flowers, such as they are, are sterile clones because there are no pollinators to serve them and none are allowed in. No birds nest in the canopy — the noise and the steam won't have them, and netting at the rim discourages the attempt. No fish in the tanks, no frogs in the channels despite the perfect look of it, no insects on the leaves that anyone can find. It should feel like a garden, and instead the total absence makes it feel like a machine that happens to be made of leaves. The plants grow, are cut, are pulped, are retted, are woven, without ever completing any cycle that requires another kingdom.

That is its rule, and it produces the others.

Nothing down here can be kept dry, so nothing is made of anything that ruins wet. Paper is kept in tin boxes or not kept at all — the few records are etched on slate and zinc. Tools are iron, brass, stone. Clothing is canvas and goes stiff with mildew if left overnight. Wood exists only as living wood or as boards already half-returned to sponge; everything structural learned long ago to be brick, tile, glass, iron, or root. If you stop maintaining a corner, the plants do not politely overtake it in a season. In this heat and wet they take it in days — a coil of hose disappears under purslane, a forgotten stair greens over and turns treacherous. The work of the place, underneath the pumps and looms, is a constant, slow pruning war to keep lanes the width of a shoulder and pipes findable by hand.

It is old enough to have settled into itself. The canal edges are worn bowl-smooth where ropes and buckets have passed for a century. Steps have dips in them. The iron has rusted and been painted and rusted through the paint into textures like bark, and in places a fig root has simply replaced a corroded support, accepted, bolted to, built around. Nothing looks new and nothing looks ruined. It looks weathered into balance — erosion finished arguing and agreed on a shape.

From the lowest duckboards you can look up and see all of it at once, which is the strange part, because it is so small. Rim to rim is a short walk. Plumb line to street is a tall room. It has the dimensions of a cathedral close, a single grove, a hamlet that chose to grow down and in instead of out. And yet crossing it takes an hour if you have business in more than one shed, because there is no straight line that is not also a canal, a vat, a root mass, a low hot pipe you have to duck shouting *line moving* to someone you cannot see through the steam and leaves.

At shift change, workers climb the iron stair in a line, wet to the knee, ears ringing, and come out blinking onto an ordinary dry street in ordinary daylight. Behind them the sound does not follow — the rim cuts it off cleanly. Only the hot, green breath keeps rising, and the drip of condensation keeps falling on the taro and the tanks and the tiled floor of the pumps, whether anyone is there to hear it or not.
