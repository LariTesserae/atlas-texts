It is not on any approach. That is the first rule of it.

From below, the peak is just a peak — the highest thing in its range, higher than anything has a right to be and still be made of rock. The range itself is young. It was not weathered up, it was pushed up, recently, in geological terms practically yesterday. The ridges have not had time to blunt. They are black volcanic glass and pale, un-lichened stone in serrated fins, knife-edges that ring faintly when the wind finds them, scree that has never been walked down into soil. Above 7,000 meters the air gives up. Above 8,000 it is a rumor. Up here the sky at noon is not blue, it is a deep, bruised violet, almost black at the zenith, and the stars do not entirely leave.

And it should be freezing. It is not.

Heat comes up through the soles of your boots. Heat hangs in that thin air in a way thin air should not allow. It presses on the back of your neck, it makes your breath shallow for two reasons at once — altitude and oven-warmth — and it turns every exhalation into a brief, visible plume, because something in here is cold enough to condense it.

In here. Because the summit is hollow.

From directly above, and only from directly above, the peak opens. A caldera, but too round, too deliberate. A cupped pocket perhaps forty meters across, sunk into the top of the world and roofed over with a dome of smoked, layered glass that is not quite glass. The dome turns the hard white sun into dusk. Everything below it is dimmed to a late-evening amber, shadows long and brown, even at midday. Light does not fall so much as seep. Some of it comes from below — a dull red breathing in cracks between slabs — so shadows sometimes fall upward, toward the rock, and confuse the eye.

You find the entrance as a crack between two obsidian fins, still sharp enough to cut a glove. Warm vapor sighs out of it. You have to turn sideways. And then a threshold: two pylons, no taller than you are, made of a white ceramic that sweats constantly. Passing between them makes your ears pop a second time, makes the ringing in your head change pitch, makes the pocket on the other side stop agreeing about how big it is.

From outside the dome, looking in through the smoke, you saw a model. A city in miniature, perfect and toylike, laid out in the basin.

From inside the threshold, you are standing in its street.

It is a city, unmistakably. Not a camp, not a station. Avenues, intersections, layered overpasses, transit tubes on pylons, towers braided with balconies and service gantries, drainage channels, plazas, sub-levels glowing under grates, another city stacked under this one and showing through where the streets go transparent. Neighborhoods differentiate themselves as you walk: a dense financial cluster of faceted towers near the center, lower residential blocks with shuttered windows to the east, a wide industrial crescent where chimneys exhale nothing at all.

And it is empty. That emptiness is the dominant material here, more than glass or stone.

No people. No animals. No birds nesting in the eaves, no insects around the few lights, no weeds — there is no green thing at all, anywhere on the mountain, and certainly not in here. The climate forbids it and something else edits it out. The streets are not ruined; they are pristine, unworn, newly extruded. A tram sighs up to the platform beside you on a cushion of silence, doors opening with a soft chime onto rows of empty seats, waits a courteous interval for passengers who have never existed, closes, and glides away. Elevators cycle in their shafts. A door across the plaza opens, closes. Traffic signals change from one color to another for no traffic. The city is running its routines, rehearsing being inhabited, at full fidelity and with no audience but itself.

The scale will not settle while you are in it. A tower that looms thirty stories above you, if you look back toward the pylons, is suddenly no taller than your knee beyond a faint shimmer in the air. You could step over its avenue. You can feel the temptation to reach down and touch a roof, and the equal certainty that your hand would meet something very large, very hot, and very real. The engineers who made this place — if engineers is still the word, at this level — used space the way older engineers used steel: as a material to be folded, stored, stressed. The city is being kept here at pocket-size, at the top of the world where the air is too thin to carry away its heat or its secrets, until it is needed somewhere. In the meantime it practices growing.

That is the motion under the quiet. Because it is mostly quiet here. Altitude swallows sound, the dome swallows the rest. Your footsteps come back to you late and muffled. What you hear is small and intermittent: the tick-tick of ceramic cooling, a bead of water dropping onto hot basalt and vanishing in a hiss, the faraway hum of a transit tube resetting, a sound like a page turning when a building decides to reconfigure a wall. Once an hour — you learn to wait for it — the whole city exhales. Vents open in the dome. Hot, mineral air rushes up and meets cold machinery carried down from the skin of the pylons, and for ninety seconds it rains.

Not real rain. A manufactured drizzle, fine as mist, falling only inside the basin to test the drains. It beads on the smoked dome struts, it runs up some surfaces instead of down where the gravity plating is still imperfect, it pools in hollows and in the lee of the sharp new rock, it spots the avenues with dark circles that evaporate almost before they darken. It is the only water here. No streams cut this peak. No snow survives the heat. Just condensation: on the cold white pylons, on the undersides of tubes, on your own mask, gathering, trembling, falling onto hot stone. Where it lingers longest, in shadowed joints between buildings, something mineral is learning to live. Not moss. Thin crystal fans, clear as ice and hard as tooth, spreading in fractal rosettes around every drip point. Little harvester discs, no bigger than coins, periodically glide out from the walls and shave them away, and the fans grow back by the next rain.

The materials tell the same story when you touch them. The street underfoot is warm, faintly flexible, and heals the scuff of your boot behind you. The tower walls are neither metal nor stone but a matte alloy that does not oxidize, does not hold a fingerprint, and is cool where the rock beside it would blister a palm. Building seams glow for a second when a district expands by a floor, cools, and goes dark. Everything smells of hot rock, ozone, and wet dust that has never been soil.

At the center, the plaza drops away into a hollow that goes down much farther than forty meters, or farther than the mountain. Looking over the rail, you see avenues below avenues, lights coming on in a dim cascade as some evening subroutine moves through the stored city, level after level until the glow is lost in heat haze. The density thins as you look: close-packed towers at street level, then vast separations, single structures floating in a maintained void, connected by threads of light. It is hollow all the way down. A city built mostly of distance.

Do not drink the condensation. That is another rule, left as a pictogram on the pylon, repeated in three human scripts and one that is not: a cup, a droplet climbing out of it. Do not ride the tram twice in a row; it will begin to anticipate a destination for you. Do not stay through two rehearsals of the rain. The heat and the thinness work on you slowly while the city politely ignores you — your heart laboring, your thoughts going a little loose at the edges — and the threshold, from inside, is sometimes hard to find because the street that leads to it has not been grown that hour.

Toward what would be evening outside, the dimness deepens to a true brown dusk. Windows do not light up. Instead, the edges of things do, fine lines tracing doors and transit routes, as if the city were outlining itself to remember its own shape in the dark. Somewhere a tower folds, silently, floor by floor, into the street, and another rises two blocks away from nothing, still steaming slightly, windows shuttered. A seed practicing. A town folding and unfolding itself in a hot, quiet pocket on a roof of black glass, above the weather, waiting for a world where it can be full-sized, and lived in, and worn down at last by feet and rain and time.
