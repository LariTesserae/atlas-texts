**The High Cup**

It is not large enough to be a valley. You come over the rim and the whole of it is suddenly there, held below you — a single bowl worn into the top of the mountain, perhaps a hundred paces across, with nowhere else for the eye to continue. Beyond its lip there is only the cloud-sea, white and level to the horizon, and a few distant summits standing up through it like islands. Inside, the air is entirely different.

Outside, on the ascent, the wind was thin and bright and cutting. Here it does not reach. The Cup shelters its own weather.

The rim is old stone, rounded to softness. Whatever sharpness it once had has been taken away grain by grain, until the rock has the look of something handled for ten thousand years — dark basalt swells and hollows, slick in places, elsewhere stained pale green and ochre where water has been seeping through it for longer than any tree below has lived. It weeps. Everywhere around the inner wall, water emerges without a source you can find: a bead forms in a pore of the stone, swells, slides a few inches and vanishes into moss; a thin sheet runs down nowhere faster than a finger's width a minute, catching the daylight and then gone. Some of the seeps are cool. Some, strangely, are warm.

That warmth is the first wrongness. This high, the place should be cold. Instead the Cup is held at the temperature of a held breath. Warmth comes up through the ground — not in vents or steam, nothing dramatic, just a steady, patient heat in the peat underfoot, as if the mountain were faintly alive far underneath. It meets the cool air spilling over the rim, and the meeting makes the place perpetually condense. Mist is always forming somewhere in here, and always dissolving somewhere else, in a rotation so slow you have to stop trying to watch it. Over an hour, a veil will gather against the north wall. Over the next hour, it will thin into individual droplets hanging on fronds. By afternoon — though afternoon here is only a softening of the already diffused, normal daylight that pours straight down into the bowl — it will have sunk and become ground-fog around your ankles.

Nothing is loud enough to interrupt this. The sound of the place is almost no sound at all. Water falls constantly, but it falls onto moss a hand's breadth thick, onto still pools, onto broad leaves angled to receive it, and each landing is absorbed rather than struck. Now and then a droplet gathers too much weight at a frond-tip and drops, and you hear a single soft *tock* swallowed instantly by the wet ground. Your footsteps make less noise than your own breathing. Your breathing seems indecently clear.

And then, listening for what should accompany it, you realise the deeper silence: there is no animal life here at all. None.

No insects cross the shafts of light. Nothing hums at the flowers — there are flowers, pale, thick-petalled, opening over the course of days with a perfume heavy and sweet in the warm air, and they open to nobody. No birdsong falls in from outside; the rim seems to cut even that off. The leaves are the proof of it, once you look. They are perfect. In any other lush place, leaves are a record of appetite — notched, tunnelled, skeletonised, curled around occupants. Here every leaf is entire, unbitten, untrodden, its edge as clean as the day it unfolded. Fruit ripens on low, fleshy stems, swells, softens, and drops straight down into the peat to rot where it fell, uneaten, its seeds sprouting sometimes out of its own collapsed sweetness. There are no trails except the water's. No dung, no nests, no burrows, no bones. Decay here belongs to water and warmth alone: things fall, they darken, they soften, they become soil, in that order, unhurried.

Which makes the lushness stranger, because the vegetation is lush in the way a single creature can be lush, not in the way a jungle is. The Cup is sparse. It is mostly emptiness — warm, wet, lit emptiness — and set out in that emptiness, at wide and irregular intervals, individual plants of great size and completeness.

A tree-fern stands alone with a crown ten paces wide, each frond arched and dripping, nothing growing in its shade but black peat and a skin of water. Twenty paces on, a cluster of giant horsetails rises in ribbed green columns taller than you, their joints weeping at every seam. Further still, a low plant spreads leaves the size of doors across the ground, leaves so engineered for wetness that rain runs uphill along their veins — you watch it, and it does, a bright thread creeping against gravity toward the raised centre of the leaf, by capillary, by surface, by some slow agreement between the plant and the water — and gathers there in a trembling lens that magnifies the leaf's green cells below.

Between these presences the ground is open: sphagnum in deep cushions of rust and gold and living green, shallow pools linked by threads of overflow, bare pans of dark stone worn into interlocking basins. The pools are another of the Cup's quiet peculiarities. They are nearly circular, one after another descending the slight slope of the floor like a staircase built for no feet, each rim worn glass-smooth, each surface holding the sky without a ripple until the overflow — an inch of water over a lip of moss — passes silently to the next. Nothing lives in them that moves. Their bottoms are clean sand and drowned leaves preserved almost intact in the warm stillness, yellowing very slowly.

Nothing here hurries, and nothing here is brief. The flowers take weeks to open and weeks to fold. The mist completes its circuit of the bowl perhaps once a day. A frond you saw furled in the morning will not have visibly changed by evening, and yet if you stayed a month it would stand open above you. Erosion has finished its argument with this place long ago and the two have come to terms: the rock no longer crumbles, it only polishes; the water no longer cuts, it only passes. Change happens at the speed of rot and condensation and growth, which is to say it happens constantly and is never once witnessed in the act.

There is one mark that does not belong to water or plant. Near the lowest pool, half-sunk in the peat at the edge of a flat slab of stone, lies a hammerstone — a cobble fitted to a palm, one face worn smooth — and beside it a flaked scraper of black obsidian, its edge still keen under a film of algae. The slab has a shallow depression ground into its centre, holding a spoonful of clear water. That is all. No wall, no hearth, no shelter, no other trace anywhere in the bowl; nothing built at all. Just the evidence that hands were once here, long enough ago that the moss has grown up to the tools' rims and stopped, as if undecided about covering them. Whoever knelt at that slab came up over the rim carrying almost nothing, did some small deliberate work, and left the same way, and the Cup has been returning to animal-lessness ever since, at its own pace, which is no pace at all.

If you stay long enough your clothes stop drying and stop quite getting wetter; you reach the humidity of the place and stay there. Condensation forms on your skin and does not run. Droplets form in the open air at the level of the fern crowns — not falling, just *appearing*, brightening on fine filaments you can barely see, until a frond bows almost imperceptibly and a dozen of them merge and go down together into the moss without a sound.

That is the rule of the High Cup, as it gradually teaches you: everything here is exchanged between stone, water, plant, and air, in a closed and patient circuit, and nothing is spent on movement or noise or hunger. The mountain keeps it warm. The rim keeps it small. The water keeps it wet. And the silence keeps everything else, perfectly intact, growing and rotting and weeping and shining, in a place no mouth has ever fed.
