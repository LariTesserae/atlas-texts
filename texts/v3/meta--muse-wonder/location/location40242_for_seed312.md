It begins far below the place itself, with the climbing.

For days the road is only a road in intention — a line of dark stones set into paler scree, a notch cut where the slope would otherwise be impassable, a cairn every half-hour's walk with a strip of undyed wool tied to it, snapping in a wind that never quite stops. The rock here is young. It has not been rounded by time. The mountains on either side rise in fresh, unsoftened angles, grey and black and a rusty brown where iron shows, their faces split into slabs and blades as if they were quarried yesterday. Scree pours from them in clean fans. Nothing grows on the scree except, in the damp shadow at its base, a crust of lichen the color of old ash and mustard.

The air thins as you go. Breathing becomes work you have to remember. Speech shortens. Above a certain height there are no trees at all, not even stunted ones — just wind, stone, and a tough, wiry grass that hugs the ground in tufts, olive and straw-colored, frost-whitened nine days out of ten.

And then the slope, impossibly, levels.

The plateau does not end. That is the first thing, and after days of looking up, it rearranges the eyes. It rolls away flat and high and cold in every direction, a continent lifted whole into the sky, big enough to make its own weather well inside its own borders. Clouds form out over its center on summer afternoons, having nothing to do with the clouds below its rim. Its far ranges are blue with distance and then white with snow and then gone. A river you cross on the third day across it is still, by local reckoning, near the edge.

It is always cold here. Not winter-cold as a season, but cold as a condition of altitude. At noon in the height of summer, in full sun and out of the wind, meltwater will run for an hour from a south-facing roof and a man can work in a woven shirt. By evening the puddles have skinned with ice again. In winter — and winter is most of the year, arriving slowly, staying long — breath freezes in beards and on the inner walls of houses, sled runners sing on packed snow, and exposed skin aches in minutes. The light, for all that, is ordinary and honest: a bright northern daylight, thin and clear, that makes distances deceptive and throws every sharp stone into hard shadow. On snow it is blinding. People wear slit goggles of bone when they travel far.

Water is scarce not because it never comes, but because it never stays liquid long, and never gathers large. There is no great river across the plateau, no lake you cannot walk around in a day. There is frost every night, and condensation, and pockets. Snowfields in the shaded hollows that feed, drop by drop through the brief thaw, a chain of stone cisterns. A spring where a fault forces water up, steaming faintly only because the air is so much colder than the ground, rimmed with a miraculous collar of green moss and tiny white flowers for two months a year. Melt-brooks that run brazen and chuckling at midday and are silent glass by dusk. Everything built here is built, half-consciously, around catching and keeping these small arrivings: roofs pitched to shed melt into a gutter of hollowed stone, courtyards sloped to a covered well, walls built double so that the night's frost forms *between* them and can be harvested in the morning rather than rotting the inner room.

And it is a city. That is the second rearrangement.

Not a city as a single walled knot, though there are knots. The plateau is urban the way a forest is wooded — continuously, unevenly, over an area too large to take in. Wherever water collects reliably, building collects. A spring becomes a ring of houses, becomes a neighborhood, becomes a town with its own market court and animal pens and granary mounds, and then the building thins to a road of packed earth and dung and crushed stone with houses strung along it every hundred paces, watch-shelters, fuel stacks, a shrine, until it thickens again at the next cistern-field into another town, indistinguishable in kind from the last. Ox-carts and sledges move along these roads at a walking pace, in both directions, all year. From a high pass you can see smoke — thin, straight, blue in the still cold — rising in a hundred places at once to the horizon, and lose count.

Up close it is dense. It has to be. Heat is wealth, and a solitary house bleeds it to the wind. So houses share walls. They crowd three and four stories high in the older towns, built of the sharp local stone laid without mortar where possible, chinked with clay and wool waste, roofed with slate and turf and flattened felt weighted with rocks. A later house is built against an earlier one, a room bridged over an alley, a courtyard roofed entirely and turned into a common hall, a stable absorbed into a ground floor whose ceiling is the floor of two families above, warmed all winter by the animals below. Alleys are narrow enough to touch both sides, and often stepped, and often roofed against snow, so that whole quarters are a kind of interior — dim, close, smelling of woodsmoke that is mostly dung-smoke, of wool grease and boiled grain and animal breath, lit by lamps of rendered fat with a single patient flame.

The technology of it is all in the hands. Stone is knapped, pecked, levered, dragged on rollers by teams of men and shaggy plateau cattle with horns as wide as a doorway. Iron is precious and mostly imported up the long road, reserved for the edges of tools that must have an edge; bronze is for fittings and bells; most cutting, digging, weaving, carving is done in stone, bone, horn, and wood brought up at enormous cost from the forests below the rim, and therefore reused for generations. A door lintel may be older than the house around it by a century. Looms are in every third house. So are grindstones, worn into saddles by use. Nothing is hurried because nothing can be — at this altitude heavy work must be done in short spells, rest, short spells again — and a substantial house is understood to be the work of a family across ten or fifteen years, wall by wall, as stone and labor and children accumulate.

The people share the density with animals, and this is not incidental. The plateau teems. Outside the towns, the grassland — sparse, close-cropped, shining with frost — supports herds that move across it in their tens of thousands: a heavy, long-haired kind of cattle, half-domesticated, following the melt from hollow to hollow; smaller, sure-footed sheep with fat tails; herds of wild antelope-like grazers that keep a wary half-mile from the roads in summer and come right down among the outskirts in hard winters, too hungry for fear. Marmot-like rodents whistle from burrow-towns that pock the ground for acres, standing upright like small sentries, vanishing all at once at the shadow of a hawk. And hawks, ravens, great lammergeier-type vultures, and in summer a wheeling, crying multitude of small brown birds work the air above it all, every day, as if the sky were another common pasture.

Inside the towns the animals do not stop. By law and custom older than any wall, the great herds have right of way. A drove coming through to water will fill a main street from wall to wall for an hour — steaming backs, clashing horns, calves calling, herders on foot alongside with long prods, entirely unhurried — and trade in the market court simply flows up onto the steps and waits, talking, until it passes. Every house keeps something: dogs, thick as bears, that sleep across thresholds; cats against the granary rats; penned sheep in the courtyard; chickens, improbably, in coops warmed by the kitchen wall, laying thin-shelled eggs that are a luxury. Pigeons and sparrows occupy the entire vertical city, nesting in every unchinked hole, and their soft, constant shuffling in the eaves is part of the sound of a house.

Which is to say it is, for all its numbers, a quiet place. Cold air and thin air do not carry noise generously, and snow, which lies in the alleys in packed form most of the year, swallows what is made. People speak low and close, conserving breath. The loudest ordinary sounds are animal and material: a bell on a lead ox, muffled in felt; stone on stone during building season; the creak and hiss of a sledge; a dog deciding, briefly, to announce itself; wind finding a slate loose somewhere in the quarter and worrying it through the night. On still evenings you can stand in a market court surrounded by three thousand souls and hear, distinctly, the drop-by-drop ticking of meltwater falling into a cistern two courtyards away.

Life follows the slow gradients the place allows. Grain — a black, bitter barley that can ripen in the short summer, and little else — is grown in walled fields near the towns where soil has been made, literally made, carried up in baskets and mixed with ash and dung over generations until it will hold a crop. It is harvested once, with stone-bladed sickles, and stored in dry cold that keeps it better than any cellar below. Marriages, debts, building projects, and journeys to the rim are all planned on a scale of seasons rather than weeks. News travels at the speed of a cart, which means a thing that happens at the eastern towns is a story, half-changed, by the time it reaches the west a month later, and no one finds this strange.

At night the plateau shows its scale differently. The towns go dark early — fat is not to waste — except for a lamp in a window here and there, a watch-fire on a granary roof. Between them the roads are empty, the grassland is empty, the herds are bedded down in vast, steaming, shifting encampments of themselves, guarded by dogs whose eyes catch light. The sky, at that height, is not strange, only very clear, and the ordinary stars are hard and numerous, and the cold comes down out of it like something poured.

In the morning there is frost on everything that was warm in the night — on the backs of the cattle, on the ropes, on the inner side of your own door — and the work of the day begins with gathering it, before the sun takes it, into a bucket, for the animals, for the wash, for the pot. It is not much water. It has never been much. It has been enough, at this density of patience, to build a world.
