It is not finished being a mountain yet.

That is the first thing you understand, coming up. For two days the lift has been climbing — not a cable car, exactly, but a vast industrial funicular, a riveted iron carriage hauled on chains thick as tree trunks, stopping at night at way-stations bolted to the cliff. Below you, weather. Below that, forests you can no longer pick out. Below that, a country that has already started getting your altitude wrong on its maps.

Above you there should be hard, thin sunlight. At this height the sun should be brutal. Instead it gets dimmer the higher you go.

By the last thousand meters you are traveling in a permanent blue-grey dusk. The sun is a pale, flattened coin behind a ceiling that is not quite cloud. It hangs perhaps a mile above the summit plateau and it does not move the way cloud moves. It breathes, very slowly. Its underside condenses and drips. Light comes through it filtered, shadowless, submarine. At noon it is twilight enough for the tram lines to run their blue electric lamps. At midnight, paradoxically, it is not much darker — the ceiling glows faintly with a stored, phosphorescent murk, as if it remembered the day badly.

The city is called Sessra, and it occupies a plateau some twenty kilometers across at an altitude no inhabited place should survive. The air should be unbreathable. It is merely cool, and damp, and strangely rich in your lungs near the ground — and then, three streets over, suddenly not. Breathable air pools here like water in hollows. It lies in the lower terraces, in market squares, in the fern-pits, invisible and knee-deep and then chest-deep. Old residents can read it by the animals. Where sparrow-flocks are skimming the cobbles, you can breathe easy. Where only the great pale moths go, beating slow wings the size of dinner plates, you hold your breath and hurry, and your ears pop, and the sound of your own steps comes back to you a half second before you make it.

No one fully trusts the altitude anymore. That is why everyone wears a small brass bell. Not for the fog, though there is fog — constant, condensing fog that beads on iron railings and runs down factory pipes in black rivulets. The bells are so that your echo cannot follow you home unnoticed.

Because the rock is new.

Sessra was not built on an old, eroded summit worn smooth by wind and ice. The mountain is emerging. It rises, a few centimeters a year, raw and unweathered, pushing up fresh basalt that has never been rain-softened. The stone at the edges of the plateau is sharp enough to cut boot leather. It comes up in black columns and glassy folds, still warm in the fissures, steaming faintly where the perpetual condensation touches it. There are no old valleys here, no rounded hills, no soil worth the name except what the city has made: crushed slag, ash from the foundries, compost, imported earth hauled up the lift at ruinous expense and now jealously guarded like flour.

And yet it is lush, in pockets, defiantly.

Wherever the city has carved a shelter — an abandoned quarry, a foundry yard, the lee of a tenement terrace — life has rushed in and overdone itself. Ferns unroll in the steam vents, huge and dark green, dripping. Cushion mosses colonize new rock within weeks, acid-yellow, violet, a deep arterial red. In the greenhouse terraces they grow lowland things that should die instantly at this height: fig trees, broad-leafed vines, night-blooming flowers six feet across that open only in the dimness and are pollinated, exclusively, by bats that arrived in crates of bananas fifty years ago and never left and are now the size of cats. The vines get into everything. They thread themselves through scaffolding and become load-bearing. Demolition crews sometimes discover a slum block is being held up less by its beams than by thirty years of intertwined roots.

The city itself is unmistakably a city — dense in intention, sparse in fact.

Imagine nine or ten industrial towns that were picked up and set down far apart from one another on a sea of raw stone, and then connected by causeways, narrow-gauge rail, and faith. Each district crowds in on itself: five- and six-story tenements of black brick and riveted iron, laundry lines, water towers, chapel spires, foundry stacks, markets under corrugated roofs where the condensation rains down all day in a warm metallic patter. Between the districts is emptiness. A forty-minute tram ride across bare, smoking rock fields with only survey stakes, a telegraph line, a solitary shepherd's hut. Then, abruptly, another knot of roofs and smoke and noise, another island of urban life insisting on itself in the void.

The trams are important. They are also loud, and the city, for all its slow pace, is never quiet.

Sessra runs on a slow industrial rhythm. The great forge-hammer in the Lower Foundry falls once every twenty-two seconds, and has, without stopping, for sixty years. You do not hear it at first so much as feel it arrive up through your teeth, a subsonic BOOM that makes the puddles on the plateau tremble in concentric rings. Piston engines sigh. Chain-lifts clank up the interior shafts of buildings. Freight elevators creak between terraces. Foghorns on the district watchtowers blow long, mournful, overlapping notes at intervals that have nothing to do with ships. And beneath and through all of it, the animals.

The fauna came up with the mountain, some of it. The rest followed the food and the warmth and the impossibility.

There are herds of shaggy, dwarf yaks that graze the moss beds between districts and sleep at night in perfect circles around the warm exhaust grates, steaming gently. There are rock goats that have learned to ride the freight platforms. There are millions of birds — alpine choughs, yes, but also swallows, pigeons, iridescent starlings, improbable parrots escaped from someone's great-grandmother, all of them mixed into clamorous, mixed-species flocks that pour at dusk between the stacks. They nest in the machinery. Half the maintenance budget of the power station goes to clearing nests out of turbines built, by birds, overnight, out of wire and blue glass and human hair.

There are things in the condensation cisterns. Pale, translucent frogs that sing in perfect mimicry of tram bells. Eels in the flooded undercrofts of the newest district, which has not, technically, been plumbed yet. Foxes with six toes that have learned the foundry whistle means shift-change and dropped lunches. In the unfinished zones at the northern rim, surveyors report herds of something tall and shy that stands perfectly still when a lamp is turned on it and is, when the lamp is turned off, several streets closer.

You learn the rules by watching what residents do without thinking.

They count stairs aloud, always, even in their own houses. Because a staircase that had fourteen steps in the morning can, by evening, in the fog, in the newer parts of town, have quietly taken on a fifteenth, which leads to a landing with a door that was not let to anyone. They do not open such doors. They mark them with chalk and notify the Office of Emergence, and a crew comes with rivet guns and seals it, politely, from their side.

They never build with straight load-bearing walls near the summit spire. The spire, at the center of the plateau, is where the mountain is most actively being born — a ragged, growing cone of midnight stone, perhaps another eight hundred meters above the city, wreathed always in its own private weather. There, formation gets ahead of intention. Streets extrude from the rock already cobbled. Low walls bud and branch. Sometimes, in the hottest fissures, a house begins: a recognizable arch, a window opening with the glass already in it, sweating condensation on the inside. The Office sends finishers, not builders — carpenters and masons whose job is to coax what the mountain has started into something habitable, to discourage extra rooms, to persuade a staircase to only go to floors that exist.

Children are forbidden from the spire not because they might fall, but because they might be finished. There are stories — always a cousin, always years ago — of a boy who came back from playing near the fresh rock a half inch taller along one side, with one ear that ever after heard sounds twenty seconds early.

Time, at this elevation, has the consistency of the air. It pools. It thins. In the Long Market, under its dripping roof, clocks run slow on wet days and vendors compensate by selling by weight rather than by the hour. Echoes are a municipal utility problem and sometimes a theological one. More than once the city council has had to rule on whether a voice heard giving instructions, in your own voice, from a sealed and unfinished room above a shop, constitutes a legal order. The ruling is: only if it also knows the bell-code.

And everything moves slowly, even the industry, even the growth. The furnaces take a week to cool and a month to fire. The herds migrate from one warm district to another across an entire season, a slow river of horns and bells in the blue dusk. A new cistern fills, drop by drop, from the condensing ceiling, over a year. The mountain rises. The districts have to be re-anchored to bedrock that is itself ascending, which means whole neighborhoods are on screw-jacks, and once a decade there is a Leveling Festival when all the bells are silenced for one terrifying, utterly silent hour — the only silence Sessra ever experiences — and ten thousand workers turn ten thousand iron screws a quarter-turn, and the city settles, groaning, back into agreement with itself.

People stay because the light is dim enough to think in. They stay because nothing up here has been worn down yet, not even grief. They stay because the mountain keeps making more world, clumsily, generously, with windows in it, and someone has to be there to open them and find out whether there is air on the other side, or a view of the plateau twenty seconds ago, or only the moths, waiting, beating their slow pale wings in air too thin for anyone but them, patiently, as if they know what is coming next.
