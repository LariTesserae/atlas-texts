It is called the Hatching Shelf, and you do not so much arrive at it as get admitted.

High up, above the true tree line, where the air should be thin and cold and clean, there is a ring. It is not a wall, exactly. Twelve pale pylons stand at irregular intervals around a bowl no wider than a hamlet — you could walk across it in ten minutes, if you kept moving, which you must — and between them the air shimmers as if over a stove. Passing through, your ears pop. Your lungs fill too easily. The wind that was cutting at you on the outer slope falls away, and a warmer, thicker weather takes you in its mouth.

Inside is dim, even at noon.

A veil hangs over the whole Shelf, hundreds of feet up, a hovering mesh of dark lenses that drink the hard mountain sunlight and let it through softened and scattered. It moves slowly, adjusting, opening a seam here, closing one there, so the light below is never direct — a green-gray, underwater brightness, as if the place were still beneath some shallow sea it remembers. Under it, everything condenses. Mist beads on stone that was dry a minute ago. Your sleeves dampen. Your hair dampens. Breath shows, not from cold, but from saturation.

And it is warm. Geothermally, technologically warm. Heat rises through the soles of your boots.

The ground is new. Not young in the way a fresh lava flow is young — this has not cooled and cracked and begun its long surrender. It has never surrendered at all. Erosion has no purchase here. There are no rounded pebbles, no soil worth the name, no gullies worn patiently down. Instead the rock is still deciding to be rock.

It comes up in low, embryonic ridges, in knuckles and spines and half-formed terraces, a raw basalt foam so recently assembled it is still slightly yielding underfoot, like walking on dense bread crust. In places it is glassy and black and ringing; in places matte, ash-pale, unfinished, the color not yet set. Veins of quartz push through it overnight like frost in reverse. If you watch a crack long enough — and people do, kneeling with their instruments forgotten — you can see it close. Not shift. Close, with a fine ticking sound, extruding a bright seam of mineral that hardens in the damp air.

Scratch your initials and they fill in before you have finished the second letter. Drop a tool and a thin stony skin begins to climb its handle within the hour. Stand still too long in conversation and your boot prints deepen, then rise around your ankles. The Shelf is under construction at the molecular scale: clouds of builders too small to see, shepherded by the pylons, knitting stone upward, correcting gravity in small local drafts, proofreading the landscape as it goes. Sometimes the correction is visible, a pebble rolling hesitantly uphill to fill a hollow. Sometimes it is audible: a deep stressed groan going through a new ridge as it learns how much weight it is supposed to carry.

Water has not learned its routes yet either.

It weeps straight out of rock faces in warm sheets. It gathers in tiered, steaming pools that form, fill, drain, and reform two terraces lower by afternoon. A stream may run clear and purposeful between mossy banks at breakfast and by evening be absorbed entirely into a field of new stone, its sound continuing a little while underground, muffled, stubborn. Rain hardly needs to fall; the veil condenses it out of rising vapor in continuous cycles. Droplets form on the undersides of overhangs the size of pinheads, swell, drop, form again. Everything drips. Everything plinks and hisses. Runoff carries a faint mineral sweetness and leaves, where it evaporates on hot stone, ephemeral maps in white salt that the next mist erases.

Vegetation is trying, but politely, and mostly failing to keep up.

There is no forest here, no grassland. Only pioneers. Thumb-sized mats of liverwort in the wettest cracks, shining a furious green against the gray. A single fern in a sheltered angle, unfurling visibly over the course of an afternoon, frond by frond, as if time-lapse were just the local speed. Lichen in orange and sulfur-yellow constellations where the builders allow it. Now and then a seedling tree appears where a bird has dropped it — brave, pale, two leaves — and within a few days it is either lovingly relocated by the keeper-drones to a nursery crate, or gently outcompeted by the stone itself growing up around its stem.

Because the animals got here before the plants, or were brought.

For such a sparse and unfinished place, it is crowded with life, and almost all of it is loud. Glass frogs crowd the warm pools at dusk, perfectly transparent except for their beating hearts and their enormous voices, a chorus of struck bells that makes conversation impossible near the water. Fat, flightless rails with iridescent throat feathers pick through the new rubble for mineral insects, barking like small dogs at each other across absurd distances. Colonies of rock martins nest in any crevice the stone hasn't filled yet and take off in coordinated bursts whenever a terrace settles, screaming as one animal. There are bats that hunt by day in the dimness. There are luminescent newts in the weeping walls. A herd of six miniature, sure-footed grazers — engineered, presumably, or just very opportunistic goats with elongated, almost fingerlike hooves — wanders from lichen patch to lichen patch, never still, tearing and chewing and calling back and forth in nasal bleats. The insects are loudest for their size: thumb-long crickets whose wings resonate against hollow new stone and amplify to a sawmill whine.

Nothing here is quiet for long, because quiet is a kind of stillness, and stillness lets the ground begin to take you.

The human marks are few, and all of them temporary by design. Near the south pylon, a monitoring hut stands on tall, adjustable stilts, its feet being perpetually un-grown by a low field that buzzes against your teeth if you walk under it. Its walls are a papery composite printed with maps that rewrite themselves as the terraces change; this morning's path may be annotated in fresh ink by evening as a small cliff. A half-bridge reaches over the central pool from nowhere to nowhere, being lengthened six inches a day, testing spans. A handful of marker flags on dissolving stakes. A single bench, constantly being moved by whoever sat on it last and remembered just in time. Nothing is anchored. Nothing expects to stay.

And the Shelf is learning other things besides stone.

On certain afternoons, the veil draws its lenses into a tighter lattice, and for twenty minutes the bowl is allowed a hard rehearsal of weather: a small, violent, perfectly local rain, with its own thunder borrowed and amplified through the pylons, hammering the new ridges to see how they sing. The frogs go berserk with joy. The rails run for the hut. The whole bowl steams afterward like a horse. Then the mist returns, and the builders emerge again in their trillionfold patience, repairing what the lesson broke, improving the drainage next time by an inch.

You understand, after a while, that the dimness is not protection from the sun so much as childhood. That this little high place — warm in thin air, wet in dry altitude, alive before it is green — is a landscape in draft, being kept from the full glare and the full wind until it knows itself well enough to be left alone. One day the pylons will power down. The veil will disperse. The pools will find the courses they have been practicing. The stone, finally told to stop growing, will begin the opposite and much longer art of wearing away.

But not yet. Toward evening the light goes amber through the mesh, the pools hold the color a moment longer than the air does, and the Shelf ticks and drips and calls and grows around you. A new spine of rock, perhaps a hand's breadth higher than it was at dawn, throws its first real shadow. Somewhere under it, water is already finding a way through, patient as an idea.
