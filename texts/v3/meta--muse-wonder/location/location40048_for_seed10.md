It is high, and it is already finished.

That is the first contradiction you have to walk through. The rock here ended its argument with the world a very long time ago. What was once a range is now a vast, cold table — a peneplain tilted just enough to dream of a sea it will never reach, its mountains ground down to whaleback domes, to smooth granite shields polished by ice that left before anything with eyes was here to see it. There are no sharp edges left in the stone. No scree, no fresh cliffs. Every pebble is a bead. Every basin is a shallow saucer worn round as a thumbprint.

And on top of that final, settled silence, everything is in violent motion.

The plateau spreads for a whole region — valley after valley of open moor without ever quite descending, horizons made only of weather. The light is low even at noon, filtered through fast, bruised cloud and through mist that condenses directly out of the air onto your skin. It is cold in the way high wet places are cold: not dry winter cold, but a penetrating, soaking chill that lives in the peat and rises through the soles of your feet. Frost feathers form in the morning on standing water and are torn to lace by wind by midday. Your breath never quite disappears.

Water is not *in* this landscape. It is barely underneath it.

Rain comes sideways in veils. It beads on lichen, sheets off granite, and vanishes into ground that sighs. Because the ground is not ground. From edge to edge, unbroken, the plateau is plant.

A living mat, ten to thirty feet thick in the old places, floats and roots and regrows itself over black water. Sphagnum in rust red and mustard and a pale, exhausted green; cotton grass; stunted sedges with silica edges that ring faintly when the wind hits them; liverworts sealing the seams between pools like mortar. You walk on a lung. Each step depresses a whole yard of it, water welling up cold and tea-brown around your ankles, and behind you the mat rebounds, erasing the print in a slow hydraulic shrug. In places the peat below has compressed over millennia into seams of slick, half-coal that exhale methane in warm bubbles. Where a dome of rock breaches, iron-eating bacteria have laid down sheets of rust, thin as tin, that curl and peel in the freeze-thaw.

Vegetation is total, and yet the place feels sparse. Empty, even. Because between presences there is so much *distance*. The emergent things stand alone, marooned in acres of low mat.

A single furnace-flower, taller than a person, its thick stem banded like a smokestack with insulating scales, its bloom a closed furnace of purple-black petals that generates its own metabolic heat — it will stand by itself in a steaming clearing it has melted clear of frost, attended by clouds of midges and snow-moths that could not otherwise survive at this altitude. A mile away, another. A mile beyond that, a grove — if you can call it that — of turbine trees, their trunks not wood but dense, slow lignin wound tight as cable, their crowns reduced to three-bladed seed vanes that spin ceaselessly in the plateau wind. They do not photosynthesize much, those crowns. They *mill* the wind. The spinning generates static that crawls blue-white down conductive bark filaments into the mat, where root-nodules store it in chemical batteries of acid and fat. On stiller nights — there are almost none — you can hear them ticking as they cool.

There are no buildings. Nothing has ever been built here. But at dusk, from across a basin, the moor sounds industrial on a continental scale.

The furnace-flowers pump. Deep pistons of vascular tissue contract in sequence, thump-thump-thump, forcing hot sap up to keep the bloom at a constant blood-warm temperature, venting excess steam in rhythmic puffs. The turbine trees whine up the scale when a gust front hits, drop to a clattering idle in the lull. Lightning rods — that is what the tallest sedge spires are, hollow reeds plated inside with conductive salts, ten meters high, vibrating — find each other arc to arc across pools, stitching the sky to the mat with brief, stuttering discharges that smell of ozone and hot pennies. Underneath it all the water itself is loud, sucking and gulping through peat channels, slapping against rock domes, boiling up in springs that arrive already carbonated from below. And over it, the wind, never not moving, editing every sound into a roar.

Turbulence is the surface law. Storm cells are born in the saucers, spin up black and fast, dump their water and die in an hour. Lakes migrate. A pool you camped beside — glassy, reflecting a sky that is not quite the sky overhead, the clouds in the reflection moving *against* the wind — may by morning have drained with a sigh into a new crack and reappeared fifty yards downhill, complete with its population of stunned, translucent frogs. Channels reverse. In the strongest gusts the spray off the bigger tarns lifts in sheets and *does* climb, streaming uphill over the granite in defiance of the slope, because the wind owns the top inch of this world more completely than gravity does.

The mat, endlessly storm-tossed, also creates and uncreates in the Solaris sense down in its dark. Peat and root and warm mineral water, churned together under pressure, extrude forms. Mostly they are aborted: a perfect feather of pure white fungus that dissolves at touch; a fish shape of compressed moss that pulses once or twice on the surface and goes still. Sometimes they are better. Walkers tell of finding their own footprint from the day before returned to them slightly ahead on the path, fresh. Or of a grazing beast — and the real beasts here are many — shadowed for an afternoon by a second beast made of woven sedge, mimicking the limp, until the wind worried it apart.

The fauna, improbably, is abundant for a place this cold and this empty. What little heat the industrial plants throw off makes archipelagos of mercy. Crane-analogues, long-legged and slate-grey, stalk the pools in loose companies of hundreds, striking at electric eels that live in the conductive shallows and light up faintly blue when seized. Herds of low, shaggy grazers with splayed, snowshoe hooves spread their weight across the mat and crop the cotton grass, moving constantly, followed by iridescent starling-things that feast on the insects their passage startles. The furnace-flowers have dedicated pollinator beetles, big as thumbs, armored in their own insulated carapaces, who winter *inside* the bloom and emerge scorched and drunk on nectar fermented by the plant's own heat. At night the frogs in the warm springs chorus so loudly that conversation near a basin is impossible; each spring has a slightly different pitch, set by its depth, so the plateau after dark rings like a foundry of bells.

The weirdness is not an event here, it is a medium, like the wet. Reflections lag. You wave at a tarn and your reflected hand completes the gesture a breath later, thoughtfully. Echoes on the coldest mornings arrive in the wrong order — the tail of your shout returning first, thin and knowing, the beginning catching up after. Seeds from the turbine trees sometimes germinate in mid-air during an electrical discharge, sprout a frantic inch of root toward a ground they never reach, and wither, falling as tiny, fully-formed, dead trees. Oldest of all is the sense that the mat remembers pressure. Step in a place where one of the great grazers died a century ago and the depression, somehow retained in the root architecture, will sigh out a bubble of air that smells faintly, horribly, of that animal's last breath.

Rules assemble themselves if you stay:

Do not trust still water; drink only from a spring you can hear actively boiling up. Do not camp in a saucer that has flowers turning to face you — they turn to heat, and if they have chosen you, the local midges have too. Cross the mat at a steady pace and never retrace exactly, because the rebounded path holds your warmth for a while and things follow warmth. When the turbine trees all stop at once, lie flat in the wet regardless of dignity; it means the air above the plateau has become briefly heavier than the air below it, and what happens next is not wind so much as the sky changing its mind.

By evening, if the cloud tears — it does, suddenly, completely, for minutes at a time — you can see how high you are. Lower cloud seas fill the valleys beyond the plateau's rim, white and slow, and the light goes amber and raking across a world that is all surface, all skin: black pools, red moss, pale stone, solitary plumes of flower-steam, the first arcs beginning to jump between sedge spires. No roads interrupt it. No roof, no wall, no fence has ever divided it. It is an engine that built itself out of plants over an ancient, exhausted skeleton of rock, running loud and hot in isolated spots across an enormous cold, producing electricity and weather and brief, mistaken animals, and caring for none of it.

Then the cloud closes. The frost comes back mid-fall, needling through the rain. The mat shivers under you, adjusting to your weight with a patience older than the concept of ground.
