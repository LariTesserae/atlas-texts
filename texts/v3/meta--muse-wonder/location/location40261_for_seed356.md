There is no edge to it, in any practical sense. There is a place where the ground lowers, the air changes, and the dark begins — and after that, the same conditions continue far enough that distance stops behaving like distance. You do not cross it. You are only ever deeper, or less deep, inside it.

It is a single low basin, continental in extent, sunk and warm and newly made. Under everything lie fresh basalts, black glassy ridges still keeping the sharpness of their cooling, unworn reefs and fracture-planes that in another landscape would have softened long ago into soil and round stone. Here they never had the chance. The plants came before erosion could begin, sealed the stone under root and mat and standing water, and preserved the young land exactly as it emerged: keen-edged, mineral, unfinished, held forever in its first form beneath a living cover that does not let weather touch it.

Above you — far above, at heights that belong to a different world — there is presumably sky. No light from it reaches here. The canopy is not a roof of leaves so much as a stacked series of roofs, stratum upon stratum of light-eating growth, each layer taking what the one above missed, until what filters to the bottom is not dimness but a true, settled darkness. It has weight. Your eyes do not adjust to it in any useful way. After a while you stop expecting to see and begin to read the place by other means: by heat on the skin, by humidity, by the faint electric prickle in the air, by touch.

It is warm everywhere, and wet, and the wetness and warmth never vary.

Water stands in shallow black sheets between the roots, inches deep, body-temperature, perfectly still. It condenses out of the saturated air onto cool conducting stems and runs down them in slow beads. It weeps from the undersides of leaves large as floors. It gathers in bladders, in trough-shaped fronds, in natural-seeming basins that steer it, always, back into the system. There is no rain here in the ordinary sense, and yet certain fixed districts are always raining — a column of falling condensation a mile across that has always fallen there and always will, feeding the same pool, which overflows along the same black channel, into the same root-mouths. A mile away the air is merely dripping. Ten miles away there are exhalation chimneys where warm vapor rises year after year without dispersing anything, because the air above is already exactly as wet as the air below. The vastness makes its own weather, and then holds that weather perfectly still: permanent fog-banks with surveyable borders, permanent drizzle, permanent dry heat over the furnace-roots, all of it zoned, all of it unchanging, like climate drawn on a map that has become real.

And everywhere, filling every zone, is vegetation. Not vegetation *on* the land. Vegetation as the land, the walls, the ceiling, the machinery, the medium you move through.

The great trunks are the first thing your hands find. They rise out of the flood in close order, slick, warm, fibrous, some slender, some wider than houses, all of them absolutely vertical and standardized in a way wild wood never is. They are not only supports. Inside each one, sap moves in separated channels the way fluids move in a plant built for industry: up one bore, down another, diverted at junctions by living valves that open and close with a softness you can feel through the bark but never hear. Some trunks are chimneys, warm inside. Some are hydraulic columns, subtly swelling and relaxing. Some are electrical — copper-rich sap creeping in insulated vessels, carrying a slow, soundless current harvested far above and spent far below, so that the whole forest hums electrically in a register below hearing. Stand between two such trunks long enough and your hair lifts. Nothing arcs. Nothing sparks. There is no waste display of power here. The current simply goes, endlessly, to where it has always gone.

Because this is a factory. Nothing in it was built.

Between the columns the production never stops and never, in any larger sense, happens. Long leaves unroll as conveyor surfaces, ribbed and perpetually moving, carrying green pulp, seed blanks, mineral nodules, sheets of pressed fiber from one organ to another. Flowering heads the size of mill wheels turn with geological slowness in baths of black oil-sap, meshing tooth against woody tooth in absolute silence, because every bearing is grown wet, padded in moss, lubricated to muteness. Bladder-trees distill. Long extrusion-trunks extrude: continuous ropes of cellulose, cordage, tubing, matting, which travel on leaf-belts to chopping mouths that reduce them again to pulp. Furnace-roots, sunk deep where the young rock is still hot, glow only in the infrared — you know them by the shimmer of hotter air and the smell of baked resin — smelting nothing that anyone will use, refining silica and iron and copper out of floodwater and stone, plating them onto growing forms, then stripping them again. In the plating marshes, identical nodules hang in electrochemical pools by the millions, acquiring perfect metallic skins, dropping, dissolving, beginning again.

Everything is standardized. That is the deepest strangeness. A leaf is not roughly like the leaf before it. It is the same leaf, grown to template, occupying the same position, after the previous one was pulped on schedule. A stem bends at exactly the angle its predecessor bent. The seed-presses stamp out seeds by the ton — hard, machined-looking, geometrically perfect — which are conveyed, sorted, planted by descending root-drills, grown into the same organs in the same arrangement, or else, in the districts assigned to that purpose, ground unused back into feedstock. Pollination happens mechanically between shuttered flowers that meet like coupling machines. There are no insects to do it. There are no animals at all: no call, no bite, no burrow, no dung, no carrion, no quick movement in the corner of anything. Life here is one kingdom only, divided not into species so much as into departments: pump and refinery, furnace and loom, capacitor and press, root-logic and canopy-power, caste after caste of differentiated plant tissue extending in industrial provinces hundreds of miles wide.

And it does not change. Individual parts move constantly — belts creep, pistons of turgid wood swell and ease, rotary flowers turn — but the movement is all circular, all replacement, all return. Growth and manufacture here are not ways of becoming something else. They are how the place manages to remain exactly itself. Cut a path and the path seals behind you into the same configuration it had before you passed, not healing toward some new shape but restoring a plan. Burn a district — if you could burn anything in this saturated dark — and the re-growth would not be succession. It would be reprint. Stand in one spot for a century and the same leaf would drip the same bead onto the same place on your shoulder, leaf after leaf after leaf, each one new, the event identical.

Sound has been almost entirely designed out by accident of materials. Everything is soft, wet, fibrous, oil-fed. The air itself is thick and loaded with floating fiber and spore-dust, swallowing what little noise is made. The biggest machines move slowly enough that their sound never assembles. The result is a silence that is not empty but upholstered — an immense, padded quiet in which your own breath seems intrusive and your heartbeat sounds like something happening in another room. Even the falling water in the rain-districts lands in vegetation and is muted to a continuous, textureless hush that, after an hour, the mind files as more silence.

What the place makes, finally, is itself. Current for the pumps, pumps for the water, water for the cooling, cooling for the furnaces, furnaces for the minerals, minerals for the conductors, conductors for the current. Fiber into pulp into fiber. Seed into press into seed. It takes in sunlight somewhere beyond sight and heat from the young stone below, and it spends both not on progress but on maintenance of an equilibrium so complete that time, inside it, has nothing to act on. There is no day. There is no season. There is no decay that is not immediately a scheduled input somewhere else.

Travel, if it can be called that, is a matter of being handed along. The floor is not floor everywhere — it is root-mat, flood, conveyor-frond, service channel of pressed bark. In places the belts will simply take your feet and carry you, slowly and surely, in the direction materials are meant to flow, between smelter-marshes and looming distillation crowns, through districts of weaving stems where sheets of living cloth close overhead, past storage silos grown as hollow trees full of identical dormant seeds, all in the dark, all at the same temperature, all smelling of warm wet lignin and faint ozone and slow fermentation — a smell that never strengthens or fades and that, in time, you cease to smell at all.

Go far enough in any direction and the provinces repeat at larger scale, the same organs, the same processes, arranged again. There is no center anyone has found, no source-trunk, no first flower. Perhaps there doesn't need to be. At this scale the forest is less an organism than a physical law that happens to be alive: if it is warm, wet, dark, electrified, plant-filled, and silent here, then it is so a thousand miles from here, and it was so before, and it will be so after, the belts still creeping, the valves still opening, the rain still falling in its appointed columns onto the unworn black stone of a young world that will never grow old.
