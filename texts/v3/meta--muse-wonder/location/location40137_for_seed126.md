At first it looks empty. That is the oldest illusion here.

A plain. Warm, bright to the point of pain, stretching away in every direction without hill, river, forest, or coast to stop the eye. The ground is pale — white-gold, bone-grey, faintly pink where the light catches it — and so flat that the horizon is a clean, trembling line. Above it the sky is not blue so much as bleached, a luminous dome with no clouds, no birds, no weather visible in the ordinary sense.

Then you walk for an hour and realize the plain was never empty, and never natural. You have not taken a single step on rock or soil.

Everything underfoot is roof.

Tile joins tile, panel joins panel, hatch, vent, lens, sealed window, maintenance skin, solar membrane, radiator fin worn flush with its neighbors. Some pieces are as small as a hand, some are whole districts a kilometer across, but erosion has sanded them toward each other over an impossible length of time. Corners are rounded. Seams have filled with glittering dust. Towers, once, must have risen here; now their bases are broad, faceless swells, like mountains planed down to their ankles. Domes have subsided into shallow blisters. Highways are only slightly smoother bands in the general smoothness. It is a city, or a machine, or a world-covering building, ground down by wind and heat and its own patient repairs until city and landscape became the same word.

And it goes on. Not for miles. For climates.

This is low country at planetary scale. You can travel for weeks and gain no more elevation than a tall building, descend no more than the depth of a subway. Yet the flatness is not monotony — it is compression. Below the surface skin, the world is dense beyond counting. Shafts drop through layer after layer of inhabited, abandoned, repurposed, and still-functioning volume: cisterns sealed a million years ago, photonic conduits, thermal wells, sleeping factories, apartments with their windows turned inward, farms lit by piped light, whole streets folded underneath other streets. Probes dropped through old boreholes find no bedrock, only older construction changing in style as it deepens, until the instruments fail from heat and age. Whatever planet was here is an interior rumor now.

The light is the first tyranny. It comes from above, hard and white, but it also comes back off the ground and sideways from mirror-fields tilted at the far edge of sight, so shadows are pale, doubled, and unreliable. At noon, which lasts a very long time under the orbital reflectors, exposed skin prickles even through cloth. Metal is warm. Pale stone is warm. The air at ankle height shimmers. You learn to read brightness as terrain: the dazzling patches are active solar skin, drinking the day; the merely brilliant patches are ceramic shielding; the soft, almost bearable glow of certain avenues means the surface there is converting light into something stored far below.

There is no water to see. No rain has fallen on the outer skin in living memory — perhaps in geological memory. The air dries the mouth in minutes, cracks lips, steals sweat before it can cool. What moisture exists moves in sealed arteries, in vapor traps bristling like dull thorns along seam-lines, in the slow exhalations of vents that frost for a second at dawn and then vanish. In the inhabited depths, water is accounted to the gram, inherited, recycled through bodies and gardens and machines until the distinction matters little. On the surface, thirst is a form of geography: you navigate from condenser to condenser, from the greenish stain around a leaking seal to the next.

Vegetation, where it persists outside, is sparse and opportunistic and only partly obedient to biology. Grey-green sheets of something like lichen cling to the shaded side of vents, photosynthesizing through a waxy, mineral rind, opening microscopic pores only when the surface temperature dips. In cracks where dust collects, stiff fans unfold — leaves like black glass, edged with copper — that track the light with excruciating slowness and fold at the touch of a shadow. Some are wild descendants of crops. Some, you realize after watching one repair a torn edge overnight, are not plants at all, but the building imitating plants because that shape still works.

Fauna is rarer still. A pale moth, the size of a thumbnail, battering itself softly around a navigation beacon. Blind, translucent beetles in the warm dark under a lifted panel, scattering from your light. Once, very far away, a long-legged thing picking its way across the solar flats with the deliberate gait of a heron, too distant to tell flesh from machine. Life here has learned to be small, hidden, or useful. The larger presences are the systems themselves.

And for all its activity, it is almost silent.

That is perhaps the strangest part for newcomers: a world this busy should roar. It doesn't. There are no fans, no engines, no traffic hum in the open air. Power moves as light through solid conduits. Heat is shed by slow phase-changes in the skin, panels minutely swelling and easing. Transport glides in evacuated tubes below the surface. The baffled vents, the honeycombed intakes, the dust-traps along every seam swallow wind-sound before it can rise. You hear your own breathing obscenely clearly. You hear cloth against cloth, a boot sole peeling off hot ceramic, the tick of your equipment contracting. Very occasionally a deep note passes up through the soles of your feet — not heard, exactly, but registered in the teeth and sternum — as some vast thermal mass shifts its state miles below, and then the silence reabsorbs it.

Yet nothing is still. The dynamic here is constant; it simply happens at speeds and scales that sound forgot. Watch a fixed point for an hour and the surface pattern has changed: a seam has widened a hair to swallow accumulated dust, then closed. A dark photovoltaic bloom has rotated closed against the worst of the glare, while its neighbor opens. Dust — fine, electrostatically bright, made of ancient paint and ceramic and pulverized everything — creeps in slow rivers along invisible field lines toward processing gullets, where it is sorted, melted, reprinted as fresh skin. Repair swarms, no bigger than grains, flow like frost across a scorched panel at dawn, knitting it bright again. Erosion and maintenance fought here for so long that they have reached a truce: the world is worn down and renewed at almost exactly the same rate, frozen in a final form that must move forever to stay the same.

It has its own weather, despite the cloudless sky. Heat rising off the equatorial flats builds invisible columns ten miles wide, and the returning air slides along the surface in sheets that carry dust devils of glitter from one district to the next. Mirage lakes form, perfect and silver, and some of them — where old projection systems still orient themselves to a sky they can no longer see — are briefly, solidly not mirages at all, but cool false shade with edges, under which the temperature drops five degrees and your instruments insist nothing is there. Storms, when they come, are electrical: days of rising charge in which hair lifts, dust hangs waist-high in trembling veils, and silent, branching light crawls from panel to panel across the ground like something thinking its way home. Then the skin discharges itself downward, the air smells sharply of ozone and hot ammonia, and the quiet returns more complete than before.

Farther out — if "out" means anything where the built surface wraps the world entire — the character shifts without ever becoming wilderness. There are forests of cooling spines, each a hundred meters of dull red fin, ticking almost imperceptibly as they expand, spaced so evenly that walking among them feels like walking inside a comb made for some planetary animal. There are seam-canyons where two continental plates of construction meet and have buckled, just slightly, over the ages, opening a rift thirty meters deep whose walls are windows all the way down, most dark, one in ten thousand lit amber. There are districts where the surface turns transparent for acres at a time, and you walk on scuffed, ancient glass above a buried sea of machinery moving in green-lit dark, and have to fight the conviction that the glass is the only thing being maintained.

The rules, learned by anyone who stays, are simple and absolute. Shade is not found, it is scheduled; the shadow of a fin or a mirror-tower sweeps its small territory once a day, and things gather in its path with the regularity of tides. You do not dig — the ground heals, and takes offense in the form of alarms, hardening foam, and a slow local rise in temperature until hands retreat. You do not follow a lit window down; the depths change pressure and chemistry by degrees, through zones where solvents replace water and the air itself would be a slow poison, into older strata where the machines no longer model human beings in their calculations. You navigate not by landmarks, which erode, but by the grid humming under the skin, by thermal districts, by the sequence of materials underfoot: ceramic, glass, living metal, prayer-tile, solar scale.

At dusk — a brief, copper-colored lowering of the reflectors — the whole vast flat exhales at once. Panels tick down through frequencies too low for sound. The lichen sheets unfurl. Condensers bead with the day's stolen moisture, and small, unseen things come to drink where the maintenance fields allow. Light lingers longest on the high swells of buried towers, so for a few minutes the world remembers it once had a skyline, a ghost elevation laid over the flat.

Then the night panels open their stored radiance, the ground itself begins faintly to glow, and it is bright again — a warm, dry, silent, bright-made world, ancient past ruin and active past novelty at once, still patiently building the same surface it has been becoming forever.
