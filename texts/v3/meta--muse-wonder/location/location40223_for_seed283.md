There is no true daylight here, and no true cooling at night. 

You enter it from the low ridge — for a few minutes there is sky, a white, hammering heat above the treetops, the canopy spread out below you in a dark green ocean that goes on for twenty kilometres to the next rise of ground. Then you go down under it, and the world closes.

Three layers of trees take the light. First the emergents, fifty metres up, their crowns flat and separated, baking. Below them a second, continuous roof where the leaves touch and overlap and knit together with lianas. Below that, a third, hungrier layer of young palms, tree-ferns, and saplings waiting in whatever green dusk leaks through. By the time any sun reaches the floor it has been filtered, stolen, and spent a dozen times. What remains is not shade so much as a permanent, humid twilight, brown-green and close, where your eyes never quite finish adjusting. At noon it is dark. At midnight it is only a little darker.

And it is still hot. The heat has nowhere to go. The basin is low — lower than the country around it — and the air sits in it, saturated, unmoving, held down by the canopy like steam under a lid. Heat comes up off the black soil at night. It comes off the trunks. It shimmers, invisibly, off pools that are warm as skin. Sweat does not evaporate; it just joins the general wetness. Cloth, hair, skin, leaf, bark — everything is filmed with condensation even when it hasn't rained, which it does, briefly and without warning, in a warm rush through the leaves that lasts ten minutes and leaves every surface dripping for hours after.

Water is everywhere and nowhere you can use it at scale. This is not a swamp and not a river country. There is no lake, no wide stream cutting through. Instead the water hides in pockets inside the larger dampness: a few slow, tea-brown creeks that meander, stall, and lose themselves in root mats; oxbow hollows left by a creek that changed its mind a century ago; depressions in the clay that fill in the wet weeks and shrink to cracked mud in the dry ones. Water sits in the cupped centres of bromeliads thirty feet up, in hollow logs, in the funnels of fallen leaves, in old animal wallows and the deep prints of heavy feet. Each one is a complete, temporary world. The forest floor itself never fully dries. Leaf litter lies three inches deep in places, sodden, fermenting underneath — layer upon layer of fallen leaves gone soft and black, held together by white fungal threads, steaming faintly with its own rot.

The ground has been like this for a very long time. Nothing here is raw or newly cut. The valley sides are worn down to long, soft shoulders. The creeks have no banks to speak of; they ease. Tree roots do the work of stone: buttress roots tall as a person, flanged and moss-slick, grip the clay against any wash. Where a giant falls it does not leave a scar. It lies down, softens, is covered in fungi and seedlings within a season, and becomes a long, fertile ridge that other things live on for fifty years. Erosion, growth, collapse, and regrowth have reached a balance so old it feels like stillness.

It is, in fact, packed to a degree that stillness cannot explain. Every surface is occupied.

Look at a trunk in the half-dark and the bark resolves into moths — dozens of them, wing against wing, patterned exactly like lichen and wet wood. Look at a leaf and there are smaller leaves' worth of insects underneath it: katydids, stick insects, sleeping lizards the colour of new growth, tree frogs with their throats pulsing silently, waiting. The air at face height is full of midges and tiny bees that find the moisture at your eyes. The leaf litter moves, not dramatically, but perpetually, at the edge of vision — millipedes like slow armoured trains, beetles shouldering through, earthworms surfacing in the wet, land crabs sidling from burrow to burrow with their claws tucked. In the pools, water beetles row, tadpoles graze algae off submerged leaves, leeches wave from the undersides of stones testing the water for warmth, and a soft-shelled turtle may be buried in the mud with only its nostrils breaking the surface for hours at a time.

Up above it continues. Monkeys move through the middle canopy in the early dark, not calling, just dislodging a rain of spent water and dead leaves as they pass. Sloths hang so thick with green algae in their fur that they are effectively another kind of branch, and moths live in them, and the moths' droppings feed the algae — a whole travelling garden, moving two metres an hour. Fruit bats pour out of hollow trees at full night in a soft, leathery river and are gone into the canopy to feed, their wings almost silent. Under them go the owls. The snakes are in the branches, in the litter, in the water, digesting meals that will take them weeks. Jaguars use the old dry creek beds as roads, because everything does. There is no empty niche, no surface unpatrolled, no fallen fruit that is not found within minutes.

And yet it is mostly quiet. This is the strangest part of being inside it. A place this alive should be loud. Instead the overwhelming fauna is a waiting fauna. Ambush and camouflage and patience do better work here than chase and display. What you hear is mostly water: drips, at irregular intervals, from every height. A soft ticking as beetles move through dry leaves under the wet ones. Now and then a frog gives a single, liquid plop of a call from a bromeliad and does not repeat it. The faint, high machinery-hum of insects is so constant it becomes a kind of silence underneath the other silences. A fruit, heavy and overripe, lets go somewhere above and lands in the litter with a thud that makes everything within ten metres freeze for a full minute.

Nothing human has been built here. No clearing, no wall, no fire scar kept open, no path that isn't an animal's path fading back into undergrowth. And still, if you stay long enough, you start to feel as if the place is industrial — not built, but *run*, with production going on at a scale and with a specialisation that has nothing casual about it.

The leafcutter ants are the clearest of it. Their highways cross the floor in bare, swept lines of packed earth, worn smooth by millions of feet, and along them through the dark comes a two-way current: workers outbound empty-jawed, workers inbound each carrying a crescent of green leaf many times its size, so that the line from above looks like a moving conveyor of tiny sails. They do not eat the leaves. They carry them down into chambers below the soil to feed fungus gardens, which they tend, weed, and harvest — agriculture, warehousing, waste disposal in separate chambers where refuse workers who never surface again haul the spent material. A big colony processes a tree's worth of foliage in a season and exhales warm, sour air from its vents.

The termites do the heavy engineering. Their mounds rise from the floor in clustered, fluted towers of packed earth and chewed wood, some taller than a person, hard as low-grade concrete, and all of them are breathing. Inside, a fungus comb is kept at a near-constant temperature in a climate that swings between steam and sauna; chimneys and galleries open and close as workers plaster and re-plaster, driving a slow circulation that pulls fresh air in at the base and sighs waste heat out the top. Put your hand near a vent at night and you feel it — the exhaust of a million bodies and their crop, fermenting steadily. Elsewhere, carton nests hang from branches, wasps raise paper in grey, layered sheets, bees fan their hive entrances with their wings in coordinated shifts so that a big wild hive in a hollow trunk drones like a small engine idling, felt through the wood under your palm more than heard.

Even the rot is a kind of manufacture. Fallen fruit collects in hollows and ferments into a sharp, alcoholic mash that draws clouds of vinegar flies and drunken butterflies. Sap wells, sours, is harvested. Dung is found, assessed, disassembled, and buried by beetles working in pairs within the hour, rolling their sealed provisions away to underground stores. Nothing is wasted because a hundred specialists are queued for every stage of any material's breakdown: the thing that eats the leaf, the thing that eats its droppings, the fungus that eats the wood the first thing lived in, the mite that rides the beetle that buries the dung. It is a factory floor where the workers are also the product, and the machinery is alive, and it has been running without a break for longer than the valley has had this shape.

The rules that follow from all this are simple and dictate everything. Light is the scarcest currency, so leaves are enormous and nearly black-green to catch it, seedlings can wait a decade in the understory for a gap, and when a canopy tree finally falls and a shaft of real sun hits the floor, it triggers a slow-motion riot — seeds that have lain dormant for years germinating in days, saplings racing, insects and the birds that eat them converging over weeks on a patch of brightness that will close again within a few years. Heat and moisture are superabundant, so nothing needs to hurry and nothing can afford to waste water; animals move slowly, digest slowly, grow slowly, live inside very small territories defended mostly by stillness. Smell carries farther than sight, so the forest at night is mapped in scent — trails, territories, ripeness, danger, readiness — laid over the visible one and far more detailed.

Seasonally, almost nothing turns over dramatically. There is a wetter time when the pocket pools join up briefly and fish trapped in one hollow can reach another, laying eggs that survive the dry weeks buried in mud; and a drier time when the pools contract, the frogs crowd into the last bromeliads, and the litter cracks in the rare places where true light reaches it. But the canopy keeps its leaves, the heat keeps its hold, the dark keeps its level. A sloth born in a particular tree may spend most of its life within sight of it. A termite mound outlasts the tree that shaded its founding. An ant highway stays in use until the food trees along it are exhausted, and then, over months, it shifts.

From the ridge, leaving, you climb back through the layers in reverse — the young palms, the touching crowns, the sudden white blast of the emergent tops and open sky — and the scale of it reasserts itself. It is not a grove or a patch. It is a whole low country, twenty kilometres of self-maintaining dark, breathing out warm air along its creeks at night, its edges fraying into scrub where the canopy breaks and the light finally increases. Inside, at any given moment, most of it is doing almost nothing, very slowly, all at once. The abundance is not in motion. It is in occupancy. Every leaf, pool, burrow, branch, and body is full, and the fullness goes on in the hot dark in near silence, processing the forest into itself, indefinitely.
