It is reached by a road that stops climbing long before it stops going forward.

For the last day of ascent the slope is almost theoretical — a grade cut into bedrock that has already been worn down to its final idea of itself. No peaks remain up here. No valleys with any ambition. The mountains that made this plateau finished eroding so long ago that what is left is not rugged, not even rolling, just gently unresolved: broad domes of dark stone a few meters high and kilometers across, hollows just as shallow, a plain that tilts and sighs but never breaks. Everything has been reduced to the hardest part of itself.

And then it simply continues, flat and warm and black, to a horizon you cannot see.

There is no day here. Whether the sun is too far, too weak, or has been permanently taken out of the sky is a distinction the place no longer makes. Above the thin, dry air the stars are fixed and needle-sharp, numerous enough to show you the shape of the ground only by where they stop. That is the whole of the natural light. To walk outside the lit districts is to walk by occlusion — a slightly deeper darkness where a wall is, the faintly less-black sheen of polished rock underfoot, warmth coming off stone that holds the day's heat because there has never been a day, only a constant geothermal and industrial warmth soaking up from below.

It is warm at this height when it should be cold. The air is thin in the lungs, it makes voices small and effort noticeable, but it comes off the plain at body temperature, smelling always of hot iron, baked dust, ozone, and old machine oil. There is no moisture in it to carry smell far, no wind to move it. Smells pool around their sources and stay for years. You can navigate the smelter quarter blind, and people do, by the sour metallic pocket around the furnaces, the ammonia bite near the coolant plant, the warm yeasty reek of the synthesis vats three streets beyond it.

There is no water. Not hidden, not deep enough. The wells drilled in the first century went through two kilometers of dry basalt and came up with hot gas and powdered salt. No rain has ever been recorded that reached the ground. What the city drinks is made, expensively, in closed chemical loops, or broken down and remade from its own waste, or condensed molecule by molecule from imported feedstock that arrives on the slow freight lifts from below. It tastes of the pipes. It is never wasted on the street. Nothing rusts here in the familiar red way — iron stays black, copper stays brown-black, exposed surfaces grow a fine, dry tarnish and a coat of dust that is never washed away.

Nothing green has ever taken. No plant, no moss, no lichen crust softening the stone. The rock faces are bare mineral: basalt, obsidian stringers, veins of quartz and hematite smoothed until they catch starlight in long, dull lines. In the waste channels behind the factories a mineral film forms — not life exactly, a chemosynthetic crust, pale and brittle, that thickens a fraction of a millimeter a decade around leaking coolant. It is scraped, refined, eaten by the few animals that persist. That is the base of the ecology, if it can be called one.

The city is urban in extent and industrial in purpose, but sparse in the way a constellation is sparse. From any rooftop — low, flat roofs, all of them, weighted with stone — you cannot see another district entire. You see points.

A cluster of habitat blocks, five stories, windowless on their outer faces, doors opening to a shared interior court lit by a single amber lamp shielded down to a pool ten meters wide. A kilometer of empty plain. A power station: squat towers, flywheels turning inside open frames with a slowness you feel in your molars rather than hear, cables on pylons sagging toward the next cluster. Another kilometer. The rail yards, the cracking plant, the long sheds of the machine shops where presses descend once an hour, every hour, with a soft concussion that travels through the bedrock and arrives in distant kitchens as a tremor in the cups. The freight terminus. The vats.

Straight lines connect them. Rail lines, pipe runs on low trestles, cable trenches, and roads that are not so much built as polished — traffic over centuries has burnished lanes into the bedrock between districts, shallow troughs so smooth they shine. Between the lanes the untouched dust lies undisturbed. Because there is no wind and no rain, footprints do not fade. Outside a doorway, outside a factory gate, the ground is a dense palimpsest of every boot that has passed in living memory, pressed into a dust as fine as ash. In older quarters the prints have themselves fossilized into a kind of pavement. Children are taught to read them. A strike, an evacuation, a funeral — you can still follow them out into the dark, line by line, until the line stops being replenished and simply goes on, unchanged, toward the edge of the settled ground.

That unchangingness is the deepest rule of the place. Temperature does not cycle. The factories do not ramp up or cool down; they have run at the same load, producing the same tonnage of cable, plate, solvent, protein paste, and replacement parts, for longer than any archive kept in paper form. The stars do not wheel in any way anyone bothers to mark. The population replaces itself. When a building is finished it is already indistinguishable from a building a hundred years old, made from the same black block, cut to the same module, left unpainted to gather the same dust. Erosion ended here before the city arrived, and the city seems to have adopted its plateau's conviction that the final form has been reached.

Sound obeys the thin air and the flatness. It does not carry well and it does not echo. The industrial plant that should deafen is mostly quiet: massive, slow, well-balanced machines, insulated by distance and by the habit of shielding everything. A low hum near the generating halls. The tick of cooling metal, though nothing ever fully cools. Every hour, the press. At night — at the hours designated night, when interior lamps dim — the districts go so silent you can hear the rare fauna.

There is not much of it. Pale, blind lizards the length of a hand bask motionless against the warm outer walls of the power station, mouths open, scraping the mineral crust with rasping tongues. They may not move for a week. Eyeless beetles work the dust inside pipe trestles. Once or twice a year something larger is reported out on the plain between districts: a long, low animal, hairless, the color of the dust, loping from one pool of factory light to the next without seeming to need either. No one has found where it breeds, what it eats when it is not seen, or a body. Its tracks join the others and continue for kilometers in a perfectly straight line, unhurried, until even those who follow tracks turn back.

At the far edge of the settled plain — half a day by rail, past the last pylon where the cable ends in a capped junction no one maintains anymore — the plateau simply stops. Not with a cliff worn by falls, just a rounded lip of stone, anciently smoothed, and a drop of three thousand meters into a darkness so complete that the lower desert, if it still exists, sends up no sound, no light, no weather. Warm air rises along that face in a constant, silent column. Standing at the lip, your clothes stir for the only time outdoors. Dust that has been still for centuries lifts, briefly, at the very edge, and falls out into the void in a slow glitter under the stars.

Behind you, scattered across twenty kilometers of final, flattened rock, the small shielded lamps of the districts burn at the same brightness they always have, reflected dully in polished roads, in pipe runs, in the quartz veins of the domes. Nothing flickers. Nothing is being built that has not been built before. The presses count the hours into the bedrock, and the footprints accumulate, one identical year over another, in a city with no water and no green thing, kept warm and running in the dark at the top of the world.
