It begins with how little vertical there is.

This is a low, warm basin that has been worn down almost to its final idea of itself — an old, old landscape where the rivers have forgotten how to cut. Whatever hills it once had are gone to low swells and polished domes, to banks no higher than a knee, to slopes you notice more in your calves than with your eyes. From any place you stand, the horizon is a long, almost unbroken line, hazed by heat and water-vapor, and the sky takes up more of the world than the land does.

The ground is flat in the way only ancient erosion can make flat. Not engineered, not abrupt, but settled. Fine silt over clay over older, finer silt. Grey-brown mud, red-brown where iron has stained it, pale almost to white where salt has bloomed through the surface and dried in a delicate crust. In the dry margins the clay has cracked into plates the size of dinner plates and platters, their edges curled up, brittle underfoot, clicking softly. Between them, a scatter of pebbles that have been rounded for so long they seem less like stones than like something the earth has been holding in its mouth — smooth, dense, unadorned, half-buried. There are almost no rocks to speak of, and the few that remain are low outcrops, their tops burnished by wind and flood, weeping dark seams after rain where water stored in their pores seeps out for days.

Water is everywhere, but it is never dramatic. It does not fall in cataracts or run in true rivers. It spreads.

Shallow pans, a hand's breadth to a waist deep, link to one another across miles of floodplain by channels so shallow they are often just a darker tone in the mud, a slight depression where the water prefers to go. After rain — and rain is frequent here, warm and brief and then again — the whole basin takes on a sheen. Sheet water creeps outward centimeter by centimeter over warm clay, filling the cracks, joining pool to pool, turning separated pans into a single, slow-moving mirror that can take weeks to arrive and weeks to leave. The deepest places are old oxbows, now just arcs of open water fringed with mud, their bottoms soft and black. In the morning the surface is glass-still, condensing the night's humidity back out of the air as mist no higher than your shins. By midday it steams faintly. The water smells warm: mud and algae and minerals, green and earthy and a little sulfurous at the edges where it has stood longest.

Because it is so flat, water enforces the rules here. There is no fast route anywhere. A flood does not rush in; it sighs in. Fish arrive impossibly far from any permanent pool, carried by a sheet of water too thin to see moving. When the water recedes, it does so by evaporation as much as by drainage, leaving its cargo behind — snails in the damp clay, eggs in the mud, seeds pressed into the cracks to wait.

Vegetation is sparse for a place so wet, and that is part of the character of the basin. The soil is too salty at the surface, too alternately drowned and baked, too fine and airless when wet, for much to root deeply. What grows, grows apart.

Tufts of sedge and salt-tolerant grass make low, olive-green islands on slightly raised ground, each tuft isolated, with bare mud between. Reeds stand in thin colonies along the deeper channels, not thickets — you can see through them to the water beyond. Here and there, at distances that can be measured in a long walk, a single flat-topped tree throws a disc of shade: scant branches, small leaves held against the sun, roots spread wide in the top layer of silt. From a distance the trees look like punctuation. Close up, their bark is deeply fissured, silvered, hosting lichen in the damp season and cicadas in the dry hours. Under the water itself, life is more generous but less visible: films of algae color the shallows emerald and rust, and submerged grasses wave slowly in water that barely flows, turning the light green-gold.

And yet, for a landscape so empty of plants, it is full of animals, and this is where it becomes loud.

The sound reaches you before the life does. At dawn and again from late afternoon through the night, frogs set up a chorus that carries across the flat water without anything to stop it — small metallic peeps, deep jug-like croaks, a rattling chatter from the reed edges, layer upon layer until the pans seem to be sounding themselves. Cicadas take the hot middle hours, a continuous, tearing sizzle from each lone tree in turn, so that walking from one tree to the next you pass from one dome of sound into silence and then into another. Waterbirds are everywhere the water is: white egrets stepping with exaggerated care in the shallows, herons frozen and then suddenly striking, ibis probing the mud in loose, scattered groups, ducks exploding up in whistling flocks when disturbed, their wings audible long after they are specks. In the breeding season, colonies of weavers and waders occupy the few reedbeds dense enough to hold them, and those places become clamorous, restless neighborhoods.

Smaller life crowds the margins. Dragonflies patrol in amber and electric blue. Water beetles dimple the surface. Turtles drag slow furrows in the mud and bask on any raised clod, dropping with a plop at a shadow. Softshell crabs and freshwater mussels work the bottom. In deeper pans, catfish and lungfish persist through the contraction of the dry weeks by burrowing into the still-damp clay, leaving small volcanoes of mud. Above it all, insects rise in the warm air in such numbers that swallows and bee-eaters spend the day in continuous, swooping attendance, and at dusk bats replace them, stitching the sky.

The fauna is abundant not in herds packed close — the basin remains sparse in its arrangement of everything — but in reach. There is always something moving at the edge of sight: a monitor lizard hauling itself from one pool to another, a snake swimming with just its head above water, footprints overwriting footprints in the drying margins. The mud keeps a perfect record: three-toed bird tracks, webbed tracks, the neat ovals of small antelope that come down to drink in the early morning, the wide splay of hippos where the deepest channels lie far to the east. Because the land is so open, you see animals at a distance and hear them even farther. A splash carries a mile. A crane's bugling call at first light arrives disembodied, too large for the thin bird that eventually resolves out of the mist.

Light here is ordinary daylight, but doubled. The sun is bright and warm on your shoulders by mid-morning, glancing hard off wet clay and open water, so that even looking down can be dazzling. Clouds build often by afternoon — tall, white, slow-moving — and their shadows move across the pans in visible patches of coolness, the water under shadow turning suddenly dark and pewter. Rain comes under a sky that bruises purple-grey at its edges, spattering first into dust that smells sweet and mineral, then stippling the pools with rings. For a little while everything drips. Rocks weep, leaves weep, the cracked clay softens and seals. Then the sun returns and the basin fills with that post-rain exhalation, the whole flat world steaming gently, smelling of warm mud and crushed green stems.

Seasonally, the change is patient to the point of seeming static if you stay only a few days. You must learn its tempo. Water levels rise a finger's width in a day. A channel that was damp earth becomes ankle-deep over a week. Grasses that were grey stubble green up from the base, not in a flush, but in a slow intensification. As the pans shrink late in the season, they concentrate: fish crowd into the last dark water, birds crowd after them, the mud rims widen and whiten with salt, frogs fall silent one species at a time. Nothing quite dries out completely. Even at its lowest, the ground gives under your foot within a few steps, damp below the crust, and if you dig with your heel you hit wet clay the color of chocolate. The basin holds water near the surface the way a held breath is held.

There are no buildings here, no fences, no cut roads — only crossing animal paths that braid and peter out in the mud, old nesting mounds built by birds and termites, hollowed trunks where something has sheltered. Travel means choosing the slightly higher ground, island-hopping between tree and sedge tuft and salt pan, reading the sheen ahead to know whether it is ankle water or just wet clay reflecting the sky. Distance deceives because everything is the same height. A reedbed that looks near is an hour's slog. A tree that seems within reach keeps its size for a long time, then is suddenly above you.

By evening the light goes level and amber, laying long reflections across every pool at once, so the basin looks for a while like one continuous sheet of beaten copper interrupted by dark tufts and the silhouettes of birds. The frogs start again, louder in the cooling air. The mist begins to gather in the lowest places, filling the old channels first, so that from a slight rise you can see the ghost map of the waterways drawn in white. Above, the sky keeps its color long after the ground has gone dim.

It is not a lush place, and it is not a barren one. It is a place that has been reduced by immense time to its essentials — water, mud, light, and life that knows how to use thin water spread over great distances — and it goes on in that reduced state, humming and splashing and steaming, mile after mile, without needing to become anything else.
