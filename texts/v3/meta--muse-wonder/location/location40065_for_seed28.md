First you hear it, long before you see anything.

The land in every direction is a white salt pan, flat to the horizon, cracked into plates that curl at the edges. No river, no well, no shade. The sun stands almost straight overhead and turns the salt into a second sun underfoot, so bright you have to walk with your chin down and your eyes narrowed behind a veil. The air tastes of dust and hot mineral.

And under that brightness, a sound. Low at first, miles away, like wind blown across the mouth of a bottle. Then louder. Then loud enough to feel in your molars.

The city is hidden in the pan. There is no wall, no tower, no smoke to mark it. You walk toward the noise across empty white ground and suddenly the ground falls away.

It is a bowl. Sixty paces across, perhaps twelve deep, sunk below the level of the salt. Not a crater exactly — too regular, too long-settled. The sides have been cut, shored, terraced, and inhabited for so many generations that geology and architecture have become the same thing. Stone steps worn into shallow dishes by feet. Mud-brick retaining walls patched with newer mud, straw, crushed gypsum, and salt mortar. Rope handrails. Reed awnings bleached almost white.

Around the inside of the bowl, in five uneven rings, is a town.

There are districts here, unmistakably. An upper ring of workshops near the rim, where the wind arrives first. A ring of granaries with thick, windowless walls. A ring of houses with painted doors — ochre, black resin, powdered blue stone. Below that, kitchens, dye-pits, stores for oil and salt meat and bundled fiber. Staircases switch back between levels. Narrow bridges of lashed poles cross from one side of a gully to the other. Laundry lines, prayer cords, baskets on pulleys, market ledges just wide enough for two people to pass with their shoulders turned.

It is urban in the way a beehive is urban: layered, subdivided, serviced, argued over. There are boundary stones between family terraces, communal ovens, a shrine hollowed into the east wall, a council ledge with seats worn smooth in a semicircle.

And it is mostly empty.

Half the doors are sealed with mud. Half the ledges have no one on them. Between one inhabited house and the next there may be three abandoned ones, their roof mats intact, their jars still stacked inside, kept swept by somebody out of obligation. The center of the bowl is emptier still: a broad floor of packed white earth where nothing at all is built. No well, no tree, no market stall. Just a clean, glaring vacancy, with a darker circle at the very middle, about the width of a threshing floor, where the ground is polished and strewn with cracked husks and fine dust.

That emptiness is not waste. It is the city's engine.

The rim of the bowl has been carved. All the way around, set into the lip at intervals, are standing stones and thick clay blocks pierced with holes of different sizes — some round, some slit, some flared like small trumpets. Primitive work, hand-drilled and fire-hardened, retuned year after year with stone rasps and wet clay. When the hot wind skims the pan and crosses the rim, it plays them.

By mid-morning the bowl begins to sing. By noon it is deafening.

The sound does not stay at the rim. The shape of the place catches it, folds it down the terraced walls, and concentrates it toward the center. Shutters rattle in their frames. Cords hum. The thousand small things the inhabitants hang in their doorways — bone discs, shell fragments, hollow reeds, shards of pottery, dried seed pods on twine — all start to clatter and tick and buzz at once. Conversations stop, not by custom but by simple impossibility. People move to sign language without thinking about it: a whole fluent grammar of hands, shoulders, tapped elbows, stamps on resonant boards laid across thresholds.

Earplugs are ordinary clothing here. Children get their first pair, molded from warm beeswax and plant resin, before they get shoes. Houses have double walls with sand poured between them. Sleeping alcoves are dug deepest into the hillside and hung with layered mats. The council does not meet by voice during the day; decisions are made at dawn, quickly, in a low murmuring rush before the wind rises.

At the focal point in the middle, the sound is a physical pressure. Stand there too long at noon — if the brightness reflected off the salt walls doesn't drive you out first — and your vision begins to shiver at the edges. Your chest aches. Small stones dance on the packed earth.

So the inhabitants put work there instead of people.

Grain is spread in shallow baskets at the center in the morning and retrieved in the evening with the husks shaken loose. Nuts crack. Dried clay is broken down to powder without pounding. Bundles of flax are loosened. Jars with fouled interiors are set out empty and left until the vibration walks the residue off the walls in flakes. Certain succulent leaves, laid in the circle at exactly the right hour, split along their seams and give up long clean fibers. Sound is their millstone, their hammer, their second set of hands.

Then there is the growth. Against all reason in that dry pan, the bowl is lush.

Vines spill from every terrace. They pour over retaining walls, mat the roofs, hang in curtains across the fronts of houses, and thread along the handrails until the stone itself seems to be leafing. There are broad squash-like leaves, dusty green; feathery, resinous shrubs with tiny folded foliage; climbers with waxy blue-green pads; hanging strands that end in pale, trumpet-shaped flowers. Under the awnings are gardens in baskets, in cracked jars, in troughs made from hollowed logs. Everything is thick-leaved, slow, and apparently immortal.

Almost none of it is watered.

Rain may not fall here for years at a time. What water the people drink comes in on mule-back twice a year in stitched skins, traded for salt, and is treated like coin — stored in sealed jars below the lowest houses, measured out by the cup, guarded by law. No one pours it on a plant.

The plants live otherwise. Their leaves are not thin and thirsty but sealed: filled with bitter gel, with oil, with a sour, translucent pulp that smells of hot stone when cut. Their roots do not go looking for groundwater. They go sideways, into the shaded backs of walls, into the cool seam where terrace meets bedrock, and they spread fine hairs that gather what little damp the night air surrenders before dawn. A leaf may take a season to unfold and last a decade. A vine that shades a house may be older than the house, older than the family, its main stem thick as a wrist and trained from grandmother to granddaughter along the same wire.

Crucially, they are pollinated by the noise. There are almost no bees here — fauna in the bowl amounts to a few wall lizards, pallid and quick, a population of black salt-beetles in the lower stores, and, most evenings, a single hawk describing one slow circle high above the rim before going elsewhere. Instead, when the bowl reaches full voice at noon, the flowers open to vibration. Anthers shiver. Pollen comes loose in visible puffs from the hanging trumpets and hangs, golden, in the bright air, shaken from plant to plant by the same pressure that empties the streets. Fruit sets in the roar. The lushness and the deafening are not two facts about the place. They are the same fact.

By late afternoon the wind over the pan changes angle. One by one the rim-flutes drop out of tune. The clattering in the doorways thins. Somewhere a shutter is unbarred, and the small wooden knock carries the length of a terrace like a bell.

That is when the town fills.

Doors open. People emerge blinking, pulling wax from their ears, stretching their jaws. The market ledge that stood vacant all day populates in minutes — dried lizard meat, salt cakes, resin, oil pressed from seeds, woven palm, dyed cord, pots, tools of flaked stone and hammered copper, all laid out on mats. The ovens are lit. Smoke, trapped by the bowl, makes a brief warm haze that smells of charcoal and cooking fat and hot clay, and for an hour the place feels close, almost humid, though the moisture is only breath and food steam.

Children who have been kept in the deep alcoves come out to play elaborate silent games in the streets that slowly, as the evening cools, acquire voices. First whispers. Then talk. Then, at full dusk, from the council ledge, singing — human singing this time, low and extraordinarily careful about pitch. People here have perfect relative pitch the way other people have balance. They can tell from inside a shuttered house, at noon, whether one of the rim stones has cracked, or whether a terrace wall has shifted enough to alter the bowl's note. A change in the city's chord is read like weather, like news, like a symptom in a body.

At night the plants open their other way. The waxy leaves unclench. The trumpet flowers turn upward, pale as small moons, and release a scent so sweet and green it seems impossible in that mineral place. Moths — perhaps five, perhaps six in the whole bowl, treasured and never touched — blunder softly between them in the dark. The lizards go still. The salt pan above goes cold and ticks as it contracts.

The inhabitants sit out on their terraces then, in the one quiet interval, and eat, and mend, and argue, and tell stories at normal volume, and listen to the bowl not sounding. The middle of their city lies below them, empty and white in the starlight — their plaza, their mill, their altar, the place no one may build on or cross at noon — holding its silence the way it holds its noise: completely, as if that were what it was made for.
