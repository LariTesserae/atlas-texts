It is the brightness you notice first, and then, after a minute standing in it, the quiet.

This is a high basin — a broad, shallow bowl lifted far above whatever country lies below its rim, ten or fifteen kilometers from one weathered edge to the other. The air is thin enough that your breath feels unfinished, and yet it is not cold. It is warm, insistently warm. The sun comes down almost vertically at midday, white and clean, with an edge that makes every shadow look cut out of black paper and laid on the ground. Rock, dust, pale grass, animal hide — everything gives the light back. On the rim, where the ground falls away in long cliffs, the glare turns to haze and you cannot tell where the basin ends and the sky below it begins.

The floor of the basin is old. Nothing here has sharp, new geology. The hills inside it are rounded to their bones, their shoulders slumped, their strata exposed in bands of ochre tuff, cream limestone, red-brown basalt, all softened by time. Wind and rare rain have carved the stone into flutes and shallow caves and freestanding towers no taller than houses, their bases undercut, their tops capped with harder rock. Between them the ground is mostly open: packed earth, gravel plains, sheets of cracked clay the color of bone. It looks dry from a distance, and mostly it is, but it is a inhabited dryness, worked over by water in small, recurring places.

There is no river. There are pockets.

In the lowest part of the basin lies a salt flat that holds a skin of water after rain — sometimes for a day, sometimes for an hour before the heat takes it. Rock hollows hold tea-dark pools. A few springs seep from under ledges, never quite amounting to a stream, spreading instead into damp fans of green-black moss and then vanishing into gravel. And every morning, regardless of rain, there is condensation. It beads on cold stone, on metal, on the broad leaves of the plants that know how to wait for it. For the first hour after dawn the basin glitters with temporary water, and everything that lives here is awake and drinking.

The vegetation is sparse but deliberate. It does not carpet; it punctuates. Tussock grasses in stiff, straw-colored clumps, spaced as evenly as if planted, though they weren't. Cushion plants hugging the ground in tight green domes, prickled, flowering for a week a year in absurd yellow. Solitary, wind-trained trees near the springs — short, thick-trunked things with silver leaves that turn their pale undersides up in the heat. Along certain straight lines that cut improbably across otherwise empty ground, the grass grows a fraction taller and darker, following something buried: a conduit, leaking warmth and trace moisture, a seam in the world that roots have found.

Scattered across all this, far apart, are the structures.

At first you take them for ruins, or for very minimal shelters. A low white hut with no windows and a door standing open, half-buried in drifted dust. A ring of six dark pillars on a rise, leaning slightly inward. A single slab, taller than a person, balanced on a point. Then you get closer, and the wrongness accumulates quietly.

The slab is not balanced. It hovers, perhaps a hand's breadth above its pedestal stone, and it is turning, very slowly, with no mechanism and no sound. When you pass your hand beneath it there is no rush of air, no heat, only a faint pressure, like the moment before an ear pops. The pillars on the rise hum below the threshold of hearing — you feel them in your teeth. On clear days you can see, if you look almost directly at the sun through squinted eyes, a faint hexagonal tessellation high above the whole basin, a lattice so fine and so high it only reveals itself as a brief interference in the brightness, a place where the light bends a degree out of true. It is why the light here is fierce but not killing. The ultraviolet that should scour a place this high and exposed has been edited out. The warmth remains.

Whatever built this system built it to tend the basin, and it is still tending it, on a timescale that treats centuries as weather.

Each pylon — and there are perhaps thirty of them, none alike, spread so thin you can walk an hour between two — draws moisture from the thin air at night by a process that looks, from outside, like simple condensation intensified past what physics should allow. Frost feathers grow outward across their surfaces in elaborate, branching crystals even on warm nights, melting at dawn into runnels that are channeled, filtered, and released at the base in a steady drip. The drip falls into a stone trough worn smooth not by the water but by tongues. Some pylons regulate the lattice above. Some appear to be doing geology: exuding, over months, a pale mineral paste that hardens into repair where their own casing has cracked, growing back like scar tissue, like shell. One, near the eastern rim, projects nothing visible at all, but rain — on the rare afternoons when a single convective cell builds over the basin's center — curves as it falls near it, droplets swerving in clean arcs around an invisible volume the size of a house.

There is no sound from any of it. In fact, near the pylons, sound itself becomes unreliable. A stone dropped clatters more quietly than it should. Your footsteps arrive muffled, as if the ground were softer than it is. Birdsong — and there are birds — cuts off when the birds cross those zones, their throats working, nothing coming out, then resumes on the other side. The whole basin is like that, only less so. Even far from any structure, the ambient noise floor is unnervingly low. Wind passes over the rim but rarely seems to reach the floor; the grass stirs at knee height in air that feels still against your face. What you hear, mostly, is close and bodily: breath, cloth, the tick of sun-heated rock contracting toward evening, the dry click of beetle wings.

And yet the place is full of animals. Abundantly, almost disproportionately full, given how little green there is.

Lizards own the stone — dozens of species, thumb-length to forearm-length, basking in ranks on warm slabs until your shadow crosses them and they vanish without a rustle into cracks. Glass beetles, translucent, their organs visible as amber knots, work the salt flat in slow lines. Ground birds run between tussocks in family groups, flightless, speckled, utterly silent on their feet, communicating in quick flashes of white tail feathers. High above, swifts and a kind of broad-winged, pale raptor ride the thermals off the hot floor in great soundless spirals, never flapping when you watch them, their shadows sweeping the basin like slow clock hands. In the spring-fed damp patches there are frogs the size of coins that do not croak; at dusk they inflate and drum their resonant chambers against the wet rock itself, so the stone carries a faint vibration you feel through your soles. Herd animals — small, dun, goat-antelope things with black, lyre-curved horns — drift in loose bands of twenty or thirty across the flats, grazing the sparse grass, drinking at the pylon troughs in strict rotation with everything else. They are not tame. They simply treat the structures, and you, as features of the terrain.

Some of the fauna is harder to classify. There are dragonflies over the morning pools with too-regular wingbeats and a faint, geometric glint at the joints, pollinating the cushion flowers with methodical, looping precision, never straying, never feeding. There is the animal like a hovering manta, no bigger than a handkerchief, that drifts through the shade of the rock towers at noon, rippling, translucent — whether it is a jellyfish adapted to thin air or a sensor platform adapted to look like an animal, the distinction seems not to matter here, to it or to anything else. It has presumably been drifting for a long time. Lichen grows, in one known case, on the underside of a hovering component, and a swift has nested in the warm vent of another.

The daily rhythm is the landscape's real architecture. Dawn: frost, dew, glitter, animals drinking, the lattice overhead briefly visible as the sun strikes it at an angle, a net of light thrown over everything. Morning: the water retreats to trough and pool and the undersides of leaves; the basin dries and brightens. Noon: fierce, still, shadowless heat, most life tucked into stone shade, only the raptors and the drifting things abroad. Afternoon: sometimes the single rain cell, building directly overhead out of a previously empty sky, raining hard and locally for twenty minutes on the salt flat while the rim stays in full sun — the frogs emerge, the flat becomes a mirror, brine shrimp wake in the temporary shallows in their millions and are immediately set upon by wading birds that arrive, seemingly from nowhere, on silent wings. Evening: the rock ticks as it cools, the pillars begin their invisible work, frost feathers start to nucleate in the last light, the herd animals move to higher ground for the night. The air smells of hot stone giving up its heat, of ozone near the pylons, of a faint resiny sweetness from the cushion plants closing.

No one lives here permanently, as far as you can tell. There are the white huts, and they contain, variously, a bunk grown over with grass, a sealed crate of something that was once food, a wall of careful tally marks that stop mid-count. Someone — shepherds, surveyors, keepers of a system that no longer needs keeping — comes here, or came here, seasonally. There is a track that connects the structures in long, straight, rarely-used segments, and very occasionally, at a great distance, a single figure crosses it and does not hurry. The basin does not feel abandoned. It feels maintained past the point where maintenance requires anyone.

That is the rule of the place, in the end: it is a wilderness with a gardener that never shows itself whole. Old stone, edited sunlight, water made from air, silence made from something like intention. Everything wild here has arranged itself around the technology without becoming dependent in any simple way — the troughs would vanish and the springs would remain; the light would turn lethal and the lizards, presumably, would go deeper into the cracks. For now the arrangement holds, settled and weathered and active, a warm bright emptiness that turns out, the longer you stay, to be densely, quietly in motion.
