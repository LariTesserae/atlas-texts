At first it looks empty. That is the plateau's oldest trick.

You come up to it from below — days of broken slopes, gullies cut and re-cut, loose scree that still remembers being a mountain — and then the rim gives out all at once. The land flattens. Not slopes, not rolls. It simply stops rising.

Ahead of you is a surface so thoroughly worn down it has forgotten the idea of height. Whatever ranges stood here have been ground, weathered, baked, washed, and ground again until there is nothing left to take away. The last hills are swells a hand's breadth high that take a kilometer to rise and another to fall. Old riverbeds cross it as faint ribbons of paler gravel, wide and shallow as a stain, with no banks. The bedrock shows through everywhere, polished by wind-borne grit into low, turtle-backed pavements of red-brown stone, black stone, stone varnished the color of old iron. Between the pavements is lag gravel — pebbles too heavy to move, packed tight, clicking faintly underfoot — and dust trapped only where something holds it down.

It goes on past seeing. In the clear, high air you can follow that flatness for thirty, forty kilometers to a rim on the far side that is no higher than this one, a thin line where the world drops again. In the morning clouds sit below the edge, white and slow in the valleys, and the plateau is an island above them. By mid-morning they are gone. The light is ordinary daylight, nothing gilded or dim about it, but it arrives unfiltered. Colors are exactly themselves: rust, ash-white, lichen black, the dull olive of dry grass.

It is warm up here, improbably warm for the height. The dark stone takes the sun and holds it. Heat pools in the shallow pans and does not drain away. The air is thin in the lungs — you breathe oftener, you walk slower without deciding to — but on the skin it is summer.

Water does not run. It visits.

There are no streams. There are pans: broad, fingertip-deep depressions in the stone, ringed with a crust of white salt and darker mud cracked into plates. Most days they are dry. At night the stone cools just enough, the clear sky pulls the day's breath back down, and condensation beads on every cold surface. By dawn the pans hold a skin of water no deeper than a tongue, the gravel is dark with damp, and every hollow drips once, twice. An hour after sunrise it is gone, except in the deepest cracks where it lingers, green and secret, until noon. When rain does come, weeks apart, it comes straight down in a warm hush, fills the pans ankle-deep for a day, and leaves just as quietly.

Vegetation has made its peace with scarcity. Tufts of wiry, gray-green grass live only in cracks, ankle-high, gone to seed-heads that rattle in no wind. Cushion plants hug the stone in the lee of boulders, dense domes the size of a plate, flowering for a morning with pinhead yellow blooms after rain and then closing again for a month. A thorn shrub here and there, waist-high at most, leafless nine days out of ten. Everything else that looks like plant-life — the black lace on the rocks, the orange dusting in the pans, the slippery green film that appears with the dew — is crust and algae, living on damp and light alone.

And then, if you stand still long enough, the ground begins to be inhabited.

The pebble by your boot is not a pebble. It lifts a fraction, on six stub legs, and resettles. The flat stone beyond it does the same. And the next. Look again at that close-packed gravel and it resolves, with a slow prickling at the back of the neck, into backs.

They cover the plateau. Not herds, not swarms in patches — a pavement of animals, shell to shell, from pan edge to pavement edge, in densities that leave almost no bare ground in the good grazing. They are flat, disc-bodied things, most the size of a dinner plate, some as wide as a cartwheel, the oldest as broad as a table and piled with smaller ones riding on top. Their shells are stone-colored, stone-textured, ringed like tree-trunk growth, scarred and lichen-spotted. Their heads, when they bother to extend them, are blunt and eyeless-seeming, mouths underneath rasping at the dew-film and mineral crust on the rock. They eat, essentially, the surface of the world — algae, salt, lichen, the iron varnish itself ground grain by grain — and they have been eating it since the mountains finished dying. Perhaps they are why it finished.

They are overwhelming not by size but by number, and layered. Big ones carry small ones. Small ones carry mites the color of rust that move in herding patterns across the shells. Hair-thin worms live in the damp underside-shadow of every shell and retreat into the cracks at sunrise. After a rain the pans come abruptly alive with translucent, swimming things that hatch, mate, and lay in a single afternoon, and the flat grazers crowd the pan edges shoulder to shoulder, drinking through their feet. Tiny, silent, long-legged birds — the only birds, no song in them at all — walk from back to back as if across stepping stones, picking at the mites, never once needing to touch the ground. In the cracks beneath the grazers are burrowers you never see whole, only their castings, fine as flour, constantly renewed. Step anywhere and you are stepping into someone's roof, road, pasture, and nursery at once.

And it is almost silent. That is the uncanny part of so much life. No calls, no cries, no insect chorus. A faint ticking begins an hour after dawn as thousands of shells expand in the warmth, tick, tick, from all directions at once, too soft to locate. A dry rasping underneath, like sandpaper on stone, when they graze in earnest at the dew hour. The occasional drip. Your own breathing, too loud. Even the wind makes less noise here than it should; it arrives across miles of flatness and is muffled against a million low, warm bodies.

Nothing hurries because nothing can afford to. The grazers move inches in a morning, following the retreating damp from crack to crack, and a path you force through them — nudging shells aside with your foot, feeling them brace, surprisingly heavy, warm from the sun — closes behind you within the hour as if you had never passed. Their young are born live under the mother's shell and stay there, stacked, for a season. Predation is a matter of patience: the long-legged birds, the burrowers waiting below, age itself. A dead grazer is an event that lasts a year. First the birds, then the worms from beneath, then the small grazers rasping the shell clean, then other grazers stacking the empty, bleached dome onto the structures.

Because there are structures, scattered and low and easy to mistake for geology. Rings of flat stones, waist-wide, half-buried — windbreaks or hut-footings from people who were here so long ago that only the placement remains, no wood, no bone, no ash. Lines of cairns, three stones high, wandering between pans in a logic that may have marked water-rights or grazing or graves; now each cairn is tenanted, shells wedged in its shelter, a garden of cushion-plant in its lee. And newer, stranger building, ongoing: arcs of empty shells, carefully butted rim to rim, sometimes two layers high, always open away from the prevailing wind. The grazers make them, mouthful by mouthful over years, pushing their dead into windbreaks for the stacked young. Inside an arc the dust stays, a tuft of grass can risk itself, condensation collects a little longer. The arcs dot the plateau by the hundreds, no two close, each one a small refusal of flatness.

The rules here are slow rules. Do not expect a trail to persist. Do not expect water to be where it was yesterday, only where it always briefly is. Do not expect the ground to be ground; test it, and expect it to withdraw its foot. Growth, erosion, birth, building — all of it proceeds at the pace of shells ticking in the sun. Even decay is patient. A footprint in pan-mud may last, filling and drying and filling again, longer than the animal that made it.

By late afternoon the light slants, ordinary and golden at the edges, and the whole plateau changes key without changing note. The grazers settle. Legs withdraw. Shells seal to the stone to trap the day's moisture beneath them, and the land looks, again, exactly like an empty expanse of pavement and gravel extending to the rim of the world. The birds fold themselves into the cairns. Somewhere under you, in the dark, the worms are working the undersides, the algae is preparing for dew, the pans are waiting to briefly become lakes.

Stand there long enough at dusk, breathing the warm, thin, faintly mineral air — stone-dust, salt, the green smell of warming algae, animal warmth like heated clay — and the emptiness fails completely. You understand you have not been alone on a barren tableland. You have been standing, the whole time, in the middle of a crowd that never raised its voice.
