It is a basin that has finished becoming flat.

Not flat the way a field is flat — flat the way a thing is flat after a billion years of being ground against itself. Whatever mountains were here have been weathered down to their ankles. A few low stubs remain at the horizons, smooth, black, knee-high hills that are more like swells in stone, and they weep constantly. Brine squeezes out of their pores in slow beads, runs down in rust-colored threads, and spreads. There is no high ground. There is no dry ground, either.

The whole regional plain, hundreds of kilometers of it, is covered in a hand's depth of water that will not freeze though the cold should stop a heart. It is not clean water. It is brine and mineral oil and antifreeze-sweet groundwater, black in the shallows, mirror-bright where it is still, iridescent where it moves. Under it: cracked ice plates, mud the color of coal ash, sheets of salt, and a bedrock so old and worn that it has the polish of a factory floor. In places the ice heaves up in slabs that look exactly like riveted iron plate, complete with seams. In places that resemblance stops being a resemblance. You cannot tell, and after a while you stop needing to.

The light is a dim iron twilight that never quite decides on a time of day. The sky is low cloud, condensing without pause, spitting rime. A pale sun shows sometimes as a bright rivet-head behind the cloud, and its reflection shows more often in the water, so there are usually two or three suns underfoot, smeared and drifting against the current. Shadows fall upward from the water as often as they fall down.

And the plain is full. Packed. There is no empty water.

It is full of animals, and the animals are industrial.

They are born, not built — there are no buildings here, no walls, no rails, no chimneys standing apart from flesh — but they are made of the processes buildings once contained. Great boiler-grazers move shoulder to shoulder in herds that extend past sight, six- and eight-legged, their hides overlapping plates of blued steel and horn and black chitin, all of it rimed white at the edges. Each has a long chimney-neck. Each neck ends not in a head so much as a valve assembly with a wet eye at its center, and from the chimneys they exhale exactly what they inhale: steam that condenses the instant it touches the air and falls back on their backs as frost. They keep each other liquid. The water boils faintly where their feet go down and freezes in lace between them. Alone, one would lock solid in an hour. In a herd, they make a climate.

Between their legs the shallows are crusted with pump-mollusks, millions of them, turbine barnacles, living intakes. They open iron shells, spin gill-fans, suck mud, spit out clean black pellets of concentrated iron and sulfur. The pellets are food for everything else. Small valve-crabs scavenge the pellets, their claws true wrenches, clicking. Gear-mites swarm in the grazers' joints and eat rust, which is also food, which is also a kind of slow blood. Overhead, low and heavy in the wet air, fly bellows-birds with fan-blade wings that do not flap so much as rotate, segment by segment, creaking around a full circle. Their cries are train whistles, small and mournful. When one dies it does not fall. Its rotation simply slows, and slows, and the others fly through it for days until it has been gently dismantled by the wind of their passing.

There is no vegetation. Nothing green, nothing soft that grows toward light. The bottom of the food chain is the mud itself — methane sighing up from ancient stone, iron filings, hot sulfur in the colder-than-freezing water where geothermal seams crack the basin floor. Everything eats mineral, or eats something that ate mineral, or eats rust off something that ate something. The grazers extrude warm slag as dung, and the slag is immediately colonized. Predation here is disassembly. A long crane-jawed hunter picks its way on stilt legs above the herd, lowers its articulated neck, and unscrews a living boar-sized feeder from its own legs, piece by screaming piece, while the feeder keeps trying to graze. The parts, dropped, crawl toward each other when the hunter leaves. Sometimes they reassemble into the same animal. Sometimes into a smaller one, with an extra eye. Sometimes into an egg.

Eggs are laid in open assembly pits — shallow craters in the mud that other animals have dug and abandoned, or that have dug themselves. Scrap gathers there. A loose piston finds a casing. Teeth find a jaw. A length of steaming intestine coils itself around a spine of rebar and begins, experimentally, to pump. Mothers, if the word applies, bring offerings: bolts they have grown in their own cheeks, glass lenses secreted like pearls, whole still-beating pumps coughed up from second stomachs. Assembly can take an hour or a decade. The pit animals are born old, already dented, already patinated with rust their grandparents carried. Their first breath is exhaust.

It is loud in a way that fills the body before the ears. A constant grinding, clanking, hissing chorus — pistons, fans, horns, shells snapping shut, ice cracking under tons of moving weight, steam tearing out of blowholes. Animals signal by horn-blast and by hammering on their own ribs, and the sound does not travel normally. In the packed air, saturated with steam and iron dust and sound itself, you can often hear a call several seconds before the animal opens its mouth. Echoes arrive pregnant. They hit the water and stand up for a moment as faint, upright shapes — a second herd, made of noise, grazing in the same place as the first — and then collapse. Silence, where it occurs between the bodies, is not safe. It behaves like a vacuum. The cold rushes in there. Things freeze mid-step in silent pockets and are carried along anyway by the press of the herd, perfect, frost-white, eyes open, still steaming years later from a chimney that should be cold.

Other rules assert themselves if you stay.

Distance is partly a function of volume. A quiet place a hundred meters away can take a day to reach. A bellowing herd on the horizon can be suddenly against your skin if it calls in unison, its sound bridging them to you.

Water remembers the last shape that moved through it and holds that shape for a while after. The plain is covered in temporary, watery duplicates — a grazer wading out of its own slightly delayed outline, birds flying in formation with their own clear ghosts.

Gravity is weaker on the backs of the animals than on the ground, for no reason that holds. Step from ice to a boiler-grazer's back and your weight lessens; loose screws drift up off its hide and orbit slowly around its chimney before settling in its frost like moons deciding to land. Step down again and you are heavy enough to crack ice.

Travel, if you attempted it, would not be walking. The water between bodies is full of mouths, intakes, filter fans. The only safe path is across the backs themselves, a warm, shifting archipelago of iron and horn that sways, rises, and lowers with breath, and that occasionally objects and shakes you into a joint where the mites will investigate you with great professional interest. The herd flows in slow regional circuits — to the chemical meadows where sulfur crusts thickest, to the nesting flats where the ground is hot enough that eggs steam, to the.stub-hills to lick brine directly from weeping rock. You go where they go. Their migration is weather.

At the far edges, where the Basin seems to thin, it doesn't end. You see ahead the same stub-hill you passed three days ago, weeping the same bead of brine, not yet fallen. You see the same white-frozen grazer carried in the herd, steam still leaving it, only now it is ahead of the herd and looking at you. The water underfoot shows a sun that hasn't risen through cloud yet.

The cold keeps everything. The water keeps everything. The animals keep each other running, eating rust, making rust, pumping the black shallows through themselves and out again. It is an ancient place at its final shape, packed to saturation with loud, living machinery, grinding gently and forever against itself in the dim — and somehow, in the steam above the pits, still assembling new parts for a world that has not yet decided whether it has ended.
