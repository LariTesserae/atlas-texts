It is a city at the bottom of a hot, dark basin, and the first thing you learn about it is that it was never meant to be seen.

There is no dawn here. Not in the ordinary sense. Above the low marshland that surrounds it, above its canals and roofed streets and wide, empty plazas, the sky stays the color of wet slate just after the last light has gone. Sometimes it lightens to a bruised brown, the way water does when mud is stirred up from the bottom, but it never clears. The heat arrives without the sun. It comes up from the stones, out of the water, down in a close, condensing pressure from the low clouds, until there is no direction it is not coming from.

You approach it across a causeway.

The land outside is flat and drowned, a marsh-edge world of black mudflats, shallow pools, and salt-crusted pans stretching farther than the darkness lets you judge. The water is everywhere but never deep at first — sheet water over clay, warm as skin, filmed with mineral rainbows that break underfoot and slowly reassemble behind you. Vegetation is spare and unwilling. A stand of jointed reeds here, too far from the next to be called a bed. A single salt-bush flattened by heat. Dark lichen in the seams of the causeway stones, holding on in patches the size of a hand. Nothing green is lush. Everything that grows does so thinly, as if rationed.

Then the stones get larger. The causeway becomes a street. The street becomes a district.

The city is built — unmistakably built — and built at a civic scale. There are terraced houses of basalt and fired mudbrick, three and four stories high, with shuttered upper rooms and deep, arched ground floors open to the street. There are market arcades, bathhouses with domed roofs, granaries on raised plinths, cistern towers, public basins, canal walls, bridges, stairways descending to water gates, long colonnades whose columns have been rounded soft by centuries of wet air. Neighborhoods repeat with variations, separated by canals and by broad, vacant intervals of paved mud and steaming pool — islands of building in a sparse urban archipelago. You can walk for ten minutes between one inhabited-looking block and the next, crossing nothing but heat, shallow water, and your own muffled footsteps.

But there is no technology in it. No wire, no glass, no lamp, no wheel-rut that is fresh, no machine of any kind. The infrastructure is all passive and ancient: gravity channels, clay pipes sweating in the walls, stone sluices worn into perfect, lip-smooth curves, cisterns that collect the roof-water and sigh it out again through overflow mouths. Everything is lime plaster gone gray and pitted, black stone beaded with condensation, roof tiles filmed in salt, timber lintels softened to a fibrous pulp but somehow still bearing weight. It is weathered in the deep way — not ruined, exactly, but settled into the shape its own decay chose for it. Steps are dished in the middle. Thresholds have become ramps. Floodlines are carved into doorposts in generations of dark bands, and the buildings have learned them.

And it is empty. Not abandoned-feeling. Empty in a more complete way than that.

There are no animals. No birds cross the brown upper dark. No insects rise from the warm water though by every law of marsh and heat they should be in clouds. Nothing flicks in the canals. No rats in the granaries, no swallows in the arcades, no fish troubling the basins, no voices, no one to have left recently. The algae mats in still water are the most active living things, and even they are sparse — olive-black skins that slowly crawl a few inches across a cistern over the course of a day, then split and begin again. It gives the wet heat a sterile quality, like a jungle with the jungle removed. A place exhaling, but not inhabited.

It is mostly quiet because of that. Sound does not travel well. The air is too thick, the stones too wet, the streets too often roofed over by later building — layer upon layer of vault and terrace and bridging room — so that noises die close to where they are made. Your breath is loud. Cloth against skin is loud. Underneath, always, water is dripping somewhere, but each drip is isolated, considered, falling into a basin or a canal or a hollow worn in a stair, with long silences between. Now and then a whole district makes a small stone sound in its foundations, a settling groan, and the canals answer half a minute later with a slap against a wall that has nothing visible to do with it.

The city is active despite the stillness. That is the next thing you learn.

Twice in what would be a day, if days could be told, a warm pulse comes in from the marsh. The canals swell without rain. Water climbs the water-stairs one step, three steps, covers the lower markets to the ankle, finds every channel and groove the builders cut for it, fills the public basins to their brims, then withdraws, leaving everything rinsed and steaming and minutely rearranged. Clay pipes knock softly in the walls as they cool. Vents between buildings exhale. Condensation forms so fast on the shaded sides of columns that it runs in sheets. This is the city's breathing, and it is regular enough to set your body by: flood, steam, drip, ebb, the long hot pause in the dark.

It is during the steam that the place stops obeying itself.

At first it is only the reflections. In the canal water, in the flooded plazas, in the black mirrors of the cisterns, the city is lit. Not brightly — but there are distinctions of amber and pale blue in the reflected windows, a reflected sky with weather in it, upper stories that do not exist above the actual rooflines. Look up, and the windows are dark and shuttered and wet. Look down, and they are open. Sometimes a reflected bridge crosses a canal at a place where the real canal has no bridge, and if you watch the reflection long enough, the water under the real, bridgeless air makes a faint, corresponding ripple, as if something were crossing anyway.

Then it is distances.

An arcade you crossed in forty paces in the ebb takes two hundred in the flood, though you can see both ends of it the whole time, and neither end appears farther away. A stairway descends eleven steps at morning-ebb and continues past eleven at steam, going down into a lower street that smells hotter and older than the one you left, where the floodlines on the doors are carved in a script of cuts no other district uses. Courtyards widen when no one is in them. You hear their dripping get farther apart.

The water does not always fall. In the roofed streets, condensation gathers on the vaults, beads, and sometimes — when the heat pulse is strongest — lets go upward, pattering softly into the underside of the stone above it, as if the ceiling were the floor of some other, inverted rain. Drops hang in doorways without falling at all, warm spheres at throat height that you must push through; they break over your face with the taste of iron and lime, and for a moment, while your eyes are wet, the street through them looks dry, sunlit, and paved with white stone. Blink it away and you are in the dark again.

There are rules, of a sort. The city teaches them physically.

Drink only moving water, and only where it moves fast enough to make a sound. Still water in this place keeps things. It keeps heat long after the stones around it have cooled. It keeps reflections after you have left the room. Once, in a bathhouse at the center of the third district, you will find a pool so perfectly still that it shows the ceiling without the cracks the ceiling actually has — and if you look long enough, your own reflection will continue looking after you have turned your head.

Cross bridges only on the ebb. On the flood, a bridge may deliver you to the bank you started from, but altered by one detail you will spend the rest of the walk trying to name: a stair that was on the left now descending on the right, your wet footprints already there, going the other way, slightly smaller than your feet.

Do not navigate by landmarks. Navigate by drip-pitch and water temperature. The high, clear ticking of the cistern towers means you are near the outer districts. The low, spaced tolling from the bathhouse drains means center. If the water at your ankles suddenly cools — the only cold thing in the city — you have come to one of the seam-streets, and you should back out the way you came, exactly, without turning around, until it warms again. What lies past a seam-street is also the city, but not continuous with this one. Its canals run uphill. Its houses weep inward. The dripping there is in unison, and it stops when it knows you are listening.

At the center, all the canals arrive at a plaza that the floods never enter.

It is the size of a market square, paved in black basalt fitted without mortar, and it is dry. Completely dry, in a city where even the interiors sweat — dry in a way that hurts the teeth, its stones giving off a stored, oven heat that has no source and no steam in it. The buildings around it have no doors facing inward. Their backs are blank, salt-blistered walls, and the floodlines that mark every other structure in the city climb those backs higher and higher the closer they get to the plaza, as if the water, century by century, were trying to see over.

In the middle of the plaza there is nothing. No well, no statue, no drain. Just the fitted stones, and a darkness above it so complete that the ordinary dark of the city seems by comparison like dusk. Sound does not cross it well. A drip from the nearest canal arrives late, and flattened, as if it had come a very long way to get across fifty paces.

Stand there long enough in the heat, and the nothing acquires a pressure. The dry stones begin, very faintly, to tick as they cool — although they do not cool. And the water all over the rest of the city, in canals and cisterns and flooded markets and the hanging drops in the doorways, pauses in its cycles for exactly the length of one held breath.

Then the flood pulse comes again. The canals swell. The reflections light their windows. Somewhere a bridge that was not there at the last ebb completes itself in reflection only, and the water beneath the empty air ripples under a crossing weight, and the city — wet, hot, unlit, unpeopled, patient as geology and busy as a lung — continues with whatever it is doing, in the dark, without you needing to understand whether you are its visitor, its memory, or one of its temporary arrangements of water.
