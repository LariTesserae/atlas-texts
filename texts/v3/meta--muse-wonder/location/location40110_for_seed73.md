The Crown Marsh sits where no marsh should be able to sit.

You come up to it through three layers of cloud, in a lift-car that has long since given up being a vehicle and become more like a slow, humming insect. Below is the hot plain, white with salt and heat-haze. Then forest, then bare black rock stabbed with waterfalls going the wrong way in the wind. Then, after hours of thinning blue, you should arrive at ice, at a wind that tears words away, at nothing alive bigger than lichen.

Instead the doors sigh open onto steam.

The air up here is thick enough to chew. It is thirty-four degrees at eleven kilometers. It presses warm and wet against your face, it fogs every lens, it makes your shirt stick in the first minute and never unstick again. The sunlight is ordinary sunlight — hard, equatorial, casting short shadows at noon — but it comes through a permanent exhalation, so everything has wet highlights. Leaves shine. Stone shines. Your own skin shines.

This is the first rule of the place: the atmosphere is kept.

Around the rim of the plateau, spaced a kilometer apart, stand the pylons. They are not tall. Most are only three or four meters, made of a dull pearl material that is neither metal nor shell, grown in crooked rings like coral. They make no visible wall. They make a very faint tone when mist drives through them, a chord just at the edge of hearing that builds, over the marsh, into a constant low loudness under everything else. Step between two pylons at the edge and look down — straight down, a cliff falling for kilometers to cloud — and lean out, and your forehead meets resistance. Soft at first. Then firmer, like leaning into deep water. A bird that stoops too fast off the edge will describe a perfect, surprised arc and be turned back in, wings scrambling.

Inside that invisible lentil of kept air, water has nowhere sensible to go, so it goes everywhere.

The plateau is the size of a small country valley, twenty kilometers across if you count the terraces, and it is old in the way a riverbed is old. Whatever sharpness it once had has been rounded off. The black basalt at its core does not jag; it slumps and humps, it forms broad backs and shallow bowls, worn stairs and natural amphitheaters polished by ten thousand years of feet and rain and roots. Established. Settled into itself. In the bowls are lakes, dozens of them, tea-dark and steaming gently at dawn, linked by slow channels where the water barely seems to move. Between the lakes the rock weeps. It weeps all day, all year — thin sheets condensing out of the saturated air onto cool stone in the shade, beading, running in black streaks, feeding moss so thick you can push a hand into it to the wrist.

And because it is hot, and because it is wet, it is impossibly lush for a mountaintop.

Tree-ferns twenty meters tall crowd the lake margins, their trunks wrapped in orchids and in smaller ferns that themselves hold tiny ponds. Bromeliads the size of bathtubs sit in every fork, and each one is a world: frogs, iridescent beetles, blind white fish no longer than a fingernail. Canopy closes over canopy. Vines hang into the warm water and root again on the other side. In the vent fields on the northern side, where the mountain's own geothermal heat rises up through cracks and makes the ground pleasantly, dangerously warm under bare feet, algae mats grow in terraces of emerald and sulfur-yellow, and flowers open that smell of pepper and rotten fruit and hot copper.

The fauna has completely forgotten it is at altitude. It behaves as if it were in a lowland swamp dropped into the sky.

At dusk the noise becomes a physical thing. Frogs in the bromeliad ponds. Frogs in the lakes. Frogs in the puddles that form in the leaf-cups and in the old footprints in the mud. Cicada-analogues in the fern crowns whose song is not a chirp but a sustained, glassy ringing that aligns, horribly and beautifully, with the hum of the pylons. Waterbirds with long toes walk across floating leaves, arguing. Bats pour out of warm caves in the rim-rock. And gliders — true gliders, membrane-winged mammals the size of large dogs — launch themselves from the highest rim trees in the evening thermal, ride it out over the abyss, hit the air-wall at speed, and use the bounce, that soft deceleration and push, to curve back in a long bank over the marsh, mouths open, eating the clouds of midges that rise off the water. The local children, in the few places where there are children, call this *kissing the sky* and can tell individual animals by the sound their wings make when they turn.

There are not many people. There never have been. 

Scattered across the terraces you find the built things, and most of them refuse to stay merely built. A weather-shrine at the end of a stone causeway, its roof long collapsed, its interior now a perfect garden because the nano-lattice in its walls still regulates humidity for instruments no one remembers. A lift-head house, empty. A row of stilt-huts above the eastern lakes, made of fern-wood and that same pearl substance, where a rotating crew of tenders live for five-year postings and spend most of their time doing very little, very carefully. The pylons need almost no maintenance — they heal, slowly, if cracked; they bud replacement nodules over decades — but the channels need clearing of fern root, and the lakes need watching, and the old agreements with the glider colonies need renewing with offerings of fruit laid out on flat stones.

Because the second rule is slowness. Everything up here that matters happens on a long fuse.

The kept air breathes on a cycle of about forty days. For three weeks the pressure inside the lentil rises by fractions, almost imperceptible except that your ears pop in your sleep and the lakes swell and the channels run a finger's width higher and all the bromeliad ponds connect, for a night, into one shining network. Fish travel between trees. Frogs sing in a different key. Then, over another three weeks, it sighs back down through the pylons in a warm wind that smells of stone. The trees time their fruiting to it. The vent algae bloom and die back on a yearly turning. A crack in a pylon that would kill the whole marsh if it failed tomorrow will instead be visibly narrower if you come back in ten years. 

Even the strange things are patient.

In the central bowl there is a channel where the water flows, very slowly, uphill. Not a trick of perspective — drop a leaf in, and it will creep up a polished basalt chute for thirty meters, gaining perhaps a meter of height, before joining the upper lake. The tenders lay their hands in it to cool fevers and say the old builders wanted a reminder. In the forest on the west side, some of the largest tree-ferns have grown through and around fallen pieces of technology so old that the tech has adapted to the tree instead of the other way: signal-lights now pulse in time with sap flow, in greens; dormant sensors open like seed pods in the rain and close at noon. At the southern rim, if you stand there long after dark, you can sometimes see the air-wall itself — not the edge, but its structure, a faint hexagonal shimmer moving at the speed of a cloud shadow across the stars, repairing, rearranging, thinking in geometries too slow for a human lifetime to follow.

Nothing up here dries. Nothing up here freezes. Specimens brought down to the plain shrivel in hours, confused. Specimens brought up wilt until they learn to drink from the air. The rocks sweat onto your palms. The paths, made of fitted stone, are always slick and always warm, and between the stones grow tiny blue flowers that close when stepped near and open again minutes later, so that a person walking at dusk leaves a brief, fading trail of darkness in a carpet of bloom.

Late in the afternoon the clouds from below rise high enough to spill, gently, over the lowest section of the rim, and for an hour the marsh is in fog and in sunlight at once. The lakes disappear. The fern crowns become islands. The gliders call to each other, lost and delighted, and their calls come back off the air-wall thrown into soft echoes, as if the mountain were much larger on the inside than it is, and still talking to itself, very loudly, in its sleep.
