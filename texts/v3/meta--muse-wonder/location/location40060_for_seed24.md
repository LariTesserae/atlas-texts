It is a hollow in low limestone country, and you find it first by touch more than by sight.

From outside it is almost nothing: a dip between pale, weather-rounded rocks, a sloping throat of gravel and dust descending out of the glare. The ground around it is flat and dry, sun-warmed even where the light is already failing, with only a few tufts of wiry grass spaced far apart in the cracks. Then the passage bends, just once, shallowly, and the world closes.

Inside, it is dark in the ordinary, complete way that a bent cave is dark. Not a blue dusk, not shadow with shapes left in it. After the first ten paces the entrance is only a paler absence behind you, a faint warm smudge where the day still exists. After twenty, even that is gone if you turn the wrong way. The walls must be found with an outstretched hand.

The chamber itself is small enough to hold in the mind whole. Perhaps thirty paces long, twelve across at its widest, the ceiling high enough in the middle that its presence is felt as an openness above the head rather than seen — a cathedral reduced to the scale of a grove, empty at the center. Everything is set wide apart. A few fallen blocks rest near the walls where they came down so long ago that dust has drifted up their sides and softened their edges. There is a clear, flat floor between them, and nothing else to cross.

The floor is packed earth and limestone flour, dry as ash, warm through thin soles. In the dimmer part near the mouth it is cracked into loose polygons, the cracks filled with finer dust. Deeper in the cracks fade; the surface is simply smooth, trodden by no regular traffic, settled. It gives underfoot with a faint, almost inaudible crunch, and the sound stops immediately. No echo travels. The stone and the still air take your footsteps, your breathing, the brush of cloth, and return nothing.

Warmth is the hollow's most constant quality. It does not come in drafts or pockets. The rock holds it evenly, day and night, season to season — a bodily warmth, in the high twenties, as if the stone had absorbed a long summer and never been asked to give it back. It is warm on the wall under a palm, warm in the dust an inch down, warm in the air at ankle height and at face height. Nothing here cools enough for dew. Your breath does not mist. Your skin dries slowly without sweating hard, and the inside of the mouth begins to taste of mineral dust.

There is almost no water anywhere in its economy. No drip, no seep darkening the back wall, no damp earth smell, no pool in the low corner where a pool ought logically to be. The limestone is pale and thirsty, pitted with old solution hollows that have long since dried and rounded. In one place a thin, white crust of salt blooms on the rock at hand-height, a few crystals wide, relic of moisture that passed this way in some other decade and evaporated on the spot. Rain, when it rarely comes to the country above, must run off the caprock elsewhere; the bend and the slope of the entrance shed it away. What reaches the floor is windblown dust, and dust is what the place is slowly made of.

Life is present, but only just, and only plants. At the threshold, where a little light still falls, three or four grass tussocks have taken hold, blades bleached straw-yellow, curled tight against the dryness, growing so slowly that a fallen blade from years ago lies beside the living ones still recognizably a blade. A single spindly shrub leans out of a crack near the entrance, its branches gnarled and widely spaced, its few leaves small, thick, and grey-green, smelling faintly of resin when the warm air near the rock happens to stir. Nothing has chewed them. Their edges are whole.

Farther in, where no leaf could make a living, roots do the exploring instead. They descend through hairline fractures in the ceiling — thin, pale cords from the plants above, feeling their way down the walls, branching into finer and finer threads that end blind in the dust-filled air. They are sparse, one here, two far apart there, and they do not move perceptibly. Without insects there is no hum around the shrub, no ant line crosses the floor, no spider has strung the convenient corners, no bat has ever patterned the dust with droppings. The absence is total and oddly clean. Dead leaves do not get carried off or eaten down; they simply curl, bleach, and eventually become indistinguishable from the dust they rest on.

Nothing is built here. No hearth ring, no stacked stone, no carving or scratched mark, no path worn by feet or tools. A loose flake of limestone on the floor is only a loose flake of limestone, sharp enough that a hand could use it, but unshaped, unclaimed, lying where the roof let it go before anyone's memory. The only way to move through the dark is the primitive one in any case: palm flat to the wall, steps placed slowly, the body learning distances that the eyes cannot check.

The place does not seem to be waiting for anything, either. Erosion has already done its decisive work — the collapse, the rounding, the settling — and stopped at a form it can keep. No pebble ticks down while you listen. No draft searches the chamber. The temperature does not fall as you go deeper, the darkness does not thin toward some second opening, the back wall, when your hand finally finds it, curves round in a blind, smooth alcove where the ceiling lowers and the dust lies deepest and softest, undisturbed in overlapping drifts.

Stay long enough and the silence becomes the main feature. It is not the silence of a pause, not a held breath before a sound resumes. It is the steady state of the hollow: warm, dry, dark, nearly empty, a single room of stone holding almost nothing, changing almost not at all, while the sparse grass at its mouth grows a fraction of an inch and the fallen leaf beside it takes another year to become dust.
