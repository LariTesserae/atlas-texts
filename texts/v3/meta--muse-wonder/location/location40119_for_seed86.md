It begins as absence in a cool plain.

Above, there is wind, pale grass, a few low shrubs flattened between stones, and then a hole where the ground simply stops. Not a valley, not a cliff you approach from the side. You walk across almost level country and come to a rim of broken limestone, and below that rim the world goes down in shadow, a hundred and fifty metres down, and keeps going sideways for longer than you can see.

The drop is not old and softened. The edges are white and angular. The fallen blocks at the bottom are still the shape of the ceiling they fell from — slabs like tables, wedges like teeth, boulders split along fresh faces that have never been rained on enough to round. Where water has touched the stone it has only begun its work: runnels an inch deep, stalactites no longer than a thumb in most places, a few larger ones hanging in clusters with their points still needle-sharp. Nothing down there has been worn comfortable yet.

And it does not end at the sinkhole. That is only a skylight.

Beyond it the roof recovers and the chamber opens — and opens, and opens again. Eight, perhaps nine kilometres from the first collapse to the far wall, two kilometres wide in the broad vaults, narrowing to passages you could cross in twenty strides and then dilating suddenly into another dark the size of a town. The ceiling in the main hall is so high that torchlight never finds it. Even at noon, when sun falls straight through the three great sinkholes, you see the roof only there, in those cones of light, as a distant, pitted arch. Between the skylights the columns that hold it stand hundreds of metres apart. A forest would be dense by comparison. This is pavement, empty air, and distance.

It is always cool. Eleven degrees, twelve at most, by the floor and by the far walls, in January and in July, at midday and at three in the morning. Your breath shows faintly. Your skin cools even while you work. Away from the sun-cones the light never rises above a deep twilight, and in the connecting galleries it fails altogether, so that the dimness itself becomes a kind of geography: bright green islands under the holes, grey country between them where you can still make out the shape of blocks and pools, then black country where shape comes only from touch and echo.

The water is everywhere and nowhere enough to gather. It is a damp place, not a wet one. Condensation beads on cold stone in the mornings that are not really mornings. A drip falls, unhurried, into a pool that has no inlet you can find. Another falls a quarter of a mile away. In the rainy weeks above, thin streams find the cracks and come down as veils through the skylights and vanish into the floor rubble within a few metres. The pools that persist are shallow, lens-clear, rimmed with a white crust of calcite, and cold enough to ache your hand. Between them the limestone is dry, dusty in places, filmed with moisture in others, slick only where a perennial drip has polished a path down a block. You can walk for an hour and find nothing to drink, then come on three pools in a hollow, each no larger than a washbasin, each reflecting a piece of distant roof-hole like a fallen coin.

Plants have almost no hold. Under the skylights, where the light cones strike, there are ferns in the cracks, moss in cushions as dark and dense as velvet, a little sour grass, once a fig sapling whose roots have come down the sinkhole wall in ropes and whose leaves turn all one way, toward its column of sun. A few paces out from the light the green thins to algae on wet stone, then to nothing. In the dark vaults there is no leaf, no root, no seed that sprouts. What pale threads spread over the dung hills and the bodies of the dead are fungal, not vegetable, and they do not need light at all.

Because the life here does not come from plants. It comes, overwhelmingly, on wings.

The bats are not an inhabitant of the cave. They are its weather.

They hang from the unseen ceiling in the main vault in numbers that cease to mean anything as numbers — millions, across a dozen species, the small ones packed flank to flank in living mats that shift and ripple, the larger fruit bats in looser companies nearer the skylights, a constant rain of discarded wings, pups, corpses, and dung underneath. At any hour some fraction of them is awake, shuffling, squeaking, dropping, circling once in the dark and settling again, and some other fraction is coming in laden from the plains above while another is going out, so that the colony never empties and never stills. Dusk above is not an event below. It is a changing of shifts. The river of bodies at the sinkhole mouths thickens, but the roar in the hall does not stop.

And it is a roar. That is the first rule of the place, learned in the body before the mind: you will not converse under the roost. The sound is layered — the high, needling chatter of echolocation at the edge of hearing, felt as pressure behind the eyes; the lower squeaks and quarrels rolling across the ceiling in waves; the soft, incessant percussion of wings, which when a mat is startled and ten thousand lift at once becomes a sound like a waterfall going upward. It echoes off walls too far away to see and comes back doubled. It enters the chest. Between the sinkholes, where swiftlets nest on ledges and navigate by clicking, their clicks stitch a finer pattern through the bat noise, sharp and dry. In the pools, frogs call in the permanent evening. Everywhere under stone, cave crickets tick, beetles rustle in the guano, water drops strike at intervals so regular and so amplified that after an hour you begin to navigate by them, each pool with its own note.

The floor beneath the roosts is not stone at all but guano built up into hills, soft, dark, treacherous, crusted dry on top and warm and ammoniac inside from its own slow rotting — the only warmth in the cave that does not come from a body or a flame. It seethes, in the quiet way of such places, with beetles, maggots, mites, cockroaches, earwigs, millipedes, in densities that turn over a spadeful of it into movement in every direction. Spiders hang at its edges. Centipedes hunt through it. Rats, sleek and fearless, commute between the dung hills and the rubble. Snakes commute after the rats, and after fallen bats, which they take still stunned on the ground. Nothing here is sparse except the space itself. The air above a fresh fall of pups is thick with flies. The pools hold blind, translucent shrimp and a few small, colourless fish that turn toward vibration, not light. Even the dust is largely powdered wing and dung and the shed skins of insects.

Everything eats the bats, in the end, or what they bring. They feed outside, on moths over the cold grassland, on midges over distant rivers, on fruit in valleys a night's flight away, and they return and deposit that whole countryside, digested, under this roof. That is why a place with almost no water, almost no light, and almost no vegetation can be loud with life. It is a trap for energy gathered elsewhere.

People come, but the cave has never let them stay.

They lower themselves at the smallest sinkhole on ropes of twisted fibre, with wooden poles lashed into ladders they take away again when they leave. They carry stone knives, bone scrapers, baskets, gourds of water, torches of resinous wood and fat that smoke and gutter in the ammoniac air. Metal, when someone has a piece of it from trade, is kept wrapped and used sparingly; sparks are useless in the damp and the good tools here are edge and fire and rope. They do not build. There is no shelter, no pen, no shrine, no marked path below — the guano swallows ash, the drips erase scent, a cairn would vanish under the next fall of droppings. They come to gather what the cave discards: fallen young bats, taken quickly before the rats; dung for fields above, dug with wooden spades and hauled up basket by basket; occasionally a snake, a sack of fat crickets. They work with cloth bound over mouth and nose, eyes watering, shouting into each other's ears, and they leave before the torches fail. Above, their camps are temporary too, a ring of stones on the plain that the wind eventually worries apart. Down here, a week later, there is no sign they were ever present except a place in the dung where the crust has been disturbed, already re-softening.

What they have learned, and what anyone learns who stays longer than an hour, is the set of unspoken laws the place enforces. Keep your mouth closed under the roost, or breathe through cloth, because the air is full of what falls. Do not drink from the white-rimmed pools; drink what you brought, or what drips clean from a stalactite point into a gourd. Walk on stone where you can; the guano hills look solid and will take your leg to the knee and fill your foot-covering with biting things. Do not trust silence as a sign of emptiness — silence here only means the nearest mat of bats is asleep, and the ceiling above you is still loaded. Navigate by sound and smell: fresher dung, stronger ammonia, louder chatter ahead; the clean smell of wet limestone and the regular note of a drip means open floor and perhaps water. And do not expect the place to change for you, or mark your passing, or give you a season to come back to.

That is its deepest character. Nothing in the deep vaults cycles except the bats themselves, and even they return to the same density, the same noise, the same warm hills slowly rising a fraction each year under the same cold ceiling. No flood rearranges the floor. No drought empties the dripping pools. No summer warms the galleries. The light cones swing a little with the hour and go out; the twilight between them does not. A bat born in the dark hears, from its first day, the roar its grandmother heard, at the same volume, in the same eleven-degree air, above the same sharp white rubble just beginning, after all this time, to be stone again.

You leave by climbing toward the one sound that is not the cave's — wind, moving in grass you cannot yet see — with your ears still ringing, your clothes stiff with ammonia, the coolth going out of your skin only slowly. Behind you the entrance swallows its light again. The frogs go on calling at the pools. The next shift is already moving through the sinkhole mouth in a column so dense it casts a shadow at noon, and the hall receives it without pause, as it receives everything: without building up, without running down, in a loud, damp, crowded equilibrium that feels less like a moment in the cave's life than like the whole of it, continuing.
