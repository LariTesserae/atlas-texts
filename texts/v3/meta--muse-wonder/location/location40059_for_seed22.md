First, from a distance, it looks like nothing.

A low, warm basin opening for fifty, sixty miles under a dim sky — not dark, exactly, but thinned. The light comes through as if through smoked glass and wet leaves, amber-grey, never quite reaching the ground. Sharp ridges cut across it, black and new. The land here has not had time to soften. It was lifted, or unfolded, or dropped into place too recently for rivers to learn it; the gullies still have knife edges, the basalt cliffs still show the bright fracture where they split, the soil is still in angular clods that haven't learned to be round. Between the ridges, jungle.

And it is loud enough to hurt.

Not loud the way a city is loud. Loud the way the inside of a bell is loud while it is still ringing. A continuous, layered roar sits on the basin, so total that after a minute you stop hearing it as sound and start feeling it as pressure — in your teeth, in the sternum, in the soft places behind the eyes. Cicada-analogues in the canopy by the uncountable billions, each the size of a thumb, sawing at a pitch that rises and falls in waves. Frogs in the pocket wetlands, bellowing through throat-sacs like wet drums. Birds — or things that have taken the bird-job, membranous, iridescent, four-winged — screaming as they stitch the air between towers. And underneath it all, lower than hearing, the infrastructure: a subsonic pulse that makes loose stones tremble in place and turns puddles to standing rings.

Because there *is* a city here. It just refuses density.

The towers stand kilometers apart. Each one is a town entire — ten thousand people stacked in a tapering, spiraling column of pale ceramic, living timber, and something translucent that is not glass and not resin, which sweats in the warm air and heals its own cracks overnight. Markets coil around their middles in lit galleries. Schools, manufactories, temples, farms, reservoirs, cemeteries — all of it vertical, layered, wrapped in gardens. Between towers: nothing built that you can see. Jungle, ridge, mist-pocket, a sudden mirror-still pool in a fresh crater. Empty miles. You can stand on a tower's fortieth terrace at dusk, look out over a region that holds three million people, and see perhaps six distant lights pricking the green murk.

The connection is invisible, and instantaneous, and therefore everywhere. Transit here doesn't move through the space between. Doorways in the towers' cores simply *agree* with doorways elsewhere — you step through a frame humming at the threshold of pain and you are forty miles east, уши ringing, clothes damp with someone else's weather. Freight goes the same way, folded through in sealed pods that arrive with a concussive *whump* the fauna have learned to ignore. There are no roads. There never were. The emptiness is load-bearing: the transit lattice only works across unbuilt, unexcavated ground, grounded through the raw young rock. Build too close together and the doorways start opening onto wrong years, wrong heights, onto solid stone. So the city keeps its distance from itself, superstitiously, by law.

Water is the other scarcity that shapes everything. It almost never truly rains. The basin sits in a rain-shadow the towers' own sky-sails make worse — vast, slow, translucent membranes drifting a mile up on no visible support, filtering the hard light down to this permanent late-afternoon, condensing what moisture passes. What water there is arrives as *events*. A tower's cooling cycle ends and it exhales: a warm fog-burst rolls down its flanks at dawn, drenching its gardens, filling cisterns, turning the jungle below to steam and birdsong for an hour. The sky-sails weep at irregular intervals, fat drops falling out of a clear dimness onto leaves the size of doors. Pools persist in shadowed fracture-basins, black and mineral and crowded with life, then vanish in a week of warm wind. Everything living here is built around intermittence. The dominant trees have leaves that cup upward and seal. The ground-plants unfurl only during a fog-burst, a whole meadow opening in minutes, purple-white, loud with bees, then folding away. People time laundry, baptisms, concrete-cures, and sex to the towers' exhalations.

And the lushness is real, and somehow not despite the dryness but routed through the sound. Half the flora here is resonant. Hollow reeds twenty feet tall lean toward the towers and drink the infrasound, converting vibration through their stems into a slow hydraulic pump that pulls condensation down to their roots. The canopy cicadas cluster thickest on those reeds, amplifying them; the reeds feed canopy epiphytes; the epiphytes drip; the frogs breed in the drip-basins; the four-winged hunters eat the frogs and their guano fires another round of explosive, oversaturated green. It's a loop with noise as its sun. In the quietest hour — and quiet is relative; quiet here would be deafening anywhere else — you can watch a clearing visibly grow, vines questing toward a newly humming doorway-frame, leaves turning to face a sound the way sunflowers once faced light.

Humans cope the way everything else has: by adaptation that looks, from outside, like ritual. Nobody speaks aloud outdoors. Nobody has for generations. Conversation is sign, is touch-alphabet on forearms, is text blooming across the inside of the eyelid through implants that filter the roar down to a manageable hiss and occasionally fail, leaving their owners weeping and grinning at a sudden full dose of the world. Children are born with, and fitted within days for, elegant cartilaginous ear-shunts grown from their own tissue — another piece of tech that current physics describes poorly, like the doorways, like the sails that stay up, like the tower-material that sweats and heals and occasionally, in warm wet weather, dreams in faint patterns of light across its surface. Indoors, inside double-skinned walls tuned to cancel the basin's frequency, there is a silence so complete that visitors panic. People go to quiet-rooms the way desert people go to water. They pay by the minute. They emerge shaken and holy.

The rules accrete from all of this, unspoken mostly:

Don't build in the gaps. Don't dig in the gaps either. Don't try to be heard over the basin; it will win, and the reed-beds will mark where you shouted by growing toward you for a season. Travel light through doorways when a tower is about to exhale — you will arrive wet, or arrive in the middle of weather, or, once in a documented while, arrive a few seconds before you left, which the transit authority fines heavily. When the sky-sails weep, get under a leaf or get inside; what's up there has been condensing for a long time and it comes down concentrated, tingling, faintly electric. When a tower goes silent — felt, suddenly, as a hole in the pressure on your chest — walk away from it across open ground and do not use its doors. It is thinking, or repairing, or listening to the rock settle under it. The last tower that went silent for more than a day came back with a new floor nobody had built, furnished, already lived-in-looking, dust in the corners.

At night the dimness deepens to something warm and brown, the membranes above glow faintly with stored light, and the noise *changes key* rather than lessening — nocturnal fauna trading shifts with diurnal, the towers cooling, the reeds playing lower notes. The pools steam. The young ridges tick as they contract. From any terrace, the basin stretches away: jungle, void, a tower lit like a slow lighthouse, more jungle, another tower, a sky-sail drifting its shadow over everything, and under it all that felt, endless, architectural roar, as if the empty land itself were an instrument the city was built — sparsely, carefully, at great distance — to keep from ever quite playing.
