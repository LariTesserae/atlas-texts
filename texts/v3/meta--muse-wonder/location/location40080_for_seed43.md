You have to duck to find it. From above there is only a roof at ground level — low, broad, made of fired tile and packed clay, steaming faintly in the warm air — and a stairwell going down inside a thick brick throat.

The steps are dished in the middle from feet. Human feet, mostly, in soft shoes, for a very long time. The handrail is not a rail at all but a rope, black and polished where palms have run it, tied off to iron rings that have worn oval grooves into the plaster. As you go down the light does not disappear. It changes. Shafts cut through the roof-vault at an angle, walled with glazed brick, throw clean, ordinary daylight in hard-edged pools onto the floor below. Between the pools it is shade, but never darkness.

Below, it is one place, and it is crowded past what one place should hold.

A single sunken hall, about the size of a small cathedral if you lowered its ceiling and filled it to the walls. Maybe thirty paces long, twelve across, but you would never walk those thirty paces in a straight line. The vault is brick, course upon course, patched in a dozen colors of clay — rose, ochre, ash-grey, a greenish brick where copper ash got into the kiln one year. The walls are brick. The floor is brick laid on edge and worn glossy. There is no earth visible anywhere. Even the ground the animals stand on is made: raised platforms of slatted wood over brick troughs, boxes of imported, sifted compost, mats woven from reed and old rope. When a board rots it is replaced. When a brick cracks it is chiseled out and mortared again with lime.

It is warm down here, warmer than above, with the close heat of bodies and breath and dung and a small cook-fire kept at the far end in a hooded clay hearth. The air is damp without ever being wet. There is no stream, no well in the floor. Water arrives in skins from above, and what else there is comes as condensation. On cold mornings the vault sweats. A bead gathers, runs along a mortar line, finds a low point that generations have learned by heart, and drips — *tock* — into a clay cup wired to the wall, or into a shallow trough black with algae, or straight onto the head of an animal that has positioned itself for exactly that. Everywhere are these small catches: split gourds, dented pots, a line of bowls under the main beam. The plaster below a regular drip is stained in a long green-black tear. That is also where almost all the vegetation lives.

Sparse, opportunistic green. A fringe of moss no wider than a hand. A pot of sharp-smelling herbs hung out of goat reach. Trays on the upper gallery sprouting fodder — pale barley shoots kept just until they green, then cut. A fig cutting in a cracked amphora by the brightest light shaft, all leaf and ambition, never fruiting properly. The rest is dry fodder brought down and stacked dense as masonry: straw bales, bundles of stalks, sacks of husk and pod.

Because the place is, above all, a byre.

Tier upon tier of made habitation, near-touching. Along both long walls, stalls rise in three levels, not cages so much as architecture — niches with low brick arches exactly like a dovecote, but sized variously, each fronted with a hand-hewn gate of lashed wood, a feeding hole, a name or mark daubed in whitewash. Pigeons own the highest rows, a shifting, iridescent population that pours out of the round holes in the vault and pours back in, wings clattering against tile. Below them, rabbits in wicker-fronted hutches, hens in stacked coops, a row of ducks who have claimed the dampest corner near the drip troughs and made it reek pleasantly of mud that is not mud but wet, trampled straw.

At floor level, larger beasts. Goats with bells too large for them, penned four and five to a platform, picking neat-footed over slats. Two mules in the central aisle stalls, patient, fly-twitching, their hooves having worn perfect crescents into the brick at the tethering rings. Piglets — not full pigs, never full pigs down here, they are taken up when they reach a certain shout — in a sunken pen lined with tile so it can be sluiced with a single bucket. Children of the place, human children, move between all of it with baskets almost as large as they are.

And around the domestic, the uninvited who have become tenants. Geckos flat on the warm vault, waiting by the light shafts. Swallows nesting in the stair throat despite all discouragement, swooping the length of the hall. Frogs the color of wet lime, stationed gravely in the drip bowls, eating the flies that rise from the lower troughs. A thriving, invisible-to-most nation of crickets in the fodder stacks, mice in the wall cavities — there is a second, mouse-scale architecture inside the bricks, worn runs and hidden stores — and bats in the one shaft that was bricked half-closed a century ago and never reopened properly.

It is loud. It cannot help being loud. The vault takes every sound and returns it a fraction larger. Goat bells, hen objection, pigeon wings beaten against air, mule stamp, the rhythmic *chuff-chuff* of someone chopping fodder on a block, the treadle of a simple loom up on the gallery where a woman is weaving new pen-mats while also, somehow, watching three toddlers and a pan. People do not speak so much as project, short and practiced, or they put their mouths to one of the clay speaking-tubes set into the pillars if they need the other end of the hall. When the feeding gong is struck — a flat sheet of beaten metal by the hearth, it has hung there long enough to have a thumb-smooth dent at its center — the whole building answers for a full minute. Every animal knows what that note means, and says so.

The activity never settles into stillness, only into rhythms. Shaft-covers of woven matting are hauled open and shut on ropes to shift the light pools and the heat. Droppings fall, or are swept, through the slats into the brick channels below the platforms, then raked by hand down a gentle, built slope to the compost pit behind the hearth wall, whose warmth comes back through the bricks — that is partly why it is always warm, and why the back stalls are prized in the cooler months for newborns. The compost breeds worms and black flies. The worms go to the hens. The frogs tend the flies. Old straw goes down, new straw comes down, animals go up blinking into daylight and other animals come down. Water is measured, animal by animal, drip-cup by drip-cup. Nothing is wasted because there is nowhere down here for waste to go that is not still inside the room.

The rules are therefore plain, and spoken to newcomers as if they were features of the building, which they are: No bare feet on the slats. No fire outside the hood. Do not block a light shaft with fodder, however convenient the stack wants to be. First drink from any new drip is left for the frogs. Knock a gate twice before you open it, so what is inside can move back from the swing. If you bring something down, you carry something up.

It has been weathered into a kind of balance this way. New patches sit beside very old ones. A pillar has a braided rope repair that has lasted longer than the original timber. There is a small, odd sweetness to the place, under the dung and straw and hot animal smell — limewash, and herb pots, and smoke, and the faint mineral tang of condensation that has been running over the same bricks for longer than anyone counts.

From the gallery, at feeding time, with the light falling in clean shafts through dust and down and straw-chafe, the hall stops looking like a barn that was built underground and starts looking like a single, densely inhabited body: brick ribs, wooden guts, animal warmth, human hands everywhere keeping the passages just clear enough to move.
