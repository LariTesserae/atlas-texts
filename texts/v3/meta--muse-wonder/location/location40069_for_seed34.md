It begins long before you reach anything you could call a street.

For a day you are climbing a cool, wet moor — high grassland the color of old straw flattened by wind, thin soil over stone, water everywhere just under the surface. Your boots do not exactly sink, but the ground gives, sighs, fills the print behind you almost at once. Pools lie in every shallow hollow, perfectly still, reflecting a normal, bright sky. There are no trees to speak of. Now and then a row of wind-bent shrubs marks a slightly drier ridge, and the reed beds thicken where the land dips, but mostly it is openness: grass, moss, black wet peat cut by pale limestone.

Then the building-stone begins to appear in the moor itself. First a retaining wall keeping a pool from spilling. Then a paved ford across a marshy runnel. Then a house, then three, then a terrace of houses following the contour of a hill you hadn't realized was a hill — and you understand, gradually, that you have been inside the city for hours already. It just has no edge.

It is vast in a way that has to be walked to be believed. The plateau it occupies is broad enough that one district can be under rain while another, still within the same continuous roofs and lanes, is in hard sunlight. Locals do not ask if it is raining. They ask where. Clouds actually form inside it in the late morning, when sun warms the thousand canals and cisterns and wet roofs at the center, and the rising damp meets the cold air coming off the higher moor. A soft, local weather turns above the middle quarters, drifts north, spends itself as a brief shower over the stone yards there, and clears. To cross from the southern marsh-gates to the northern quarries is two days on foot through inhabited land the whole way, and three if the middle canals are high.

And yet it is dense. Not tall — nothing here is built to scrape the sky — but close, layered, near-touching. Houses of unmortared and then re-mortared limestone, slate, and dark timber crowd shoulder to shoulder up every slope in step-like terraces. The roof of one house is the yard of the house above it. A lane will pass under a second story, emerge as a stair, become a wooden boardwalk over a canal, and continue as an alley so narrow two people in wet wool coats have to turn sideways. There are no wasted spaces. Stone plinths lift every building a knee's height above the seeping ground; beneath them, water moves constantly, audibly if you kneel, in culverts polished smooth by centuries of flow.

Water is the organizing fact. It weeps out of cut rock faces along the higher streets. It condenses at night on north walls and runs down in black streaks by morning. It is caught from every roof into household butts and public cisterns marked with carved water-lines. It is led, by gravity and patience and an immense inherited knowledge of gradients, through open channels down the center or edge of nearly every street — some no wider than a hand, some wide enough for flat-bottomed punts loaded with stone, cabbages, firewood, slate. There are no machines to move it. There are sluice-boards lifted by hand, counterweighted with stones in rope slings. There are tread-wheels and buckets where a terrace needs lifting. Cellars do not exist; anything stored low would rot or drown. Instead there are lofts, wall-niches, raised granaries on stone feet, and a universal smell of damp wool, woodsmoke, limestone, and green algae.

That green is most of the vegetation the city allows itself. Moss in the mortar. Lichen, orange and grey-green, on the sunward side of old walls whose rainward side is bare washed stone. Slick algae in the slow canals, combed each week with wooden rakes. Reeds planted deliberately at canal bends to slow and clean the water, cut back hard each autumn. On the roofs too long untended, sedum and short moor-grass take hold and are left, because they hold the slates against wind. Real gardens hide in courtyards — walled, drained with fanatical care, growing kale, onions, herbs, a single plum tree trained flat against a warm wall — but from any height the impression is of stone stippled with water, mile after mile, the green a kind of weathering rather than a covering.

It is cool even in summer. The altitude thins nothing to danger, but it sharpens things. Sunlight up here is ordinary daylight made cleaner; shadows have hard edges, white walls hurt the eyes at noon, and within ten minutes of cloud the air goes October-cold. People dress accordingly all year — layered wool, leather aprons for the wet trades, wooden-soled shoes that click on pavement and go silent on boardwalk. Breath shows in the morning lanes. Fires are lit in the evening even in the warm months, not for drama but for drying: boots by the hearth, coats on pegs, bedding aired over stone warmers. No glass to speak of, just shutters and oiled cloth; no lamps in the streets. When night comes, the city goes dark in a way that completes its daytime quiet.

Because it is quiet. That is the strangeness you notice second, after the size. A city this dense should roar. It doesn't. The water and the stone and the damp air seem to eat sound, and the inhabitants have adapted into the hush until it became custom and then something like law. Voices are kept low not by decree but by habit; a shout carries obscenely far on a still morning and everyone turns. Carts move on wooden wheels wrapped in rope where the paving is good, and most heavy things go by canal anyway, sliding past almost silently. Workshops — masons, fullers, dyers, carpenters — sit in sunken, thick-walled yards that swallow their own noise. Even the markets are subdued: a murmur, the slap of wet fish on stone, the clink of a scale. The loudest regular sounds are water in a stepped channel after rain, doves under eaves, and the wooden clatter of a hundred sluice-boards being raised at dawn.

Animals are scarce. A few grey cats, long-haired and suspicious. Ducks on the outer canals, where the city thins back into marsh and reed-fringed pools full of frogs. Corvids riding the plateau wind and settling on the highest chimneys. Pack ponies come in from the moor farms with peat and wool and leave the same day, their hooves muffled in sacking within the bounds, as custom requires. People eat fish from the raised ponds at the city's edge, sheep from the high grazing, and a great deal of stored and pickled things. There are almost no dogs. In the canals, if you look long, small blind-looking fish drift in the cold shadow of the boardwalks, and in spring the surface of the cisterns stars briefly with insects before the swallows — the one reliably lively presence — arrive to work the air above the water for a few frantic months.

Nothing here is new-looking, and nothing is quite ruinous either. That is the balance it has reached. Every stair tread is worn into a shallow bowl where uncountable feet have turned at the same place. Door lintels are blackened, thresholds grooved by buckets. The carvings on public cisterns — hands, fish, rain-clouds, the founders of districts whose names no one now pronounces the old way — have softened into suggestions of themselves. When a wall fails, its stones are sorted on the spot and go back up within the season, the mossy faces turned inward this time. When a canal silts, the neighborhood that depends on it turns out with shovels. The city does not expand so much as continuously re-settle itself, terrace by terrace, like a slope finding its angle.

And it is always in motion under the quiet. At dawn the sluices open and the night's collected water begins its long descent through the quarters, turning a mill here, flushing a dyeyard there, carrying refuse down to the reed-ponds south of the last houses where it spreads, slows, clears, and rejoins the moor. At midmorning the interior cloud gathers. At noon, evaporation lifts a visible shimmer off the wet slate roofs of the lower town while women spread washed wool on the upper terraces to catch the brief hard sun. Afternoon brings the local rain, a rise of an inch in the middle canals, boys poling floating market-boats one street farther uphill than they could in the morning. Evening reverses everything: boards down, cisterns filling from the roofs, shutters closing, the ponies gone, the swallows gone, the pools out on the moor and the pools in the courtyards both taking the sky's last light and holding it a while after the streets have gone grey.

You could live a year on a single terrace and know its water the way you know a face — when it runs clear, when it carries peat-stain from the moor after storms, when it freezes at the edges in a winter snap and the whole district wakes to the soft ticking of ice against stone. You could live ten years in the city and never see its far side. It has no center, really. It has heights and watersheds, and where one watershed's channels begin to run the other way, people begin, almost imperceptibly, to speak of themselves as belonging to the other town — though it is the same unbroken stone under the same moving clouds, all the way to where the roofs thin, the boardwalks end in marsh, and the high wet grassland takes over again without a wall to mark the place.
