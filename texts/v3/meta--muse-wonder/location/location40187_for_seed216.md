It begins with the absence of ground.

Very high up, where the mountain ranges have thinned into cloud and warm wind, there is no rock left to stand on. There may have been rock once, and soil, and ordinary slopes. If so, they are gone so completely that no one finds them anymore, not even in the deepest diggings. What is there instead stretches from horizon to horizon, continent-wide, swaying almost imperceptibly: a world made by hand, and then remade by growing.

It is a mat. It is a thousand mats. It is a lattice of root, vine, cane, and woven stem lashed together over uncountable generations, suspended between distant peaks like a hammock too large for its anchors to be seen from any one place. From far below — and almost nothing lives far below — it would look like a green underside to the sky, dark and dripping. From above, it looks like marshland floating in air.

Everything is built, and everything built is alive.

The builders used, and still use, almost nothing that could be called technology. Stone adzes. Bone needles. Knives of sharpened shell and flaked river glass kept long after the rivers that made them were lost. Rope twisted from bark fiber on the thigh. Fire, but only in small clay bowls, to harden the tips of stakes. With these they did not so much construct the place as persuade it. They planted pillars and pruned them into towers. They braided saplings across ravines and waited twenty years for a bridge to thicken. They hollowed giant gourds while the gourds were still growing on the vine, inserting carved wooden plugs so the fruit swelled around a doorway, a window, a sleeping alcove.

Then the plants learned the shapes, and began to repeat them on their own.

That is the first wrongness, and after a while it stops seeming wrong. A house cut down will return in the same place next spring, a little greener, its doorway a little lower, as if remembered by the root system. A path no one has walked in a lifetime will clear itself of leaf-fall before festival season. Staircases grow where feet expect them — not always, but often enough that the old people test empty air with a toe before trusting a new route, and sometimes feel a young, springy step forming under their weight, rushing to meet them.

The mat itself is old beyond erosion. Where water has run in the same channel for a thousand years, the woven stems have mineralized into something between wood and stone, grooved and silvered. Handrails are polished by palms. Bowl-hollows worn into living trunks hold black, still water. Whole districts have settled into their final sag, their own balance of weight and growth and rot, and creak only in the strongest updrafts — and even then, barely. Nothing here echoes. The felted moss that covers every surface drinks sound the way it drinks rain. Footfalls are felt through the soles and through the vine-work underfoot long before they are heard. Voices arrive softened, close, as if spoken through wet cloth.

It is dim even at noon.

Three canopies stand between the marsh-surface and the sun. The highest is made of umbrella trees whose trunks rise a hundred meters from the mat and open into interlocking leaves big as houses. Beneath them, a second, broken layer of feather-palms. Beneath those, the hanging gardens. Light comes down altered: green, brown-green, gold where a gap lets a shaft of mist-brightness fall. Most of the day is a warm twilight. The air is close and smells of sap, wet bark, crushed herbs, and the faint iron smell of the bowl-water. It condenses on skin. It condenses on leaves and runs down them in beads. Clothes never fully dry; they are made, sensibly, of woven reed and oiled fiber that do not mind.

Water is everywhere but rarely moves fast. Rain falls from the real sky perhaps once a day, warm and brief, but there is a second, local weather underneath the canopies: evaporation rises from the thousands of marsh-bowls grown into the mat — broad leaf-cisterns, cupped trunk-tops, artificial ponds lined with clay and root — until it meets the cool underside of the high leaves, condenses, and drips back down. A perpetual interior rain, fine as mist in some districts, heavy as a shower in others, cycling forever. Some of the drips fall upward. Not many. Just enough that experienced travelers do not comment on it, and catch the upward water in their mouths as it passes. Seeds, too, sometimes loosen from their pods and drift up into the canopy, and the children are taught that planting is simply a matter of encouraging most of them to come back down.

For all the totality of the vegetation — plant as floor, wall, roof, boat, bridge, soil itself in the form of compost packed into woven baskets — the place is profoundly empty.

The living structures are vast and the spaces between them are vaster. A tower-tree might be hollow through its entire height, a cathedral of smooth inner bark with a single family living in a wart-like swelling halfway up, and nothing else for a vertical half-kilometer except swallows' nests and hanging moss. Villages of twenty gourd-houses hang miles from the next village, connected by a single swaying bridge-line that takes two days to cross, the mat falling away on either side into luminous green distance and cloud. You can stand on a high pruned platform at dawn and see weather forming in the middle distance — a rain-veil moving slowly across the bowls, a pocket of sun opening — and see no other human habitation at all, only architecture continuing to the curve of visibility: grown spires, woven causeways, terraced leaf-fields, the occasional sail of a great white flower turning to follow a sun it cannot see.

And yet it teems.

The fauna is abundant and almost entirely quiet. This seems to be an old agreement. Things that made loud noises here vibrated the young bridges, attracted the mat-wasps, startled the grazers mid-step on narrow ways — whatever the reason, selection or custom, the living world has gone soft-voiced. There are frogs the size of dogs in the bowl-marshes, iridescent and patient, who communicate by inflating colored throat-sacs in sequence, a conversation of silent pulses. There are grazing beetles as big as oxen who prune the outer edges of the mat, their chewing felt as a faint tremor for miles. Moths with wings like wet paper drift between the canopy layers at dusk, hundreds of thousands of them, shedding a faint green dust that is luminous for a few seconds after it lands. Gliding lizards, membrane-winged, hunt them without a sound. Herds of long-legged, long-necked browsers move along the wider causeways in single file, placing each foot with ceremonial care, their bells — because the herders do hang bells on them — stuffed with moss.

The predators are quietest of all. You learn of the hunting cats not by sound but by behavior: the moths suddenly all drifting one way, the frogs' sacs going dark in sequence from a point outward, your own skin prickling as the fine hairs on the mat's surface, which orient to warmth, lean toward something behind you.

Below the mat is the Underlooming, and no one builds there by preference. To go below is to climb down through the root-tangle into a hanging forest growing upside-down toward the cloud-sea, its leaves pale, its fruit falling endlessly into the white. Things live down there that have adapted to the inverted fall of light and the sideways gravity near the great anchor trunks — for near a massive, living substrate, *down* is partly a matter of opinion, and a dropped tool may slide slantwise through the air and come to rest against a pillar rather than falling clean. Old builders tie everything. Old builders tie themselves, loosely, even asleep on wide floors, out of habit older than fear.

Seasons here are processes, not dates. There is the Pruning, when human and beetle together cut back the year's exuberant overgrowth before it can weight the mat dangerously, and the whole world smells of green juice and the bridges sing faintly underfoot with released tension. There is the Flowering, when the high canopy opens all at once for nine days and the dim world fills with drifting pollen and a light almost like morning, and every bowl of water is filmed with gold. There is the Long Sway, when winds from below the cloud-sea set the entire continent moving in a slow, oceanic rhythm, and people in distant districts, who will never meet, time their planting and their marriages and their deaths to the same shared motion, feeling it in their knees.

And there is the Repair, which is not a season but the permanent condition. Somewhere the mat is always rotting through. Somewhere a grown house is always splitting its seams with new growth. Young people walk the lines with stone adzes and coils of fresh fiber, listening — not with ears, but with the bones of their feet — for the dull place, the place where the great woven world has gone thin over the abyss of warm cloud.

They cut the dead weave out. They plant the new. They lash, and wait, and while they wait they hang their hammocks over emptiness a mile deep, in the green dusk, among the silent frogs and the upward rain, on a continent that no stone supports and no map finishes, because the edges are still being grown.
