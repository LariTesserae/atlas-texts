There is no ground here that was not laid by hand.

You can dig anywhere — through the dark planting soil, through the wet clay under it, through the layer of crushed shell and charcoal beneath that — and you will not find earth that belongs to itself. You find another floor. Baskets woven tight as brick. Mats of reed pressed and fired. A pavement of broad, unglazed tiles, still holding the handprints of whoever set them. Below that, soil again, but soil that was carried in, sifted, mixed with ash and dung and river silt in exact proportions. Below that, another pavement, upside-down, with an older garden growing from its underside into a cistern that has no bottom you can reach.

It goes on like that. Hills are hollow. Meadows are roofs. The low rise in the distance, soft with grass and a single flowering tree, is a kiln-house underneath, or a granary, or a covered reservoir where black fish turn slowly in water that has never seen the sun and yet is somehow lit.

The whole country is low — scarcely a man's height between its highest terrace and its lowest canal — and it is built at the scale of a small valley, a town spread out until town and landscape became indistinguishable. From any of the raised walkways you can see across it: districts of gardens, courtyards, ponds, and little stepped pyramids of planted earth, repeating until the brightness eats the detail.

It is always noon.

The light does not come from a point. It comes from everywhere above a certain height, white and shadowless at first glance, until you look down and find your shadow directly under your feet, small and dense as a second sole. There are no mornings. Condensation forms on the undersides of leaves at noon. Water beads on cold copper eaves at noon. The heat does not shimmer or move; it simply rests on the skin, constant, damp, intimate as breath inside a greenhouse.

And it is wet, in the way a place is wet after rain that never quite happened. The canals hold water to the precise lip of their stone edging, neither rising nor falling. Lotus ponds are bright mirrors freckled with green. Water weeps steadily from the joints of the aqueducts — bamboo troughs lashed with fiber, carried on wooden trestles across roofs and courtyards — but the level in the troughs never drops, and the puddles below never spread. Paths of pale lime plaster are darkened with moisture. Moss makes emerald seams in every wall. If you touch a post, a leaf, a rope railing, your hand comes away damp.

Yet nothing flows. That is one of the quiet wrongnesses. The drops fall from the aqueducts, but they make no rings in the ponds. The canal water has a current if you trail your fingers in it — you can feel it, cool against the wrist — but a floating petal placed on the surface will stay where you put it, turning very slowly, for hours.

The vegetation is lush to the point of insistence. Taro with leaves broad enough to shelter under. Papyrus in green fans. Vines loaded with gourds pouring over trellises. Flowering trees with canopies shaped, over generations, into perfect domes. Bananas, sugarcane, reeds, creeping groundcovers stitched into patterns along the edges of the plazas. Every growing thing was planted: in rows, in rings, in spirals cut into the stepped earthworks, in hanging baskets under the eaves, in the shallow soil of the rooftops. Nothing here volunteered. Even the weeds, when you look closely, are the same weed, in the places weeds would be expected, spaced like an ornament.

But for all that green, the place is sparse. One tree to a courtyard big enough for fifty. One garden pavilion at the far end of a long, empty terrace of sun-blasted clay. A single heron standing in a pond built for a hundred herons. The distances between things feel deliberate, merciful — as if whoever made this world was afraid of crowding it, and left more emptiness than presence, more bright plaza than shade.

The fauna made the opposite decision. Life crowds the water and the air. Dragonflies hang over every canal, blue and red and hammered-gold, so many that their wings make a faint visual grain. Frogs sit in ranks along the pond rims, throats pulsing. Turtles stack on warm stones. Fish rise under the lotus leaves, mouths opening at the surface. White cranes pick their way through the taro. Bees work the flowering vines. Water buffalo stand belly-deep in the flooded paddies, chewing, their hides shining, their horns hung with little woven charms. Butterflies drift across the empty courtyards in twos and threes, always in twos and threes.

Almost none of it makes a sound.

The frogs' throats swell and empty in silence. The bees pass your ear without a hum. A crane may open its beak and stretch its neck in the posture of a cry, and some pressure will touch your ribs, below hearing, and pass. The buffalo shift in the water with no splash. Your own footsteps on the wooden walkways are softened, as if the boards were wrapped in wet cloth. Now and then a drop falls somewhere far off and you hear it several seconds later, small and clear, in the wrong direction.

The tools that made all this are still lying about, and they are simple things. Stone adzes. Wooden mallets polished by palms. Baskets, rope, bone needles, copper knives gone green, clay pipes, shell hoes. There is no iron to speak of, no wheel ruts, no engine noise under the world. The aqueducts are lashed, not nailed. The walls are mud brick and lime, the roofs thatch and split bamboo, the bridges planks laid over barrels and stone piers. Everything was done at the speed of hands — which means the scale of it slowly becomes impossible to hold in the mind. How many hands. How many lifetimes of carrying soil up onto a roof that was itself carried, basket by basket, onto another roof.

And the work is fresh. That is the other wrongness. Edges are sharp. The corners of the stepped gardens could cut you. The carved drainage mouths — fish, frogs, open hands — have not had their features softened by a single season of water. Paint is bright on the plaster: red ochre, charcoal black, a blue made from crushed shell and plant ash that should fade in this sun within weeks. It has not faded. The thatch has not silvered. The rope has not frayed. A basket left half-woven on a bench still smells of cut reed.

Nothing erodes here. Nothing rots at the proper rate. Cut a banana leaf and return the next day — at noon, always noon — and the cut is exactly as you left it, neither browned nor healed. Break a tile, if you can bring yourself to break one, and look away, and look back: the tile is whole, but your memory of breaking it remains, and somehow the unbroken tile is worse for that. Fruit does not drop and spoil. It hangs ripe, at the same ripeness, until it is picked — and where it was picked, after a while when you are not watching that particular branch, there is fruit again. Not growing. There.

The world maintains itself by conforming to itself. That seems to be its deepest rule, deeper than water or gravity. The canals stay full because canals here are full. The gardens are trimmed in the shapes they were first cut into because those are the shapes of gardens. Fish swim in the rooftop channels at the same level, somehow, as fish in the ground-level ponds, and if you follow a channel as it climbs a terrace in a series of pretty clay steps, you will not be able to say where the water decided to flow uphill, only that upstairs and downstairs have become politely confused. A path that goes around a pavilion may bring you back to the pavilion from inside it. Your shadow, small at your feet on the open plaza, stretches long and thin in the narrow alleys between storehouses, as if the sun remembered evenings in enclosed places.

Even the sky is involved in the making. High above the heat there is a whiteness that is not cloud. On very clear days — all days are clear — you can see faint seams in it, a vast woven regularity, and places where the light is warmer, as if coming through oiled cloth. Whether it is a roof at an impossible height or the inside of something else, it does not move, and it does not dim, and no bird flies up far enough to test it. The birds fly in circuits, low over the water, from garden to garden, in patterns that repeat. The cranes cross the same courtyard at intervals, in the same numbers, casting the same brief shade.

There are houses, but few, and they too are gardens: walls open on all sides, floors raised against the wet, roofs deep with hanging plants, clay ovens, sleeping platforms, stored seed in painted jars. Bowls are set out. Sandals wait by doors. Someone has recently swept, folded, repaired, fed. Someone is implied by everything and encountered almost nowhere — now and then a figure far across a paddy, bending; now and then, rounding a corner, the warmth of a cookfire in an oven whose fire makes no smoke and does not consume its wood.

Stay long enough and your sense of time thins out, not unpleasantly. Hunger comes and goes without urgency. Thirst is constant and mild, answered by water that tastes of clay and green stems. The heat never becomes a storm. The wet never becomes a flood. Abundance never becomes decay. It is a world that finished becoming itself and then, politely, stopped — a made valley holding its breath at the brightest hour, full of silent animals and dripping leaves, its paths warm underfoot, its empty plazas shining, its unfinished baskets waiting forever for hands that have just stepped away.
