It starts as a flaw in white ground.

Not a cave mouth, exactly. A sink. A place where the frozen plain has sighed and dropped, maybe thirty paces across, and in dropping has revealed that it was never solid underneath. You go down not by climbing but by being lowered through layers — first wind-scoured snow, hard as fired clay, then blue ice full of trapped air, then a band that is neither ice nor soil, a dark, warm-smelling sponge that gives under your gloves.

And then your boots do not find ground. They find root.

Everything below that line is root, leaf, stem, frond, and the wet that moves through them. There is no floor in the ordinary sense, no exposed stone, no dirt to kick. The pit is lined, floored, roofed in part by living mass that has grown so thick over itself that plant has become geology. Trunks as thick as your thigh are woven sideways. Smaller roots braid into rope, then into mat, then into a spongy membrane that trembles. Leaves pave the gaps. Moss fills the seams in the moss. If you push a hand down, forearm deep, you do not reach an end to it — only wetter, colder, denser growth, knotted around things that might once have been separate plants and are now just places in the one continuous body.

It is below the world, and it is cold in a way that has direction.

Above, the cold was weather. Here it radiates. The plants give off cold the way a stove gives off heat, slow and steady from their cores. Frost feathers out along every vein. Leaves are rimed white at the edges but supple in the middle, bending without snapping, their surfaces beaded with brine that will not freeze. Your breath does not rise. It falls in a cloud and settles into the low places between roots, where it is immediately harvested — you can watch the nearest fronds uncurl a fraction, their undersides opening into thousands of hair-fine mouths that drink the vapor before it can ice.

Water is everywhere, but it never gets to be a stream. It weeps. It condenses out of air that is already saturated and drops, patiently, from leaf-tip to leaf-tip. It travels up stems as often as down, in clear beads that climb against gravity along grooves that pulse once, then wait a long time before pulsing again. It pools in cupped leaves black as glass. In those pools things live and look up. When you lean over one, the surface does not reflect you immediately. A second passes. Then your face assembles, a little displaced, as if the water has considered it first.

The light is low and it has no source. A dim blue-green suffusion, brightest near the center of the pit where the canopy of interlaced branches fails to close completely and a column of overcast sky shows, far and small. Elsewhere the illumination comes from the plants themselves, but reluctantly: a vein lighting along a stem, going out; a cluster of translucent berries that brighten when something moves near them and fade when it stops; patches of lichen that glow only on their far side, so you see halos around edges but never the lit thing itself. Your eyes adjust and keep adjusting. Shadows do not stay still. They lag.

And it is loud. That is the first wrongness that becomes a rule.

For a place so cold, so slow, you expect muffling. Instead sound is amplified, layered, near-touching like the growth. Drips strike leaves engineered — no, grown — into taut drums, each leaf a different pitch, and the pit is raining internally at all times. Roots creak under their own tension, a wooden groaning that travels around the bowl and comes back to you from behind. Ice forming inside hollow stems expands and sings, high thin notes that last too long. Under it all is a hum you feel first in your molars and in the small bones of your feet through the root-mat: a cycling, almost electrical thrum that rises and falls over minutes, not seconds.

The fauna makes most of it.

They are abundant beyond reason for the temperature. Translucent frogs the size of thumbnails sit in the leaf-pools, their hearts visible, calling not with croaks but with clicks that synchronize across the pit until whole quadrants are ticking together like clocks agreeing. Pale moths, winged in frost that they never shed, blunder heavily between fronds and make a soft clattering when they collide. Worm-things move through the root braid, segmented and warm to the touch — startlingly warm, little furnaces — and as they pass they leave a temporary thaw behind them that other things rush, slowly, to use. Beetles carry glowing mites. Mites carry smaller things still. Birds, or things that have taken the bird shape for this place, do not fly so much as fall controllably from the upper weave to the lower, calling once on the way down with voices far too large for their bodies. Their call comes back to you from the walls before they have finished opening their beaks.

Nothing here moves fast. That is the second rule.

A frond that began to recoil from your shadow when you entered is still recoiling now. An insect midway through taking off has been midway for some time, wings beating once, hanging, beating again, the sound of each beat separate and enormous. Even the drips hesitate at leaf-edges, swelling, swelling, considering. Your own movements begin to adopt it without your permission. You reach and find you have been reaching for a while. The cold is not only cold. The grove has a tempo and it imposes it. Chemistry made patient. Processes drawn out across durations that make them visible.

That is the technology of it, if technology is the right word. Nothing here is built. There is no artifact, no casing, no cut edge, no seam that was made by a tool. But nothing here is wild in the old sense either.

These plants do things current growth should not do. Their root-mat is superconducting in the cold it maintains for itself, and thought — if it is thought — moves through it without loss, without heat, instantaneously from one side of the pit to the other while the bodies that carry it grow at a glacial creep. Leaves fold before contact, not in response to touch but a breath earlier; the whole weave reroutes a footpath around your boot before you weight it. Sap carries more than sugar. In the clearest stems you can see packets moving, bright motes in train, sorting themselves at junctions, pausing, recombining — information made liquid and pumped by a heart that is the whole plant. The berries that light when you near them are not warning you off. They are reading you. The frost on a broad leaf by your knee resolves, as you watch, into branching diagrams, into patterns that repeat at smaller and smaller scales until the pattern fails and reforms into something that looks, for a moment, like the vascular system of a lung. Then a frog clicks and it shivers back to frost.

It is a machine that was never assembled. It was seeded, perhaps, or it seeded itself from instructions carried in cellulose, a slow computer for incubating worlds, grown down here where the cold keeps it coherent and the dark keeps it undisturbed. Or it is a nursery. The geology around it is still embryonic — no true stone yet, only mineral precipitating in soft curds among the roots, proto-stalactites that are still jelly, mud that has not decided to be clay. The pit is not eroding. It is accreting. The plants are precipitating a cave around themselves, molecule by molecule, teaching water where to leave its load, teaching cold where to pool. In a thousand years there might be walls. In ten thousand, a cavern system. At the center of it, kept dim and wet and humming, an ecology already complete before its container exists.

The ecology is closed, and tightening. The warm worms eat fallen leaf-matter and excrete heat and ammonia; the fronds drink both. The frogs eat mites that eat the luminous lichen; their clicking at certain frequencies shakes loose spores that would otherwise never fall in the still air. The moths, dusted with engineered pollen that repairs itself as it travels, blunder because blundering covers more ground than precision. Everything eats light that is not light, heat that is given off by living, sound that is made by dripping. Your presence has already been entered into it. The mat under you is slightly warmer. A dozen hair-roots have found the seam of your boot. Not aggressively. Curiously. Probing the possibility that you might be a new kind of weather.

Stay long enough and the loudness resolves. It is not noise. The drips are not random; they keep time against the hum. The frog-clicks entrain to the drips. The root-creaks answer at intervals that feel like grammar. The grove is talking along its own length faster than its bodies can move, and the sound is the overflow — the part of the conversation that has to become vibration because the rest is moving through frozen root at speeds sound cannot approach. You are standing inside a sentence being said very slowly and very loudly at once.

Near the far wall — if wall is the word for a vertical tangle of stems braced against unformed mud — there is a hollow where the growth parts around nothing. No plant enters a space roughly the shape and size of a curled sleeper. The air there is coldest. The sound there is clearest, the hum almost a tone you could match with your voice. Condensation forms in that hollow without a surface to form on, beads hanging in empty air in a loose, shifting lattice, holding their positions, drifting a millimeter a minute. Things are being kept there, in potentia. Seeds, perhaps. Or fauna not yet needed. Or the next version of the grove. The lattice rearranges when you look away.

You should not stay until you slow to its full tempo. You can feel the invitation of it, in your joints, in the growing deliberateness of your attention. The grove would keep you well. It would drink your breath and return filtered air scented faintly of resin and ozone. It would cushion you in root that knows your shape before you settle. It would take a very long time to decide what you were for, and that deciding would be comfortable, and loud with dripping and ticking and humming, and dim, and green. Other small warm things would come, in their slow way, to investigate the thaw of you.

Going back up, you pass again through the sponge band, through the blue ice, and the wind on the white plain seems crude and fast and silent by comparison, though it takes you some time, afterward, to trust the speed of your own hands.
