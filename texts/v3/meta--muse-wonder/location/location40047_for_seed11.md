It is the top of an old mountain range that has forgotten it was ever jagged.

You come up for days through valleys and broken slopes, expecting a summit, and instead the world levels itself out. At four, five thousand meters — high enough that your lungs work thin and your footsteps feel light and wrong — the land stops rising and simply *continues*. A high, warm, worn-down plateau, extending past any one view, ridge after soft ridge to the horizon.

Nothing up here is sharp anymore. Erosion has had so long to work that it has finished its work. The hills are loaves. The exposed rock is rounded, polished, humped out of the soil like the backs of buried animals. Even loose stones have no edges; the wind and grit and ancient rain have rubbed them into eggs, discs, smooth bowls. There is no talus, no cliff that looks newly fallen. This is a landscape in its final form, settled, almost flattened despite its extreme height, and it will not change in a human lifetime.

The air above it never stops changing at all.

The wind is the first fact, and after an hour it is the only fact you can hear. It does not gust so much as it *boils* — a continuous, deafening roar pouring from the west, tearing over the plateau, dropping, lifting, reversing in eddies around every low dome. It screams in your ears, flaps clothing until the seams sting your skin, makes conversation a matter of shouting mouth-to-ear and still losing half the words. Loose pebbles chatter. Grass whips and hisses. In the lee of larger rocks it whistles at a higher pitch, almost musical, and then a fresh blast smothers it in white noise again. At night it does not diminish. It shakes felt, rattles stone on stone, and you wake with a ringing in your head that is not silence but its afterimage.

Because of the wind, everything living keeps low.

And a surprising amount lives. Between the bare, wind-scoured rises, the ground is lush — not with trees, there are none upright, but with a dense, springy, knee-high ocean of tussock grass, cushion moss, and low herbs jammed together against the ground. The grass is emerald and gold in patches, bending in long travelling waves so the whole plateau appears to be moving sideways like water. In hollows it grows thicker, tangled with tiny white and purple flowers that open only a centimeter above the soil. The moss holds water like a sponge; press it and it darkens and weeps, even on a dry day. Lichen paints the sheltered side of boulders orange and pale green, in maps centuries old.

It is damp here, but never properly wet. Rain arrives as squalls — you see them coming for miles as grey veils racing across the openness — and hits sideways for three, ten minutes, needling exposed skin, turning the shallow pans brimful and frantic, and then it is gone, blown off the edge of the plateau, sun again. Most water lives in pockets. Black peat bogs in level basins where your boots sink and sigh. Mirror-thin lakes with no inlet you can find, fed by night condensation and hidden seepage, their surfaces constantly roughened to spray at one end. Streams that start in a moss-bed, run bright for a hundred meters over smooth stone, and vanish into cracks. In the morning, everything is beaded: grass, stone, wool, metal left out overnight runs with dew wrung from the racing clouds that sometimes sink and drag their bellies directly across the ground. By noon the wind has drunk it again.

It is warm, strangely warm for such a height — equatorial sun, thin clear light falling straight and honest and heating the dark soil through the wind. Your face is hot in the sun and cold in a blast at the same time. Steam rises off wet grass after a squall. The smell is of warm peat, crushed herbs, wet stone, sheep dung.

Fauna handles this place by being scarce and transient. A hare the color of dry grass, flattened ears, gone before you register it. Ground-nesting birds that do not so much fly as allow themselves to be thrown and then fight their way down to their nests again. A single huge, broad-winged scavenger on some afternoons, hanging absolutely motionless a thousand feet up in the gale as if nailed to the sky, then gone. Insects appear in the calm pockets behind rocks — fat bees, bewildered butterflies tumbling end over end. Most days you walk for hours and see nothing move that isn't grass, cloud-shadow, or water being ripped into mist.

Human presence is scattered to the point of rumor. Every few valleys — and on this scale that might be half a day apart — there is a mark: a cairn of smooth stones stacked man-high and humming faintly when the wind finds the gaps between them. A windbreak, two walls in a V, built of unmortared rock and turf, opening away from the west. A shepherd's hut so low you mistake it for another hump of ground until you are on it: round stone walls, a roof of sod and felt weighted down with boulders and rope nets staked deep, door barely waist-high, smoke flattened instantly and dragged away in a ribbon. Inside, the roar drops to a muffled buffeting, and there are the few tools this altitude allows — wooden bowls, a hand loom, iron shears gone bright at the edge, stone weights, braided grass rope, a fire of dung and root peat that burns hot and quick.

People up here, when they come seasonally with their hard, shaggy flocks, move like the plants: bent, anchored, efficient. They weight hems with small stones sewn in. They tie hats under the chin, tie tools to wrists, tie children, lightly, to themselves in the worst passes. They communicate up close, foreheads nearly touching, or with arm signals that have grown elaborate in compensation, or, between distant huts on still-er edges of storms, with drums whose thump travels through the ground better than voice travels through air. Nothing unsecured is ever set down. A forgotten cup can be found, in pieces, a valley away.

The strangeness accumulates slowly. The old, worn stones sing — not metaphorically; certain hollowed boulders, ground into flutes by ten thousand years of grit-wind, produce steady tones, each rock a different note, so that walking through a field of them on a strong day is walking through a broken, endless chord you feel in your teeth. On some slopes the grass grows in perfect spirals around stones, combed that way by rotating eddies that return year after year to the same spot. Sometimes the wind brings a voice — a shout, a bell from a flock — impossibly clear from over a ridge you know to be empty, carried compressed along a layer of air, and then cuts it off mid-sound. Dust devils are common in the hot part of the day, pale columns wandering the basins, picking up grass seed and spray and small shining insects and depositing them, inexplicably sorted, in drifts. Cloud shadows race you at terrifying speed, cold as water, and pass.

Geologically, nothing is happening. Meteorologically, everything is, all the time. Clouds boil up from the unseen lowlands at the plateau's edge in the afternoon, spill over, shred, reform. A basin that was a lake in the morning is a glittering mudflat cracked with white salt by evening, wind having worried the water away as spray. The tussocks rebuild after every flattening blast within minutes, springing upright, shaking off rain. Even the light seems turbulent at ground level, broken by moving grass and blowing mist into a constant shimmer.

It does not feel empty because it is lifeless. It feels empty because everything in it — hut, cairn, flock, bird, walker — is so far from the next thing, and so small under a sky that takes up three-quarters of the world, and because between presences there are miles of just grass, stone, wind going about its deafening work on a surface too old to be changed by it anymore.
