You hear it long before you are high enough to see it.

Not as a sound arriving, but as a pain settling behind the teeth, a pressure in the sinuses, a vibration in the loose cartilage of your ears. Below, in the last valleys where there is still soil deep enough for shrubs, the herders stop talking at a certain cairn-line — not because of reverence, exactly. Because talking stops being useful. They point instead, tap two fingers against the ground, against the sternum, against each other's wrists. They fit themselves with plugs worked from greased wool and resin and wadded grass, and slit-goggles of polished bone, and they go up the rest of the way breathing through their mouths, in the thin air, deaf already by choice.

Above that line the world opens and does not fill in.

It is a high saddle on the equator — that is part of its strangeness and part of its sameness — a broad, shallow bowl of pale rock slung between peaks at an altitude where the body never quite stops protesting. Five and a half, six thousand meters up, where the air has less than half its proper weight. The heart works all the time. The blood sings thinner. A cut clots slow. Nosebleeds come without warning and dry almost instantly to a rust crust in the nostril.

And because it is equatorial, it does not change its mind. The day is always twelve hours. The sun climbs nearly straight up, hammers down nearly straight. The cold is always the same cold — not winter cold, not a freeze that comes and goes, but a permanent, sunlit chill. In direct light your face burns in minutes, cracks at the lip, blisters at the nose. In the shadow of a single boulder your fingers go stupid with numbness. There are no seasons to wait for. No thaw that transforms it. No migration of weather. The same wind has been pouring over the same notch in the eastern rim, without pause, for longer than this range has had its present shape. It pours today exactly as it did ten thousand years ago.

That wind is the place.

The eastern rim is broken by a gorge perhaps two hundred meters wide, cut into black and rust-red columnar rock, and through ten million years of abrasion the gorge has been fluted, bored, undercut. Wind from the wet lowlands on the other side is funneled up, compressed, accelerated, and forced through that throat at a constant, screaming velocity. It does not gust. Gusting would be a kind of relief. It sustains.

On the plateau it is deafening in the literal, physiological sense. Unprotected ears are damaged in an hour, ruined in a day. Speech, even shouted speech at arm's length, does not exist; the sound takes words apart behind the teeth before they leave the mouth. Whistles, drums, horns — none of them carry. The noise is not one note but a chord under pressure: a low roar you feel mostly in the ribs and the soles of the feet, a mid-range howl that makes loose teeth ache, and a continuous high shriek off the sharpest edges that seems to come from inside the skull rather than from any direction. Fine dust hangs in visible standing ripples near the gorge mouth, not moving forward, just trembling in place. Small dry pebbles on flat slabs jitter, settle, jitter again into the same patterns, day after day.

And yet, because it never varies, everything here has grown up inside it the way reef life grows up inside a current.

The bowl itself is vast and mostly empty. From rim to rim perhaps twenty kilometers of wind-polished granite, pumice fields the color of ash and old bone, scree slopes sorted by the constant blast into long, immaculate tails behind every larger stone. You can walk for an hour and pass nothing taller than your ankle. The light at this height, under that sun, with almost no moisture and no dust high enough to soften it, is merciless and clarifying. Shadows are not grey; they are black cuts. Distance deceives. A cliff that looks an hour away is a day. A bird that looks close is enormous and far. The sky overhead is not blue so much as a deep, hard violet at the zenith, bleaching to an almost white glare at the horizon. At noon there is practically nowhere to hide from it.

There is no free water to speak of, and the place is not dry in the simple way a desert is dry. It is damp in pockets, in moments, in skins. Night — brief, cold, just as windy, the stars whetted to points by the thin air — lays a rime of frost over the lee sides of stones and the hollows of the rock. Two hours after sunrise that rime sweats, beads, runs a few centimeters, gathers in natural cups and solution pits no bigger than a saucer, a hand, a cooking pot. By early afternoon they are gone. Some of the deeper pits, shaded all day inside cracks on the southern walls of the gorge, never fully dry; they hold a permanent black slick of moisture, a skin of ice at their centers even under the equatorial sun, rimmed with salt and brilliant green algae. Condensation forms on anything metal, on teeth if you sleep with your mouth open, on the windward side of your clothes while the other side stays bone dry.

The plants live entirely by that stolen half-hour of water. They are sparse to the point of insult. Cushion plants no wider than a dinner plate, dense as felt, hugging pits where the wind is fractionally less; they may be a century old and show only a dusting of tiny white flowers at their centers, flowers that never open fully but are pried at by insects from the side. Rosettes with leaves like grey leather, edged in frost. Lichens everywhere but never lush — thin crusts of sulfur yellow, burnt orange, soot black, following the damp tracks down rock faces in maps that repeat exactly, year to year. In the stillest hollows, a grass the color of straw grows in single tufts, all leaning one way, permanently, as if combed. Nothing here tries to be tall. Tall things are torn, desiccated, and deafened to death, if a plant can be deafened: the vibration alone shakes pollen loose before it can set, shakes soil out of any crack that isn't already locked. So the vegetation stays pressed to the stone, in the stone, patient beyond any human scale.

At first, because of the emptiness, you think it is barren of animals. Then the brightness adjusts, or your hearing — your loss of hearing — adjusts, and you realize the plateau is busy almost to franticness, but distributed the way stars are distributed: colonies of density separated by huge, lifeless gulfs.

The cliffs inside the gorge are honeycombed with swifts. Tens of thousands of them, soot-dark with white throats, nesting in cracks where their eggs are incubated as much by the constant vibration of the rock as by their bodies. They do not call. Nothing up here calls. They communicate by flashing — banking hard in the screaming air, which they ride better than any other living thing, showing the sudden white underside of a wing in patterns. They pour out at dawn not to feed so much as to harvest what the wind itself has brought up from the lowlands in the night: insects, seeds, spiders ballooning on silk, entire plankton of the air, stunned and delivered. Below the colonies the rock is streaked white and, in the lee, thick with beetles living off the droppings, blind cave-pale beetles that navigate by the tremor of the stone under their feet.

On the open floor, vizcacha-like rodents live in burrow systems under slab piles, emerging to sit upright in the sun with their ears folded permanently flat, sealed. They warn each other by thumping — you can feel a good thump through the sole of a boot from ten meters away, a language that goes through the ground because the air is unusable. Their predators, small rust-colored foxes with disproportionately thick fur and inner ears choked with wax and hair, hunt entirely by that same channel, chins low, listening through their jawbones. Lizards, black as the basalt, press themselves to sun-hot surfaces and go rigidly still until the shadow of a raptor crosses them; there are raptors in numbers that make no sense until you see the rodent colonies. Ground tyrants, caracaras, a great pale owl that hunts the bowl at dusk entirely by sight, its facial disc useless, its eyes enormous and forward.

Under almost every loose stone there is something. Scorpions, translucent. Centipedes. Spiders that have given up webs for vibration-sensitive trip lines of silk laid flat on rock. Moths that never fly by day but walk, wings folded, from one lichen patch to another. The little permanent pits of water are fringed at noon with a brief, furious ecology that assembles and disassembles daily: fairy shrimp waking in water that was ice at dawn, water beetles, the larvae of a midge whose adult life is twenty minutes of vibration-winged mating in the roar before desiccation. Animals here are born, for practical purposes, deaf, and unconcerned by it. Sound is not information. Sound is weather. Sound is the medium they have had to route around.

People — and there are people, sometimes — route around it the same way. No one builds here. There is nothing to build with that the wind wouldn't worry to pieces, and no way to coordinate labor when you cannot speak, and no reason to stay. The bowl is crossed, not occupied. Crossing parties appear at the southern rim with their slit-goggles and earplugs and their bundles lashed flat, led by guides who navigate by cairns that are not so much built structures as single distinguished stones, memorable boulders, a split rock shaped like a tooth. They carry everything in and everything out. Their tools are what the parameters of this place allow: hand-knapped obsidian from a flow a week's walk below, bone awls, wooden digging sticks hardened in fire far away, nets of woven grass for catching swifts at the gorge — a harvest done by feel, at night, hands in cracks, utterly silent. They gather lichen for dye in small quantities, gather swift feathers, gather the white droppings for fields far below. They leave nothing, not even footprints for more than a day; the wind sorts the dust back into its old patterns.

They also leave quickly. A night on the plateau is survivable. Two makes the ears ring even through plugs, makes the hands tremble at a frequency not your own, makes sleep shallow and full of the sense that the ground is trying to say something it will never finish saying. Three is dangerous. The body starts to sync its complaints to the vibration. Old men who guided too many crossings in their youth are stone deaf below the rim too, and can still tell you, by putting their palms flat on any rock anywhere in the cordillera, whether the wind is blowing on the high bowl. It always is.

That is the final rule of the place, and every other rule follows from it: it does not stop, and it does not change, and life here is not a struggle against an occasional hardship but a complete redesign around a permanent condition. The rock is worn smooth not by catastrophe but by constancy, worn into forms so settled they seem intended — flutes that will still be sounding in another ten thousand years, bowls in stone exactly the size of the frost that melts in them, ripples in sand that reform in the same places if you scuff them, because the wind that makes them has never once varied enough to make them anywhere else. Empty, bright, cold in the sun, loud beyond the meaning of loud, and crawling, flying, thumping with life in the narrow places where life could negotiate a truce.

Stand in the middle of the bowl at noon, plugged and goggled, and it has a purity nothing lower down can manage. Your shadow is a small black puddle at your feet. You cannot hear your own breathing. You can see fifty kilometers of polished stone, a colony of birds flashing white a mile away like signals from a ship, a single fox crossing an acre of scree by ground-tremor and sunlight. The water in a rock cup beside your boot trembles without pause, throwing a tiny, perfect, permanent ripple against its rim.
