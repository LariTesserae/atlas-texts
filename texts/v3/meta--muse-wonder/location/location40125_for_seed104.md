It begins as a sea where there should not be a sea.

High up — far above the cloud-line of the world below, above the weather that belongs to other people — the old mountains have been worn almost entirely away. What is left is not peaks but a continent-sized hollow, smooth as a bowl licked clean by ten thousand years of water. And the bowl is full.

Not a lake. Lakes have shores you can understand. This has horizons.

You come to it from below, if you come at all, by climbing through warm cloud for days, through forests that get shorter and wetter and darker as you rise, the trees pressing closer together, their leaves turning black-green to drink what little light passes through the vapor. The air gets thin and then, impossibly, it gets thick again. It gets wet inside your lungs. The path, where there is a path — old stone steps cut so long ago their edges have rounded into ripples — goes up and up and then simply continues under.

There is no beach. There is a place where the mist becomes rain and the rain becomes mouthfuls and then you are not breathing wet air anymore, you are breathing something that decides, moment by moment, not to drown you.

The Upper Sea does that. In its uppermost layer, for perhaps a hundred feet down, the water is so full of air — of exhaled cloud, of bubbles trapped from below, of foam that never quite breaks — that it holds you like a hand. You can open your mouth and inhale and feel it go in warm as bathwater, heavy, tasting of minerals and leaf-rot, and your chest will rise. Below that, it forgets to be kind.

Light here is always late afternoon, even at noon. From above, the sky over the high plateau is bright and close, stars pricking through blue at midday at the rim. But the sea shades itself. It is dense, not with filth but with life and suspended matter: veils of plankton like green dust, long curtains of algae, fallen pollen kept from sinking for years by slow currents, tiny pale seeds ticking against your skin. The sun enters as columns, slanting, olive and amber, and loses itself after a few hundred feet in a perpetual dusk. Further down it is blue-black, and the plants stop pretending to need the sun and begin to need other things — heat, minerals, each other.

Because it is warm all the way down. Unnaturally, evenly warm. At the surface it steams in the cold high winds, sending up towers of vapor that become the clouds that hide it from below. Deeper, where the eroded floor lies — and it does lie, somewhere, a drowned continent of domed hills no higher than houses, of river valleys softened into mere suggestions, of stone so old it has the texture of wet clay and will take the print of your palm and keep it for an hour before smoothing it away — warm vents mutter in cracks. Not erupting. Breathing. The whole sea is kept at blood temperature by the earth remembering it was once molten.

The vegetation has taken that warmth and run mad with it in the quiet. There are no waves here to tear things apart, only tides that move like slow thought. Twice a day the entire sea inhales, rising hundreds of feet to swallow the stilt-forests at its rim, and exhales, stranding miles of shining meadow that steam and drip and are underwater again before they can dry. In the shallows near the rim, trees grow down from rafts of matted root and up from the floor at the same time until they meet in the middle and confuse themselves. Their trunks are swollen, their bark gone soft and spongy, their leaves huge and translucent with water held inside the leaf itself. They make forests you swim through rather than walk through, corridors of shadow where fronds brush you and close behind you.

Deeper, the kelp — if it is kelp — grows not toward the light but sideways, following the warm currents in great horizontal fields that stretch for provinces. A current above you may carry a meadow, rootless, flowering as it travels, its underside hung with clear bells that ring against your skull not as sound but as pressure when you pass underneath. A current below you, colder by a degree, might carry nothing but a slow rain.

For it rains inside the sea. This is one of its rules and you learn not to argue.

Rain falls from the clouds above, strikes the surface, and does not stop. It becomes a different, darker rain, heavier droplets moving at a dream's pace through the lighter water, falling in straight silver lines through forests and fields, pattering — silently, all sound here is eaten within feet, becoming only a tremor in your teeth and sternum — on the roofs of the drowned things below. Where deepest, the rain does not reach the floor. It accumulates. It forms a second surface, upside down, a ceiling of gathered rainwater miles beneath the true surface, so clear and still that you can swim down, push your head through it, and find yourself looking up out of water into water, breathing that airy upper medium again in a pocket sealed under the sea. There are stacked seas here. Three, sometimes four when the seasons shift. Fishermen who never existed would have gone mad trying to net them.

Animals have mostly declined the invitation. This is a plant's world, and bacteria's, and the slow mineral world's. The water is too warm, too thick, too full of competing perfumes and poisons exhaled by ten million leaves. Now and then — once in a summer, once in a childhood — something large moves at the edge of visibility. A pale shape, long-necked, blind, its body fringed not with fins but with photosynthetic fans it opens like a peacock to the dim columns of sun. It does not flee you. It may not have the concept. The vegetation parts for it with a kind of respect. Where it has passed, seeds burst open all at once in the warmth of its wake.

Or, rarer still, on the floor among the ruins: something that walks. Not swims. Walks as if the water were not there, as if it still lived in the time before the flood, its hooves finding purchase on air. The locals below the clouds, who have never seen the sea, have a word for seeing it anyway in dreams. It erodes from memory as you try to describe it.

The ruins are there, though. Scattered, not clustered. The people who lived on this plateau when it was dry land — if they were people, if it was ever dry — built low and round, wisely, against wind, and the water has been patiently unbuilding them for an age. A causeway that goes fifty miles in a dead straight line and ends in a staircase descending into a deeper basin. A tower, its top still, incredibly, breaking the upper surface on the lowest tides, its stone bell-chamber full of trapped air where the inside walls are dry and dusty and covered in paintings of a sun with too many rays. You can duck into that chamber from below and stand, dripping, in a room that has not been wet in — possibly — ten thousand years, listening to your own breath come back loud after the silence outside. The dust will not settle while you are there. It hangs, considering.

Leave a mark on that dust, write your name with a finger, and watch erosion catch up in fast-forward. The line softens, widens, heals. The room forgets you while you watch. Outside, the same is true of footprints in the clay floor, of broken fronds, of griefs held too tightly. The Upper Sea is ancient not because things have lasted here, but because nothing is allowed to remain sharp. It wears even time smooth.

That smoothness extends to direction. Near the old river channels, sediment does not fall down. It falls along the channel, sideways through the water in brownish streams that follow the ghost of a current that stopped flowing before the sea existed. Bubbles from the vents do not always rise. Some sink, gathering in silver pools on the undersides of ledges, waiting. At the far eastern rim, where the bowl of the continent finally fails, the sea pours over in waterfalls that are visible from a hundred miles below — if the cloud breaks. They fall a thousand feet, unravel into mist, and the mist, instead of dissipating, thickens, coheres, and begins to rise again as warm rain back into the sea. Almost nothing is lost. Almost nothing leaves. The system feeds on its own escaping.

It has its own weather down there, self-contained. Storms that never touch the sky: sudden upwellings that lift entire meadows and spin them slowly for weeks, a green cyclone turning in blue-black water, shedding flowers. Cold fronts you can see approaching as a wall of clearer water that makes the plants close. Lightning, occasionally, but far down and soundless, a flicker inside the vents that makes all the translucent leaves flash at once, for miles, like a thought passing through a brain.

To travel here is not to swim, precisely. The density helps you. You drift, you pull yourself along trunks and old walls, you let the tidal inhalation carry you inland for hours and trust the exhalation to bring you back. You speak and your words make small, warm clouds that hang in front of your face, legible for a moment as vibration before the water takes them. At night — which is only a deepening of the dusk — the vegetation gives off a faint, uneven glow, not bright enough to read by, bright enough to lose the darkness. Fields pulse in sequence, following the currents. It looks, from a high root-raft where you might rest half-submerged, exhausted, ears full of pressure-silence, like another sea inverted above a drowned sky.

And above you, through layer after layer of falling rain and rising bubble and steaming surface, the real sky goes on being thin and high and indifferent. The sea pays it no mind. It has been here, wearing its continent down to a final, gentle shape, long enough to develop its own sky underneath, its own rains that fall forever without landing, its own dry rooms inside the wet, its own slow green weather.

It will be here after the last mark anyone makes in it smooths away.
