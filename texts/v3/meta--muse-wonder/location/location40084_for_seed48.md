It begins as built land without a town appearing at all.

A causeway crosses the tidal flat for almost two miles — straight concrete, low guardrails eaten by salt, mud on either side shining under a white-gray sky. The water is never far. It lies in sheets over the mud, collects in the borrow pits beside the road, seeps up through cracks in the pavement where the groundwater presses only inches below. At high tide it comes under the causeway in slow brown channels. At low tide it retreats and leaves the whole basin glistening, as if the ground itself were sweating.

There is nothing green. Not sparse green — essentially none. The mangroves that once held this coast were cut and buried under fill long ago, and the fill was capped with concrete, asphalt, crushed slag, and salt-poisoned clay. No trees were replanted. Nothing volunteers in the cracks for long; the soil is too saline, the reflected heat off the yards too fierce, and the maintenance crews spray and scrape the drainage lines as part of keeping the pumps clear. From the causeway the place reads entirely in mineral and manufactured colors: wet mud the color of tea, oxidized steel, black bitumen, pale concrete gone gray-green with damp, roofs of corrugated iron streaked rust-red and white with salt. An occasional plastic flower in a dormitory window is the brightest vegetation-colored thing for blocks.

The heat is total and wet. By nine in the morning it is already in the high thirties, and it does not so much rise from the sun — the sun, most days, is only a paler disc behind low cloud and industrial haze — as come up off the pavement and out of the buildings themselves. The light stays dim even at noon, flattened, shadowless in the open and genuinely dark under the warehouse eaves. Air conditioners weep steadily from every occupied block. Walls condense. Metal handrails are warm and slick at once. Paint blisters and keeps blistering in patterns that were repaired years ago and have settled into permanence: this is not a new industrial estate, not a ruin either, but a place that reached its weathered form decades back and has been maintained inside that form ever since — patched roofs, re-poured aprons where the slab subsided, tide gates replaced twice, everything functional and everything stained.

It is unmistakably a city in plan and almost empty in fact.

The port quarter alone covers ground a market town could fit inside: grain silos on a dredged channel, a cold-storage plant, a fertilizer shed as long as four football pitches, container hardstanding, an ice factory, a small power station whose cooling towers breathe out vapor that never quite separates from the low cloud. Between them — avenues built for trucks, rail spurs, pipe runs on low trestles, turning circles, weighbridges, fenced yards. All organized, all urban in grain. And at midday you can stand at the central junction, by the shuttered canteen and the water tower, and see no person in any direction. The work here is scaled and mechanized enough that it runs with skeleton crews sealed inside cabs and control rooms, and what shifts can be moved have been moved to the night. The emptiness is volumetric, too: open a warehouse door and the dim interior goes back a hundred meters, forklift lanes empty, pallets stacked high and still, the air inside somehow hotter than outside and smelling of grain dust, ammonia, and warm cardboard.

The animals have taken the difference.

This is not a place with wildlife in it. It is a place being continuously occupied, in shifts, by animals — and they do not need vegetation, because everything here is fed, ultimately, from the sea and the cargo. Spilled grain along the rail spur supports pigeons and sparrows in numbers that darken the silo ledges. Rats work the same lines after dark. The tidal drains bring in fiddler crabs by the thousand, orange and mud-brown, pouring sideways out of grates when the tide falls, their one enlarged claw raised, clicking faintly as they cross hot concrete to reach the mud again. Monitor lizards patrol the main canal at the speed of a slow walk. Geckos cover the lit walls at night, motionless around every lamp, snapping at moths that come in off the water in soft, endless drifts.

Indoors is worse, or better, depending on tenancy. Bats roost in the uninsulated roof void of the old fertilizer shed in a colony you can smell before you reach the building — a warm, ammoniac density hanging in the dark under the trusses — and at dusk they leave in a column so steady and so quiet it registers first as a change in the air above the street rather than as sound. Swifts nest in the ventilation shafts of the cold store. Feral cats, lean and heat-flattened, sleep all day in the shade of parked trailers and hunt the yards as a loose, inherited population that no one exactly owns and everyone feeds. Ants find every cable conduit. Cockroaches live behind the warm compressors. Mosquitoes breed in the one place water is guaranteed never to be fully drained: the perpetual puddles under the air-conditioning lines, in blocked gutters, in the condensate trays, in a discarded tire behind the pump house that fills and refills no matter how often it is tipped out.

And yet it is quiet. That is the strange consistency of it. The heat puts a lid on sound. At midday there is no birdsong, no human voice carrying, almost no traffic — only the low, continuous thrum of refrigeration compressors felt more in the fillings of your teeth than heard, the drip of condensate onto concrete, now and then the hydraulic sigh of a distant loading arm or a pump kicking on. The crabs make almost no noise for their numbers. The bats in the day-roost shift and squeak at the edge of hearing. Even the gulls out on the channel seem subdued, standing in the shallow brine pans in rows, waiting.

The water governs every schedule. Twice a day the tide tests the gates; twice a day the pumps answer. Streets in the lowest quarter — the workers' dormitory blocks, built on a raised plinth that has settled unevenly over thirty years — flood to the ankle if a pump cycle is mistimed, and everyone there reads the tide table the way farmers elsewhere read rain. Work shoes live on racks above floor level. Drain covers are chained. No door in the town opens directly outward without a screen, a lip, and a brush strip, and no one leaves a ground-floor window open after dark, not for thieves but for the sheer biomass that will come in toward the light and the cool. The shade side of the warehouse is the real pedestrian network; people time crossings between buildings the way you would time crossing open ground in hard rain.

The residential blocks themselves are four stories of weather-sealed concrete around courtyards with no planting at all — just shade cloth on frames, water tanks, laundry lines, parked scooters under tarpaulins, and the constant small rain of the cooling units. Food comes in by truck and barge: rice, oil, frozen meat, tinned things, vegetables from a hundred miles inland that wilt within the day. At the small market under its iron roof, everything is sold from crates and cold boxes, and by late afternoon the offcuts and meltwater have drawn flies into a slow, dense hovering over the drains that you walk through rather than around.

Nothing here is still, even in the empty hours. The tide is moving under the streets. Brine is being pumped from pan to pan out on the flats, drying in the heat to a white crust that front-loaders peel and carry off. Grain is settling in the silos with faint internal ticks. Crabs are re-excavating the drainage outfalls as fast as the crews clear them. In the evening the whole sequence inverts: shutters roll up, the sodium lights come on in the haze, trucks begin to move in ones and twos with their headlights making tunnels in the vapor, the bats go out over the channel, the geckos take their positions, and the town reveals that it was never abandoned at all — just running on a cycle set by heat, tide, and cargo, in which people are the scarcest large animal, and the built shell of the place belongs, most hours of most days, to everything that has learned to live off warmth, salt water, spillage, and shade.
