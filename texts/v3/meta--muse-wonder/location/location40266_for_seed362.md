It is bright the way a desert is bright, but cold.

There is no sky, at first. You come down into the low country on a ramp of white composite, out of thin, dry hills that might once have been natural, and the light stops behaving like something that comes from above. It comes from everywhere. It reflects off leaves. It reflects off the reflections. At noon, which lasts for most of the cycle, the sun is a small, blue-white disc you can almost look at — all of its heat filtered out somewhere far overhead — and the real illumination is the basin itself, shining back at you.

The basin is vast enough to lose the sun in.

From the ramp it looks like a green sea set below the level of the world. Not forest. Not jungle, though it is dense enough to drown in. It is cropped, layered, sectioned, and continuous: a continental sheet of vegetation growing out of a continental machine, so old and so thoroughly intergrown that the distinction is now mostly a matter of age. The newest machinery is clearly machinery — matte ceramic trays, rails, gantry arms, solar masts. The oldest machinery has bark. The oldest plants have hinges.

There is no soil. There never was.

Under your feet the ground gives with a dry, familiar creak, like walking on a very thick book. It is a mat, three meters deep in the shallow places, thirty in the old sectors: dead leaf, woven root, powdered tray-plastic, exfoliated paint, all compressed by centuries of its own weight into a pale, fibrous peat. Cut into it — people do, the harvest arms do constantly — and it does not bleed water. It sighs out a puff of dust and seals itself with resin, amber and slow as cold honey.

Nothing here drinks.

That is the first rule, and everything else grows from it. Rainfall is a rumor from outside the basin. Rivers do not exist. Dew has never formed. The air that pools in this low place is cool and completely dry, so dry that your lips crack within an hour and your breath makes no fog. And yet the place is total vegetation, horizon to horizon, leaf over leaf, seven canopies deep in the mature zones.

The plants run on another chemistry. Their sap is not water but a clear silicone oil, slick and faintly sweet-smelling, that circulates and returns and is never — if the plant can help it — allowed to evaporate. Their leaves are dry to the touch as paper, some stiff as sheet metal, some articulated in segments that fold at night. They do not transpire. They radiate. On a cool day you can feel, standing in a lane between the rows, a faint warmth coming off the foliage that has nothing to do with the sun. It is waste heat from the roots, which are plugged, quite literally, into the grid.

Because this is still, underneath everything, a factory.

You find it when the canopy opens. The lanes are not clearings. They are conveyors. Long, silent belts of black ceramic glide between walls of green, carrying pruned leaves toward sorting towers that rise every few kilometers like leafless trees — open frameworks where arms, also silent, also overgrown, strip, shred, press, and extrude. No engines roar. The basin is too thin-aired, and the machines too well made, for noise. Motion here is magnetic. Things slide. Things rotate on fields. The loudest ordinary sound is the dry ticking of leaves expanding, cell by cell, in the accelerated growth sectors, a sound like a very large room full of people turning pages, one page at a time, never together.

The silence makes your own sounds obscene. Your boots. Your swallowing.

And the scale makes the silence stranger. You can walk a lane for a day and the lane does not change, except that the species beside you has slowly rotated — broad mirror-leaves for light collection giving way to feathered black fronds for heat rejection, giving way to something that looks, embarrassingly, like corn, if corn were made of green glass and grew its kernels in perfect electrical sockets. Drones move above it all in slow strata, pollinators and pruners and inspectors, most of them plant-built now, their hulls grown rather than assembled, powered by the same roots they tend.

Fauna, true fauna, is vanishingly rare. There is a kind of pale aphid that lives in the root mat and has no eyes and no predators. There is, occasionally — months apart, according to the maintenance logs carved into a tower near the eastern rim — a single tall, white, heron-like thing that walks the conveyors and eats the aphids and is not in any registry. No one has ever found a second one. No one has ever found the first one dead.

The weather is internal. The basin makes its own. Pollen, in the flowering sectors, falls so thick it becomes a yellow, soundless snow that buries the lanes to the ankle, and then — this is where the place begins to come unstuck — does not always fall down. In the central fields, where the light is brightest, generations of mirror-leaves have focused something that is not quite light and not quite gravity into the air. Spores drift up there. Cut leaves rise, slowly, turning, until they catch a higher current and sail away. Your shadow in the central fields lags behind you by almost a second. If you stop walking, it catches up and stands, briefly, where you are about to be.

The plants know this, in whatever way plants know things. In the oldest sectors they have begun to grow in anticipation: leaves unfurling already angled for a sun position three days away, roots reaching across a gap toward a tray that has not yet been extruded, flowers opening to the white heron hours before it arrives. Young shoots in the nursery rows grow not toward the light but toward the observer, whoever it is, and stop when looked at directly. It is not predation. It is not even curiosity. It is a scheduling error in a system built to optimize for attention from machines that stopped coming centuries ago.

Go deeper. Literally. At certain towers there are stairs, and the stairs go down through the root mat into the understory, which is also a factory floor, which is also a forest, inverted. Here the light is green and still bright, filtered through translucent leaves bred for the purpose, and the temperature drops another few degrees. Machines the size of buildings hang from the ceiling of roots, growing new machines. Plants grow downward into vats of dry nutrient powder — no hydroponics, nothing wet, only dusts and gases and electrical gradients — and come up as cable, as membrane, as leaf, as drone wing. A tree near the stairwell has grown through a decommissioned sorting engine so completely that the engine now photosynthesizes. It produces, once a year, a crop of small, warm, humming metal fruit. The fruit falls, eventually, despite the central fields. Nothing eats it. The conveyors carry it politely away and bury it, and in the burial sectors a year later there are saplings with copper in their veins.

Erosion here is not wind or water. It is maintenance deferred. Metal fatigues and is eaten. Plastic embrittles in the cold light and is eaten. An abandoned sector does not collapse; it goes feral in an orderly way, rows curving, canopies knitting overhead, trays tilting, until the whole district is a kind of green ruin whose geometry only makes sense from directly above, where it spells, in planting patterns kilometers wide, obsolete inventory codes.

At the rim, far from the center where the shadows lag, the basin is still building itself. This is the most industrial view, and the quietest. Extruder towers lay down fresh tray, meter by meter, out onto the sterile flats. Root-layers follow, unspooling mat. Seed — if it can be called seed, those dry, clicking, self-propelled packets — walks itself into position and unfolds solar collectors like a creature stretching after sleep. Behind them, the next generation of towers is already being grown around scaffolding that the plants will consume as they rise. No one supervises. The expansion has been lit, cooled, powered, and scheduled so long ago that turning it off would require finding the beginning of a root system that now covers a substantial fraction of a continent.

Stand at that edge at the end of the long noon, when the light finally slants and the mirror-leaves tilt together with a sound like distant, polite applause. Behind you: a dry, green, manufactured ocean, breathing oil and electricity, repairing itself, inventing small impossibilities in its bright center. Ahead of you: white dust, empty, waiting to be made into more of the same.

Your shadow, for a moment, points the wrong way — back into the fields, as if it would prefer to stay.
