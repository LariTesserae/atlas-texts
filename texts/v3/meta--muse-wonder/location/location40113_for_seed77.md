At first it can be mistaken for a desert, or a dry lake bed, or a plain that has been in the sun so long it has forgotten any other state. That mistake only holds from a distance. In every direction the ground goes to the horizon without rising, without breaking, white and tan and pale red under a normal, merciless sky, and it looks empty.

Come closer and the emptiness has seams.

The ground is laid. Everywhere, for as far as anyone has ever walked it, the ground is laid by hand.

Underfoot are panels of rammed earth, sun-fired clay, lime and ash beaten flat, mud brick set in courses so wide each course is a field. Some sections are smooth enough to show the scrape of wooden trowels. Some still carry basket-marks, palm prints, the paired lines where wet material was dragged into place on mats and left to cure. There is no soil beneath it within reach. Dig through one panel and you find another, older and harder, and under that rubble, packed fill, lengths of fired tile to keep the whole mass from sinking. It is not ground built upon. It is ground built instead.

It is low. It stands only a few meters above the wet country that surrounds it, and in places barely one — a table raised just enough to refuse the flood. And it is vast enough that the outside weather stops being reliable once you are a day or two in. It is vast enough to make its own.

The heat is the governing fact. By mid-morning the surface is too hot to touch with a bare palm. Heat shivers off it in a clear sheet, softening the horizon, making the few distant landmarks waver as if underwater. Light here is ordinary daylight, but unfiltered: no tree canopy, almost no shadow, the sun reflecting back up from the pale lime until the glare comes from both directions. The air smells of dust, hot clay, faint mineral salt, and, after rain, wet plaster.

There is rain, but only its own kind of rain.

Moisture arrives intermittently, and almost never as a through-flowing system. There are no rivers on the Table, no streams yet, almost no erosion at all — the place is too young for water to have learned its routes. At night the great mass cools just enough for dew to bead on the harder panels, a brief dampness that is gone by nine. In the interior, the heat rising all afternoon builds a single slow tower of cloud directly over the center, as if over an invisible mountain, and toward evening it lets go in a short, warm shower twenty or thirty kilometers across. You can see it coming for hours: a grey curtain dragging across the white, hissing faintly, erasing the horizon on one side.

Where it lands, the water stays. The builders made the surface to shed nothing in particular; the panels are imperfect, very slightly dished, jointed with low lips of clay. So every shower leaves hundreds of shallow pans, inches deep, miles apart and miles wide at once, mirroring the sky for a day, sometimes three. Algae tint the oldest of them green at the edges. Salt blooms white as they shrink. Then they are gone, leaving a darker stain on the panel and a ring of cracked mud no thicker than paper.

Because it is so new, life has barely negotiated a foothold. No animals have established themselves in the interior — no birdsong in the morning, no insect hum over the pans, no tracks except the ones people bring in with them and lose as the fresh surface hardens. Far out toward the wet margins there may be a heron or a fly, but the center is zoologically silent. Vegetation is almost as sparse, and entirely opportunistic: a single tuft of wiry grass forced up through a joint, its roots spreading sideways in the damp seam; a mat of dark moss in the permanent shade on the north side of a survey pillar; a few salt-tolerant seedlings in a pan that held water a week longer than the others, already yellowing. They do not soften the place. They mark the places where water once stood still long enough to be noticed.

What is present, at great intervals, are the leavings of the building itself. A stack of unfired bricks under a sagging mat of reeds, gone the color of bone. A low mound of raw lime. A square curing pit, its walls still damp. A stone pillar stamped with a panel number and a year-mark, standing alone with nothing to survey but more panels. Sheds miles from any other shade, built low from the same clay, their roofs weighted so the wind cannot worry them loose. These things are so far apart that density is not really the word — it is more a rhythm: hours of nothing, then one made thing, then hours of nothing again. Even the building crews belong to the edges. In the cool hours at the far eastern and southern rims, lines of workers with baskets add the newest ground meter by meter, dark and steaming-wet where it has just been laid, squeaking faintly underfoot for its first days. By the time the sun is high they have withdrawn, and the interior belongs to the curing.

And the curing is not passive. For something so quiet, the Table is restless. The panels tick and ping as they expand. A fresh section sighs out vapor at dawn. A hair crack travels across an older slab in the heat of the afternoon with a sound like a fingernail dragged over pottery, and by nightfall it has become a seam someone will later have to fill. Wind, with nothing to interrupt it, drags loose dust in long, slow veils that settle preferentially along the joints, so that from the air — or from the top of one of those rare pillars — the whole vast work must look faintly gridded, a geometry being drawn and redrawn by weather. The afternoon cloud forms, breaks, refills the pans. The pans evaporate, lay down salt, crack. The rim advances.

The rules for living on it follow from that. Shade must be carried or made; it does not occur. Water must be caught in the night or found in a pan whose age you can judge by its rim — too fresh and it tastes of lime, too old and it is salt and green film. You do not follow watercourses, because there are none; you navigate by panel numbers, by pillars, by the subtle change in color that marks one decade's clay from the next, whole territories of rose giving way to whole territories of chalk-white. Fires are lit sparingly and never directly on a finished panel, because the heat will lift a blister in the surface and start a flaw that outlives you. And you do not expect the ground to receive you — no burial deeper than the fill, no well that reaches anything but older work. However lonely it feels in the center, where the silence is broken only by expansion ticks, a far-off rain-hiss, and your own steps coming back off the hot flat, you are never standing on nature.

You are standing on a floor the size of a continent, still warm from being made, not yet old enough to have been worn down into a place.
