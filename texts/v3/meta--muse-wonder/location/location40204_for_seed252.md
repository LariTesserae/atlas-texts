It is not on any surface map. It is a hollow left under the ground, three hundred metres down, where a new basalt plain is still cooling above it.

From above, there is only black lava — eleven years old, still sharp enough to cut boots, still holding the day's heat after sunset. No entrance, no vent obvious to the eye. Hairline fractures run through it where rainwater disappears. The water goes down through hot rock, gathering minerals, losing its oxygen, arriving weeks later in a place that was never meant to be a cave at all. It began as a gas bubble in the flow, stretched as the lava moved, roofed over, and sealed. Then the dike shifted below it, and the bubble did not quite collapse.

The chamber is small enough to know all at once, and yet it does not feel small from inside. About four metres long, two and a half wide, a domed ceiling less than two metres above the water at its highest point. You could stand on the shore of the pool — if shore is the right word for a hand's width of black glass grit — and touch both walls without turning around. Everything beyond that narrow room is solid basalt.

It is bright down here. That is the first wrong note, and then immediately not wrong at all. There is a crack in the floor of the pool, no wider than a finger for most of its length, and at the bottom of it the rock is incandescent. Not flame. Not an open river of magma. Just a deep, steady orange line, hot enough to light the whole room through the water above it. The pool glows from underneath, amber at the center, greenish at its shallow edges where the light passes through dissolved silica and copper. Wet walls catch the reflection and throw it back. Even the steam is lit, moving in slow veils through its own illumination. There are almost no true shadows; the light wells up, is scattered by condensate, softened, and settles on everything.

It is hot in layers. The air at the ceiling is near seventy degrees, saturated, pressing on the skin like the inside of a kiln that has learned about water. Near the pool the surface is eighty, eighty-five, just below a rolling boil that never quite arrives. The dry ledges of basalt on the north side are too hot to keep a hand on. Only the weeping wall, where groundwater enters in a dozen thin threads, is merely warm — and that water arrives at forty degrees, having already been heated on its way down.

Water is everywhere without ever being abundant. It seeps through the ceiling in beads that swell, tremble, and fall. It sheets almost invisibly down the east wall. It condenses out of the steam onto any slightly cooler projection and rains back in a delayed, irregular patter. The pool itself is perhaps thirty centimetres deep at the center, clear enough that the glowing crack looks closer than it is, its surface unbroken for long stretches and then dimpled all over by condensation returning. Along the margins, mineral skins form — translucent silica, milky calcite, a rust-red fringe of iron oxide — and are dissolved again when the chemistry swings. Nothing here has had time to be worn smooth. The floor is fresh fracture and volcanic glass. Obsidian shards lie in the grit like black knives. The little mineral chimneys growing up around the pool's rim, some no taller than a thumbnail, others a hand's breadth, have edges as crisp as cut paper. They are growing measurably, fed by supersaturated water, and they are also, at their tips, dissolving. Creation and undoing in the same centimetre.

The air tastes of stone and sulfur and hot metal. Carbon dioxide pools low over the water; hydrogen sulfide comes and goes in faint pulses. There is almost no wind, but the air is never still. Heat lifts off the pool, cools at the dome, sinks along the weeping wall — a slow, closed circulation, a room breathing its own weather. Every forty to fifty minutes the cycle sharpens. Bubbles begin to rise in strings from the floor crack, hesitantly, then steadily. The pool surface shivers. Steam thickens until the far wall is only a suggestion, the orange light turns diffuse and total, and pressure finds a narrow throat high on the west side and sighs out through it into some further crack system no one has mapped. It is not loud. It is the quietest part, in a way — a long exhalation that relieves the room of speech. Then the steam thins, condensation falls in a brief, warm rain, and the water is clear again.

For sound, mostly, there is dripping. A drop from the dome every few seconds, swelling the silence rather than breaking it. The small tick of basalt expanding as its temperature shifts by a degree. Bubbles collapsing softly at the pool's edge. Underneath all of that, if you pressed an ear to the rock — which you could not, for long — a hum so low it is more a condition of the jaw than a sound. That is one of the two signs.

There is no plant life here, and there never could be. The orange light has almost nothing in it a leaf could use, and no seed could arrive intact through those hot fractures. What lives on the walls is simpler and older in its habits. Where the groundwater weeps in, thin microbial films spread in patches, widely spaced: white filaments that sway in water no thicker than a coat of paint, rust-colored mats that trap iron as it oxidizes, a faint iridescent sheen on the pool margins where sulfur-oxidizing bacteria harvest the chemistry of the crack. They do not cover the rock. They occur, the way stars occur, with a great deal of dark stone between them. It keeps the room feeling empty even where it is most alive.

Animals are rarer still. Now and then a pale amphipod, blind, translucent, no longer than a grain of rice, arrives through the water-bearing fractures from some cooler aquifer far above and to the side. It survives in the inflow threads where the temperature is survivable, feeding on the bacterial film, and it cannot cross the pool. They come singly. Months may pass without one. There is no population here, only visitors the plumbing occasionally delivers and the heat eventually takes back. Once, sensors recorded something slightly larger — a shrimp-like shape at the inflow, there for eleven minutes, gone. The chamber is not a habitat, exactly. It is a place life passes at the edge of its tolerance, and passes rarely.

And the sensors. That is the second sign, and you have to know to look. Scattered in the glass grit on the dry ledge, among a hundred thousand natural grains, are perhaps a dozen grains that are not quite natural. Same size, same dull black. But they do not tumble when the condensation rain strikes them, and every few hours their surfaces adjust by a fraction — turning, the way a sunflower turns, except toward gradients of heat and chemistry rather than light. They drink the room's warmth and answer it through solid rock as if the rock were not there, passing their measurements to receivers kilometres away by means that make the basalt, at certain frequencies, effectively transparent. There is nothing else. No cable, no housing, no drilled shaft. The probe that placed them came down a decade ago by melting a hole the width of a hair, and the hole healed behind it within a season.

The larger engineering is in the stone itself, and it is invisible. Around this chamber, extending perhaps sixty metres in every direction, the basalt has been reworked at scales below microscopy — a lattice of thermal pathways grown through the rock by machines that were never assembled and have long since dissolved back into inert mineral. It draws heat out of the dike below, steadily, silently, and feeds it into a regional grid that warms a town on the coast. That drawing is why the pool never quite boils, why the crack in the floor holds at its orange threshold year after year instead of widening or sealing. Twice a day, when the grid's demand peaks, you can feel the room cool by two degrees in as many minutes — the steam thins suddenly, the dripping slows, the glowing line dulls toward red — as if something vast and distant had taken a sip. Then the heat gathers itself again.

Nobody built this room. That matters to the people who tend the grid, and it is also true. They found it — or their instruments found it, listening through the planet with a patience older technology would not have had — and they chose not to open it, pipe it, or line it. They tuned what surrounds it and left the inside alone. If the lattice were switched off, the silica would win within a year; the chimneys would grow together, the inflow would seal, the pool would thicken to gel and then to stone, and the bubble in the basalt would finish becoming rock. If the lattice drew too hard, the room would cool, the films would die back, the glow would go. It stays because it is held, very lightly, at the point where it already wanted to be.

So it continues, hidden, unvisited, lit from below. Water arrives thread by thread and leaves as steam sighing into the dark of the fracture system. Minerals rise into fragile towers and dissolve at their tips. A single pale creature grazes in the inflow, for a while. The grains on the ledge turn almost imperceptibly toward the warmth, and the rock around everything carries the heat away to light rooms that will never know this one's name — a small, hot, bright pocket of the young earth, doing its work in near silence, entirely and deliberately untouched.
