It is called the Governor Pit, though the governors have not governed anything for a long time.

From the harbor you go down. First a brick stair in a dry, narrow well-house, smelling of cold iron and lamp oil, the sea noise going blunt behind a riveted door. Then a second stair, iron, its treads worn into shallow cups. Then a third, and the air changes. It gets cooler — eleven, twelve degrees, year round — and wetter, and louder, until the steps simply continue down into black-green water and do not stop.

The pit is all there is below. One room. About eighteen metres long, twelve wide, seven deep, built in 1887 as the lower chamber of a tidal pumping station. No earth in it, no rock, no harbor bed. The floor is cast-iron plate, buckled and re-bolted and patched with concrete. The walls are glazed engineering brick over concrete, gone the color of tea where the glaze has failed. The ceiling is a low brick vault. Even the pillars holding it are made things — two rows of squat iron columns, flanged at the base, braced across the top with girders that have lost every sharp edge they ever had.

Water fills it almost to the vault. At low tide there is a lens of trapped air under the highest part of the ceiling, perhaps forty centimetres deep, where you can lift your mouth and breathe and hear everything differently. The rest is submerged, and always has been in living memory. They tried to drain it once, in the fifties. The sea came in through the gate seals, the culverts, the pipe joints, the wall itself, faster than the pumps could send it out. Now the pumps in the dry room next door — big, slow, industrial things, still electrified, still oiled — do not try to empty it. They only keep the upper stairwell from joining it.

Light is poor and green. At midday it comes in through the harbor grating high in the east wall, a barred window below sea level, and falls in slanted, dusty columns full of drifting particles. Away from that, two caged work lamps burn amber behind thick glass on the west wall. They have been underwater so long their housings wear beards of brown algae. Everything beyond a few metres is not dark so much as thick — shapes resolving late: a ladder, a valve wheel, a chain hanging from a beam and vanishing down.

For a built place, it is remarkably empty. The machinery was meant to leave the centre clear for moving water. In the middle stands the old turbine housing, a riveted drum taller than a man, its inspection plates green with verdigris, its disconnected shaft turning, very slowly, whenever the tide runs. Along the north wall, two sluice culverts the size of railway tunnels, gated and chained. Along the south, a rank of pipes, a catwalk that now runs a metre beneath the surface, a control stand with brass levers worn bright where hands used to fall. That is all. The water owns the volume between.

And the water is never still for long.

Twice a day the pit breathes. When the tide outside rises higher than the lagoon behind the station, pressure finds the hairline gap under the north gates and the whole room begins to move. First a tremor in the chains. Then a low roar, felt in the teeth and sternum more than heard, as seawater forces itself through in a flat, hard current that pins drifting weed and loose tools alike against the south grating. The turbine sighs around. The governor above it — iron balls on articulated arms, disconnected, encrusted — stirs and clanks in its cage, faithful to a motion it no longer regulates. Air bells under the vault ring softly as bubbles worry loose from the brickwork. Rust and brick dust and old coal silt lift off the floor plates in slow clouds, and visibility drops to an arm's length.

When the levels equalize, for perhaps twenty minutes, there is an astonishing quiet inside the noise of the pumps next door: a steady, muffled thump through the wall, like a heart in another body. Then the tide turns, and the pit exhales the other way.

Life has taken the room precisely as it is. It did not need soil. Mussels have paved the lower walls and the bases of the columns in black, irregular cobbles of shell, opening and closing in the current, filtering the green water clear again after each tide stirs it. Barnacles whiten the gate frames. Sea anemones, dull red and olive, have colonised the pipe flanges where the flow is richest, tentacles swaying in unison like something planted — though almost nothing green is planted here. Vegetation is sparse: a little sea lettuce luminous near the grating, stringy algae on the lamp chains, a single kelp frond that comes in most summers and spends the season streaming from the ladder, feeling for light that is not quite enough.

The animals are the true abundance. Grey mullet circle the central columns in a loose, perpetual school, turning all at once when the gate current starts. Sea bass hold station in the lee of the turbine drum. Crabs walk the iron floor plates with a faint metallic ticking, picking at the mussel beds; starfish have flattened themselves over the patch plates and old bolt heads. Eels live in the dead conduits and come out at dusk into the lamp light, unhurried, completely at home in a machine. In autumn, cuttlefish drift through, hovering over the catwalk. Everything arrives through the gratings or the culvert gaps; everything that stays has learned the tide table better than the maintenance divers have.

Those divers are the only people who go down now, tethered, in pairs, and the rules of the place are simple and physical. You do not work while the tide is running — the current will take you to the grating and keep you there. You do not trust a handhold until you have tested it; a century underwater has rounded the iron and rotted the mortar to soft sand between bricks, so that beautiful old surfaces crumble under a glove. You keep one hand on a line, because in the silt-outs the lamps become two amber smudges and up is only a rumour told by bubbles. Your tools are tied to your wrist or they are gone, settled into the final, flat layer of debris on the plates: a wrench, a lost torch, horseshoe shapes of slag, all acquiring the same rust skin, all being slowly flattened into the floor of the room.

Because that is what the water has been doing here all along: finishing it. The pit was square and painted and precise once, with stencilled numbers on the pipes and white lines on the floor. Erosion has taken the numbers, the paint, the edges, the distinction between repair and ruin. Bolts are nubs. Steps are slopes. Chains are fused into single, swaying strands. It is ancient not in years but in condition — a place that has reached the shape it will keep.

Above the water lens, under the vault, the sound doubles back on itself and you can shout and hear the shout return off wet brick while the pumps thump on, indifferent, on the other side of the wall. Below, in the cool green dimness, the mullet keep circling the columns, the turbine turns without purpose, and the tide comes in through the gates again, filling a room that was built to control the sea and has become, instead, a small and complete piece of it.
