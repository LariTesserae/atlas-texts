At first it seems simple: a floor.

A floor that goes on past any reasonable horizon, low and hot and steaming under a dim sky, made of stone slabs so fresh their chisel-marks are still sharp enough to cut a bare foot. Some slabs are basalt-black. Some are pale, unbaked clay still darkening in the kiln-heat that rises from underneath. Some are copper-veined, or glazed a dull green where hot water has stood on them too long. Between them, hairline seams of wet lime breathe out steam.

There is no ground beneath the floor. If you pry at a loose corner — and corners come loose by themselves, often — there is another floor under it, newer, still warm, its pattern not yet decided. Under that, scaffolding. Under that, more scaffolding. The world has no natural underside. It is built all the way down as far as anyone has ever dug, and digging only produces fresh masonry, wet mortar, numbered stones waiting to be lifted back up.

It is vast in the way weather is vast. The paving has its own climates. Miles of it lie under a perpetual condensation-haze, where moisture gathers on the underside of the unfinished sky and falls as a warm, mineral rain. Miles further on the slabs are dry and ticking with heat, splitting hairline cracks that seal themselves overnight with injected lime. There are districts of shallow flooding, an ankle's depth of clear hot water sheeting for a league over white tiles, so still and dim that the sky is more visible in the floor than above you. You wade for hours and the water stays exactly ankle-deep, even where the tiles visibly slope away, even where you descend a broad ceremonial stair into what should be a reservoir, and then the water is ankle-deep on each stair, separately, as if each step had agreed to its own private flood.

No plants grow in the cracks. No insects skate the pools. The water is sterile and tastes of clay and copper. Where it evaporates, salt ferns crystallize in an afternoon — perfect, branching, utterly mineral — and are scraped away by the world itself when the tiles beneath them turn over. That is as close to vegetation as it gets: patterns that imitate growth without ever becoming alive. There are placeholder trees in some of the unfinished avenues, fired-clay trunks with fired-clay branches, hollow, numbered at the base, waiting to be replaced by something that hasn't been invented here yet. Their shadows are sometimes leafy. The trees themselves have never cast that kind of shadow. You learn not to comment on it. The shadow arrives first, often days before the object. Whole boulevards lie shaded by towers that are still only chalk outlines and a smell of wet rope and lime.

Because everything, including the sky, is under construction.

Look up and the dimness resolves into structure: a vault. Not clouds — or not only clouds — but a low, continental ceiling of dark ceramic panels, unfinished plaster, and taut mineral-fiber matting stretched between stone ribs. The ribs march away into haze until perspective fails and they seem to be holding up nothing but more distance. Condensation forms on the vault and rains gently, constantly, somewhere. In other places the panels have not been laid and there is only a hotter darkness beyond, without stars, without depth, like the inside of a kiln before firing. Work-lamps burn in niches along the ribs, small clay oil lamps with blue, nearly smokeless flames, though no hand trims them and the oil is a kind of rendered stone that sweats slowly from the lamp itself. Their light is sparse. Kilometers of pavement may have only one lamp, burning in a wall niche in a wall that stands alone, a single brick elevation in the middle of the wet plain, with a door-frame, a lintel, a lamp, and nothing behind the door but more floor — until, on a later visit, there is.

No builders are ever seen.

That is the central fact and it does not become easier. The tools are everywhere, and they are primitive to the point of insult against the scale of the work: stone mallets with unworn faces. Copper chisels, soft-edged from the heat. Wooden — no, not wooden, there is no wood, wood has not happened yet — clay-handled scrapes and levers of knotted basalt fiber. Rope made from braided copper wire and spun glass. Hand-wound hoists. Mixing troughs big as houses where lime slurry turns itself over and over, slowly, moved by paddles that pause when you watch them directly and are in a different position when you look back. Scaffolds of fired-clay pipe rise twenty, thirty stories beside structures that do not yet exist, lashed together around empty air in the exact shape of a future tower, and sometimes the tower obliges and grows inside the scaffold overnight, soundlessly, each brick warm and wet as a mouth.

The construction is also the storm.

Turbulence here makes almost no noise. A district can be in violent upheaval — foundation stones crawling against each other like a slow shoal, walls extruding from the paving and folding back into it, a half-built aqueduct re-routing its arches across a flooded basin — and the sound is only a deep ticking through the soles of the feet, a sigh of displaced steam, now and then the soft ceramic *clack* of two vast things meeting with unnatural precision. Entire avenues migrate in the night. Kilns, squat and glowing at their seams, sparse across the plain, find themselves at dawn on the far side of canals that were not there at dusk. The canals are hand-dug, beautifully finished with tile and bitumen-less black glaze, and they carry hot water uphill as readily as down, because gravity in the unfinished zones is still a draft proposal, roped off with copper cord and small clay warning bells that ring only after you have crossed.

Reality here is not broken so much as not yet ratified.

Distance is one of the unratified things. A stockpile on the horizon — and the stockpiles are how this low world rehearses its future mountains, enormous sorted heaps of numbered boulders, each one chiseled with the coordinates of a peak it will eventually join — can remain on the horizon for three days of walking, gaining no size, while the paving underfoot unspools fresh-laid behind you so that your footprints set instantly into permanent tile, adding a narrow road to the map that other travelers' maps will then disagree with. Other times a distant kiln-glow is suddenly near enough to blister paint, with no intervening ground remembered, only a brief impression of having been folded, gently, like wet clay in a cloth.

Time misbehaves more quietly. Mortar cures before it is mixed in some districts; you can watch a wall weather a century of soot and rain-pitting in an hour and then, dissatisfied, return itself to bright new brick with a sound like a held breath released. Rain falls upward near the curing fields, drawn in trembling sheets from the flooded pavement back into the vault, carrying flecks of lime that build inverted stalactites along the ceiling ribs — icicles of architecture, growing down to meet pillars growing up, and where they meet, a doorway exists for a while. Walk through such a doorway and the temperature is identical, the light is identical, the floor continues, but the lamps now burn on the other side of their niches and your wet footprints lead away ahead of you.

Ecology, if the word can be stretched to fit a place with no fauna and no flora, is a matter of water, heat, and salt. The flooded districts warm and cool in long cycles that have nothing to do with day, which in any case is only a slight brightening of the vault-lamps and a thinning of the steam. In the hottest pools — fed by weeping seams where copper pipes sweat mineral water from no visible source — colonies of black bubbles form under a glassy skin on the surface, swell, arrange themselves into rings and chains of startling regularity, and disperse when a tile shifts beneath them. They are not alive. The world is careful about that. Once, in a basin left undisturbed long enough, something like a simple green film began at the edges of the light, and by morning the entire basin had been re-paved, sterilized by fresh kiln-tile, the water re-routed through a clay settling tank carved with what might be a warning or might be a recipe. Life is a finishing step. It has not been scheduled. The clay placeholder animals in the empty courtyards — low, smooth, speciesless shapes on plinths, with blank spaces where the plaques should describe them — keep their patient, eyeless watch over nothing.

The smell is of hot lime first, then wet clay, then the penny-on-the-tongue reek of heated copper, then a faint sweet charcoal odor from the kilns that seems to come from inside your own chest. The air is thick enough to drink in the low places. Sound carries wrong: a single dropped chisel can return as an echo hours later in a different district, by which time the chisel has been built into a wall and the wall has moved. Mostly there is quiet. A dripping quiet. Steam-hiss, lamp-hum, the remote ticking of the floor deciding itself. Your own breathing is frequently the loudest event for miles, and the world, courteously or hungrily, sometimes paves over the pause between breaths and gives you back a silence that is already solid.

Sparse is the other truth. For all its total built-ness, the place is nearly empty. Structures stand far apart, each given an unreasonable estate of bare pavement: a lone cistern-house in the middle of a steaming lake of tile. A bath complex — empty basins, elegant steps, hot water piped through clay manifolds by no pump anyone can find — alone for a day's walk in every direction. An observatory with a copper-bound viewing slit aimed at the inside of the unfinished vault, its floor mosaic showing constellations that do not correspond to the painted guide-stars being applied, far overhead, by brushes on scaffold arms that move only in peripheral vision. Between these presences, the plain performs its slow storm unwitnessed, tiles lifting like scales, foundation pits filling with clear scalding water and then with staircases, staircases flattening into ramps for materials that never arrive by any road you can keep fixed.

It will not finish. That may be its rule, or its malfunction, or its youth. The erosion that would soften it into a real landscape — wind, root, footfall, time — has been postponed, so everything remains at the aching pitch of emergence: mortar damp, edges keen, paint wet, laws unsigned. Finished sections are the most unstable of all. A perfect courtyard, lamps lit, cistern brimming, placeholder trees casting their borrowed leaf-shadows, has the feel of a held note; stay too long and the paving under you warms, the seams widen, and the whole composition begins, politely, silently, to take itself apart into numbered components and be carried by no one to a stockpile where a future mountain is learning its name.

Stay anyway. Wade the ankle-deep staircases. Pass the doorways that exist only where ceiling-growth meets pillar. In the dim heat, under the dripping vault, the world is still deciding what a world is — and it is deciding at continental scale, in brick and water and copper and steam, without hands, without voices, without any living thing to ask whether it should stop.
