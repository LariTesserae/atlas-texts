You find it through a drain that should not be there.

Behind the seawall of the coastal arcology, below the pump galleries and the tide turbines, there is a service crawlway that floods to the knee at high water. At the end of it is a round hatch, shoulder-wide, unmarked on any plan. The wheel turns too easily. Beyond it there should be a sump — a concrete pocket, a few meters across, for catching backwash.

Beyond it there is a city, underwater, at noon.

The wrongness is not immediate darkness or pressure. It is the light. Ordinary, clean daylight falls through thirty meters of green-black water as if there is no water at all. It falls in shafts between buildings, it makes hard shadows on wet pavement, it glitters on tram rails. And there is no surface above you to have come through. If you look up from the hatch threshold you see a low ceiling of black basalt three meters over your head; if you climb — and you must climb, hand over hand down a maintenance ladder that keeps going — that ceiling recedes without ever moving. Street after street opens below.

The pocket expands inward. That is its first rule.

The second is that you can breathe, after a fashion. The water here is cool — teeth-cool, wrist-cool — and densely oxygenated, structured by something the seawall engineers built and no longer fully model. It fills your lungs without drowning reflex. It tastes of copper, ozone, and faintly of mint from the filter-vegetation. It holds your body up but not quite buoyant; you sink slowly between the buildings if you stop kicking. Pressure does not increase with depth. Sound, however, does.

It is loud down there. Loud in a way that lives in your sternum.

Pumps the size of houses throb behind ceramic walls. Water-hammer knocks travel for blocks along iron arteries. Every seven minutes a flood bell rings — not a warning anyone heeds, there is no one to heed it — and sluices at the ends of avenues slam, reversing the current. Trams screech riderless around corners on submerged rails, braking for empty stops, doors sighing open onto floods of bubbles. Construction extruders on rooftops keen as they lay down fresh wall, a high wet whine that sets the fillings in your teeth humming. Rain hisses perpetually somewhere above the roofs, though when you look for clouds there are only more streets, inverted, their lamps lit.

This is a low city. Nothing rises more than five or six stories, and everything crowds to fill the horizontal. It is packed in a way surface cities only pretend to be. No lot is empty. No alley is only an alley. Buildings stand shoulder to shoulder in white ceramic, fresh basalt, and unfogged glass, their corners still knife-sharp, their mortar still bright, their edges unworn by any long erosion. This place is young. It smells — through the water, impossibly — of hot stone dust and new plastic. Between the apartment stacks are wedged power exchanges, coolant chapels, vertical pipe-farms, market halls roofed in translucent gel, stair towers wrapped in cabling like ivy, bridges, then buildings built on the bridges, then cisterns built on those. If there is a gap wider than an arm, something has been grown or printed into it: a condenser fin, a shrine-sized battery, a loom of optical fiber ticking with light.

You can swim down a main avenue — the current helps you, then fights you after the bell — and pass shopfronts at eye level. Cafés with tables bolted to floors, cups still chained by their handles, menus laminated and perfectly legible for customers who will never sit. Laundries where pale coveralls turn slowly in glass drums. A school with rows of desks and a green board still covered in fluid-dynamic equations that erase and rewrite themselves every hour, slightly differently each time. Sleeping pods stacked twenty deep, their blankets tucked, their air membranes inflating and deflating in a slow, respiratory rhythm.

There is no animal life. That absence becomes louder than the pumps.

No fish nose at the glass. No shrimp pick at the curbs. No barnacles roughen those sharp new corners, no mussels, no worms, no stray dog paddling in bewilderment, no people. Even the expected microbial film is missing; a suppression field keeps the water sterile to a polish. Hands run clean off railings. Your own skin, after an hour, feels lightly inventoried, as if the city is deciding whether to permit you as contamination.

What lives, sparsely, is engineered vegetation. It is not decoration.

Pale kelp curtains hang in the purification avenues, ten meters long and almost transparent, swaying against the current, each frond edged with a faint blue metabolism. Tufts of white roof-grass tremble above the sleeping pods and drink dissolved minerals from the flood. On the north faces — if north has meaning where the compass spins politely and settles on the nearest pump station — there are sheets of black moss no thicker than felt, collecting heat. That is all. A few green seams in the ceramic sprawl. Enough to filter, to oxygenate, to signal health on a dashboard somewhere above. Not enough to soften anything.

Because the city is not abandoned. It is rehearsing.

That is the third rule, and you learn it by staying still too long.

If you hover in an intersection and watch, the district begins — courteously, industriously — to revise itself around you. A maintenance frame unfolds from a wall panel and offers you a handrail. A current nudges you toward the nearest transit tube. Street signs rotate to present, in high-contrast lettering, the route to SURFACE / HABITAT / MEDICAL, though following them only brings you to other, newer neighborhoods still being printed. Buildings that were housing yesterday of your subjective time extrude new balconies; buildings that were storage retract their windows and become flood tanks. Goods move. Elevator cages of bottled gas, mineral slurry, folded furniture, and sealed meals rise and descend the outside of towers without recipients. Somewhere two streets over, a crane is assembling a perfect replica of the café you just passed, down to the chained cups. Somewhere else, its twin is being dissolved, the water clouding briefly with eatable-looking silt before the kelp curtains draw it in.

The materials are mostly made here, out of the flood itself. Dissolver fields pull calcium, silica, iron, polymer feedstock from the structured water and lay them down molecule by printed molecule. That is why everything looks freshly quarried and yet seamless. That is why the water holds light the way it does — filaments of something smaller than plankton, technological and not quite describable in optical terms, catch surface daylight from the real world above the seawall and re-emit it at depth without dimming. At night, by the clock on the school wall, those filaments do not turn off. They shift. It becomes a bluer noon, a cooler noon, but noon it remains.

There are weather systems, contained and purposeful.

Every forty minutes a warm — relatively, pleasantly less cold — upwelling rolls inland from the artificial harbor, carrying the taste of salt and making the kelp stand straight out like banners. Then the bell, then the cold return. Twice a day the inverted streets above disgorge rain: water falls up out of the pavement drains of the lower city, beading unnaturally on undersides, gathering into falling-rising sheets that hammer the rooftops of the district hanging above. You can swim through a storm that is traveling toward its cloud, which is a lake held against a ceiling by surface tension and fields, shivering with tram-lightning.

Gravity, politely, is negotiable around the lakes. Not absent, just locally persuaded.

The deepest avenues lead to the harbor that is also a door. There a basin wider than the rest of the pocket should allow opens between printing gantries. The water there is darker, not because the light fails but because the space does. Cranes stand in a ring, holding in their claws the folded skeleton of a bridge, a seawall section, a hospital wing, like insects holding leaves. Under them, on the harbor floor, lies the city again, smaller and denser — the same streets compressed to the size of a table, then a suitcase, then a brick, each iteration lit, flooded, and busy with its own even smaller bells you can feel rather than hear.

It is a seed.

Not a ruin of a drowned place, not a habitat awaiting people in any simple sense. A full settlement kept at pocket scale, submerged in its own working ocean, kept young by constant unbuilding and rebuilding, so that if the coast above fails — when the storm takes the arcology, when the real seawall goes — a hatch like the one you crawled through can be opened from the other side, the folding fields released, and this low, packed, empty city can be unfolded into the flood at full size. Sharp corners and all. Trams ready. Cups chained. Kelp already filtering the new sea.

Until then it practices being submerged. It floods and drains neighborhoods that are already flooded. It runs evacuations for no one. It holds noon at midnight, prints schools that rewrite their lessons, lets rain rise to lakes in its ceiling, and rings its bell every seven minutes so precisely that, after a while, your heartbeat tries to keep time.

When the current turns, it takes you with it — past the cafés, past the sleeping pods inflating in unison, past a new district extruding itself in a scream of hot bubbles — and deposits you, gently and without discussion, at the ladder under the hatch that is both three meters and three hundred meters above the street.

The water in your lungs leaves as easily as it entered. It does not wet your clothes on the other side.

Behind you, the wheel spins itself shut. From the crawlway, all you can hear through the steel is a faint, ordinary pumping.

And if you put your ear flat to it, underneath that, a bell.
