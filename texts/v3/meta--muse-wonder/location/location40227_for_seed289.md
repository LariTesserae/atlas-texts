It is under something ordinary, and so small you would not call it a landscape at first. Six feet, maybe less, beneath a cool pasture where the grass is thin over pale ground. There is no entrance, exactly. A root-slit. A badger's old exploratory scrape that went too deep and stopped. You have to go on your side, cheek in the damp, and let your shoulders learn the width of it.

Then it opens — only just. A pocket. A room that never quite decided to be a cave.

It is perhaps four feet across and not high enough to sit up in without your hair brushing the ceiling. That is the whole of it. There is nowhere else to go, no passage continuing, no branching dark. A single hollow, embedded in the earth like an air bubble in bread. And yet it feels larger than countries you have walked through, because there is no emptiness in it at all.

You expect darkness below ground. That is the first rule this place breaks.

It is blinding.

Not a shaft of light, not a crack letting the day in. The light has no source and no direction. It comes off the walls, off the wet air, off your own skin once you are inside, equally, from everywhere. There are no shadows. When you hold up a hand, the palm is as lit as the back. When you close your eyes, the lids glow red-white. The brightness does not flicker, does not thin toward the corners. It is noon, trapped and flattened, and it has been noon here without moving for as long as the pocket has existed.

The sound is the same.

It should be quiet underground. Instead there is a loud, constant chord, at the edge of what you can stand in such a small space. Crickets, but not the night kind. Hundreds of blind cave crickets packed shoulder to shoulder along the seams of the walls, their antennae crossed over each other like woven thatch, rubbing out one unbroken note. Under it, a lower hum from moths — cellar moths, winter moths, tiny white moths with powder wings — crowded so densely on the ceiling that the ceiling appears to be made of them. Their wings do not open and close. They vibrate, just enough, forever, at the same speed. And under that, a clicking, a wet ticking from beetles in the floor, and the thin, almost electronic peeping of frogs no larger than your thumbnail, crammed cheek-by-jowl in the dampest corner.

No one song rises or falls. No call gets an answer. Everything is already answering everything else, all at once, at full volume, all the time. Your own breathing is lost in it. If you shout, the shout does not echo. There is no room for echo. The air is already full of sound the way a jar is full of grain.

The rock itself explains nothing. It is not old rock. It has no layers, no fossils, no grain you can follow. It is a raw, embryonic geology — soft chalk and marl and something like curdled clay, pale cream and grey, still dentable with a fingernail. You can press it and it gives a little, like the wall of an egg from inside. But the dent does not remain. Slowly — no, not slowly; it was never there. The wall is smooth again, unmarked. Erosion has not begun here. Water has not carved it, wind has not scoured it. It is pre-formed, a cave that has not yet learned how caves are supposed to weather, and it will never learn. It stays in that first, unfinished state, cool to the touch, faintly damp, smelling of cold iron and wet limestone and animal musk.

The damp is precise. It is not wet. There is no pool, no stream, no drip you can catch. Condensation beads on the low ceiling in perfect, hanging spheres, and they do not fall. In one hollow of the floor, no bigger than a saucer, an inch of clear water holds absolutely still, so still its surface looks solid, lit from below. Roots have pushed a few exploratory hairs through the upper wall — three or four pallid threads, and a patch of moss so sparse you can count the individual leaves, each translucent, each holding light inside it. No plant can do much here. There is light in excess, but no cycle to use it by.

That is the deeper rule: nothing cycles.

The fauna are abundant because nothing leaves and nothing arrives and nothing, it seems, dies. The crickets eat the thin biofilm on the damp rock. The frogs eat the crickets' droppings made small life. The beetles eat what the frogs leave, and grind it back to film. The moths eat nothing you can see; perhaps they eat light, or condensation, or each other's shed scales, which drift down constantly and are eaten off the floor before they accumulate. There are white salamanders, two at least, or maybe many more folded invisibly among the frogs, their skin so translucent you can see a dark thread of gut. There are earwigs and woodlice and springtails so crowded in the cracks that the cracks move, if you stare, with a granular restlessness — but they never empty out and never overflow. The population is a fixed chord too.

Even the structures fit without disturbing it. Built things, but small, scattered, made by mouths and feet rather than hands. A mud-dauber's abandoned nest, a perfect clay thimble stuck under a lip of rock, cracked open and empty. A second one beside it, whole. A mouse or wren once managed to weave a little cup of grass stems in the driest nook; it has collapsed into a flat, fragrant mat. And near the still pool, inexplicably, a cairn — three pebbles, each no larger than a pea, stacked. Who stacked them would have had to lie here just as you are lying, in the deafening light, and balance pea on pea with numb fingers. They have not fallen. They will not fall. The dust that should have buried them never settles; it is held in the bright air, suspended, each mote lit, packing the last spaces between animal and animal, sound and sound.

The air itself is the densest thing. Cool — steadily, evenly cool, against your face and in your lungs, never warming with your body heat the way a small space should. It feels packed in your ears. It presses the sound into you. You understand, after a minute, that there is literally no vacancy here for anything new to happen in. Light cannot diminish because it has nowhere to go. Sound cannot decay because it is already bumping against itself. A droplet cannot fall because falling would require a change in arrangement, and the arrangement is complete.

You can disturb it, in the small sense. You can turn your head, and the moths under your breath shiver. You can speak, and feel the chord push back against your teeth. But you cannot introduce a difference that lasts. Your footprint in the soft floor smooths itself. The crickets you displaced are somehow already back, antennae woven. Your shadow never appears.

It is not peaceful. Equilibrium rarely is, up close. A never-changing thing at this density and volume is an insistence: a tiny, hidden, unfinished room under the earth holding its single moment so tightly — bright, loud, cool, damp, crowded with life — that time, arriving at the root-slit, simply cannot find a place to sit down, and goes on without it.
