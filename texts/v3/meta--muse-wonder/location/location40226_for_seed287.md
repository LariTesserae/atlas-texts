You have to go down to it.

Not far — three flights of switchback iron stairs bolted to a retaining wall, a handrail hot enough to hold only through your sleeve — but the drop does something decisive. At the top there is glare and dry wind. By the second landing the light has thinned to a green-brown dimness, the wind is gone, and the heat has changed. It is no longer weather. It is enclosure heat, engine heat, held in a pit the size of a small cathedral.

The floor is maybe sixty meters across, oval, sunk well below the surrounding ground. Concrete walls rise on all sides, stained in long vertical fans of rust, lime, and black algae. Near the top, a broken lattice of steel grating and glass panels once roofed it — some panes still survive, opalescent with mineral film — and the plants have grown up to meet it, so what light enters has to push through glass, then leaf, then steam. At noon it is twilight down here. At noon, it is also forty-eight degrees.

It is loud in a way that lives in your teeth.

A low turbine note comes up through the soles of your boots, constant. Over it, slower, a set of industrial fans turn behind louvered housings on the east wall — not fast, but enormous, each blade passing with a soft, concussive *whup* that stirs the hanging vines. Somewhere out of sight, a pump keeps a patient, uneven rhythm, and every six or seven minutes the whole system sighs: valves tick open in sequence, pressure equalizes along the pipe runs, and a warm exhalation moves through the pit, ruffling leaves that have not felt true wind in decades.

That sigh is the closest thing here to weather.

This was a cooling station, once. A geothermal condenser, perhaps, or a factory's buried heat-exchange yard, built and then demoted and then half-abandoned without ever quite being shut off. The machinery is still industrial and still, in parts, running: squat boilers wrapped in cracked ceramic insulation, a rank of heat-exchange columns like organ pipes, copper mains going green at the joints, black rubber hoses, junction boxes with their doors hanging open, a tiled control booth at the far end with its windows sweated opaque from the inside. Catwalks cross the space at two heights and sag in the middle. Tool lockers have fallen face-forward. There is a single work shed, a loading winch, stencilled numbers on the walls that no longer correspond to anything anyone can read.

But the forgetting has not been fast, and it has not been a collapse. Everything is eroded into agreement. Rust has passed beyond flaking into a deep, pitted skin. Concrete has softened at the edges and grown coats of moss thick as upholstery. White and ochre mineral crusts feather out from every leak. The place looks less ruined than finished — as if the pit, left to itself long enough, finally settled on what it actually wanted to be.

And what it wanted, mostly, was plants.

Despite the dimness, despite there being almost no standing water anywhere, the vegetation is lush to the point of pressure. Ferns grow out of oil drums and cable trays. Broad, dark leaves — almost black-green, heavy as wet leather — unspool from the sides of the boilers. Vines have braided themselves through handrails and fan guards and into the split seams of insulation, emerging on the other side frilled with pale new growth. Moss climbs the north faces of pipes and stops, precisely, where the metal gets too hot to hold. There are fleshy, low rosettes in the gravel that open only when the steam passes. There are reeds, of a kind, standing in the shallow condensation pans beneath the coldest pipe runs, their roots white in an inch of warm water.

For the water here is intermittent and it falls in the wrong places. Nothing flows. Instead, moisture arrives as breath and settles. The turbines exhale steam; the steam touches the cooler underside of the roof lattice; beads form, swell, and let go — not everywhere, but in narrow, local rains that drift slowly across the pit over the course of an hour, following the temperature gradient. For a while it will be raining softly on the control booth and nowhere else. Then, imperceptibly, the rain will be falling through the fern bank by the stairs. Leaves have learned to be there. Under each habitual drip-line the growth is denser, glossier, taller. In between, the concrete stays merely damp, dark, faintly steaming, covered in a treacherous green film.

There is, impossibly, no animal life to go with all this green.

No flies, though the air is warm and wet. No ants in the gravel. No birds nesting in the fan housings, no lizards on the hot pipes, no moths around the caged amber work-lights that still burn along the catwalk. The silence underneath the machinery is absolute where a living smallness ought to be. It may be the ozone that hangs around the old electrical cabinets, sharp as a struck match. It may be the copper, leaching green into every damp surface. It may be the sound itself — that constant low note that would make a hollow bone of your skull if you stayed — driving out anything that navigates by vibration or breeds in still water. Even decay seems to have been assigned to other kingdoms: fallen leaves do not rot so much as subside into black mats of fungus and slime, which spread, patiently, over dead cables and discarded gloves and once, memorably, a boot.

The plants do not seem to need animals, or light, in the ordinary way. Up close you can feel why. Their leaves are warm. Some of them are warmer than the air, faintly, along the midrib, as if a slow current were moving inside them. They have turned away from the roof — once you notice, you see it everywhere — and angled themselves instead toward the hottest pipes, toward the boiler casings, toward the vibrating column of the main exchanger. Not toward brightness. Toward heat and tremor. When the pump stutters in its cycle, which it does once or twice an hour, whole banks of them answer, a barely perceptible tightening and relaxing of stems, a communal flinch six minutes long.

This is the rule of the pit, the thing it teaches your body if you linger: everything down here is coupled. The plants insulate the pipes, and where their cover dies back the metal overheats and the safeties vent, raising the humidity, which feeds the plants. The vines muffle the fans just enough to keep the note bearable; the fans stir the steam just enough to keep the leaves from cooking in place. Roots find the hairline leaks human maintenance would have sealed, drink there, swell, and seal them better. Rust feeds the moss its iron; the moss holds the damp against the concrete and keeps it from cracking in the heat. It is not a machine overgrown. It is not a garden with ruins in it. It is a third thing, running itself at a tempo too slow to fix and too steady to die.

Your own presence does not fit the tempo. Sweat comes immediately and does not cool you; the air is already saturated in patches, bone-dry between them, so you pass from sauna to kiln by taking three steps. Your shirt sticks. Your hearing dulls to the useful frequencies — a shout carries, a whisper is simply taken by the fans. Condensation will not fall on you where you stand, only where the gradient eventually carries its slow rain; people who have worked down here, if the chalk tallies in the control booth mean anything, used to move a chair a meter an hour all shift, chasing the drip, sitting in their own weather.

Stay an hour and your notion of scale shrinks to the pit's. The world becomes these walls, this density, everything within arm's reach of everything else: leaf against pipe, pipe against catwalk, catwalk against vine and cable and nesting trays of tools gone to oxide. Stay longer and the loudness stops feeling like noise and starts feeling like the medium you are suspended in, the way water is a medium. The steam sighs. The rain migrates. A leaf, fed on heat and iron and trembling air, opens in the dimness like it is listening.

Nothing here is in a hurry. Nothing here is waiting for anyone, either.
