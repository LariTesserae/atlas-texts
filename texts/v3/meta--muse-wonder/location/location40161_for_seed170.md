The High Anvil is not hot the way a desert is hot.

A desert is hot at the surface. This place is hot in its bones, hot at altitude where the air should be thin and freezing. Four kilometers above any sea that still exists somewhere below the haze, the plateau just keeps radiating. The basalt holds the day long after dark. The pumice fields tick and expand at noon. The black glass dunes — slow, settled, older than any weather you could name — give off a shimmer so thick it has texture, like looking through the exhaust of an engine the size of the horizon.

There is no water here. Not hidden, not deep. No aquifer, no seasonal pan, no dew worth collecting. What little moisture arrives with the wind from below evaporates fifty meters above the ground, drawn back up into an invisible, rising river. Sometimes, on still mornings, you can walk through a ghost-rain that never lands: cool on the forehead, gone on the tongue. The animals do not drink. Nothing here does. Their chemistry sealed itself off from water a very long time ago, and invented something else instead — hot oils, liquid salts, mercury-bright humors that move sluggishly through mineral veins and do not boil in this heat because they have never known how to be cool.

There is no vegetation either. No grass punching through the cracks, no scrub, no trees twisted by wind. The only living film on the rocks is a rust-colored crust in the lee of boulders, a chemolithotroph mat that eats iron and exhales a faint vinegar smell, tough as fired enamel. It takes a century to grow a hand's breadth. The grazers scrape it up anyway, patient as geology.

And from a distance, from any human vantage, the Anvil looks empty.

That is the first lie it tells, and the kindest.

### The Open

The plateau extends past what sight can finish. Yardangs carved by ten thousand years of grit-wind stand in long, parallel processions, all facing east, their tails drawn out for a hundred meters. Between them are flats of cracked obsidian, salt-white playas that are not salt but precipitated sulfur and borate, clicking underfoot. Fumaroles breathe at intervals so wide you could walk half a day from one to the next. The scale is regional, valley-after-valley without a valley's enclosure, just feature succeeding feature at a distance that makes travel feel ceremonial.

Density, in the visible sense, is almost nothing. A stone. An hour later, another stone that might be the same one. Empty sky, normal and pitilessly blue at this height, a sun the ordinary yellow-white it should be, no alien color, which somehow makes everything else harder to accept.

But your ears will disagree. Your feet will disagree. The ground is loud.

It is loud everywhere, all the time, a full industrial roar with no factory in sight. Pistons sighing in vast cylinders. Pressure valves releasing in long, controlled screams. The grind of rotary teeth on stone, the deep hydraulic whine of something enormous shifting its weight, the percussive clang of metal plate on metal plate, the crackle of high-voltage discharge. At night it does not diminish. If anything the cooling rock carries it farther. You learn to sleep inside it the way miners sleep inside a working seam, or not at all.

There are no buildings. No ruins, no rails, no slag heaps laid out by hands, no chimney. The industry here was never built.

It walks.

### The Herds

They are out of phase. That is the only useful way to say it.

A Smelter may appear, clearly and in full detail, two hundred meters to your left: thirty meters at the shoulder, its hide a overlapping scale of natural iron plate and black ceramic, six columnar legs with piston-joints that vent white steam-that-is-not-steam, a head lowered to the ground on a crane neck while diamond-edged mill-teeth quarry basalt in slow rotation. A furnace glow pulses in the slatted vents along its ribs. Copper-bright cables of tendon run exposed along its flanks and arc, occasionally, to the spines on its back, which drink lightning before there is a storm and shed it again in sheets. It is producing, audibly, the sound of a full foundry — smelting what it grazes, separating iron, silica, rare earths in gut-chambers hotter than the plateau, excreting at intervals a perfect, still-glowing ingot of slag-waste that cools over days into something indistinguishable from the natural boulders.

You will hear that Smelter to your *right*. Thirty meters away. Close enough that the ground jumps under your boots with a footfall it has not taken yet where you see it.

Its body is actually there, on your right, invisible and massive and displacing a wind you can feel on your skin. Its image is lagging, or leading, nobody has ever determined a consistent offset. Sometimes the displacement is ten meters. Sometimes a full minute in time: you watch it bend to graze on empty glass while, invisibly beside you, the real teeth are already grinding the crust you are standing on, and your fillings ache.

So the plateau is empty to the eye and *overwhelmingly* crowded in fact.

Every square meter is tracked over. Footprints six meters across press half a meter into glass sand, fade in a decade, and there are always newer ones on top. Herd-sign is everywhere: the ingot droppings in cooling rows, molts of conductor-sheath left like shed factory piping, scorched arcs where a bull grounded a charge through the earth and fused a circle of sand into a lens. Small things live in the attendance of large ones — hammer-mites that ride the piston joints and eat escaping sparks, turbine crows with fan-bladed primary feathers that spin in the exhaust updrafts and generate a shrill, needle whine, tick-like prospectors that clamp to a Smelter's belly and tap furnace heat to crack the ore-pellets they carry. The air at ankle height seethes with a glittering, iron-filing insect that navigates by the herds' magnetic fields, in such numbers that when the fields shift they fall all at once, a brief metallic rain.

Higher, the electrical ecology repeats itself: sail-backed gliders circling the thermals for years without landing, pregnant with static, trailing long filament tails that sometimes connect two animals in a sudden, blinding circuit. Predators exist — of course predators exist — long, low, furnace-mouthed things whose approach you detect not by sight but because your compass begins to spin and all the loose grit on the ground stands up on end.

Nothing here was engineered by anyone you could point to. The technology is evolved, inherited, grown. Gear teeth are molted and replaced. Dynamo organs are born gravid with a starter charge from the mother. A calf comes into the world already idling, its chest-pistons stuttering until the herd gathers around to jump-start it with their own fields, a low, tender booming you can feel in your sternum. They live for centuries. A single stride can take a minute to complete. A migration across the Anvil takes longer than a human civilization is likely to last, and yet the herd has a destination, and remembers it, passing the route down in infrasound too low and loud to be heard so much as suffered.

### The Rules, As Far As They Hold

Do not navigate by sound. Sound arrives first and is the least honest sense here.

Do not trust the visible distance of a large animal. Walk, instead, by paying attention to absence: the patch of plateau where the grinding *stops*, where the grit insects fall silent, where your own shadow suddenly bends — shadows fall toward the nearest real mass, not always away from the sun, when a herd is near. There are mornings when every rock has two shadows, one solar and one animal, and the second is moving.

Distances themselves are compliant when nothing is looking, and stubborn when a herd is passing through them. A fumarole an hour away can take three days to reach during a crossing; your footprints behind you stay fresh for a week and then, some nights, you find a second set pacing them, larger, filling in over yours. Echoes do not repeat what you shout. They repeat what you are about to shout, a fraction early, flattened by heat, and occasionally they answer in the voices of the plateau, which are valve-screams and piston-sighs shaped almost like syllables, and it is unwise to keep the conversation going.

Gravity mostly works. In the sulfur playas it occasionally remembers an older agreement, and loose plate-scales — shed, animal, enormous — drift a few centimeters off the ground for an afternoon, turning slowly, ringing faintly, before settling back with a sigh of displaced air.

None of this happens quickly. The weirdness has the same tempo as everything else: slow, established, eroded into routine. A reality fault in the east basin has been in the process of happening for nine hundred years. In the center of it, a Smelter is perpetually mid-footfall, leg raised, image smeared across perhaps forty horizontal copies of itself like a film slipped in the projector — and the *real* animal walked out of that spot long ago, or never arrived, or arrives a little more each spring. The locals — there are no locals, but the plateau has opinions — would say it is still deciding.

### What It Is Like To Stay

Your lungs will complain first. The air is thin at this elevation and hot going down, chemical with ozone and sulfur and hot dust, and every breath near a herd carries the taste of a struck match and the prickle of a field building on your skin. Your hair stands. Your instruments lie or give up. At noon the glass flats are loud simply from expanding, a crackling like distant applause that never quite starts.

By evening the light behaves again — an ordinary sunset, red from dust — and the whole regional expanse comes into its most honest state: a nearly empty, black-glass furnace floor, eroded into long calm shapes, thermally breathing, populated past bearing by machines that are animals and animals that are infrastructure, all of them displaced a little from where they seem to be, all of them roaring and grinding and sighing at full industrial volume into a sky where no rain lands.

You will see perhaps four creatures before dark.

You will be aware, in your teeth, in your shadow, in the standing grit, of thousands.
