You come in low, through a gate that was made for stooping.

It is fired clay, the gate — two short pillars of unglazed brick, a lintel of charred wood bound with rope — and on the other side of it there is no outside to look back at. Not in the way you expect. If you turn, the corridor you followed is already planted over, its tiled floor covered knee-deep with dry ferns. Ordinary daylight falls on everything. That is the worst part. Nothing hides.

The place is small, and it is entirely made.

Sixty, perhaps eighty paces across, sunken in a shallow bowl of terraces. There is no ground. Underfoot are tiles: square, hexagonal, long boat-shaped tiles, tiles glazed green and then unglazed again by heat, laid edge to edge without a gap wide enough for dirt. Where a tile is lifted — and keepers are always lifting them, with bone wedges and wooden mallets — there is another tile beneath it, cleaner than the first. Beneath that, presumably, another. The soil here is a manufactured thing carried in baskets: crushed brick, ash, powdered lime, shredded reed, black grit from the kilns. It is spooned into built beds, into wall-pockets, into hanging pots, into the hollows of roof-beams. Nothing grows from the earth because no earth has ever been admitted.

And yet it is lush to the point of embarrassment.

Vines have taken the arches and made second, softer arches beneath them. Feather-palms rise from chimney pots, their fronds dry as paper fans, warm to the touch and faintly crackling. In the beds there are cabbages of waxed green leaves that never wilt, thickets of resinous herbs, flowers the color of wet paint — magenta, copper-red, a blue so saturated it hums against the terracotta — that open tissue-dry and stay open for months. Tendrils find every mortar joint. A kind of moss, though it is not moss and never damp, spreads in emerald felt over the shaded sides of the walls, and the keepers scrape it with wooden combs and re-lay it like carpet.

Nothing here drinks. There is no well with water in it, no cistern, no dew in the morning. The central well, beautifully bricked, with a wooden windlass and a rope in perfect repair, lowers its bucket into a darkness that smells of hot stone, and brings it up again — slowly, over the course of an afternoon — full of seeds. Small, black, warm seeds, none of which anyone remembers putting there. Rain is a story the oldest tiles contradict. If a leaf is cut it does not bleed sap. It exudes a clear oil that hardens in the light, or a fine perfumed dust, or nothing at all, just a smell of heated straw.

It is warm the way a kiln yard is warm an hour after firing. Heat lives in the brick and is given back evenly, day and night, though nights are only somewhat cooler and the stars, when the roof-vines part, are entirely ordinary.

The keepers live here in a hamlet folded into the garden, or the garden lives in the hamlet; the distinction has been built away. Houses are low platforms of plastered clay with roofs of woven reed laid over curved poles, each roof planted, each wall carrying its own shelf of pots. Looms stand in doorways. Baskets are being made everywhere and finished nowhere in particular. A woman cards grey fiber beside a bed of scarlet lilies. A man reties a trellis with strips of bark, using his teeth. Children move the pots — this is the main labor — a hand's breadth sunward each week, following marks chalked generations ago. All the tools are wood, horn, fired clay, knotted cord. A copper knife, much honored, is used only to cut rope for funerals.

And the animals. For a place so quiet, it is crowded with life.

Lizards occupy every sunlit tile in shifts, patterned exactly like the tiles they are not currently on. Beetles polished black and green carry grains of brick-dust from bed to bed on their backs, deliberate as porters. Moths come out in the full noon and sit on the flowers with wings open, their bodies fat, their flight completely silent. There are birds — small, brown, abundant, tame enough to be stepped around — that open their beaks on the perches and produce a visible, trembling effort and no note at all. Dry frogs the color of lime plaster live in the empty rain-barrels. Snails without any shine to them move through the felt-moss leaving a trail that is not slime but a faint rearrangement of the fibers, as though the moss had been combed. Ants cross the terraces in lines that go up walls and continue, without hesitation, across ceilings and down the far side, through the houses, over the sleeping mats, around — never over — a sleeping child.

Sound does not travel properly here, which accounts for much of the quiet. A mallet tap at your elbow is soft. A mallet tap across the bowl is softer, then, a moment later, arrives again inside your teeth. Footsteps are eaten by the felt, the reed matting, the leaf litter that is always dry and never deep. Sometimes a tile ticks as it expands. Sometimes, from the central kiln that has not been lit in living memory and is warm anyway, there is a sound like a long breath being decided against.

At first the geometry seems merely dense. Paths wind because the beds are full. But you will walk a tiled lane between two planted walls for what must be twenty minutes, at a patient pace, past the same blue pot three times — each time a little more overgrown — and emerge at the gate you entered by, facing inward. The terraces are low; you can see the far wall from anywhere. You simply cannot always reach it by walking toward it. Staircases of nine steps sometimes deliver you to a roof. Sometimes to the same nine steps, from above. The keepers navigate by tasks rather than directions: not *north of the well* but *after the re-potting*, not *behind the kiln* but *before the vines are cut*. If you ask how long they have lived here, they show you the carvings on the gate pillars — tally marks, birds, a spiral of thumbprints — all of them sharp-edged, unsmoothed, as if cut last spring, though the bricks beneath the carving have darkened with a century of hands.

Nothing erodes here. That takes longer to notice than the loops. Edges stay fresh. Rope frays backward, its fibers rejoining over a season if it is left in use. A dropped pot cracks, and the crack, over slow weeks, fills with a paler clay that the keepers do not apply. This is not miraculous to them; it is why sweeping is done so carefully and so slowly, with short brooms, in the afternoons. The brooms must be persuaded, not hurried. Left leaning in a doorway at dusk, a broom will often be found at dawn a few inches into its work, the dust arranged in a small, perfect arc. No one comments. The tools were made here. Making, here, is a habit things can catch.

The plants have caught it furthest. A cutting taken from a vine and laid on a tile will, over the next month, manufacture its own pot — first a suggestion of grit around the cut end, then a wall, then a rim — coarse, serviceable, fired without fire. Seeds planted on a Tuesday were, the previous Friday, already seedlings in the neighboring bed; the keepers therefore plant faithfully and without surprise, to give the seedlings a past they can rely on. Flowers face the sun at noon. At dusk they do not close. They turn, very slowly, to face where the sun was at noon three days ago, and the terraces fill with this outdated light-seeking, beautiful and mildly unbearable.

Fauna becomes vegetation without any visible crisis. An old bird, feathers going pale, will take to standing in a bed for longer and longer portions of the day until its feet are difficult to distinguish from stems. A lizard left undisturbed on a wall becomes, across a summer, an arrangement of glazed scales among the leaves, breathing — you would swear — after it has become ornament. What becomes of old plants, the children can show you if you are patient: a palm at the end of its growth sighs its dry fronds once against the tiles, and in the morning there is one more small brown bird on the roof beam, blinking, already knowing where the brick-dust is carried.

The keepers themselves are not exempt, only slower. Their skin takes the warmth wonderfully. Their fingernails grow in season, in rings, like reed. The eldest keeper — everyone agrees she is eldest, though she has the sharp, fresh face of someone recently carved — sits by the dry well winding rope that lengthens as she winds it, and tells you, in the tone of a person discussing weather, that the well was dug before the garden was built around it, and the garden was built before the well was dug, and both statements are kept because each does different work.

Stay for a meal if you are offered one. It will be seeds, roasted leaves, a paste of ash-white tubers grown in a chimney and tasting of smoke and pepper, eaten from a bowl still slightly warm from having made itself around last year's meal. Eat slowly. Everything here proceeds at the pace of a crack unforming in clay. On your skin, after a few days, the dust will begin to settle in patterns — along the creases of your knuckles, tile-joint fine. It does not wash off. There is, after all, no water to wash it with, and by then the gate, when you stoop to look for it, will be a little further along the path than it was, planted over behind you, patiently expecting you to arrive.
