At the top there is no mountain left to stand on.

The peak has been capped, levelled, and paved over so completely that the last approach is not a climb but a stair inside masonry — forty-one broad, shallow steps of hand-made brick rising through a roofed gallery, the walls built out over the drop on corbelled courses until the natural rock is only a rumor underneath. You come up stooped. The air gets warmer as you get higher, which is wrong for the altitude, and thinner, and drier, and then it begins to press on your ears.

Not near the top. Long before the top.

The place itself is small. One chamber, perhaps twelve paces across if it were empty, under a single dome. It has taken centuries to make it this crowded.

It was built by people who had stone-cutting and brick-firing and rope and beaten copper, and nothing finer. You can see it everywhere once your eyes adjust to the dimness: thumbprints still pressed into the clay, bricks that slumped a little in the firing and were used anyway, mud mortar squeezed out in beads between courses, wooden lintels gone dark and hard as iron. No machined surface anywhere. No right angle holds for long. And yet nothing is crude in effect. They knew exactly what they were doing with air.

The dome has no opening at its crown. Light comes through nine slits high on the south-east side, each stopped with a lopsided sheet of mica, amber with age and dust, so the light that gets in is brown and tiered and falls in visible sheets. It is never bright. At what must be noon outside it is like late evening in here, and the dust shows in the light not as motes but as membranes, trembling planes hanging in the air, because the dust is not drifting.

It is being held.

The sound is the architecture. Everything else is just what has been built to house it.

It is deafening in the simple, physical sense — past shouting, past thought at the front of the head, a single continuous tone with a roar folded inside it that fills your sinuses and makes your teeth ache in their sockets and sets the skin between your ribs buzzing. Coming up the stair it does not get gradually louder the way a waterfall does. It crosses thresholds. First your ears ring. Then the ringing is irrelevant. Then the sound stops feeling like something you hear and becomes the medium you are moving through, like wading into deep water, except there is no water here at all, there has never been water here, your mouth dries in minutes and your lips go papery and the inside of your nose stings with hot dust.

And it does not change.

That is the uncanny tenure of the place. Outside, above the paved summit platform, wind must be scouring the high slopes and clouds must be tearing past — this is high country, the light through the mica brightens and dims almost imperceptibly as things pass the sun. Nothing in the tone answers it. No gust makes it swell. No lull lets it fall. Hour after hour it holds the same pitch, the same unbearable volume, the same pressure against the sternum, as if it were not being produced but simply existed here, the way weight exists. The stair gallery below acts as a buffer, a long bent throat of brick. Somewhere under the floor, sealed in and inaccessible, is a much larger chamber over a warm fissure, and the rising hot air passes an edge — a shaped lip of fired clay the size of a doorway — and the whole system locked, centuries ago, into a resonance that the mountain's weather cannot reach. The builders tuned out the world.

The chamber is packed solid with the tuning.

Hundreds of clay vessels hang and stand and are wedged in ranks from the floor to the springing of the dome: globular pots with narrow mouths, tall tubes open at both ends, double-chambered jars joined at the belly, shallow bowls inverted on pegs, ranks of bottle-shaped resonators shoulder to shoulder on tiered brick shelves that ring the walls five deep. Most are plain fired earth, red, buff, smoke-blackened. Some are slipped white and have gone grey. Between them, partitions of thin brick, no thicker than a hand, divide the round room into a tight spiral labyrinth, so you cannot walk straight to the center. You turn sideways. Your shoulder brushes a hanging pot and — nothing. No clink, no swing. It is already vibrating against its rope, against its neighbors, a blur at the rim, and your touch is absorbed like touching a beehive through a wall.

Everything in here is fretting itself slowly to powder. That is the erosion. Not rain, not wind, not feet. The vessels have worn each other where they touch, bright polished facets grinding out on dull clay, rims rounded, shelf edges scalloped by a thousand years of minute shaking. The steps outside are hollowed not so much by pilgrims as by the grit that the sound keeps in perpetual agitation, scouring. Fine ceramic dust covers the floor in drifts — but not evenly. It collects in curved ridges and crescents, swept and held into patterns by the standing wave, little dunes along the quiet lines of the room, bare polished brick along the loud ones. If you scuff a drift with your foot it does not stay scuffed. Over minutes, visibly, the grains crawl and resettle. The room keeps redrawing its own floor.

There is no life. No plant has rooted in the mortar; the heat and the dryness and the vibration would not allow it, and the masonry skin is total — floor, wall, dome, platform, stair, all made, no soil anywhere for a seed to consider. No bird nests in the eaves. No insect has established itself in the warm dark corners, though the warmth, a steady blood-heat that soaks out of the floor bricks and makes the chamber feel uncomfortably like the inside of a body, would suit them. Nothing animal comes here and stays. The tone empties it. What collects instead is mineral: a faint efflorescence blooming on the lower bricks in feathery white maps, salts sweated out of the clay over centuries and crystallized in the sound-nodes into patterns so regular they look woven. In dim light they glitter slightly when you move, all together, as your angle changes — and then, because the vibration never stops, a tiny piece will occasionally detach and fall straight down through air that feels too thick for it.

You cannot speak in here. Not as a rule — there is no one to enforce rules, no keeper, no inscription, the builders long gone and whoever added the last, smallest tubes to the outermost shelf also long gone — but as a fact. Your voice goes into your own skull and stops. If two people entered, they would have to communicate by touch, by writing in the dust ridges and watching the letters jitter apart. Objects behave oddly in small predictable ways. Loose things migrate to the quiet lines. A dropped copper disc will skitter, half-levitated on its air cushion, until it finds a node and settles, ringing faintly. Your own body is an obstacle the wave goes around, and you can feel the shape of your acoustic shadow behind you, a cooler-feeling, lesser pressure in the small of your back.

And then, following the spiral in, the labyrinth does the thing it was built to do.

The partitions tighten. The vessels get smaller and older toward the center, fist-sized, thumb-printed, black with firing, hanging in a dense cluster like fruit. The pressure builds and builds in your chest until you are braced for it to become impossible —

— and the last half-step takes you into a space the size of a standing person where it cancels.

Not silence. You can still feel it in the floor through your feet, in your molars, in the dome overhead working like a drum skin. But at head height, in a column no wider than your shoulders, the waves arriving from every side meet and erase each other, and you are suddenly inside the loudest place in the world listening to the blood move in your own ears. It is warm. It is still. The dust there hangs absolutely unmoving in a single brown shaft of mica-light, the only unmoving thing. Your breathing sounds enormous and ragged. Your heartbeat sounds indecent.

The builders left the center empty. It is the only emptiness they allowed.

How long the column holds you is not a decision your ears make. After a while the ache behind the eyes, the dried mouth, the sheer metabolic effort of standing in that much energy begins to tell, and you back out through the spiral — sideways, brushing the blurred humming pots, through the tiered brown light and the dancing dust-membranes, the pressure closing around your head again all at once as you leave the center, down the brick stair through the long throat where the tone sheds itself in stages, out onto a paved platform in thin high sunlight where there is, again, no natural ground under your feet, only courses of old brick going down over a vanished summit.

Behind you the dome goes on sounding. It was sounding in the night. It will be sounding in the winter, under snow that never settles on its warm crown, at the same pitch, at the same volume, over its floor of sorted dust and its salts and its thousand worn vessels, empty.

It is not waiting for anything. It has arrived.
