You come to it from below, and for a long time you do not believe the sound is water.

The escarpment rises out of cloud for almost five thousand metres — not a peak, not a ridge you can point to, just a wall of old country lifted whole. The road up is cut into black granite so rounded, so polished by age, that it has no edges left to cut back. This was a mountain range once, Himalayan in its youth, and then it was given several hundred million years of weather. The high summits wore down to their roots. The valleys filled with their own ruins and wore down too. What was left was a peneplain, a table of stone planed almost flat, and then, much later, the whole table was heaved up into the sky again.

On top, the rivers never learned how to leave.

So the top is drowned.

It is a sea at altitude, in bright ordinary daylight, with the plains of the low country visible on clear mornings far beneath a second sea of cloud. The air up here is thin and cool enough to show your breath in summer, cool enough that the water coming off the table at noon will numb your hands in a minute. And yet everything is green, and wet, and growing at a frantic rate, because the water never stops arriving.

The prevailing wind hits the escarpment, is forced straight up, cools, and empties itself. For a hundred days it rains, not always hard, but always returning — warm sun for an hour under a blue sky, then a grey wall rolling across the table, then sun again striking steam off the reeds. The plateau catches it all. Its surface is too flat, its rock too old and sealed, for drainage to organize itself into a single river. Instead there are hundreds of shallow lakes joined by channels, flooded meadows under a hand's breadth of clear water, peat bogs under a metre of brown water, and low granite domes rising like the backs of whales, the only reliably dry ground for miles.

From any dome the view opens for thirty, forty kilometres — lake, reed-bed, lake, meadow, dome — repeated until the glare takes it. Dense does not mean crowded with land. It means layered. Trout move under submerged grass. White water-lilies open over black depths where the old soil drowned. Sedge grows five feet high out of standing water, and behind the sedge is more water, full of frogs. There are islands of floating turf, torn loose in a storm, drifting a mile in a day with a whole miniature meadow and sometimes a nesting grebe still aboard.

And everywhere, movement.

The table may be flat, but the volume is enormous. Every channel runs. Sinkholes in the limestone patches at the northern end suck whole ponds down with a permanent whirlpool and a hollow gulping roar, to resurge two kilometres away in springs that boil up through sand. Rain-swollen lakes overtop their reed banks in the afternoon and sheet across the meadows, ankle-deep, fast enough to push against your legs. By morning the level has fallen again, leaving fish stranded in warm puddles and herons stabbing methodically through the wreckage.

At the rim, all of that water finds its exit at once.

No single waterfall could carry it. The southern and eastern edges are a frayed lip of the hardest granite, over which fifty, sixty rivers pour in separate white threads that merge halfway down into cloud and thunder. Standing ten kilometres inland you hear them as a constant bass note through the stone underfoot. Standing at the rim itself you shout. The spray climbs a thousand metres back up the cliff in its own weather system, drenching a vertical forest of moss, ferns, dwarf birch bent permanently inland, and orchids jammed into every wet crack. Swifts live in that updraft permanently, tens of thousands of them, screaming, hunting the insects that are also being carried up. Otters — improbable at this altitude until you have seen how many fish there are — hunt the plunge pools on ledges halfway down, in water no lowland river could keep so cold and so oxygenated.

Industry learned what the birds learned: use the lip.

There is no city up here, no town even, but there is iron. A century and a half ago engineers hauled boilers and riveted turbines up the escarpment road in pieces and built low, slate-roofed power houses on the driest domes at the rim. Each one sits alone, taking its river through a stone leat as big as a canal, spinning its generators day and night, sending current down the face on pylons that vanish into cloud. The sound inside is not distinguishable from the sound outside, only more regular — a turbine's howl locked inside the falls' roar. Rust streaks everything. Flumes of creosoted timber run for miles across flooded country on stone piers, leaking at every joint, green with algae. Locks lift cargo punts from lake to lake, though the difference in level may be only a man's height.

The scattering of other buildings obeys the water's rule: build on domes, or do not build. Stone bothies with roofs weighted down against the wind, boathouses half-submerged by design, a chapel on the highest dome with its graveyard in imported soil, because there is nowhere on the flat where a grave would not fill. Causeways run between settlements a few inches under the surface, marked by posts; you walk them apparently walking on the lake, water streaming over your boots, minnows scattering ahead of you. In a surge you take the boats — flat-bottomed, poled, fitted with small electric motors charged at the power houses — and you navigate by reed patterns, by bells.

The bells are the other sound under the waterfalls. Every channel village, every turbine house, every lock keeps one, not for time but for water. A particular pattern means a lake upstream has overtopped. Another means the sinkholes are pulling hard. People up here read a rise the way lowlanders read a sky. The fauna does too — frogs go silent a quarter-hour before a surge front arrives across a meadow, and the bitterns and rails and moorhens explode up out of the sedge in a single clamour, and then the brown wave comes, carrying seeds, dragonflies, cut grass, somebody's lost bucket, a raft of flowering turf.

Summer life runs hot despite the cold water. The breeding season has to be short and it is therefore frantic. Clouds of midges and mayflies over every sheltered bay. Dragonflies hunting them in the sun between showers, fish rising under them, terns diving through the rises. Amphibians in numbers that make night travel by lantern surreal — the shallows are beaded with eyes, the chorus from the reed-beds is almost as loud as the rim. Alpine flowers grow not on dry turf but in the saturated margin between water and stone: gentians, butterworts, cotton grass, marsh orchids, all rooted in black peat that quakes underfoot. The few trees — rowan, birch, stunted pine — grow on domes and in the lee of domes, their roots in cracks, their trunks furred with moss to the crown.

There are no dry smells here. The place smells of cold stone, peat, crushed sedge, hot electrical machinery from the power houses, wet wool, woodsmoke that flattens under the damp air and drifts across the water in blue sheets. Your boots never dry. Paper swells. Iron rusts in weeks; brass and slate and granite are the honest materials.

By evening, if the rain pauses, the whole table steams. Mist forms first on the warmest shallow lakes, then joins into one layer a few feet deep, out of which domes and reed-heads and the roofs of boathouses rise. The rim-thunder continues, unseen. Bells answer each other across the mist, nearer and stranger than they should be in the thin air. Something large — a pike, an otter — rolls in the channel beside the causeway.

Nothing up here feels young, and nothing feels still. The rock reached its final shape before there were trees to root in it; the water, arriving new every day at the top of the world, refuses to let that shape rest.
