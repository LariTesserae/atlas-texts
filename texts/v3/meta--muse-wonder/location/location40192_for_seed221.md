It begins as a vibration in the teeth of the mountain.

Not on the mountain — *in* it, or in what has been put inside it. From outside, at that height, there is almost nothing to see. Wind-scoured snow, a service building of poured concrete squatting beside the fourth pylon of the hydroelectric tramway, the cable going up and down into cloud without moving. The air is thin and clean and brutally cold, the light above the snow so white it erases shadow. Silence, except for the wind finding the edges of sheet metal.

If you press your gloved hand to the south wall of the turbine house, the concrete hums.

There is a hatch behind the main manifold, low, oval, the size of a ship's door, painted safety yellow a long time ago and never repainted. Its wheel handle wears a beard of rime on the outside. On the inside the same handle is wet and warm — not warm, exactly, but always precisely above freezing, beaded with water that never quite falls. You need both hands, and ear protection, and then more ear protection than you brought.

Because this is the rule of the place: it is very, very small, and it is deafening.

The room is nine feet long, six feet wide, and not quite tall enough to stand in. That is all of it. A pocket. It was not dug, it was made — every surface of it. Floor, walls, ceiling, even the lumpy boulders in the corners are cast concrete shells bolted together over steel ribs, their timber formwork still visible in the grain of the pour. There is no rock in here, no soil horizon, no natural substrate at all. Galvanized panels, rivets, copper pipe, rubber gaskets, ceramic drain tile glazed the pale green of a swimming pool. Conduit runs in perfect right angles. A single caged bulb burns dim amber behind wire glass, and a second, smaller pilot light, green, reflects everywhere because everywhere is wet.

It is a new cave, embryonic. The concrete is still curing in spirit — still leaching lime. From the ceiling pipes hang the first stalactites, no longer than eyelashes, soft white nubs that will need a hundred years to become anything. Mineral has only just begun to learn how to be geology here. The floor drain has only just begun to stain. Nothing is eroded. Nothing is old. It is construction that has paused right at the point of becoming a place.

Water is at two centimeters over the tiles, at eleven centimeters in the sump channel, at a permanent mist in the air. Misters in the ceiling — industrial nozzles, brass, scaled — exhale every few seconds, though you cannot hear their hiss. Condensation crawls down the pipes and gathers and trembles. Weeping is constant: the walls weep, the valves weep, the undersides of the fake boulders weep. Your clothes are damp in a minute, and then cold through, because the temperature holds at two degrees Celsius. It has always held at two degrees. The dial thermostat, its glass fogged from inside, says so. The pressure gauge says so with a needle that vibrates furiously and does not move.

Vegetation, what there is of it, was introduced in pads and has mostly refused. A few cushions of moss, nearly black-green in the dimness, cling to the pipe joints where the mist hits them directly. A film of algae makes the sump water slick and faintly luminous where the bulb touches it. One pale fern, bleached almost to translucence, has put out three fronds from a crack between two concrete shells and stopped, perfectly, at three. White rootlets explore the grout lines and go nowhere. There is just enough green to feed the lower levels of things, and no more.

Because the animal life has taken the invitation completely differently.

They are frogs, first — boreal chorus frogs, thousands of them, each no longer than your thumb, olive and brown and striped down the back like wet seeds. They coat the pipes. They stack two and three deep on the valve wheels. They line the fake boulders at the waterline, shoulder to shoulder, throat sacs inflating. They float in the sump with only their eyes above the surface, and the surface itself is moving with smaller life — white isopods by the uncountable hundreds, water fleas, a living grain in the water. The floor where it is not submerged is carpeted with cave crickets, their antennae longer than their bodies, all of them slowly waving. Above them, wedged into the concrete seams of the ceiling, are moths, pale, dense as shingles, wings folded, waiting for a night that never arrives inside.

And they are all calling. Or the frogs are calling, and the crickets are rasping underneath, and the moths and isopods and dripping are adding their friction to it, and the concrete-and-steel box is taking the sound and refusing to let any of it die. It comes back off the tile, off the water, off the copper. It arrives at 117 decibels and it stays there. Not rising, not falling. A single, total, layered chorus chord, so constant and so complete that after the first physical pain of it — in your sternum, in the fillings of your teeth, in the bones behind your eyes — it begins to feel less like sound than like pressure. Like being underwater. Your own voice, if you shout, makes no dent in it. The water in the sump stands in tiny, regular standing waves that do not travel.

Some droplets, very small ones, do not fall. That is the strangest part, and it takes a while to notice because your vision is vibrating. Near the loudest pipe junction, where the frogs are thickest and facing, all of them, in the same direction, like an audience, mist droplets hang in the air at the nodes of the sound, each one catching the amber bulb for a fraction longer than gravity allows. They jitter. They hold. Below them, a drip forms on a valve, grows, detaches, falls, and hits the pool exactly — exactly — as another forms. The pump under the floor recirculates. The mist exhales. Condensation returns what evaporation took, at the same rate, in the same places. A cricket eats algae. A frog eats a cricket in a movement so quick it is mostly sound. Somewhere in the water, eggs unwrinkle and become more frogs, at the precise pace at which old frogs become still and become part of the green film.

Nothing here is progressing. That is the deeper rule, underneath the noise. This was a prototype — you can read it in the stenciled plate by the hatch, if you squint: ARTIFICIAL HIBERNACULUM MODULE 0, TEST ATMOSPHERE, DO NOT STOCK — built by a crew who wanted to learn whether a completely constructed cave could hold an ecosystem steady while the real caves above the treeline failed. They built it total. They powered it with waste heat and a pump and a thermostat. They stocked it anyway, and sealed it, and the mountain winter sealed it further, and the company changed hands twice.

It found its note and locked. Two degrees. Ninety-eight percent humidity. One hundred and seventeen decibels. Eleven centimeters. Three fronds. The frogs that were singing when the hatch was last opened seven years ago are, in every way that can be measured from the hatch, still singing. Individual throats tire and others take the exact pitch. The population count a biologist once did through the porthole, and did again the next season, came out identical to the animal. Not similar. Identical.

Outside the hatch, altitude and weather will kill you in an hour if you are careless. Inside it, in this hidden, high, freezing, sodden shoebox of industry, life has become so dense and so loud that it has stopped needing time. You can watch a frog mid-leap from one pipe to another, perpetually startled and perpetually landing. You can feel the sound holding the frost off the inner walls in a perfect, narrow margin. You can stay until your lips go numb and your ears ring straight through the plugs.

When you spin the wheel closed behind you — and you will, you cannot think in there, only endure — the yellow hatch takes the chorus and folds it back down to a hum in the concrete, a vibration in the teeth of the mountain. The snow goes on being silent and enormous above it. And in the dark, at two degrees, on the wet tile, the chord does not pause to notice that you were there.
