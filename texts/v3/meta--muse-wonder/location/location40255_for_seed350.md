First you hear it, before you are even in it.

Not a sound you listen to. A pressure. It comes through your teeth, through the water under your feet, through the fillings in the back of your jaw. A roar so complete it makes a kind of second, deafening silence inside itself — rain hammering, turbines screaming, water falling in sheets, steam venting, pumps beating in a rhythm too fast and too irregular to be a rhythm. When you shout, you don't hear yourself go missing. You feel the shout happen in your throat and then nothing, as if the air had already been used up by louder things.

This is a flooded lowland, a warm basin that never finished deciding whether it was land.

It might have been a valley once. Now it's a shallow inland sea, town-sized, valley-sized, stretching dim and green to a horizon lost in rain. The water is everywhere and at every height. It lies two, five, ten meters deep over drowned fields. It falls ceaselessly from a sky you cannot see. It hangs in the air as mist thick enough to breathe wrong. It runs *up* tree trunks in beaded veins. It sheets sideways off leaves. In places it falls upward — whole curtains of warm rain lifting out of the black surface and vanishing into the canopy, droplet by droplet at first, then in ropes, then in sudden, silent columns that the roar somehow swallows.

The light is a deep, wet dusk even at noon. Cloud, canopy, and water have divided what sun there is between them and kept most of it. What remains is green and amber: green filtered through a million wet leaves, amber from sodium work-lights strung on cables underwater, glowing beneath the surface like a drowned street grid. They flicker as things pass over them — not fish. There are no fish. Shadows of leaves. Shadows of moving machinery.

The vegetation is lush to the point of aggression. Mangroves with broad, oil-slick leaves stand in dense, near-touching ranks, their prop roots braced in the current. Ferns the size of houses unfold black-green fronds that curl and uncurl in minutes. Vines as thick as industrial hosing climb anything vertical, then keep climbing into empty air, searching. Moss covers stone in a luminous, saturated carpet that steams faintly in the warmth. Everything is young, sharp, un-eroded. The stone outcrops are fresh basalt ribs, still angular, newly heaved out of the water this season or this week. The plants have not softened them yet. They split them instead; roots wedge into cracks and audibly — impossibly audibly, under everything — tick as they expand.

And there is no animal life. Not a bird through the canopy, not an insect on a leaf, not a frog nor a worm nor a minnow turning in the shallows. The leaves are uneaten. The flowers — huge, waxy, white and bruise-purple — open and rot unvisited, dropping whole into the water to be shredded by propellers. The only movement that isn't plant or machine or water is the pollen: dense, yellow-green clouds of it shedding off the canopy in the rain, coating your skin, your eyes, the water's surface, though nothing ever fertilizes anything in a way that makes sense. Seeds the size of fists fall like hail, crack open on impact, and are already rooting before the splash settles. Some of them root in the past. You will pass a sapling, note it, pass it again an hour later on a channel that didn't exist an hour ago, and find it a buttressed giant strangling a pump house with yesterday's vine already flowering around windows that have not yet been built.

That is the rule here: cause does not keep its place.

The industry is scattered and total. There are no grand factory halls, just small structures everywhere, half-submerged, repeated until repetition becomes a system. Pump houses on concrete stilts. Valve stations roofed in corrugated green plastic. Catwalks that connect nothing to nothing and end over open water. Diesel substations sweating heat into the flood, their exhaust stacks coughing black, vertical rain. Dredge arms, conveyor gantries, centrifuge towers wrapped in fern, a lattice of pipe — steel, ceramic, translucent flex-pipe where you can see bright chemical water pulsing the wrong way. Everything new, painted safety yellow and institutional teal, edges still sharp, bolts still bright, as if installed last month and abandoned to the jungle the same afternoon. Yet it is running. All of it, all at once, at full capacity. Turbines spin inside flooded housings. Pistons drive underwater and throw up crowns of spray with each stroke. A row of cooling towers, squat and moss-capped, does not release steam upward: they inhale the storm clouds and exhale boiling water at their bases.

What do they produce? Water. As far as you can follow the pipes, the system takes water from below the basin and delivers it above the basin. Deep wells hammer day and night, shaking the submerged ground. The water comes up hot and mineral and faintly lit, is filtered through mangrove root beds grown over copper mesh, is sprayed, churned, electrified between humming pylons until it crawls with blue sparks, and is then released to fall as rain. Or to rise as rain. The product feeds the input. The factory is a loop trying to outrun a flood by flooding itself faster.

Because the place is turbulent, not metaphorically. Channels cut and seal in hours. A dredged canal will boil, silt itself shut with a sudden bar of black sand and root-mat, and open a kilometer away through what was solid pump-yard. Floating islands of vegetation — hectares of interwoven reed, vine, and young trees with their own soil — break off, spin in the current, collide with infrastructure and either tear it loose or are torn apart and pulped through intakes, their green shred storming out of outflow pipes downstream. New islands precipitate out of nothing at the convergence of two currents, first a slick, then a mat, then shoots. The water level doesn't rise; it *redistributes*. A pump house that stood in ankle-deep wash at dawn is a roof-peak at dusk, and by night the water around it is flowing through its second-story windows, carrying leaves through rooms where gauges still swing and alarms still patiently blink unheard.

Reality frays most at the edges of sound. Where the roar peaks — near the main turbine bays, where ten engines couple through a shared flooded shaft and try to tear each other apart — the rain hesitates. Droplets stop, tremble, and arrange themselves into brief grids, into text-like patterns too fast to read, into the shape of the leaf they were about to hit, an instant before hitting it. Your reflection in the black water does the plant's part of this: it grows. Not metaphorically. Thin green filaments spread across your reflected face, root at the eye. Above water, your hands stay clean, if soaked. Below the surface, everything has already become something else.

Travel is by flat-bottomed electric skiff, by catwalk, by wading chest-deep and holding a cable. The current takes suggestions from the pumps, from the windless storm, from underground hammering, and sometimes simply reverses for no reason, sliding whole rafts of fern past you in dead silence under the roar. Landmarks persist but not locations. The twin stacks are always visible through the murk, northwest of everywhere. The drowned chapel — if it was a chapel, a small concrete utility shrine with a cross-shaped vent — is always nearby, never approached. Its interior is dry. Everyone who has entered it insists on this separately, and that inside, the sound finally stops, and in the stop you can hear the plants growing, and that is worse.

At night the dimness deepens to a luminous black. The underwater lights come up through the flood like a second, inverted town. The vegetation phosphoresces faintly where the electrified water touches it, every leaf edge stitched in green fire. The machines do not slow. The rain does not slow. Upward falls catch the light and burn as they rise.

You will leave soaked through to the marrow, warm, half-deaf for days, with seeds rooting in the seams of your boots and a feeling that the basin is still busy behind you, inventing and drowning its own shores, feeding its storm with its flood, growing a forest at industrial speed over machinery built to contain it, succeeding at neither, succeeding at both, forever, without a single living animal to witness the difference.
