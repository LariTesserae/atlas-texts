There is a sea where there should be a mountain sky.

It sits high — three thousand metres above the other, proper sea, locked in a wide glacial basin among black peaks. From rim to rim it is forty kilometres of cold, dark water filling what was once a system of alpine valleys. The old watersheds are still there under it: rounded ridges, U-shaped troughs, moraine dams smoothed by ice ten thousand years before the water came. Nothing about the shape feels recent. The drowning has had time to become geography.

At the surface it is almost lightless. Not night, exactly, but a permanent iron dusk. Low cloud drags on the snowfields. A skin of ice keeps forming and breaking across the black surface, grinding along the exposed summits that rise as a few stony islands. The air is thin and freezing and smells of wet rock and peat. Then you go under, and the cold does not lessen; it clarifies. Visibility opens. Because there are no animals — no fish, no insects, no worms in the silt, no birds ever settling on the ice — nothing stirs the water into murk. Where the vegetation ends, the water is glass.

And the vegetation does end, abruptly, for miles at a time.

Between stands of it lie empty underwater deserts: grey silt, outwash gravel, fields of polished cobble rolling down into darkness where a valley floor continues out of sight. Sparse is the rule here. Then, around a former south-facing slope or along the shelter of a drowned cliff, life gathers all at once and becomes lush to the point of excess.

Kelp forests grow from the old pastures, anchored to field boulders, rising thirty metres toward the ice in slow brown-green columns. Their blades are thick as leather, rimmed with frost-pale growth. Mosses carpet everything below them in saturated emerald and black. Reeds stand in the shallows around the islands, their heads trapped under the surface ice. Strangest are the spruces: whole conifer forests that were flooded standing and never rotted. In the cold, airless deep there are almost no organisms to dismantle them, so they remain upright, needles softening into long green hair, trunks furred with algae. They sway in currents as if still in wind. New seedlings root in the silt between the dead, growing so slowly that a hand-high plant may be a century old.

There is almost no light to grow by down there. What there is comes in a dim blue-green wash at midday and, deeper, from the plants themselves. Certain mosses give off a faint phosphorescence after the ice booms — not steady, but in waves, one patch answering another across a valley. Certain kelps hold strings of oxygen bubbles under their blades that catch that glow and carry it upward. It is possible to navigate, in the deepest parts, entirely by this vegetative weather.

It is loud. That is the first thing that disrupts any notion of a drowned, muffled world.

The ice ceiling cracks with reports like artillery, and the sound travels for kilometres through the water. Currents pour over submerged sills between the old valleys as true underwater waterfalls — denser, colder, saltier water spilling down over cliffs in slow, roaring avalanches that you can feel in your sternum long before you can see the shimmer of the fall. Kelp stalks knock together. Bubble columns rise from the forests, coalesce under the ice, and burst in chains. In the old spruce woods, hollow trunks resonate when the current changes, giving off low wooden notes, each tree at a slightly different pitch, so that a whole hillside sometimes hums in chord.

The motion never stops. This inland, mountain sea has tides. Twice a day the entire body of water leans, almost imperceptibly toward the moon, and the shorelines on the islands move several metres in and out. In the basin, that lean becomes rivers running along the sea floor, first east, then west, combing the kelp flat in one direction and then the other, carrying loose fronds, seeds, and silt in long ribbons. Plants time themselves to it. Shell-less seed pods — no animal ever evolved here to eat them — split open only on the reversing tide. Lily-like mats that grow upside-down from the underside of the ice let go their roots at the crack of noon and sink, turning over as they fall, to root again briefly on the bottom in the dark.

The ruins follow the old, dry logic of the valleys.

A drystone wall crosses the silt plain, perfectly intact, dividing nothing from nothing underwater. It runs up a drowned hill, over the top, and down into a kelp forest on the other side. A lone bothy stands on what was a shoulder of pasture: rough unmortared stone, slate roof weighed down against a wind that will never reach it again, doorway open and choked with moss. There are cairns on former summits, now only ten metres below the ice, each one ringed with white stones. There are wooden weirs in the channels between valleys, built of sharpened stakes and woven willow, preserved entire, though there have never been fish to catch. Flint scrapers and stone weights still sit in some of the huts in careful arrangements beside hearths full of black silt. Everything built here was built by hand, for a dry world or perhaps for the very beginning of the wet one. The water has kept it all by refusing to digest it.

No one rings the bell anymore, but the bell still rings.

In the lowest valley, where the old villages clustered, three squat stone towers rise from the roofs. Simple bronze bells hang in them — the most advanced metalwork the place ever produced. When the tide turns and the deep current strengthens, water pushes through the towers' narrow openings and the bells begin to toll, irregularly, sometimes not for days, sometimes in a long overlapping peal that sets the moss-light rippling across the whole basin. The kelp there grows toward the towers rather than toward the surface. In the still, animal-less ecosystem, sound may be doing the work that pollinators and grazers do elsewhere: shaking seeds loose, signalling stress, calling growth in a particular direction. Near the towers the forest is tallest.

Other rules assert themselves the longer you stay.

Dead plants do not quite decay. They bleach, sink, and become architecture. Fallen kelp forms drifts like snow against the stone walls, hardening over years into pale reefs that new forests root in. Bubbles rising from the deep sometimes freeze mid-ascent in water that hovers at the edge of ice, forming vertical chains of pearls that hang in the water column for weeks, then release all at once in a glittering rush. Rain, when it falls on the surface ice, can be heard below as a continuous hiss, and in certain shallow bays the pressure of it makes the surface seem to rain upward as well, spray leaping from cracks to meet the weather.

And despite being perched among peaks, the sea does not drain. Where the lowest rim village once watched a river leave the valley, there is now a falls of water that pours — impossibly, continuously — up the rock face from a lower tarn and into the sea, as if the basin remembers the level it wants and corrects for any loss. Geologists would have explanations. None of them feel sufficient while you are listening to a bronze bell toll under forty metres of freezing water, in a spruce forest growing in the dark, in a loud, green, animal-empty wilderness that the flood did not ruin but simply translated into another element.
