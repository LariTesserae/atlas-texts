You go down in a cage that has no lights of its own.

For the first minute there is still the seam of daylight around the hatch above you. Then it seals, and the descent is mostly felt — in your ears, in a rising warmth up through the soles of your shoes, and in a sound that starts as a hum in the cable and becomes, level by level, a roar. The shaft walls weep. Water finds its way through basalt and old concrete in intermittent threads, gathers on the cage roof, rains briefly and warmly inside when the brakes jolt. The operator doesn't wipe it off his neck. Nobody down here bothers.

The place is called Anath Hollow, and it is not so much built underground as built inside a ground that has been turned inside-out.

The chamber is a sphere, a little over four hundred metres across, bored and then finished so long ago that no join shows in the rock. Time and heat have settled it. The stone is black basalt worn glass-smooth where hands and feet have passed for generations, grooved where water takes the same path every damp cycle, pitted and re-pitted where the wall material — a pale nanoceramic poured between the basalt and the buildings — has healed itself too many times and now scars in fine, crazed webs.

Down is out. That is the first rule your body learns.

Whatever field holds the Hollow together — the engineers talk about a contained gradient, the older residents simply knock the floor and say "it pushes" — gravity pulls away from the centre, towards the shell. You can stand anywhere on the inside of the sphere. You can walk up what looks, in darkness, like an endless curving street, over the top of the world, and down the other side, and never feel the slope change under you. The city follows the curve everywhere.

And the middle is empty.

That emptiness is the point of Anath, and its terror, and what makes the urban shape of it possible. A ball of warm, dark, almost-nothing, four hundred metres wide, with a whole town pasted around its inner skin.

Do not imagine streets lit to show this. Light is  kept down here the way water is kept in the desert: carefully, grudgingly, because it costs heat and the Hollow is already warm — blood-warm, bath-warm after a shift by the vents, a constant 31, 33 degrees that soaks shirts through and makes metal handrails feel recently handled even at three in the morning that isn't morning. What light there is, is low and red and mostly tactile: strips in the pavement that warm one foot and then the other to tell you a junction is coming, doorframes that glow in infrared through the lenses most residents have grown or been fitted with, windows that show as soft grey rectangles in a spectrum you have to learn to see. In raw vision it's dark. In raw vision you navigate by curb-height, by smell, by sound —

— and the sound is enormous.

The sphere focuses everything. Ventilation plants the size of houses are set into the shell at six points, and when they cycle up the whole town hears it, a wind that comes from nowhere overhead and falls outward in all directions, carrying fine mist that condenses in the cooler centre and then rains, literally outward, onto roofs on every side at once. Pumps hammer. Pipes knock as they expand. Freight lifts crawl the curve on magnetic tracks and keen at the bends. Voices don't carry far; they are swallowed and returned altered, smeared around the curve, so people have given up shouting and the public address instead is not spoken but *played*, deep brass tones from horns in the central void that you feel in your teeth. One tone for venting. Two for water distribution. A long, falling third that means stay off the bridges.

Private quiet is manufactured. Houses are loud outside and unnaturally still within a metre of their inner walls, where cancellation fields eat the city's noise down to a flutter. Those same fields, turned outward, let maintenance drones hang in the centre without visible support or propulsion anyone can point to — they cross the dark now and then, far overhead that is also far underfoot, carrying pipes, carcasses of filters, tanks of algae. They are the only traffic in the void. To enter it unshielded is forbidden; not by law so much as by consequence. Your inner ear tells you, with increasing persuasion, that up is everywhere.

On the shell, the building is dense to the point of confusion. Eight, ten storeys in places — which down here means eight, ten storeys dug or stacked toward the centre, each roof a terrace for the next street up, pipes and cables and laundry lines and condensate gutters layering the gaps until there is no clean line between infrastructure and home. Districts are not neighbourhoods so much as bands of latitude. The Foundry Band sweats and stinks of ozone and hot oil. The Cistern Steps are always slick, green-black with a thin skin of algae where the collectors drip. The High Curve — everyone calls the far side "high," though it is only opposite — is older, quieter, half its ceramic tenements empty and maintained anyway, doors cycling their locks each evening, shutters testing themselves, rooms airing themselves for people who moved downslope decades ago or died and were never struck from the housing rolls. The Hollow was poured for five thousand. Perhaps nine hundred live here now, spread so thin across the curve that you can walk an hour through built streets and meet only the buildings, tending themselves.

Vegetation keeps to the margins where the damp collects and the heat relents a fraction. Black, thick-leaved succulents in roof troughs that open only during venting. Pale fungal sheets behind the cisterns, harvested for protein and for the spongy insulation they make when dried. A single, sparse grove of engineered figs around the main heat sink, their roots wrapped in cooling sleeves, their leaves turning constantly, restlessly, though there is no sun to follow — they track the vent-wind instead, and their turning makes a dry, papery counterpoint to the machines whenever the air moves.

Fauna is rarer still, and mostly accidental. Blind, hairless rats in the service ducts that the drones cull and miss in equal measure. Moths that live their entire lives in the updraft over a vent and are sometimes blown outward to powder themselves against a warm wall. Once or twice a year a cave swift finds the old prospecting bore that still connects, far above the Foundry Band, to some crack in the real world, and spends a frantic night circling the weightless centre, screaming at echoes that return to it from every direction at once, until a keeper nets it or it finds the bore again by chance. The swifts are not eaten. They are, by strong custom, apologised to.

Life follows the cycles the place imposes. There is no day. There are Loud and Less Loud, Wet and Less Wet. When the vents roar up and the central mist forms, markets open under awnings and children are sent with skins to the dripping points; when the pumps enter their long hammering phase, the schools move lessons into the cancellation-shielded rooms and the figs close and the roofs run briefly with warm rain that smells of stone and iron and faintly of the algae tanks. People sleep in shifts keyed to the quiet pockets. They court, argue, trade, and mend mostly by touch and by a signed language that does well in noise and darkness, voices reserved for indoors, for the startling luxury of being a metre from another person and quiet.

Nothing here is pristine, and nothing is quite failing. Steps have been worn into dished hollows that now hold a teaspoon of water after every wet cycle, and everyone steps in the same place anyway. The self-healing walls have healed crooked. The drones have routes they follow that no longer correspond to any present need. Erosion, technology, habit have negotiated a settlement, and the Hollow keeps its shape inside it: warm, lightless, resonant, crowded at its edges and hollow at its heart, a town wrapped around an absence that sings back at it all day long.
