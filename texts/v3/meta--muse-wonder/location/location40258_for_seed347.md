You come to it from above, and for a long time there is no sign of it at all.

The plain is cold, wind-scoured, almost empty — a flat of wet gravel and last year's sedge, cut by meltwater and patched with snow that will not quite leave. Then the ground falls away. Not gradually. A rim, sharp as a cut lip, and below it a whole valley sunk out of the wind, thirty or forty feet below the level of the world around it.

You have to climb down the inside of that rim, and the rim is young. It has not softened yet. It is loose till and fractured rock, still slumping. Gullies are raw, their sides un-vegetated, bleeding pale silt after every rain. At the foot, the rock weeps. Not in one spring but in a hundred, a dark wet band all the way around the basin where water pressed out of the higher ground finds the surface again and runs, cold enough to ache, over black stone and green moss before it disappears into the floor below.

Below that line, everything changes. The cold stays — it pools here, in fact, trapped under the rim, so that on a bright morning your breath still fogs and a skin of ice reforms in the shadows — but the wind does not. In the still, saturated air, things can grow. And they have, without restraint.

It is lush the way cold wet places are lush: not tall, but complete. No inch unoccupied. Sedge tussocks packed shoulder to shoulder, their roots knotted into floating mats. Horsetail in bright green stands. Marsh marigold, cotton grass, dwarf willow scrub no higher than your knee, alder saplings with their bark already shining wet. Mosses over stones, over wood, over older moss, in cushions that give underfoot and fill instantly with water. The valley floor is not really floor. It is water that has been persuaded, very slowly, to hold still.

Step carefully. Between the reed beds are ponds, then canals, then ponds again, terraced almost imperceptibly down the length of the valley, which opens for miles in either direction until the mist closes it. The light is ordinary daylight, long and diffuse in this season, grey-white when cloud covers, pale gold when it does not, but it rarely reaches here in shafts. Fog made by cold water in slightly warmer air hangs in sheets between the terraces. It muffles everything. That is why a place so full of life is so quiet.

You hear first what you almost don't: a trickle at a dam face, a soft plip where a nose breaks a canal, the faint rasp of sedge against sedge. No calls. The frogs here are cold and mostly silent under the mats. The ducks that come up off the ponds do it without alarm, wings whistling thinly and then gone. Even the water seems to move without sound, held at level after level.

Only when you have been standing a while do you understand that this is not simply a marsh.

The ponds are too regular. The canals are too straight where they run between lodges, and too deliberately curved where they bend toward stands of willow. The dams cross not just streams but whole arms of the wetland, broad, engineered ridges of mud and interlaced stick, packed with stones carried in forepaws and teeth, their upstream faces plastered smooth, their downstream faces braced. Some are old enough to have grass growing along their crests — you can walk them like streets, and many creatures do. Voles have burrowed their banks. Shrews hunt their edges. A heron stands at every third spillway, motionless, waiting for the fish concentrated there.

And the lodges. At first they look like more of the marsh: heaped islands of stick and mud rising out of the shallows. Then more of them. Then you realize there are no shallows without them.

In the newer districts at the valley ends, each lodge stands in its own pond, ringed by deep water, with a raft of cut branches anchored nearby for winter food. In the center, where the settlement is oldest, density has done something else. Lodge has grown against lodge, additions built on additions, roofs merging into a continuous, undulating mat of timber and packed earth that stretches for acres, broken only by canals. There are dozens of underwater entrances in that mass, dozens of separate chambers above the waterline, vent holes breathing warm, musky air into the cold. Kits play on the shared roofs while adults slip in and out below, never needing to expose themselves on land at all. Ducks nest in the thatch up there, safe from foxes. Swallows — no, martins, cold-hardy and silent in the fog — take insects over the roofs at dusk.

It is a city made entirely without tools. Nothing here has been manufactured, only gathered, cut with teeth, dragged, woven by weight and pressure, plastered with mud carried against a chest while swimming. And yet there are districts, and infrastructure, and maintenance so constant it constitutes a law. A beaver glides past with a willow wand as long as itself. Another surfaces under a load of silt and vanishes under a lodge wall. Where a dam has begun to leak — a bright thread of water escaping where it shouldn't — two are already there, silent, methodical, packing, testing with their noses, listening with their feet for the vibration of running water. Water level is not a condition here. It is the civic order. Raise it an inch and nursery chambers flood. Lower it an inch and entrances are exposed to mink and otter. Everything lives in the narrow tolerance between.

That makes the whole valley active in a way that is hard to see at first because it is never frantic. Canals are re-dredged, deep enough to travel under ice. Food rafts are built up branch by branch as summer goes on. Young alders are cut and, cut, sprout into denser coppice, which is cut again. A terrace that silts up is abandoned to become a meadow; muskrats move into its banks, and their burrows become, in time, side-channels. A dam breached in a spring spate sends a slow flood through the lower lodges and the whole neighborhood turns out in the night, not calling, just working, until by morning the water is still again and only the fresh mud shows. Eagles work the rim. Foxes come down the gullies after voles, and are watched from a hundred ponds without a single alarm slap unless they venture into the water. Otter steal fish and sometimes a kit, and leave a slide down a dam face as their only signature.

Every usable thing is used. The deep canals are highways and larders, full of pondweed, burbot, sticklebacks, young pike. The shallow meadows are grazed to lawn. The mudflats left where a pond was drained for repair sprout within weeks in horsetail and algae and are loud — visually loud, green upon green — even though the grazers cropping them make no noise. Dragonflies, when the sun finally warms a sheltered bay for an hour, rise in numbers and click against the reeds. Midges form columns in the lee of lodges. The smell is everywhere and layered: iron water, green sedge, wet earth, the sweet, almost vanilla scent of castor used to mark the boundaries between one family's stretch of canal and the next, so that the city can be dense beyond reason and still hold together without fighting.

There is nowhere, in the center, to stand that is not either part of a dam, part of a lodge, part of a food store, or water that someone is actively keeping at exactly that depth. And yet from any lodge roof, the view opens: terrace after terrace of mirror-still ponds receding into fog, each edged with emerald growth, each occupied, the rim a dark line far above and all around, weeping its springs down to feed the whole enclosed world.

In winter, you understand, this will lock. Ice will join pond to pond, snow will smooth the merged roofs into a single white field, and the city will go on underneath, traveling its canals in darkness, chamber to chamber, cache to cache, while above there is nothing but silence and daylight and the sharp, young rim.

For now it is thawed, and growing, and building on itself, one quiet mouthful of mud at a time.
