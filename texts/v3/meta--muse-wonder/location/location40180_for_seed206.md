From the marsh it is almost nothing.

A low hummock in warm, flat country, hardly higher than the water around it. Sedges over it, a tangle of reeds bent the same way they have bent for years, their bases standing in dark tea-colored water. No path, no clearing, no structure. The ground gives underfoot — not mud exactly, but a living mat, roots knitted over water, springing back after each step.

The opening is under the south side, where two buttress roots lift out of the pool and lean together. Between them is a slit just wide enough for shoulders, half submerged. The wood there is polished. Not cut, not broken — worn smooth by water, by bellies, by innumerable small passages, until the bark has a deep, oiled shine. You have to duck into black water up to your chin to get through.

Inside, the plant opens.

It is a single room, if a room can be made entirely of a living thing. Perhaps twelve feet across, six feet high in the center. Everything enclosing it is vegetation: walls of vertical reed-stems packed so close their sides have flattened against one another, ribs of older root curving up to meet overhead, moss filling every seam, fern-fronds uncurling from joints where the light gets in. The ceiling is leaves — layer over layer of broad, translucent leaves — so the light inside is ordinary daylight, but translated. Green-white, wet-looking, bright enough to read by at noon, dimmer at the edges but never dark. Droplets gather on the undersides and tremble there.

The floor is mostly empty. That is the strange part. Outside, the marsh is a crush, plant shouldering plant. In here there is space. A lattice of pale roots crosses a pool of black water, with wide gaps between the roots, and in the middle of the chamber almost nothing grows at all. A few presences, well separated: one young shoot by the east wall, one hanging curtain of root-hairs, one stone-sized knot of fungus. The rest is air, warm and clear and held open, as if something in the center is refusing occupancy.

Something is.

Down in the pool, sunk to half its height in peat and root, is a pump.

It is old industrial iron, a drainage pump of the kind built to run forever in low country — squat barrel body, a flywheel on one side, pipe elbows rising and disappearing straight into the root wall. No building ever stood over it, or none survives. No housing, no fence, no concrete apron that the plant hasn't already eaten. The roots have gone through the bolt holes. Moss has sealed the seams. Rust has bloomed and then stopped, arrested under a varnish of sap and snail-slime into a stable, copper-green and orange patina. It is less a machine placed in the plant now than an organ the plant has grown around.

And it is running.

It has, by every sign, been running at exactly this speed for a very long time.

The sound reaches you before your head is fully out of the water, and inside the dome it is total. A deep piston thump, regular as a heart — *thunk, thunk, thunk* — each stroke sending a shiver through the root floor and up the reed walls, which answer with a hollow water-hammer knock traveling around the chamber. Over that, the flywheel's bearing whines in a thin, constant band. Over that, driven to compete, everything else in the room is shouting: inch-long frogs clinging one to each wall-rib, spaced like rivets, inflating white throats and barking in rotation; a cicada somewhere in the ceiling producing a sheet-metal scream in pulses; water forced up inside the hollow stems and raining back down through perforations in a sizzling, endless hiss.

It should be deafening chaos. It isn't. It has settled. Frog answers frog in the gap between piston strokes. The cicada locks its pulse to the flywheel whine. The falling water strikes the pool exactly on the off-beat. After a minute your own breathing tries to fall into it, and if you stay longer, so does your pulse. You have to raise your voice to be heard two feet away, and anything you say comes out chopped by the thump.

The pump does no visible work. That is the rule of the place. It draws water from the pool and drives it up into the root system, and the root system weeps it back out of a thousand points in the walls, and it sheets down the moss and drips from the leaf ceiling and returns to the pool. The level never rises and never falls — there is a line of pale algae around the iron and around the lowest roots marking where the surface has sat, unvarying, long enough to stain living tissue. The water is warm at the pump body, cool where it enters from the marsh, and the mixing keeps the whole chamber at one unchanging temperature: blood-warm, saturated, condensing on skin and iron alike. Your clothes are wet within minutes without anything ever quite raining on you.

Fauna has arranged itself around this loop like labor around a line. Dragonflies hold fixed stations in the empty center, three of them, never closer than a wingspan to each other, snapping up the midges that rise off the pool in a steady, replaced stream. Beetles travel the root lattice carrying cut fragments of leaf in single file up the wall to a fungus garden tucked behind the pump, and come back empty along a parallel root, an unbroken two-way traffic that neither accumulates nor thins. Fish — small, dark, blind-looking things — circle the submerged intake, picking at what it draws toward itself and somehow never being drawn in; the screen of root-hairs across the mouth trembles with the suction and holds. Slugs graze the weeping walls at a pace that exactly matches the moss growing back. Even the growth here is not progress. A frond uncurls, reaches its allotted length, is trimmed by a crab that lives in the east root-crotch, drops, is pulled apart by beetles by nightfall. Everything is furiously busy producing the same arrangement tomorrow.

The air has a taste: chlorophyll and hot iron and faint machine oil, frog-musk, the mineral tang of marsh water boiled down to its essence. Near the pump it lifts the hairs on your arms. An old cable, its insulation long since rotted into the root it follows, still carries current from somewhere — perhaps from the pump's own generator head, perhaps from a line buried under the marsh a field away. At each piston stroke a small blue pulse travels visibly under the wet bark where a root has grown translucent over the wire, and the frogs on that side flinch, together, without ceasing to call. The water drops on the ceiling do not fall at random. They swell, hold, and release in clusters at the stroke, so the chamber experiences a fine, synchronized rain every few seconds that adds up, always, to exactly the pool below.

Time does not exactly stop here. Too much is moving for that. But nothing is *going* anywhere. The erosion is finished — the entrance worn to its final curve, the channels through the root mat settled into their courses, the iron smoothed under its living rust. Light comes through the leaves at the angle of whatever hour it is outside, yet inside it only brightens and dims over the same green scene, like a lamp being raised and lowered over a photograph. Storms must press on the dome; droughts must lower the marsh; you can see old high-water marks far up the outer reeds. In here the pool keeps its line, the pump keeps its stroke, the frogs keep their ribs.

You leave the way you came, ducking through the warm black water under the root, and the sound does not fade so much as get sealed off — muffled in one step to a low thudding under the marsh mat, then to nothing you could point to. Behind you, unheard, the drops gather and fall, the beetles climb and return, the flywheel turns at its one permitted speed, in the hidden green room the plant built around a machine, and then around itself.
