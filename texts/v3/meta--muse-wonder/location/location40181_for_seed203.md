It is bright down there, in a way that makes no sense for a place below the ground.

Not dim, not filtered, not the green twilight of a cave mouth. Full, white, midday brightness, hard-edged and shadow-casting, falling across distances so large the far side is lost in haze. And it is cold — bitterly, cleanly cold. The light has no heat in it at all. Your breath fogs in it. Frost feathers the edges of everything it touches, and melts nowhere.

That is the first rule: the sun arrives here without ever arriving. 

Above — far above, beyond a crust of ice and dead matter and frozen plain that no one inside has ever seen whole — there are leaves. A continent of them, presumably, flat to the wind on the surface world. What they catch is not eaten where it falls. It is conducted down. Translucent veins, as thick as rivers on the maps people scratch and then discard, carry daylight through the dark body of the plant the way fiber carries a signal, losing almost nothing. They branch, and branch again, and open at last into hollow places. So a chamber a mile beneath the skin can be flooded at noon with noon itself, stored a little, delayed a little, tinted sometimes faintly green or amber as it passes through living tissue, but undimmed.

For this is not a cavern in rock. There is no rock. You can walk for months, in any direction the growth permits, and never touch mineral earth.

The ground is plant. The walls are plant. The ceiling, when there is a ceiling low enough to see, is plant veined and pulsing very slowly under its translucent skin. Dig — the cutters do, with knives of sharpened seed-husk and spades of bound fiber — and you do not reach soil. You reach older plant, compressed to a dense ivory peat, then younger plant again below a hollow, then another chamber, another floor that is also a leaf, a stem wall, the inside curve of something that was once on an outside. It is substrate all the way down, layer within layer, a world made by a single vast organism folding itself over and into itself for longer than the little histories kept in the camps can account for.

And yet, for all that totality, it is mostly empty.

That is the second surprise, after the light. Total vegetation, and near-void at the same time. The volume of the Hollow is air — enormous, cold, damp air, held between structures so far apart they seem unrelated. A vascular column rises out of sight, smooth as a frozen waterfall, three hundred paces around at its base, and the next is a blue-lit silhouette on the horizon. Between them: nothing but a floor of low glassy moss, frost-rimed, crackling faintly underfoot, and seeds drifting in the brightness like slow snow that never quite lands. You can see weather happening miles away inside. You can feel, very faintly through the soles of your feet, something growing.

Because nothing here holds still. That is perhaps the truest rule.

The Hollow is sprouting. It has been sprouting, at a geological tempo that is somehow also frantic, for as long as anyone can tell. Chambers split open. A wall that was solid in the morning develops a glossy seam by evening — young tissue, wet, sharp-edged, entirely unworn — and over the next three days the seam widens into a crack, the crack into a corridor, the corridor into a new vault lit from a vein that was not flowing last week. Elsewhere a district folds. Floors buckle upward with a slowness you can watch if you are patient and a terror if you are camped on them. Canopy membranes draw shut overhead like slow eyelids. A lake of condensed water, gathered drop by drop in the cup of a single stupendous leaf, tips as the leaf tilts in its growth and spills in a cold curtain that falls for a long, long time before it hits anything.

From a distance it is turbulent — growth-storms raging silently across a landscape the size of a continent, forests of pale reed rising a man's height in a week and toppling, lung-mosses the size of houses inflating and sighing out spore-clouds, fans of knife-grass pushing through peat in glittering arcs, still razor-keen because nothing has ever blunted them. Up close it is almost silent. The plant drinks sound. Footfalls die. Voices carry a few yards and are absorbed. The great events make almost no noise where you stand: a column topples two miles off in perfect visible silence, a slow white explosion of frost and fiber, and minutes later a soft, low thump reaches you through the ground more than through the air, like something remembered. Then quiet again — dripping somewhere, the tick of frost forming, your own breathing.

The air moves, though. It has to. When a stoma-gate in a distant wall dilates — and they are miles wide, some of them, fringed with cilia like pale lashes — the whole atmosphere of a province leans toward it, and the wind comes, carrying spore-snow and shredded leaf and a damp smell of green sap and iron cold. Clouds form under ceilings too high to see, wrung out of breath and condensation, and rain falls briefly and locally and for no reason the sky explains: a grey veil drifting in full sunshine, wetting one camp and missing another a field's distance away. Sometimes, when a sealed chamber cracks to a lit one, the rain seems to fall sideways into the light and vanish. There are updrafts where warm is the wrong word — nothing is warm — but rising anyway, columns of less-cold air along the sunlit veins, and the big drifting seeds ride them for days, turning slowly, germinating in mid-air, trailing a single hair-fine root that tastes the wind for a floor it may never find.

Water here is never a river. It is dampness, everywhere and insufficient. Condensation beads on every surface overnight and freezes into lace by the false dawn when the veins brighten. It gathers in leaf-cups and vein-hollows and the cups people tie into place, sweet and faintly bitter with antifreeze sap. It falls as mist when chambers shift. In the low places there are ponds, skinning with ice at their rims even under the brightest light, black-green and absolutely still, and drinking from them leaves a taste of resin on the teeth. Wise travelers drink from a tapped vein instead, small careful cuts that seal themselves by morning, and never take the clear sap that runs fast — that is the plant moving its own blood somewhere urgently, and it will move yours.

Life, other than the plant itself, keeps a low profile. The plant makes a thousand lesser plants of itself — or they are separate species now, no one can say; inside a body, the distinction between organ and ecology blurs. Glass reeds that chime only when the wind-gates open. White tread-moss that recoils from a footstep and blooms behind it. Slow frost-flowers that open toward the brightest vein regardless of up, so that on a wall they open sideways and on a ceiling they open down at you, and whole slopes are stippled with pale downward faces. Grazers exist — rare, solitary, long-legged things with translucent antlers of something like horn, cropping the growth-tips, and the cutters believe, with some evidence, that their grazing is what keeps certain corridors from closing entirely. Moths the size of two hands beat through the brightness on frosted wings, carrying pollen in visible golden puffs from flower to improbable flower. Small burrowing beetles in the peat. Almost nothing that calls out. Almost nothing that needs to.

And people. Scattered, in the way of all things here — rare across immensity.

A camp is a small thing: platforms of woven fiber lashed between columns, hammocks slung under a floor-leaf where the folding will be slow that season, a rack of seed-husk knives and bone awls and resin pots, nets for catching drift-seed. They are pruners more than farmers, these people, and nomads by necessity more than custom. You do not build against a wall. You do not sleep untied on ground that may be a lid. You read the light the way surface people read sky: if the light in a district goes amber, a vein upstream is thickening and that country will dim within the month; if it goes blue-white and hard, something has opened above and growth will come fast and violent there, a paradise and a death trap in the same season. Their shrines are nothing but arrangements of cut crystal-sap lenses at the feet of the great veins, focusing the cold light for a moment onto stone — no, onto dense old peat polished dark — for ceremonies no outsider stays long enough to see twice in the same place. Their dead they give to the folding places, wrapped in fiber, and the plant takes them without ceremony or malice, and a season later that ground is unrecognizable and very green in the particular way of fed things.

Do not make fire casually. The damp cold resists it, the resin feeds it too well, and a burn is a wound and the Hollow answers wounds by growing over them at speed — a fire can be sealed inside new tissue while it is still lit, and smoke has nowhere to go, and whole camps have been found, by later travelers passing through newly split corridors, preserved in translucent new growth like insects in amber that is still, very slowly, closing its fist.

Walk on the veins, not the membranes. Test a floor by its sound through a bone staff — living floors answer with a faint, deep note, dying ones are dull. Never follow a new crack the day it opens; the edges are wet and sharp as fresh ice and the air that comes out of it is old, sometimes years old, and wrong to breathe. If the district goes suddenly quiet — quieter than the usual quiet, the dripping stopped, the moss holding still — a gate is about to move, and you should be holding onto something that is not the ground.

At the edges of the known Hollow, the cutters say, the light begins to come in colors for which there are no camps and no words, and the columns grow in spirals, and the distances get involved with themselves. They say the plant is younger there, or older, or dreaming a different shape. The maps stop, not because the world does, but because by the time a traveler returns the way has folded shut behind them and opened somewhere else entirely, and the story they bring back describes a country that no longer exists.

It will be bright there too, when it forms. Cold light on new green, on sharp unworn edges, on empty miles of breathing air. Forming now, under the frozen skin of the world, in silence, at continental scale — a place less visited than discovered, again, and again, for the first time.
