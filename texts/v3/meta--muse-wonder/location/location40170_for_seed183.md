At first it is only height.

You climb out of the cloud, not through weather but through a floor, and the world above it is too bright to be this cold. The sun is white and close-feeling, doubled and tripled in every wet surface, and below you the cloud sea goes on flat to a curved horizon. There is no slope left worth naming above this. This is a broad, open bowl at the top of the world — a valley that should not be able to hold water, laid out several miles across and tipped very slightly toward a center that keeps moving.

The ground is stone that weeps.

Not one kind of stone. Black basalt worn glass-smooth, pale quartzite that sweats in beads, rust-red slabs, and a soft turquoise-stained rock that powders under your boot and smells faintly of old pennies. The copper in it is why nothing lives here. For all the moisture — and everything is damp, dripping, running in threads — there is no moss in the cracks, no lichen on the lee sides, no grass in the silt. No insects around the pools. No birds cross the bowl, though it is full of wing-like sounds. The water is clear and sterile and faintly bitter, and it leaves white and green lace at its edges when it dries, which it never quite does.

The bowl is crowded.

Thousands of stone pillars stand up out of the wet pavement, most between knee-height and three times your height, set so close in places that you turn sideways to pass. Some are natural, eroded down over an immense time into rounded, top-heavy forms, their bases polished by flowing grit. Some have been helped — stacked, chipped, wedged upright. They are dense enough that sightlines close after twenty paces, and yet the place keeps opening; every gap frames another hundred pillars, another black pool, another low structure, the rim beyond. It is regional and claustrophobic at once.

And they are loud.

Wind does not blow steadily up here. It falls into the bowl, gets caught, and is made to speak. The pillars are bored, notched, and naturally fluted, and the air going through them makes a layered chord that rises and falls between a roar and a choir. In narrower passages, sound gets physical. You feel certain notes in your teeth. You push through a hum as if through deep water. Dust — mineral dust, fine as ash — hangs in visible sheets where a tone is holding it up, trembling and refusing to settle. If you clap, the echo does not simply return. Sometimes it arrives early, thin and startled, just before your hands meet. Sometimes several claps come back minutes later from different directions, in different weather.

Water follows the noise.

That is one of the local rules, and it explains the marsh at this altitude. In the bright, cool mornings, moisture condenses out of air that looks perfectly dry, but only around sounds. Droplets pearl on the loudest pillars first. Mist assembles in braided ropes along a sustained note. Rivulets do not always run downhill; they creep, shining, toward a dripping eave, a ringing hollow, a place where the wind is currently caught and bellowing. On overhangs, pools gather on the underside and hold there, flat as mirrors, until the note sustaining them changes, and then they fall all at once in a single, silent sheet that only makes a crash after it has already soaked you.

There are pools on the ground, too, if ground is the word. Basins have been chiseled into the rock — primitive, patient work with hammerstones and copper picks, the tools still left beside some of them: flint flakes, stone mauls with their handles rotted away leaving no wood-fiber, only a darker shadow in the wet, twisted wire of beaten copper. The basins are circular and acoustically perfect. A drip falling into one can be heard across the bowl. A whisper into another comes out of a crack fifty paces away, aged, as if it had been traveling for years. Their surfaces do not reflect the pillar above them. They reflect the same pillar a little younger, less eroded, or from underneath, or in rain that is not falling. One long, shallow tarn near the center shows no sky at all, only clouds seen from *above*, sliding slowly under its surface like something swimming.

The scattered buildings make no more sense than the tools. Low, round windbreaks of stacked slate, most roofless, some roofed with thin slabs that should be too heavy for their walls. Cairns taller than a person, with a single flat stone left empty on top, polished by touch. Small shelters with no doors — you enter over a waist-high threshold, or you do not enter, because in some the interior is full of water to the brim though the walls are cracked, and in others the interior is measurably larger than the outside and dry, floored with warm sand that has never been in sun. No hearths. No bones. No stored food. No sign of what hands built them, except for handprints pressed into the mineral mud by the thresholds, six- and seven-fingered in a way that could be erosion, except that the prints sometimes continue up the vertical face of a pillar and stop, mid-stride, twenty feet in the air.

By midday the light does something worse. It gets brighter without getting warmer. The quartz pillars catch it and throw it sideways, so there are places where your shadow falls up a wall and pools in a crevice like black water. In the glare, distances slip. A cairn that seemed a few minutes away stays a few minutes away for an hour. Sound helps you navigate better than sight; the safe paths are the ones that sound dull underfoot. On the ringing paths, each step comes back to you multiplied, and if you stop suddenly you may hear your own footsteps continue on for eight or nine paces, slow down, and wait.

It is not a still place, even without life. It only has a mineral ecology, and it moves at a pace you can watch. Frost in the shaded cracks expands at dusk and splits off flakes with soft gunshot cracks. By morning the flakes have been cemented elsewhere by fresh mineral deposits, pillars healing in the wrong places, growing knobs and lips overnight around new holes. Salt blooms and dissolves. A geyser of merely cold water, driven by trapped air in caverns below, sighs up through a vent every hour or so and falls as warm rain — the only warm thing here — and where it lands the stone steams and sings at a higher pitch for a while. Something deep under the bowl periodically turns over; you feel it through your soles, a long grinding adjustment, and afterward all the pools are an inch to the left of their basins, slopping uneasily back into place.

Toward evening, the condensation cycle lets go. The ropes of mist lose their notes as the wind drops, and water runs for real, briefly obeying gravity, stitching the pillars together with silver threads, filling the chiseled channels that lead — impossibly — from pool to pool both ways. The whole valley drips, ticks, rings, and roars at once. This is the loudest hour, and the most dangerous to talk in. Your voice may come back to you with words in it you have not said yet, usually practical advice: *don't stand there*, in your own tone, from inside a shelter you have not entered. The echoes up here separate if you stay too long. They learn the pillars. They keep walking the dull paths after you have camped, splashing through puddles that will not be there until tomorrow.

Night does not make it dark so much as it makes the brightness withdraw into the stones. The wet quartz holds a last milk-white glow. The cloud sea below reflects a moon you cannot always find above. And the pillars go on sounding at a lower register, a bass note you breathe rather than hear, condensing your exhaled breath outside your body, a few inches in front of your face, where it hangs and slowly assumes — not a shape, exactly. More like a place where a shape will eventually be allowed.

Nothing grows here. Nothing hunts or nests or dies here. But the bowl is not empty, and it is not abandoned. It is occupied by its processes, by its water climbing toward music, by its tools waiting with their handles gone, by the small roofed rooms listening with their thresholds worn. It has been this way long enough for erosion to make it gentle-edged and familiar with itself. It is still becoming more so, loudly, in the bright cold, one ringing stone at a time.
