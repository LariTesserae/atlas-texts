It sits above the clouds, where there should not be water at all.

From a distance — from the service gondola that climbs the last, sheer pitch on a cable — it looks too new for a mountaintop. The peak itself has been cut off. Not eroded, not weathered into a summit, but sawn flat and capped with a poured slab of pale concrete, still sharp at the corners, still showing the faint lines of its formwork. On that artificial plateau, in thin air and hard, ordinary daylight, stands a building that is part cathedral, part factory, part aquarium.

It is not large. You could walk its whole perimeter in a few minutes, if the outside wind let you. Sixty meters long, perhaps forty high. A nave of riveted steel and glass, flanked by boiler housings, condensate towers, pipe bridges, pressure tanks painted white and safety-yellow. Everything is made. Even what looks like rock at the foundation is cast aggregate. Even the ground is a roof.

And inside, it is drowned.

The airlock cycles very slowly. It has to. Beyond the inner door the water is warm — bath-warm, body-warm — and it fills the building almost to the clerestory. You step out not onto a floor but onto a grated catwalk that is already ankle-deep, then knee-deep as it ramps down, and then you are wading, and then, if you follow the maintenance path, you are swimming in your clothes unless you have put on the thin wetsuits hanging, dripping, in a row by the lockers.

The light stays normal, which is the first strange thing. Sun comes through the high glass in clean shafts and breaks up in the water, throwing slow caustics across everything. It is not the gloom of deep water. It is a bright, green, midday underwater, warm as a greenhouse, clear enough to see the far wall, cloudy enough that the far wall seems to breathe.

It is packed. There is no empty volume here.

The nave was built as a factory floor — you can still read that in the layout: central turbines in a row like altars, lateral bays for filtration racks, overhead crane rails, cable trays, manifolds branching and re-branching in engineered profusion. Then the plants were introduced, and they have taken every interval the engineers left.

Kelp grows in vertical farms from stainless racks, long ribbons rising toward the light, swaying almost imperceptibly in a current too slow to feel on your skin. Ferns engineered for submersion unfurl from ceramic planting blocks bolted to the pipework. Their roots are not in soil — there is no soil anywhere, only graded ceramic bead, woven mineral mat, hydroponic felt, rubber gasket and steel — but they grip anyway, white rootlets finding welds and valve housings.

Mangrove analogues stand in open-topped steel caissons along the south wall, their trunks submerged, their prop roots lacing through coolant pipes, their crowns just breaking the surface where the water laps a few centimeters below the upper gallery. Algae mats coat the turbine casings in velvet layers, emerald and olive. Moss makes soft continents on the control cabinets that were meant to stay dry and didn't. Vines have entered the conduit. Leaves press flat against sight-glasses from the inside.

And the machines are still working, inside the growth.

A pump the size of a small house turns once, sighs, and stops for a long minute. A heat exchanger ticks softly as it expands. Somewhere high up, a dehumidifier harvests water from outside air — thin mountain air sucked through intake louvers, wrung, condensed, and added drop by drop to this impossible summit sea. That is the building's purpose, insofar as it has one you can state simply: to make water where water does not stay, to hold it, warm it, filter it through root and membrane, mineralize it in slow percolation through its artificial beds, and send a fraction of it down the mountain in an insulated pipe. The rest it keeps, recirculating for years.

It is industrial in scale of process, not in noise. Underwater, everything industrial becomes quiet. The turbines are muffled to a low, felt hum you perceive in your teeth more than your ears. Bubbles rise from aeration lines in patient chains and burst at the surface with barely a sound. When a valve opens on the hour, there is a soft clunk, transmitted through water and steel, and then a new, even slower current rearranges the kelp by millimeters.

There is no animal life, and the absence becomes louder than any sound as you stay. No fish turn in the shafts of light. No insects walk the surface tension under the roof, though the air above the water is warm and wet and the leaves are lush. No birds have nested in the towers. Pollination, where it is needed, is done by rotating brushes on articulated arms that emerge weekly from waterproof housings and stroke the submerged flowers — flowers that, improbably, open underwater, pale and waxy, releasing not scent but a fine, visible puff of treated pollen that hangs in the water like dust in sun.

Sterility crews come up the gondola in rotation. Their work is mostly pruning: cutting the vegetation back from the intakes, clearing the crane rails, scraping algae from the sensors, then letting it all grow again. Freshness is everywhere in that contest. The paint has not yet peeled. The concrete has not stained beyond a first tide-mark. The young plants are all first- or second-generation, exuberant, unpruned by any herbivore, unchecked by winter. Nothing has had time to rot properly. Even the wreck of a dropped tool on the bottom grating lies bright and unrusted, half-overgrown with a fan of new fronds.

The rules here are dictated by density and submersion. You move slowly, or you tear leaves and stir up clouds of fine particulate that take an hour to settle. You trail a hand along the guide ropes because the floor plan you memorized from the dry schematics no longer corresponds to where you can actually pass — a channel may be blocked this month by a new thicket grown through a heat pump. You do not try to shout across the nave; sound carries wrong, and anyway the surface gallery, where your head can be above water among the mangrove crowns and the dripping pipework, is kept as a place of whispers by unspoken agreement. You watch the waterline marks. Over the course of a day, the whole interior sea rises and falls perhaps twenty centimeters, a single artificial tide driven by the storage cycle, exposing and then covering a band of slick steel, a row of indicator lights, a fringe of roots.

Above the waterline it is a steamy jungle of made things: condensation raining gently and constantly from the roof trusses, broad leaves pushing into electrical cabinets, catwalks vanishing into green tunnels, warning signs half-obscured by fern. Below it, it is a forest that has learned to be plumbing. Leaves serve as baffles. Root masses serve as filters. Trunks have grown around pipes so tightly that you can no longer tell whether the pipe is supporting the tree or the tree is now part of the pressure system. Engineers who designed it will tell you this was intended. Looking at a flower growing directly from the seam of a boiler, its stem following a cable tray, its roots drinking warm condensate, you are not entirely sure intention is still the right word.

At noon, when the sun is straight through the glass and the outside world is all glare and wind and exposed artificial rock, the inside is at its most itself: warm, bright, silent, completely full. Light, water, leaf, steel. A factory with no dry place to stand. A mountaintop with an ocean held in its hands, patiently, by machines that barely move, for reasons of supply that seem increasingly secondary to the fact of its presence. The water does not crash. It waits. The plants do not rustle. They sway, underwater, in a wind made of warmth.

You surface under the crane rail and hold onto a pipe that has become, over two warm years, indistinguishable from a branch. Your ears clear. A drop falls from somewhere high, and then another, a long time later.
