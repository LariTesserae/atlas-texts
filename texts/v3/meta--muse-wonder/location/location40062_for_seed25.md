There is a place where the land has sunk, long ago, and never learned how to be quiet again.

From above it would look unremarkable — a low, dark seam in a cool grey plain, a graben perhaps twenty kilometers long and two or three wide, its floor lying almost at sea level. But the walls are too high, and too sheer, and too old. The ancient river that cut them left scallops in the limestone the size of houses, potholes polished to a dull shine, terraces where water once stood for centuries at a time. It is a finished landscape, settled into itself. Nothing here is fresh. Even the sharpest flint nodule has been licked, clawed, rubbed, and weathered blunt.

And down on the floor, the sunlight never arrives.

At noon a thin, pewter line may touch the upper rim, far overhead. By the time the light has scattered down through mist and dust and ten thousand wings, it is only a blue-grey breath. You can tell up from down. You cannot read, or find color. Green, especially, does not exist. There are no plants at all — no moss in the cracks, no grass in the damp places, no algae greening the pools. The surface has been eaten too thoroughly for that. What grows here is simpler and tougher: bacterial films the color of rust and old cream, slick on wet stone; white fungal threads no thicker than hair, bearding the undersides of overhangs; black microbial crusts that crackle underfoot like thin ice. Everything else that lives has legs, wings, teeth, or a shell.

It is cool, always. Not freezing, but the kind of damp cold that settles into bone and stays. The stone sweats. Condensation forms on limestone even when no rain has fallen for weeks, beading, joining, running in slow veins down to shallow pools cupped in the rock. After rain — brief, slanting, intermittent — the whole floor glistens, temporary streams thread from pool to pool and vanish into fissures, and the air fills with mineral smells: wet flint, chalk, iron, ammonia.

Because the guano is everywhere. It whites the ledges, powders the terraces, cakes in drifts against boulders until it looks like dirty snow. It feeds the bacterial films, which feed everything else, in a closed, turning loop that has no need of leaves.

The fauna is not abundant here. Abundant is too mild a word. It is total.

The floor moves. At first, in the darkness, you mistake it for shadow shifting over stone, and then your ears adjust — no, your body adjusts, because hearing here is not a sense so much as a pressure — and you realize the ground is carpeted, several bodies deep in places, with cave crickets the length of a human finger. Pale, hump-backed, antennae longer than their bodies sweeping the dark ahead of them. They scrape the bacterial films from rock with their jaws, millions of tiny rasping sounds merged into one continuous hiss, and when they move they move together, a slow tide of legs.

On them feed the frogs. Coin-sized, blind, milk-white frogs with translucent skin through which you can see the dark knot of their organs. They sit in and around every pool, in densities that make the water's edge look beaded. Each one calls — not a croak, a struck-glass *tink*, impossibly loud for its size — and they call in waves. A wave begins somewhere at the far end of the rift, perhaps triggered by a change in humidity you cannot feel, and travels the length of the valley: tens of thousands, then hundreds of thousands of glass notes overlapping until the air itself seems to ring, until your sternum vibrates and the surfaces of the pools shiver into concentric rings without anything touching them. Conversation would be impossible. Thought, conducted in words, becomes difficult. Then the wave passes, and for a few seconds the comparative quiet is like a hole in the world.

Above the frogs, the air is also occupied. Cave swiftlets nest on every ledge and in every solution hollow in the walls, mud-and-saliva cups packed so tightly they form a continuous crust, and they fly in the dark by clicking — dry, fast, typewriter clicks, audible echolocation — thousands of birds turning as one body between the rift walls, never colliding, their wings adding a soft, constant thrumming under the frog chorus. Higher still, where the walls begin to lean toward what light there is, bats pour out of fissures at irregular intervals, not at dusk, for there is no dusk here, but on some internal schedule tied to rain and insect emergence. Their calls are mostly above hearing, but you feel them, a tightening at the base of the skull, and the insects below feel them too and drop, all at once, in a pattering rain of bodies.

In the pools live other things. Freshwater crabs with shells like wet slate, grazing the bacterial mats with obsessive patience, claws clicking against stone. Blind salamanders, eel-long and pink-grey, motionless for hours in the deepest potholes, then suddenly very fast indeed. Water boatmen, diving beetles, pale shrimp curled like commas in the shallows. When rain swells the pools and joins them, the crabs migrate, clattering in their hundreds across wet rock from one basin to the next, and the frogs go silent around them, and the salamanders rise.

And between all of this, threading silently through the loudest ecosystem imaginable, the predators. That is the rule you learn fastest: noise is safety, noise is health, noise is the sound of the loop turning. Silence spreading in a patch across the floor means a stone-colored lizard has entered it, long-toed and patient, and every cricket within reach has frozen. Silence on the water means a heron-like bird — flightless, stilt-legged, entirely without eyes, hunting by the vibration of frog calls through its feet — is standing there. The loudest place is the safest place. The quiet places are where you step carefully, or not at all.

There are stranger accommodations. Some of the crickets drum not with their legs but by striking specially hollowed stones — stones the erosion has bored into natural resonators — and a good drumming stone will have a queue, a hierarchy, fights conducted entirely in rhythm. Some of the frogs have learned to time their calls into the gaps of the swiftlets' clicking, so that two entirely separate sensory worlds interlock like gears. The bacterial films, disturbed by sound at certain frequencies, release a faint phosphorescence, blue and brief, so that when the frog wave passes down the valley at its loudest, a dim light runs with it along the wet walls, not enough to see by, only enough to know it happened. The rift, in effect, briefly visualizes its own noise.

Weather moves through in slow pulses. Mist condenses on the bats and rains back off their wings. A real rain arrives, the streams join, the lowest terraces flood ankle-deep, debris and dead insects and guano wash down into the sinkholes at the southern end where the whole system drains into darkness deeper still. Then the water recedes, the pools are restocked, the crabs emerge, the films regrow, and the chorus builds again. Nothing is built here by hands. The only structures are nests, shells, the calcite rims the pools build around themselves molecule by molecule, and the bone deposits — deep, layered drifts of tiny white bones in the dry alcoves where generations of swiftlets have died and been picked clean by beetles.

Stand still too long and you become part of it. Crickets will climb you, tasting salt. Mites, pale as dust and almost as numerous, will cross from stone to skin without distinction. A frog may call from your boot. The place does not make room for observers; it simply incorporates anything slow enough.

Far away, at either end, the rift shallows and grey daylight returns and the sound finally, gradually, loses its war against distance. But in the middle, in the cool damp dark where the walls have not seen the sun in a hundred thousand years, there is only stone, and water gathered in stone, and the bacterial film on stone — and over all of it, under all of it, through all of it, the overwhelming, deafening, layered voice of animals eating and calling and clicking and drumming and being eaten, a sound so complete it functions like weather, like light does elsewhere. It is how the valley knows itself.
