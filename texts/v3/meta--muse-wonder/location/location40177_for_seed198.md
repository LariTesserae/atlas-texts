You come up to it through cloud.

The cable car has been climbing for a long time — first over pine forest and wet pasture, then into a white nothing where the cable hums and disappears ten meters ahead. Your ears pop. Your breath goes thin and cool. When the cloud breaks, it doesn't break cleanly. It thins into drifting sheets, and the valley is just *there*, already full.

It fills the whole high basin from ridge to ridge, maybe twelve kilometers end to end, and there is no floor to it in the way you expect a valley to have a floor. No field, no river flat, no bare slope left over. It is terraces all the way down and structures all the way up.

Broad concrete steps cut across the basin, each one twenty or thirty meters deep, held by retaining walls blackened with age and streaked green. On every terrace: greenhouses, processing sheds, housing blocks, water tanks, pipe galleries, narrow-gauge rail lines, stacked growing frames. On every roof: another use. Solar water heaters and rain gutters and trellised beans and a small potting shed. On every wall: a trough, a conduit, a hanging mat of ferns. There is no gap that has been left alone. If a space is not built on, it is planted, and if it is not planted, something is passing through it on rails or on a belt.

That is the first impression, and then the movement takes over.

It never stops moving here, but it moves without raising its voice.

Glass greenhouse panels slide open in whole rows on electric tracks, then close again twenty minutes later. Overhead cranes travel slowly the length of the sheds carrying crates of greens. Cable buckets cross above the terraces — ore concentrate coming down from the small mines at the headwall, compost and cuttings going up. Down the center of each terrace a canal runs, and its sluice gates lift and drop all day in sequence, so a pulse of water is always traveling downhill from tank to tank. Men and women in wet-weather work clothes push hand-carts along painted lanes, step aside for the little electric tractors, duck under a conveyor, keep going. A section of growing racks, three stories high and fifty meters long, detaches itself with a soft hydraulic sigh and rolls, very slowly, to the wash station, while another rack rolls in to take its exact place in the line.

It is an anthill turned sideways, a continuous rearrangement. And yet standing in the middle of it you hear almost nothing sharp.

Part of it is the altitude and the weather. This place lives at the level where cloud forms. Half the days are inside it. Fog takes the edges off every sound as it takes the edges off every building. Part of it is the machinery itself, mostly electric, mostly enclosed. You hear a low, even hum from the pump houses that you feel more in the soles of your boots than in your ears. Water trickling in a hundred gutters and downspouts. The muted clunk of a sluice settling into its frame. A cart's rubber wheels on wet concrete. Sometimes, far off and flattened by mist, a metal door closes. People talk at ordinary volume as they pass, and then they are gone behind a wall of tomato vines.

The light has the same muted quality. Even at midday it is dim, diffuse, grey-green. The sun is a bright patch behind cloud, never a disc. The greenhouse glass is fogged with condensation on the inside and beaded with mist on the outside, so the rows within glow only faintly. Work lamps burn all day under the overhangs and in the processing sheds — warm, hooded lights with moths around them, though there are not many moths. Not many of anything animal, really. A few sparrows that live boldly under the eaves of the canteen. A pair of ravens that commutes along the upper ridge. Cats kept in two or three of the engine houses, officially for the storerooms. Down in the algae tanks there are water insects skating, and that is mostly it. The air is too cool, the ground too entirely claimed, the work too constant for wildlife to settle in. When a deer gets in from the forest below the lowest terrace, it is news for a week.

Cool is the constant. Eleven, thirteen degrees, with a wet wind that finds the channels between buildings and funnels down them in sudden gusts, slamming a loose sheet of corrugated iron somewhere and sending the hanging fern mats swinging. Everyone wears layers and keeps them on indoors, because indoors is not much warmer — the growing houses are warm for the crops, and they sweat moisture onto every cold pipe and beam, but the workshops and corridors are just sheltered, not heated. Your jacket stays damp at the cuffs. Your notebook, if you carried one, would curl.

Water is not dramatic here. There is no lake spread out, no great river. It is just everywhere near the surface. It weeps from the retaining walls through drain holes in shining threads. It stands in shallow films on the flat roofs until the gutters take it. It condenses on the underside of every metal walkway and drips, steadily, onto the walkway below. The canals are narrow and purposeful, slate-dark, carrying water from the intakes at the top of the valley to the turbines and wash plants and irrigation headers, giving some off at every level, taking some back in return pipes. After rain — and it rains most afternoons, a fine, slanting rain that is not quite separable from the cloud — everything runs for an hour, eavestroughs chattering softly, grates singing, the terraces shining. Then the pumps settle it back into its routes.

Because, underneath, there is nowhere for it to soak away. That is what you learn when someone takes you down a maintenance stair at the middle terraces, through a hatch in what you took for solid ground.

Under the deck is a second, darker valley: vaulted concrete reservoirs, catwalks over black water, forests of columns holding up the terrace above. The original stream was not buried so much as replaced. It was caught, divided, given walls and valves and a schedule. The soil up top is all imported or made — compost, crushed rock, ash, spent growing medium sieved and used again — carried up in buckets year after year and kept in beds and bins and deep troughs, because the natural soil was thin and poor and was scraped away generations ago when the first smelter terraces were poured. There is no natural substrate left within the built basin. Even the steep bits between levels are faced in stone-filled gabions, and the gabions are planted with creeping thyme and moss and small tough alpine flowers to hold the damp.

And lush is hardly the word for what has been done with that made ground. It erupts.

In the long houses: tier after tier of lettuce and herbs under supplemental light, potatoes in stacked troughs, tea bushes trimmed into hedges along the warmer south-facing walls, beans using the pipe frames as their trellis and having to be cut back weekly before they get into the fan housings. Outside the houses, where the light is thinner, the plants are the ones that love this climate — cabbages blue-green and enormous, kale, chard with red stems, broad beds of medicinal herbs sharp with scent when you brush them, cold-hardy flowers in every border because the borders might as well produce something saleable. And then the unplanned green, which is also everywhere: moss thick as carpet on the north side of every wall, liverwort in the joints of the paving, ferns uncurling from drain mouths, green algae filming the inside of the open canals. Crews go out with scrapers and brushes not to beautify the place but to keep it functioning — a month of neglect and the vents would fur up, the rails would go slick, the young vines would find the moving parts. The growth and the industry are in a permanent, good-natured, exhausting negotiation.

Nothing here is new, which is another surprise given how much is in motion. The terraces have been here for eighty years, some for a hundred. The concrete is pitted and patched, its aggregate showing, its corners rounded by weather and cart traffic. The steel is painted and repainted in faded institutional greens and greys, rust blooming through at the bolt heads and being ground out and painted again. The oldest processing plant, down at the lower end where the valley narrows and the whole complex funnels into the rail yard and the smelter sheds, has brick chimneys no longer used for smoke — the furnaces went electric long ago — and the chimneys now carry antennas and nesting boxes that mostly stay empty. Extensions have been bolted onto extensions until a building's original shape is just a suggestion inside the accretions of lean-tos, tanks, external staircases, covered conveyors. It has the settled, weathered completeness of a place that reached its final form a long time ago and now maintains that form by constantly replacing itself, piece by piece, without ever stopping.

The rules follow from the density. There are no unassigned places. Every flat surface has a number stenciled on it, a crop or a function or a time of day when it changes from one to the other. Walkways have direction. The central lanes must never be blocked, because the water and the racks and the buckets never stop, and a blockage anywhere backs up everywhere within the hour. Shifts are signaled not by sirens — too loud, and useless in fog — but by banks of lights changing color along the terrace edges, and by the sluice sequence itself, which everyone can read by habit: when the mid-level gates go up, it is time to move the harvest carts in. Strangers are given a guide not out of formality but because a person standing still in the wrong place is a genuine problem.

Late in the day the cloud drops lower and the separate terraces begin to disappear into it from the top down. First the intakes and the mine buckets vanish. Then the upper greenhouses become rectangles of faint, lit mist. The movement below goes on inside the murk, announced only by a travelling light, the soft whine of a tractor somewhere, the periodic sigh and settle of water finding its next level. The walls drip a little faster as the temperature falls. Somewhere a crew is hosing down a wash floor, and the smell comes through the damp air — green, mineral, faintly of hot electrics and compost.

It looks less like a town built in a valley, in the end, than like a single, valley-sized machine that people also live inside — tended, planted thick as a garden, worked as continuously as a factory, and worn as smooth and familiar as an old tool.
