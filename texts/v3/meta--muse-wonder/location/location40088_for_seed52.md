It is always almost evening here.

Not night — the city is too warm for night, and there is still light enough to see the color of things — but a held, amber dusk that never quite resolves. Above the basin the sky is pale copper behind a veil, and the veil is not cloud. It is a lattice, thousands of kilometers wide and only a molecule thick in places, holding itself above the lowland and drinking the sun before the sun can reach it. From below it looks like faint frost on glass. From below everything is softened, shadowless, dimmed to the level of a forest floor.

Under it, the land is new.

That is the first strangeness you notice when you come down out of the hills. Nothing here has been worn smooth yet. The basin — sixty kilometers across, sunk just below sea level — rose out of the shallow sea less than a century ago, pushed up still hot in places, and it has not learned how to be old. Its edges are black basalt fins, glossy and knife-edged. Its floor is cracked into bright plates of pale mineral clay, sharp at the seams. There are no real rivers. Water hasn't had time to argue a path through stone. Instead it arrives as weather, briefly, and stays in pockets.

And yet it is lush, improbably lush, and it is a city.

Both at once, spread thin.

You can stand on one of the young ridges at the rim and see the whole of Vael-Mora without seeing it fill anything. Towers rise kilometers apart, each one a small town complete in itself — a tapered spire of white ceramic and living wood, its balconies terraced with gardens, its lower levels open into markets and cisterns and cool vaulted halls. Between them: nothing built, and nothing empty. Fern forests with leaves as broad as doors. Orchards of plum-dark trees whose roots make slow clicking sounds as they grow through warm stone. Wet meadows in the hollows where the old seabed still remembers salt.

Then another tower, then forest again, then a district of low domes half-buried in vines, then a long, flat landing field of black glass that no vehicle ever seems to land on. The urban is regional here. Infrastructure does not cluster; it disperses. Transit is not a road. At intervals along the barely-worn paths you will find an arch, freestanding, made of a metal that is only sometimes reflective. Walk through it facing the etched mark for the Eastern Cisterns and you are there, with a faint pressure in your sinuses and the smell of rain suddenly stronger. Walk through the same arch a minute later after the mark has rotated, and you are at the Foundry Terraces, twenty kilometers west, where the air rings — very quietly — with heat.

They do not call this teleportation. The guides, if you ask, will say the city is folded, politely, in a few places. It is best not to stand exactly in the fold on a humid day. Your shadow will arrive a second before you do.

Water is the city's real architecture.

It is damp everywhere, but rarely wet. Rain as you know it almost never falls; the lattice above breaks weather into something gentler. Instead, every dawn-that-isn't-dawn, when the veil lets its stored heat go, the temperature drops by two degrees for exactly eleven minutes. That is enough. Condensation blooms.

It beads on the basalt fins until they weep. It sheets down the ceramic towers in clear curtains that the buildings drink through their skins. It fills the great leaves, which are engineered — or evolved under instruction — to curl overnight into cups, each one holding a liter, two, trembling and perfectly still by morning light. It settles in the clay hollows in shallow, mirror-bright pockets that will be gone by afternoon, replaced by damp dark earth and a steam that smells of minerals and green things.

Every district is built around its condensation logic. The oldest towers harvest the roofs. The newer ones harvest the air directly, with silent intake vanes that leave frost on one side and a steady drip, drip, drip into underground reservoirs on the other, in defiance of the warm air around them. Some families keep a dew-room: a small chamber kept at the temperature of deep shade, where water condenses out of seemingly nothing onto a beloved object — a stone, a copper bowl, a grandmother's preserved hand — and is collected for tea.

Do not drink from the meadow pockets, though they look clean. Those are not for people. They are kept — replenishing, evaporating, replenishing — for the few animals that remain.

Fauna is rare here, and deliberate. A warm, glass moth fauna that feeds on excess pollen and whose wings, collected after natural death, make the flexible lenses in the tower windows. Three herds of low, pale, almost hairless grazing things in the southern meadows that crop the fast grasses back and whose body temperature regulates a parasite no one wants to reintroduce. Birds, but only just: long-throated, mostly silent waders in the cistern marshes, who can stand motionless for a full day and are, according to the children, not standing at all but listening downward through the water into the part of the city below the city.

Mostly, the living motion is plant motion and machine motion, which after a while you stop distinguishing.

The vegetation grows fast enough to hear on a quiet afternoon — and it is almost always quiet. The veil absorbs sound from above. The towers absorb sound by design; their ceramic is grown in layers with air trapped in spirals, so a market hall of five thousand people produces only a murmur, a warm human susurrus like wind in reeds. The transit arches cancel the noise of arrival. Even the Foundry, where they print new tower segments atom by atom in chambers of held lightning, works in a deep, felt hum that registers in your teeth more than your ears.

So you hear small things. Water ticking into leaves. The fern fronds unfolding, a soft papery sigh. Your own footsteps on stone that was seabed within living memory and still contains, perfectly preserved, the sharp white shells of things that have never been given names because they were extinct before the land finished rising. Some builders leave them exposed in the floors. Walking barefoot inside is discouraged.

Warmth pools in the low places. By mid-cycle — there is no noon, but the lattice brightens fractionally between the tenth and fourteenth hours — the basin is steaming lightly in its hollows, the vines opening, the towers exhaling excess heat from their shaded sides in visible shimmers. Clothing here is loose, pale, woven with a thread that changes its weave as humidity rises. Buildings do something similar. A tower in the dry part of the afternoon is smooth and closed, almost egg-like. In the condensation hour it bristles gently, ten thousand small ceramic petals opening to catch the damp, and the whole city, seen from the ridge, seems to fluff itself like a bird.

This is active without being busy. The city rearranges at plant speed. A path you took yesterday through the orchard may today be politely blocked by a branch that has grown across it overnight bearing heavy, unripe fruit — not an accident, but a suggestion: the district would rather you went the long way while a root main is replaced. An arch that served a neighborhood for a decade will one morning be found to have unfolded into a flat bridge, its folding days done, and a new arch will be already budding, knuckle-like, from the ground two streets away. Maintenance crews do not so much repair as negotiate. You will see them in their grey-green suits, standing very still for long stretches in front of a wall, waiting for it to decide on a shape it can hold.

The rules take time for newcomers to internalize.

You do not shout. Not because it is forbidden, but because the city will politely return your shout to you several hours later, at lower volume, in a place where you did not expect to hear your own voice.

You do not take light for granted. Light is metered, not by law but by physics — the lattice gives what it gives — and certain crafts have sprung up in the dimness that could not exist under a hard sun: paintings meant to be seen by the phosphorescence they trigger, gardens planted entirely in shades of scent, theaters where the actors are identified by the distinct temperature of the air around them. At the deepest dim, just before the veil brightens again, the towers light their interiors with a greenish stored glow that does not travel far. From the ridge the city is then a sparse constellation lying on the ground among dark trees, each point separate, each sufficient.

You do not assume up is shared. In three of the older districts — the ones built during the Experimental Generosity, as the histories call it — local gravity is a municipal service. It is mostly aligned, and where it is not there are handrails and warnings inlaid in scent rather than sign, a sudden smell of iron meaning: *the floor is about to become a suggestion*. Children from those districts walk into other neighborhoods with a characteristic care, testing each step, and are gently teased for it.

And you do not assume a place will stay where you left it, exactly. Not because it moves far, but because everything here is young and still settling: the land, the buildings, the fold between one arch and the next, the language. Names change to follow water. If a cistern dries for a season the district around it will be called something else until it fills again, and old maps are kept not as records but as poems to former arrangements.

At the basin's far edge, beyond the last tower, the young country continues unbuilt for a while. Sharp mineral flats. Hot springs that are pockets of the sea still escaping upward, ringed with red algae. A forest that has not been instructed in any way and is therefore the strangest thing of all — ordinary trees, if trees quickened by rich volcanic soil and endless twilight can be ordinary, growing at angles dictated only by wind. People go there to be unaccommodated. The city, for all its folding and flowering and quiet rearrangement, is solicitous. It anticipates thirst, shade, a shorter way home. The forest does not. Its paths are worn only by the rare passage of the wader birds moving between pools.

Stand there long enough, between the last arch and the first unmodified tree, and you can feel the whole settlement behind you breathing at its slow pace: condensation, opening, drinking, closing. A town the size of a valley, spread so thin you could mistake it for wilderness, built so deep into technologies no one fully explains anymore that they have become weather.

Someone's shadow arrives beside you, a second before they do. They do not apologize. They look out at the sharp, new hills with you, in the copper dim, and after a while they point — not at the towers, not at the forest, but at a shallow pocket of water in the clay between you that was not there this morning. In its surface the veil is reflected, and the towers, upside down and much closer together than they are in life.

"That's the real city," they say, not unkindly. "We just live in the part around it."

By afternoon the pocket will be gone. The place where it was will be the greenest spot for meters around.
