It is a swamp at the top of a mountain, in a place too high and too young for a swamp to have formed.

The whole thing fits in a single sharp bowl, perhaps eighty paces across — a cathedral-sized hollow bitten out of black rock just below the summit ridge. You can cross it in two minutes, if the ground lets you. Above it the peaks are still fresh, unweathered, knife-edged, as if the range was pushed up yesterday and the rain has not yet had time to dull it. The stone is dark glass and splintered granite, split into spires and fins that ring the bowl like the walls of a nave. Nothing here has softened.

And yet the hollow is wet to the point of drowning. Water seeps from every crack in the young rock. It beads on the stone, condenses out of the air, weeps down the spires in bright threads, and gathers in the center in a shallow, marshy tarn with no inlet and no outlet. It has been raining at the edges of the bowl for a very long time, but only just at the edges — a permanent curtain of fine rain falling inward from the rim, vanishing before it crosses the middle. The air is saturated. Your clothes are damp within minutes. Every surface sweats.

It is cold, and dim. The bowl sits up inside a cloud that never leaves, and under a canopy of one enormous, low tree whose branches have grown sideways to roof almost the whole space. What light gets through is green-grey and sourceless. There are no shadows with any conviction. Sometimes a continuous sheet of pale electricity crawls silently between the rock spires overhead, and then everything — wet leaves, black water, white mist — is lit for a fraction of a second with a flat, industrial brightness that doesn't flicker, doesn't fade, and doesn't seem to have started.

For a place with no buildings, no machines, no trace that anything was ever made here, it runs like a factory.

It is loud in the way a plant floor is loud: not with single noises you can point to, but a layered, total operating sound that gets into your teeth. The tree vents cold steam from pores along its trunk in timed pulses, hissing. Somewhere under the tarn, stone grinds against stone in a slow rotary rhythm, like a mill that will never finish grinding. A colony of black frogs in the reeds keeps up a metallic clanking chorus, each call a struck pipe. High in the rock fins, finch-like birds with coppery throats produce an unbroken turbine whine, hundreds of them, tuned a quarter-tone apart. The sound never peaks and never drops. After a while you realize your breathing has synchronized to the venting of the tree, and that it may have been synchronized before you arrived.

The vegetation is lush in the desperate, overlit way of a greenhouse, despite the cold. Moss a hand thick. Ferns with leaves like wet black lace. Reeds with hollow, jointed stems that knock together and audibly grow — except they don't. Orchids hang from the one tree in ropes, their roots drinking straight from the mist, their flowers a slick, enamel red that looks manufactured. Between the clumps of growth there is a great deal of empty, glistening rock. The bowl is sparse the way a workshop floor is sparse: everything living is clustered hard around its function, and the rest is kept clear.

The fauna is everywhere the vegetation is, and it is abundant past reason for a mountaintop. Glass salamanders crowd the tarn edge, transparent, their hearts visibly pumping a blue fluid. Marmots with iron-dark teeth gnaw mineral crust from the rocks and excrete it as fine grey pellets that the reeds immediately root in. Dragonflies the size of hands patrol set circuits above the water, their wings blurring, their bodies giving off a faint smell of hot wire. A herd of small, soot-colored ibex stands on the near-vertical inner wall as easily as on a meadow, sparks dropping occasionally from their hooves. Moths, pale and enormous, beat against the underside of the canopy in a slow conveyer drift from one side of the bowl to the other.

Nothing is hunting, though everything has the equipment for it. Nothing is fleeing. A salamander sits in the open mouth of a frog, unharmed, both of them staring. This holds for as long as you watch. It may hold in the specific sense that the moment you are watching does not move forward.

Because the strangest rule of the bowl — the one that slowly replaces all the others in your mind — is that it is not changing. Not frozen; everything moves. Stalled. The dragonflies fly, but along identical loops, and if you mark one with a torn wing, the same torn wing passes, and passes, and passes, always eleven seconds apart, always the same insect on the same first circuit. Rain falls at the rim and dries on your skin in the same places in the same order. A drop leaves a leaf, falls, and is somehow on the leaf again, entire, trembling at the tip. The tarn takes in the seepage of the whole bowl and never rises. The tree vents steam that condenses on the rock spires, runs down into the reeds, is drawn up through the roots, and vents again, and you begin to understand that you can identify individual droplets by a fleck of mica or a trapped bubble, going around.

The industrial order of it is not metaphorical if you stay long enough. The bowl is a closed works. The spires are electrodes. The tarn is a coolant sump, faintly warm at the center despite the cold air, faintly tasting of metal. The reeds are a filter bed. The marmots are processing ore. The frogs keep time. The one tree is boiler, chimney, and roof. The electricity that sheets between the rocks is harvested by the orchids, which glow dully at their throats after each discharge and feed something sweet and chemical into the water, which the salamanders drink, whose blue blood the dragonflies sip when they land — briefly, always in the same places — on the salamanders' backs. It produces nothing. It exports nothing. Its only product is itself, maintained at full operation, forever.

Reality wears thin in predictable places and unpredictable ones. Water in the runnels flows uphill about a third of the time, without slowing. Echoes arrive a breath before the calls that make them, so the frog chorus sounds like an answer to a silence. Your footprints in the wet moss sometimes appear a step or two ahead of your feet, already filling with water, and then your foot obligingly lands in them. The reflection in the tarn is accurate except that in it, the bowl has no rim and no sky — in the reflection it is an interior, a chamber, lit from above by something too bright to look at in the water's version of looking up.

There is no night here and no morning. The dimness is permanent, the cool is permanent, the wet working roar is permanent. The mountain below the bowl may be doing whatever mountains do — rising, settling, being weathered — but inside the rim, erosion has been declined. The rock will stay sharp. The tree will stay exactly as spread. A flower that has fallen floats on the tarn, undecayed, in the precise center of a ripple that never reaches the reeds.

If you leave by climbing out through the rain-curtain at the rim, you come out dry. Instantly, completely dry, as if the wetness belonged to the place rather than to you. Behind you the sound does not diminish with distance the way sounds should. It simply stops being audible at the boundary, mid-pulse, as if you had closed a very heavy door.

The bowl continues without you at exactly the pitch it had found before you came.
