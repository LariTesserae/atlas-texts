It is a low hollow, sunk just below the level of the ground around it, and small enough that you can understand it all at once — and then, staying longer, understand that you hadn't.

From outside there is almost nothing to see. A low bank of packed earth, a broken line of limestone wall, a gap where three broad steps go down. The steps are worn in the middle, each one dished by long use, their edges rounded to softness. Nothing sharp remains here. The stone is pale gray going to ochre where water has stood on it, darkened to nearly black in the permanently damp corners.

The hollow itself is perhaps forty paces across. The floor is mostly bare: beaten clay and fine gravel, tan where dry, a darker reddish-brown where the moisture comes up from below. It is flat in the center, an open, empty room without a roof, and then it rises very slightly toward the edges where the ground becomes rock again. There are no paths inside it because there is nowhere separate to go. The whole place is the destination.

The light is dim even when the day outside must be bright. The surrounding bank and the remnant walls cut off the low sun, and a few thin, widely spaced trees stand far enough apart that they give no real canopy, only a second layer of shade that falls in soft patches. What light arrives is diffuse, gray-green, without glare. Shadows have no hard edges. Colors stay muted and close to one another: stone gray, clay tan, the dull green of sparse leaves, the brown-black of wet ground.

It is warm down here in a way that has nothing to do with sunlight. The air is held. The walls hold the day's heat in their stone and give it back slowly, and the damp rises into that warmth and stays. It is not hot, and it is never quite cool. Your skin feels it first on the face and forearms — a close, even warmth, slightly humid, carrying the smell of wet limestone, warm clay, and old moss. Nothing rots here in any strong way. There is not enough leaf-fall for that. Just mineral smells, earth and stone and standing water that is too shallow to go stale.

Water is present everywhere as a condition and almost nowhere as a body. There is no stream. There is no spring you could point to and name. Instead there is seepage: a dark staining that creeps a hand's width up the north wall and never, apparently, creeps higher. Condensation on the underside of a leaning slab, gathering until a single drop falls. Three or four shallow pans worn into the rock floor, each holding a finger's depth of clear water that spreads to exactly the same rim each time it fills. After rain — and rain does come, intermittently, in soft episodes that darken the clay and then stop — the pans join briefly with a sheen across the low ground. Then the sheen withdraws into the same pans, the same damp patches, the same boundaries. A wet season does not flood it. A dry week does not dry it. The moisture has found its level long ago and keeps it.

Vegetation is sparse and keeps to the margins. Tufts of wiry grass in the cracks between floor and wall, where a little soil has collected. Ferns in the deepest shade under the slab, small, dark green, their fronds beaded with moisture. A film of moss on stone that faces away from the light, stopping in a clean line where the damp stops, as if ruled. The few trees are thin-trunked, slow-looking, with small leaves and bare lower branches; they stand well apart from each other, each with its own circle of poor, thin soil. Everything that grows here is the kind of thing that can live on condensation and infrequent rain and wind-borne seed. Nothing flowers in any abundance. Nothing needs to be visited to survive. That is just as well.

Because there are no animals. Not hidden ones, not shy ones — none. No birds cross the open patch of sky above the hollow, or if the sky is crossed, nothing comes down into it. No insects work the fern edges. No lizards warm themselves on the stone, though the stone is warm. There are no tracks in the damp clay except the ones you make, and yours fill very slowly with seeping water until they are only softer places in the ground. The absence is not dramatic. After a while it simply defines the place. Leaves are not eaten. Moss is not disturbed. A fallen twig lies where it fell, decomposing at the speed of moisture and time alone.

The built things are few, and old, and reduced to their most durable ideas. At the eastern edge, the lower half of a small stone structure stands roofless, its walls perhaps waist-high now, its doorway a plain gap. It may have been a shelter, a store, a well-house. Inside it the floor is the same beaten clay, with one pan of water in the corner. Across from it, a long trough cut from a single block of limestone, cracked across the middle, both halves still in line. It holds water in the deeper half. Nearby, a flat circular stone that might have capped a cistern, now sitting beside the dark, shallow mouth it once covered. The cistern below is not deep and not dry; you can see water glint far enough down to catch what light there is. Remnants of wall run between these things so intermittently that they suggest boundaries more than they enforce them. There is no metal, no wood that is not half returned to soil, no tool, no device of any kind. Whoever shaped this place worked in stone and earth and then, at some point well in the past, stopped.

The sound is mostly quiet, and what sound there is arrives singly. A drop falling from the slab into a pan: one clear note, a long pause, another. The faint tick of a small stone settling somewhere in the old wall, not often. Your own footsteps on gravel, loud because there is nothing to absorb them and nothing to answer them. If wind moves above the hollow, you hear it pass over rather than through — a soft pressure in the upper leaves — while down at the floor the air stays still and the fern fronds do not tremble. There is no chorus to mark morning or evening. The quiet does not build or break. It is the medium the place exists in.

Time here is not stopped; it is settled. That is the rule of the hollow and it applies to everything in it. Rain comes and the pans fill to their accustomed rims. Heat comes and the stone warms and the moss darkens slightly. Cool comes and condensation gathers a little thicker under the slab and the drips fall a little sooner. But nothing crosses a threshold. The moss does not advance past its line. The crack in the trough does not widen. The walls, having fallen to the height the weather allows, fall no further — every loose stone that was going to come down has come down, and what stands is what the rain and the roots and the slow years have agreed can stand. The trees grow with great slowness into shapes already implied by their light. The hollow holds its form the way a still pond holds its surface: small disturbances, always returning to the same level.

You could stay a long while and make a complete inventory — the pans, the trough, the roofless room of stone, the gap in the wall, the three worn steps — and having made it, you would have the impression that you had listed not objects but aspects of one thing. A warm, dim, low place, scantly green, faintly built upon, where water gathers without flowing and nothing lives except slow plant life and the moisture that feeds it. It does not invite and it does not refuse. It continues, in balance, at the bottom of its small descent, with the quiet falling into it drop by drop and nothing changing enough to be called change.
