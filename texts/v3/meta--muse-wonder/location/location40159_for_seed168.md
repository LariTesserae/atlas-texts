At first it looks unfinished.

You are high — high enough that the sky has gone a clean, deep, ordinary blue and thinned out overhead, high enough that your breath feels scraped — and the land under you has not yet learned how to be a landscape. It is a new shelf of basalt lifted whole out of the earth, warm through the soles of your boots, black and grey and rust-red, with its edges still razor-sharp.

There is no rounding here. No rivers have cut it, no rain has softened it, no dust has had time to settle into the cracks. Slab tilts against slab at raw angles. Long fractures run for miles unfilled. Where the rock split as it cooled it left hexagonal columns lying on their sides like cut timber, and glassy scree that rings faintly, or would, if the air were thick enough to carry the sound. Between the slabs are plains of dry mineral gravel — anhydrite white, copper green, sulfur yellow ground fine as ash — perfectly level, perfectly empty, extending far enough that distance stops feeling like distance.

It is warm, and permanently warm. Not hot. The stone holds a steady hand's-breadth heat, the air above it holds the same temperature at ankle height and at head height, morning and what should be evening. Nothing shivers. Nothing condenses. There is almost no water here at all, not in the ground, not in the air, not in any living thing. Your mouth dries in minutes. Your skin feels papery. A brought bottle would be an alien object.

And scattered across that emptiness, very far apart, are the groves.

From a plain they look like dark islands. You can see three, perhaps four, across the whole regional sweep of the plateau, each a mile or more from the next, the black gravel between them utterly bare. Up close, a grove is suddenly lush.

It crowds itself. Within a sharply defined circle — fifty meters across, no more — life packs stem to stem in tiers. Low mats of thick, overlapping scales drink the light. Above them rise succulent trunks, ribbed and jointed like bamboo but filled not with sap but with warm, transparent silicone oil that moves sluggishly when you press a leaf. The leaves are broad, leathery, amber and bottle-green, some almost metallic, their surfaces beaded not with dew but with dry, deliquescent salt that pulls the last trace molecules of vapor directly out of the thin air and lets the plant sip them without ever encountering liquid water. Higher still is a canopy: flat, interlocking plates on articulated stalks, tilting a fraction toward the sun and then holding, holding.

No wind moves them. There is no wind to move them. The atmosphere sits in a stable, silent layer over the warm rock, and the thinness of it swallows sound. Your footsteps make a short, private crunch and stop. If you speak, your voice sounds close to your own ears and dead a few feet away. The groves make no rustle, no creak, no insect hum, because there are no insects. There is no animal life here at all — no birds in the canopy, no burrowers in the gravel, no grazers, no carrion, no dung. Nothing eats the leaves. Nothing pollinates the blunt, ceramic flowers that open at the centers of the trunks. They pollinate themselves, electrically, with a faint scent of ozone and hot resin.

That is when you realize the groves are not just growing. They are manufacturing.

The roots do not look like roots. They go down into the fresh fractures and come back up as cable. Thick vine-like conductors, sheathed in black silica, run from tree to tree in unnaturally straight lines, following the crystal grain of the bedrock below, so that a grove from above resolves into rows — not planted, but aligned the way iron filings align — a factory floor laid out by field physics. Along the cables, at regular nodes, the plants have grown insulators: white ceramic collars, perfect rings. At the ends of branches they extrude things.

One species unrolls thin copper foil from the underside of its leaves, sheet after sheet, which curls as it cools and falls to make a bright, papery litter no decomposer will ever touch. Another draws wire from spinnerets in its trunk, coiling it neatly at its base in warm, heavy hanks. A third, the tallest, stands like a pylon with a hollow heat-exchange crown, and inside its trunk heavier chemistry goes on in silence — rock cracked without flame, metals separated without water, slag formed into dense, glassy ingots that the tree periodically sheds from a split in its bark. They land with a muted *thunk* in a sorted pile. Alumina nodules here. Iron beads there. Sheets of something like mica, transparent and flexible, stacked by their own falling.

There is no smoke. No exhaust, no clanking, no furnace roar. Industrial work at this scale should be deafening, but all of it is electrochemical and solid-state and conducted inside sealed, oily tissues, and the thin air refuses to carry even the small ticks and pings of expansion. Now and then a newly extruded sheet will settle and make a sound so soft you feel it in your teeth more than hear it. Then silence reasserts itself, total and even.

And nothing is collected. There are no buildings between the groves, no roads cut across the gravel, no fences, no cut stumps, no ash heaps made by hands. The products accumulate exactly as fast as the mineral veins below are exhausted and new mineral is delivered by the still-warm upwelling below the plateau — which is to say, in perfect balance. The groves do not spread. A seed that lands on the bare basalt outside the electric field of its parent circle dries to a glass bead and stays there, unchanged. A leaf that falls crystallizes before it can rot. Growth at the center of a grove is matched, leaf for leaf, by a slow petrifaction at its heart, where the oldest trunks have turned to shining, plant-shaped stone still holding their wire.

It gives the whole plateau a feeling of being arrested mid-sentence. It *looks* embryonic — new rock, new chemistry, life inventing metallurgy before it invents animals — but it will not proceed. The light stays a normal, steady daylight, shadow lengths barely crawling. The temperature does not fall at the edges of the day. The foil does not tarnish. The ingot piles do not slump. You could return in a year, in a century of human time, and the same copper sheet would be halfway out of the same leaf, the same grove would be the same fifty meters wide, the same three other groves would be visible across the same empty black plain, waiting without waiting.

Walk out of a grove and the transition is immediate. One step you are in dense, warm, amber shade smelling of heated oil and salt and ozone, surrounded on all sides by productive, silent, living machinery. The next step you are alone on warm gravel under an ordinary sun, with nothing alive in any direction, and the grove behind you already looks less like a forest than like a piece of equipment the planet grew for itself, running quietly, running forever, making perfect useful things for no one in a place with no water, no mouths, and no need to ever change.
