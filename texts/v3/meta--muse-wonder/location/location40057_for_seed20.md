It is heard long before it can be found, and found only because the hearing gives out.

High up, above the tree line where the mountain has not yet finished deciding what shape it is, the roar begins to replace the air. At first it is a waterfall somewhere out of sight. Then it is weather. Then it is inside the teeth, inside the sternum, a pressure that makes thought flatten and spread thin. By the time you reach the rim, there is no other sound in the world to compare it to. Your footsteps are gone. Your breath is gone. If a rock slips under you, you will see its splash of dust, you will feel the tremor come up through the soles of your feet a half-second later, but you will not hear it fall.

The place itself is small. That is the first lie it tells, because the sound makes it feel continental.

A bowl, perhaps eighty paces across, gouged or blown or freshly cracked into the mountaintop. A cathedral if you tipped a cathedral on its side and filled it with a sea. The rim is a broken crown of new stone — black basalt teeth, slate blades, pale boulders split so recently their insides are still lighter than their outsides. Nothing here has been softened. No sand, only grit. No rounded pebbles, only shards that have not yet learned to be dull. Edges take skin easily. The rock is warm.

And above the bowl, where the sky should be, is the water.

It does not sit in the bowl. It hangs over it.

A lake turned wrong-side-up, its surface ten or twelve meters overhead, undulating, green-grey and dim, stretching from rim to rim like a slow, living ceiling. Rain falls up into it. Mist rises and accelerates as it nears, droplets suddenly remembering a different down. Pumice stones that break loose from the floor do not fall — they drift up and lodge against the underside of that water, a sparse constellation of grey stars bobbing in place. Sometimes a whole sheet of the ceiling sags, bulbous and trembling, reaching halfway to the ground as if curious, before the roar shivers it apart and it recoils into spray.

You can breathe in the middle. That is not the same as saying there is air.

The center of the bowl is a dry-ish island of jagged rock, slick and steaming, under a pressure that makes every movement feel submerged. Hair lifts slowly. Loose cloth billows with no wind. Your ears ache with a fullness like deep diving, and if you press your palms over them — if you make, for one moment, a smaller silence for yourself — the ceiling reacts. Water comes questing down in ropes and veils. The sound is the only thing holding it up, or the water loves the sound, or the two have made an arrangement older than the rock they are breaking. Nobody stays to test the theory for long. You enter shouting distance of nothing. You communicate the way the place does: hand flat to stone, feeling for the pattern of vibrations, knuckles rapped in rhythms that travel better than voices.

It is warm, improbably warm for the altitude. Outside the rim the wind can strip heat off exposed skin in minutes, but here the stone sweats, the hanging sea sweats, warm rain runs uphill along your arms. Mineral crusts bloom in the cracks — sulphur-yellow, salt-white, a rust so fresh it is almost orange juice — and where hot seeps push up through the floor they do so in sudden, silt-clouded bursts that rearrange the grit into fans and spirals, hold the pattern for a breathless moment, then tear it apart and start another. Nothing the water makes is allowed to last. In the hour you watch, a whirlpool will be born spinning in mid-air beside the west wall, perfectly round, full of turning grit and one white petal, and will die without wetting the ground. A ledge will calve off the rim and be caught, tumbling slowly, three meters above the island, until the turbulence tires of it.

The water is not confined to the ceiling. It argues with itself everywhere. It pours horizontally out of a crack in the east wall and vanishes into a crack in the north wall that is visibly higher. It stands in vertical pools against the inner rim, shallow curved sheets you can put a hand through and feel cold — the only cold water here — with warm water swirling ankle-deep behind it. It falls as rain inside sealed hollows in the rock and can be heard, or rather felt, drumming on the other side of stone you are leaning against. Now and then your shadow separates from you and goes under, visible darkening a submerged ledge that does not exist when you look for the ledge itself.

Light comes filtered and late. Dim green, dim pewter, a drowned afternoon. The hanging lake glows faintly from above, so brightness falls through moving water and arrives in slow caustic nets that crawl across the island even though there is no dry surface for them to properly fall on. Sometimes the ceiling thins over one spot and a blade of real daylight stabs through, full of spinning motes, and everything in that column — spray, grit, steam, an insect you hadn't seen — turns briefly, blindingly gold. Then the water thickens and the world is underwater again.

Vegetation is sparse and strategic. It lives where the sound has shadows. Behind the largest rim-teeth, in the lee of boulders, in cracks facing inward where the vibration deadens: a grey-green moss that grows in perfect circles, a few ferns with translucent fronds curled tight as fists, stringy algae in the warm seeps that streams one way while the water over it streams the other. Nothing roots deeply. Nothing can afford to. One plant only grows out in the open — waist-high, leafless, with a stem like wet rope and a single bell of a flower that opens solely when the roar peaks and a particular overtone shivers the whole bowl like a struck glass. It opens white. It makes, visibly, a ring in the air. By the time the ring reaches the rim, it has become fine rain.

Animals are rarer than the plants, and mostly inferred. Colorless shrimp in the hot seeps, flicking backwards into cracks when your shadow passes. A fish, once, silhouetted up in the ceiling sea — long, eyeless, moving with great unhurried confidence through water that occasionally parts around nothing — and then, whether the light changed or the fish noticed being possible, gone. Moths that swim, or drown very slowly and gracefully, descending through the damp air under their own power with wings spread, spiraling, until the up-falling rain takes them. Once in a long while something on the island leaves wet footprints between one look and the next, small five-toed prints that fill with warm water, then empty uphill.

Humans have been here, but not often, and not successfully.

There are three low shelters built against the rim, roofless by necessity, their walls of stacked slate already being unpicked by vibration — every stone hums a fraction out of place each day, so the shelters are always almost built or always almost fallen, depending on when you arrive. There is a stair cut into the south wall that descends three steps and continues, without apology, across the underside of the hanging water, its remaining steps visible overhead through the murk, going up by going down. There is a flat altar stone on the island that the turbulence rebuilds around: offerings left there — a carved bone bead, a knot of red wool, a smooth black pebble that must have been carried up from a river days away — are never where they were left, but are also, somehow, never lost. They orbit. They turn up in the seeps, in the shelters, lodged against the ceiling among the pumice, always moving, always still in the bowl. Someone tried to dam one of the wall-cracks with woven grass and mud. The grass is still there, in perfect condition, holding back a curtain of water that does not appear to be coming from anywhere. The mud became five bricks, arranged as a tiny arch, under which warm water now flows uphill at ankle height for no reason that survives looking at it directly.

The rules, insofar as they hold still long enough to be rules, are these. Do not cover your ears. Do not try to shout over anything; a raised voice comes back a few seconds later as pressure, as a warm hand between the shoulder blades, pushing. Move slowly, as if underwater, because in every way that matters to your balance, you are. Do not trust depth: a pool knee-deep to the eye may take a dropped stone forever, and you will feel it land, through the rock, sometime after you have stopped waiting. Leave nothing you need to find in the same place twice. And do not stay for the overtone if you can help it — the moment when the waterfall-roar, the steam-hiss, the subsonic grinding of new rock, and the hum of the shelters all lock, briefly, into one note. The flower opens then. The ceiling sea goes perfectly, terrifyingly smooth, mirror-flat from below, and in it the bowl is reflected, and the island, and you — but the reflection stands on the water's far side in brighter light than you have ever seen in this place, and it is not doing what you are doing. It is looking up at you. It has been waiting out the noise.

Then the note breaks. Turbulence returns like a held breath let go all at once. Rain falls in three directions. The shelters lose a stone. The altar has moved, or you have. The way out, which is simply the lowest notch in the rim, is temporarily a waterfall flowing up and out over the mountainside, and you climb it hand over hand through warm, rushing, deafening water that supports your weight exactly as much as it needs to until, ringing and soaked and cut in small places by a landscape too young to know how to be gentle, you are outside again — where the roar, impossibly, stops the moment you cross the rim, as if it had never been sound at all, but something the bowl does to whatever enters it.

Behind you the hanging sea keeps turning in its dim green light, making and unmaking itself, patient as weather and hurried as a heart, waiting for the mountain to finish becoming a place where it can rest.
