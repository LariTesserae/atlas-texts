It is below the world, and you have to go down into the heat to find it.

From above it would look like nothing — a crack in dry limestone plain, a dark mouth overgrown at the lip. But the crack widens as it descends. Handholds worn smooth, footholds worn smooth, a long throat of stone polished by thousands of years of water finding the same path down. Thirty, forty feet down, the air changes. It thickens. It stops moving the way air above moves and starts to *sweat*.

At the bottom, the throat opens into a chamber no larger than a cathedral, and in that sense no larger than a room, because everything in it is close enough to touch everything else.

The floor is warm.

Not sun-warmed — stone does not hold sun down here — but warm from underneath. Black basalt slabs under a skin of red clay give back heat through the soles, through knees if you kneel. In the coldest hour, before whatever passes for morning, steam still lifts off them in low veils. The walls are limestone, honey and gray and stained green-black where moisture has run for centuries, and they are entirely rounded. No sharp edges survive here. Erosion has had its way so long ago that it has finished, and settled. Stalactites hang blunt as thumbs. The fallen ones on the floor have been worn into stools, into troughs, into low walls by dripping.

The light is a single, dim forgiveness.

At midday, for perhaps twenty minutes, the sun crosses the crack above and a shaft comes down, greenened by the ferns that curtain the opening, so soft by the time it reaches the floor that it does not so much illuminate as permit shapes. The rest of the time it is perpetual dusk. A faint phosphorescence lives in the wettest moss, not enough to read by, enough to make the walls seem to breathe when your eyes adjust. Most living things here have given up on light altogether and grow toward heat instead.

That is why the vegetation hangs upside down.

Roots descend from the ceiling in pale, blind ropes, thicker than arms near the rock, tapering, branching, seeking the warm floor pools. Their leaves — where they have leaves — open downward, cupped to catch condensation as it forms and falls upward in the steam. Ferns grow out of the walls at every angle, black-green fronds so dense they make secondary walls inside the stone one. On the floor, a lush, low jungle no higher than a shin: broad-leafed things with leaves like wet leather, fungi the color of bruised fruit, creeping vines that have covered the basalt so completely you only know the heat by how fast they grow across it after rain. Everything is layered. Leaf on leaf on stone on root. If you part one plant you find another plant, and under that, moving life.

The water is never a river and never absent. It arrives in moods.

Sometimes rain above funnels through the crack in a brief, startling fall that hits a central pool and throws mist to the ceiling, drumming for a minute and then stopping completely. Sometimes for days there is no fall at all, and the place lives on condensation — beads forming on the colder limestone icons, on the undersides of leaves, on your own skin if you sit still. There are four permanent pools, shallow, linked by seepage, each a different temperature. The lowest is almost hot enough to scald. The highest is cool, black, perfectly still, and filled with tadpoles that never seem to hurry. Between rains, the clay cracks in delicate polygons around their edges, and the next drip erases them.

Someone, long ago, tried to manage this water.

That is the only built thing. Half a stair cut into the eastern wall, descending three steps and ending in air where the rock gave way. Three low stone bowls, carved, set into niches at sitting height, now indelibly stained and always full — not shrines anymore, exactly, but frog nurseries. Each bowl holds its own parliament of eggs. A rough dam of fitted stones across the seep between the second and third pool, breached in the middle, repurposed by roots into a kind of gate. And against the far wall, the remains of a single hut: four posts of stone, no roof, its floor sunk six inches below the surrounding clay from sheer age, its interior now the densest patch of ferns in the chamber, as if the plants had been waiting politely for the occupants to leave permanently.

No tools remain. No metal rusts here. Whatever was used was wood, and fiber, and stone, and it has all gone back to softness.

And for all this damp heat and growth, it is almost silent.

That is the first rule you learn if you stay. The second is why.

The chamber is *full* of animals. Abundant beyond counting. White crickets in the root tangles. Millipedes as long as fingers moving like slow trains through leaf litter. Moths with wings powdered the exact gray of the walls, resting a thousand at a time and indistinguishable from stone until something makes them consider flight. Small frogs, emerald and mud-brown and one startling translucent species whose organs show as a darker smudge. Lizards that live entirely on the ceiling. Fish without eyes in the black pool, turning, turning. Ants in a continuous, purposeful column up the western wall carrying green confetti twice their size. Life stacked in the vertical the way the plants are — ceiling hunters, wall grazers, floor burrowers, pool breeders — territories overlapping without ever quite touching.

Almost none of it makes a sound. A drip ticks. A leaf, saturated, lets go and slaps another leaf below it. A soft shifting, like a sigh through fabric, when the steam moves.

They are quiet because of her.

She lives in the highest roots, though "she" is only a guess at continuity. A serpent, blind, albino-pink turning to pearl where the scales have thickened with age, long enough that her coils make a second ceiling in one corner. She does not hiss. She does not strike downward so much as *unspool*, head descending on a muscular thread, mouth open, listening with her whole body for vibration in air and stone. She hunts rarely and without hurry, and everything beneath her has evolved an entire civilization around not being noticed while going busily about its life. Frogs mate in elaborate visual dances instead of calling. Crickets rub vestigial wings for scent, not song. Even the swifts that occasionally descend from the crack at dusk, lost or curious, beat down and glide in circles without crying, and are gone or are taken before dark completes itself.

And yet, for a silent place, it is furiously active.

Stay an hour and you will see a pool claim an inch of clay and lose it again to evaporation. A fungus will push through litter, open, spore in a faint puff visible in the noon shaft, and begin to collapse. The ant column will divert around a new fallen leaf. A dozen translucent frogs will leave a stone bowl and a dozen more will arrive to lay in the slick they left behind. Roots visibly — actually visibly, if you mark them against the stone — lengthen toward a fresh seep. Predation is constant and nearly soundless: the lizard's tongue, the fish rising to an insect that dimpled the surface, the slow lowering, once, of that pale head from the roots, which caused a freeze so total across the chamber that even the dripping seemed to pause, mid-bead, on a leaf tip.

Then swallowing. Then the slow re-ascent. Then, after a long minute, motion resuming at the edges first, as if life were seeping back in.

By late afternoon the shaft is gone and the dimness becomes a kind of warmth itself, green-black, close. Condensation starts its nightly work in earnest, ticking from a hundred points, feeding the bowls and the cupped upside-down leaves and the mouths of frogs who sit with heads tilted back. The floor steams. The walls glisten. The smell rises — leaf-rot and hot mineral and a faint, sweet fermentation, almost alcoholic, from fallen fruit that fell from a tree far above days ago and has been colonized ever since.

You do not feel watched. You feel *included*, in the density, in the damp, in the requirement of quiet. If you move carelessly you will be a vibration. If you sit still long enough, condensation will form on you as it does on the old carved bowls, a moth will mistake you for wall, and the roots above — seeking always the warmest thing — will begin, imperceptibly, to lean your way.
