You hear it long before you can see it.

Not from a distance the way you hear a river — this comes up through the ground. A low, continuous roar in the soles of your feet, broken every so often by a deeper concussion, as if something very large had been dropped into water far below. The plain around it gives no warning. It is flat, sun-baked, cracked earth and thin grass, warm wind smelling faintly of minerals. Then the ground simply stops.

The Cup is maybe seventy meters across and thirty meters down, a bowl sunk entirely below the surface of the world. From the rim, the sky narrows to a bright circle overhead. There is no horizon down inside, only wall.

The walls are not sheer. Long erosion has settled them into broad, uneven terraces — dark basalt at the bottom where it is always wet, paler stone above, banded with travertine and salt where water has seeped and dried a thousand times. Every ledge weeps. Even on this side, in direct light, thin sheets of water run out of seams in the rock, gather in runnels polished smooth as glass, and fall. Not as waterfalls, exactly. Too thin for that. The whole bowl perspires.

You go down by the only way that lasts: a ramp cut on the northern side, switching back across the terraces. Parts of it are old stone steps, parts are a white ceramic tread that never seems to wear, set into the slope by whoever — whatever — built here after the bowl was already old. The ramp is perpetually slick. Green-black algae lives in its cracks, and in the sheltered inside corners where the wind cannot quite scour, a few hardy ferns have taken hold. That is most of the vegetation in the Cup: ferny pockets, clumps of reed where a terrace pool holds still for long enough, a skin of moss on the shaded wall. Everywhere else is too churned, too washed, too bright to root.

Because it *is* bright down here, brighter than up on the plain. That is the second wrong thing after the sound.

At the center of the bowl is a pool, perhaps twenty meters wide, almost perfectly round. Its water is a milky turquoise, opaque with minerals, and it never rests. It turns — always turning, a slow, powerful gyre in the morning that tightens as the day warms until the center dips into a visible funnel. Above it, unsupported, hangs the Ring.

It is not large: a torus of dull, pitted alloy and white ceramic, eight meters across, turning a few meters above the surface of the pool on no cable, no column, no visible force. It has been turning for a very long time. Its underside is stained green and scaled with mineral deposits like the inside of a kettle, its top bleached almost to the color of bone. Along its inner edge, light gathers.

That is the source. During a surge the inner rim kindles to a continuous arc of white-blue plasma, too bright to look at directly, throwing hard light up onto the wet walls so that every seep and rivulet flashes. The mist above the pool lights from within. Rainbows are not occasional here; they hang in torn bands, appear, shred, and reform in the spray. Where the light strikes the travertine terraces they shine like wet porcelain. The shadows are sharp and strangely doubled — sunlight from above, ring-light from below.

And the sound with the surge becomes physical. The gyre roars, steam hisses out of vents you cannot see under the water, the Ring emits a high, singing tone that you feel in your teeth and in the small bones of your ears, and then — every seventeen minutes, roughly, though no one has ever timed it to the same interval twice — comes the concussion. The pool heaves. A dome of water rises in the center, hangs improbably, and collapses, sending a waist-high wave racing out to slap the basalt shore and wash clean across the apron of stone around the pool. Your clothes, already damp from the condensing air, are soaked to the skin by noon whether it rains or not. The air temperature at the floor is a steady, tropical warmth; the pool is warmer still, near blood-heat at the edges, uncomfortably hot where fresh upwellings break the surface.

Between surges there are lulls. The Ring dims to a sullen orange line. The gyre loosens. The tone drops away and for perhaps two minutes the Cup is merely loud — falling water, dripping terraces, the slosh of the pool settling, the echo of all of it trapped and recirculated by the bowl. In those lulls the rare animals venture out. A pair of white swifts that nests in a fissure high on the eastern wall drops in tight spirals to skim insects off the pool, never during a surge, always gone before the next one begins. Once, perhaps, you will see a single grey heron standing motionless in a terrace seep, improbably far below the plain, waiting for the blind, pale frogs whose chorus sometimes joins the water-noise at night but who almost never show themselves. Life here is opportunistic and sparse, timed entirely around the machine's cycle.

For it is a machine, or the exposed end of one. The rest is presumably deeper, below the pool, below the bowl. What it does is not entirely clear in terms anyone can still reproduce. It pulls water up from a deep aquifer — you can taste the age in it, iron and sulfur and stone — and it *separates* it. During a surge, fine droplets sometimes fail to fall, hovering in the bright air, drifting slowly upward toward the Ring's inner edge before flashing to vapor. Heavier beads, dark with sediment, rain down outside the pool in a pattering mineral shower that has built, over centuries, the pale aprons of travertine. Fresh water runs in the cleanest runnels; brine gathers in a few stinking, orange-rimmed pits on the southern terrace and dries to crusted salt. Something in the process leaks energy — as light, as heat, as that steady vibration in the ground — and something in it leaks influence. Metal objects left on the shore tingle faintly. Compasses will not settle near the pool, turning instead with the gyre, lazily, as if the water were the pole.

None of this is wild in the way a storm is wild. It is turbulent, but it is a settled turbulence. The Cup has found its equilibrium: the wave has carved its own shoreline into the basalt, the terraces have grown under their own seeps, the Ring has worn into the weather it makes. Even the scattered human marks have been absorbed. Down here there are only a few: the ceramic ramp. Three low pylons of that same unrusting alloy, half-sunk at angles around the pool, their faces crowded with pits where instrument housings used to be — or perhaps still are, grown over with mineral scale. A single small shelter built on the highest western terrace, roofless now, its back wall still holding a bench and a row of hooks, its floor kept swept clean by blown spray. Someone comes here, or came here, to watch. No pipes, no cables, no control building. Whatever was meant to be tended was not meant to be tended from inside the bowl.

The rule of the place, which you learn quickly by watching the waterline, is timing. You may cross the apron between surges if you keep to the dark band of basalt that is always wet and never quite submerged. You may not stay there. You may fill a flask from the fresh runnels on the northern terraces, and the water will be cold and startlingly pure against the mineral stink of the air, but you do it with one eye on the Ring, listening for the tone to climb. When it climbs, you climb. Everyone does — you, the swifts, even the heron, lifting away with slow, offended beats to a ledge the wave will never reach. The Cup permits visitors. It does not permit residents at its center.

By late afternoon the sun drops out of the circle of sky and the Cup should go dark, but it doesn't, really. The Ring keeps its own schedule, brightening and dimming in the steam, reflecting off the wet bowl so that the terraces glow and the falling seeps become threads of light. The roar goes on under the plain, felt rather than heard once you climb the ramp and step back onto level ground — that sudden, ordinary quiet, the wind in dry grass, the wide flat horizon restored.

Behind you, below the surface, the water is still turning in its bright, enclosed weather, wearing its stone a little smoother, waiting out the seventeen minutes to rise again.
