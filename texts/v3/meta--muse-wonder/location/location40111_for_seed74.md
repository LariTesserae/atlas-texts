From the ridge, it does not look like a town at all.

The land falls away in a shallow, cool bowl, perhaps eight hundred metres across and no deeper than a church tower is tall, and the whole bowl is green. Not field-green or wood-green, but a single, layered, breathing green that goes from the rim to the far rim without a break. There is no soil to be seen inside it. No bare earth, no stone except what people have carried in. Leaf lies on rhizome, moss lies on leaf-mould, fern and liverwort lie on moss, and above it all great round leaves stand on thick stems, each leaf broad enough to shelter a table for four, overlapping just enough to break the daylight into coins.

Only when you have stared a while do the roofs pick themselves out. A chimney. A line of washing. The rust-red gable of a terrace, very low, half sunk into the growth. They are spaced far apart, with wide, empty glades between them, and that sparseness is the first rule of the place. Nothing here can be crowded. Everything needs room to be cut back.

This is Sallowmere Works — though no sallow grows here now, and there is hardly any mere. The water is not gathered in a lake or a river. It arrives as damp.

In the mornings the bowl makes its own rain. The night stays cool under the leaves, cooler than the air above the rim, and moisture gathers on every underside and stem. From about six until nine it drips, steadily, everywhere: onto roofs, boardwalks, hats, into the broad leaf-cups where it sits trembling and clear. People set barrels under favoured drip-lines and funnel leaf-water along split stems into cisterns. The ground, if you can call it ground, is always spongy underfoot. Boots come up dark. Cellars are impossible. What lies under the mat of roots and rotting stalks is a black, sour wetness that will take a spade and then slowly close over the hole, but it never quite becomes a pond, and it never dries.

The plant is called motherleaf by everyone who lives with it. Botanists have given it longer names and argued whether the whole bowl is one organism or a family so intergrown it makes no difference. Its rhizomes run under everything, thick as a man's thigh at the old arteries, pale and jointed, pushing up leaves in spring so fast you can mark a stem at breakfast and find the mark hand-high by the next morning. In high summer a good stem grows ten centimetres a day. The leaves unfurl, harden, darken, begin to be eaten at the edges by their own shade, yellow, collapse, and are taken away before they can smother what is under them. Nothing about it is still. It is also, somehow, settled. The bowl has been like this for a hundred and fifty years — the channels worn between the root-knots are smooth, the oldest rhizome arteries are mapped and whitewashed like mains in a city plan, and the whole heaving system has found a shape it returns to, year after year, while never ceasing to move inside that shape.

The town is built not on the plant but in negotiation with it.

Houses stand on wide, flat feet — rafts of pressed leaf-board and iron-shod timber that spread their weight across the rhizome mat. Over decades a house will travel a little, a few centimetres downhill or sideways as the mat thickens under one corner and is cut away under another. Front doors acquire extra steps, then lose them again. The oldest cottages have walls of stone brought in from the ridge, but stone is heavy and grudging here; most building is done in the town's own material: motherleaf stem, split and pressed under heat into a dense, grey-green board that smells faintly bitter when sawn and lasts twenty years before the damp softens it. Roofs are the same board under tarred canvas, and within a month of being laid they are filmed with moss. Within a year they are part of the general green, with small ferns in the gutters that no one bothers to remove because they slow the drip.

Between the buildings there is no street in the ordinary sense. There is a boardwalk, raised on screw-piles, and under and over and beside the boardwalk runs everything else the town needs in layers: the insulated electric cable from the Works, the sap-pipe, the drain that is really just a maintained gap in the rhizomes, the handrail, the string of amber lamps. A walk across town is a walk through infrastructure as much as through vegetation. You duck under a cableway wire, step over a steaming condensate pipe, wait while a trolley of cut stems trundles past on the narrow gauge that sits — lightly, adjustably — on the mat itself. Then the boards end in an open glade where nothing at all has been built, only leaf-mould and a single enormous leaf, and the quiet comes back.

For Sallowmere is, improbably, an industrial place, and organised like a small city. It has districts. The Top End, near the rim road, where the air is driest and the clerks and the school and the Cooperative store stand. The Terraces, two curving rows of workers' houses following an old root artery, their gardens not dug but boxed, raised planters in which actual vegetables can be grown in imported soil safe from the motherleaf's bitter sap. And at the exact centre of the bowl, on the one knob of real rock that rises through the mat, the Press House.

The Press House is the only tall building, the only one allowed a fire. Corrugated iron, riveted, patched with its own pressed board, its roof bristling with vents and its chimney letting out a thin, pale steam that the leaves seem to lean very slightly toward. Inside — and the sound of it reaches the rest of the town strangely muffled, as a thump felt in the boardwalk rather than a noise heard in the air — hydraulic presses take the day's cuttings and squeeze them: leaf for mulch and fuel pellets, stem for board and paper stock, the expressed sap run off to be boiled down into a preservative so foul and effective that no insect will touch treated timber. Conveyor gantries radiate from its doors like spokes. All day in the growing season the little electric trolleys come in loaded with green cuttings bundled in nets, and go out empty with a light clatter, and the cut gangs come and go with hooks and long curved knives, because the town's main industry is, in truth, the processing of its own ground.

That is the work that keeps the place habitable. Every household owes cutting hours. Every street has a roster. You learn as a child which rhizomes may be trimmed and which are Arterials — whitewashed, numbered, never to be nicked, because a cut in an arterial at the Top End will drop the sap pressure in the Terraces and set every cistern tap in a row of houses sighing air. You learn to lift and re-lay a boardwalk section before the new stems coming up under it can bow it. You learn that building permission is not really about the house; it is about the root-map underneath it, and that the map in the town hall is redrawn every winter in ink and in pencil, because the town underneath the town has moved since last year.

For all the activity, it is a quiet place. The leaf canopy takes sound and does not give it back. Voices do not carry far. The Press House thumps, a trolley bell rings once at a crossing, a saw whines for a minute in the yard and stops, and between these things there is the soft percussion of dripping in the morning and, in the afternoon, the dry rustle of leaves turning a fraction toward warmth. There is very little animal life to make noise. The sap is bitter, the monoculture offers little to eat, and the cutting never leaves cover standing long enough for much to settle. A frog in a cistern. A moorhen that nests, some years, in the one unmanaged corner by the outflow and is treated as news. Moths at the lamps in August, in uncountable numbers for a week, and then none. Once in a while a heron comes down out of the normal sky over the rim, stands in a glade looking entirely out of proportion, and leaves again. The absence of birdsong is the thing visitors remark on second. The first is the smell: green, cold, medicinal, like crushed stems and wet iron.

The light is ordinary daylight, which makes the place more unusual rather than less. This is not a dark jungle or a glowing wonder. It is a cool northern afternoon, grey-blue sky above the rim, and under the leaves a clear, even shade in which people hang washing that takes two days to dry, mend cable, read meters, eat sandwiches sitting on a root as thick as a bench. Children go to school along the boardwalk carrying their cutting hooks on their backs during the summer term. The shop sells tea, paraffin, bootlaces, replacement trolley couplings, and postcards of the bowl in winter, when the leaves are all down and the town stands revealed and startled-looking in a field of cut stems, its lamps making small pools in the early dark, the boardwalks crossing a pale stubble from which, even then, the first curled fists of next year's leaves are already pushing.

At night the growth can be heard if you know how to listen — not a sound so much as a creak travelling through a house raft, a board settling, a pipe ticking as something underneath it thickens by a millimetre. People sleep through it the way people elsewhere sleep through a river. They keep their doors latched not against animals or thieves but because an unlatched door, in a house that has shifted in the night, will swing in the morning and let in the drip.

Living here means accepting that the place is finished and unfinished at the same time. The bowl is old; its forms are worn smooth by long use, its systems in balance. The bowl is also growing ten centimetres today, and will need cutting tomorrow, and the map will be wrong by spring. Outsiders ask what the town makes, expecting the board, the pellets, the paper stock that go out on the rim railway. The people of Sallowmere tend to answer that those are just what is left over.

What the town makes, mostly, is the clearing it stands in.
