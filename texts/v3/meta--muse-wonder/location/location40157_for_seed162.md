There is a plateau so high and so wide that it stopped being a place on the continent a long time ago and became a condition above it.

From below, you don't see it. You see the cloud-sea that breaks against its flanks, weeks of white weather piled up around black foothills, and above that, nothing — the top is hidden inside its own climate. From above, if you could get above, there is no single summit to orient by. There are thousands of summits, all of them new. The whole landmass is still rising.

It is warm up there, which it shouldn't be. Eight, nine, ten kilometers up and the air is bath-warm, thick, wet on the skin. The rock gives off heat from underneath. The clouds give it back from above. Between the two, a permanent, dim greenhouse is trapped — a lid of ochre vapor a few hundred meters over the highest spires, turning noon into a green-brown dusk. Direct sun arrives as a diffuse glow, sourceless. Shadows are soft and unreliable. At night it does not get truly dark either; it only gets differently dim, lit from below by cracks, pools, and living things.

And it goes on. It goes on with its own interior. There are weather systems in the deep valleys that are born, circle for a season, rain themselves out, and die without ever knowing there is an edge to the world. There are migrations that take generations. There is a slow hurricane parked over the central basin that has been turning for as long as anyone could have watched it, if anyone had ever been there to watch.

No one has built anything here. You'll find no road, no wall, no cut stone, no artifact that stays an artifact. That is not because the place is empty. It is because building is something that happens to matter here, briefly, on its way to becoming something else.

### The Young Mountains

The terrain has not had time to learn how to be soft.

Everything is sharp: knife ridges of black basalt still glassy at the edges, fangs of obsidian, columns split yesterday and showing bright, unweathered interiors — copper-green, iron-red that rusts while you look at it, seams of native metal exposed like bone. There are no rounded hills. Rounded things here are either alive, or they were made round in an instant, by heat.

The ground moves. Not in earthquakes, exactly — more continuously than that, a turbulent restlessness in the stone. Slopes groan and shed fans of glittering scree. A valley you cross in the morning may be a lake of hot mud by evening, or may have folded shut, or may have sprouted a row of new spires along its floor, still steaming, still ticking as they cool. Lakes appear in fresh craters, boil for a week, plate their shores with salts and bright electroplated crusts, then drain through a crack that opens without warning.

Soil barely exists. What passes for it is rock flour, ground fresh by the uplift, warm mineral meal held in cracks and hollows. And yet —

### The Damp

Water is everywhere and almost nowhere. There are few true rivers; the land is too young and too broken to let one keep a course. Instead there is condensation.

Moisture arrives as traveling fog banks, as sudden, intensely local rains that fall on a single spire or a single stand of trees and nowhere else, as dew so heavy it runs. In the mornings — what passes for mornings — every leaf, every metal surface, every animal's back is beaded and dripping. Broad leaves are shaped into funnels. Hollows in the stone hold warm, clear pockets a few meters across, full of tadpoles, jewel-beetles, microscopic swarming life. Then a cloud snags on a ridge and for an hour there are waterfalls pouring off nothing, off wet air itself, and then they stop.

Steam is the other water. It rises from fissures in white, silent columns, condenses on the underside of the cloud-lid, and falls back as a fine, warm mist. In some of the high forests it is raining gently upward: droplets forming on the hot ground-cover and lifting, beading on the cooler leaves above, raining down again. A closed loop, humming to itself.

The plants love the uncertainty. Roots don't wait for rain. They drink air.

### The Green Industrial Layer

Vegetation is lush, layered, and deeply strange about what a plant is for.

There are fern forests with fronds the size of sails, slick and dark, growing straight out of fractures with no soil at all, their roots splayed over bare rock like hands. There are bamboo-analogues that grow a meter in a night, hollow, jointed, knocking softly together — except the knocking is too regular, and if you cut one (it will seal itself behind you) the interior is lined with coppery tissue and carries a faint electric charge.

And everywhere, the chimney trees.

They stand in groves on the hottest slopes, trunks like tapered smokestacks of bark and blackened wood, crowns reduced to a circlet of heat-shimmering leaves. Inside, they are refineries. Their roots mine — truly mine, following metal veins, dissolving ore with acid, drawing it up in solution. In the heat of the trunk the metals separate, layer by layer. Pure copper weeps from lenticels on one side, beads of tin on the other, iron scales slough off the bark in red plates. At the crown, excess heat and gas sigh out in a steady, almost silent exhalation.

Nothing harvests them. The metals fall, are eaten by rust-mosses, dissolved by rain, carried into the plating-lakes, taken up by roots again. It is an industry with no product and no factory building, a complete metallurgy running at continental scale inside living wood.

Other plants spin, weave, pump. Vine systems in the Loom Valleys actually loom themselves — growing across a gorge in woven mats strong enough to walk on, tightening and loosening with the humidity, repairing tears within hours. Piston-flowers open and close in the steam vents, driving air through underground chambers. Turbine reeds stand in the hot runoff channels, their seed-heads spinning ceaselessly in water too shallow to turn anything, spinning anyway, generating a charge that passes root to root through whole marshes. On still evenings the marshes tick faintly with stored lightning and small fish leap between the reeds, silver arcs completing circuits.

It should be loud. A foundry the size of an ocean should deafen. But the air is thick and the moss is everywhere, a deep, sound-eating pelt over rock and machine alike, and most of the work here is hydraulic, pneumatic, chemical — pressures and flows, not hammering. The loudest regular sound is dripping. Beneath that, felt more in your teeth and sternum than heard, is the subsonic thrum of the whole plateau at work and at rise. Occasionally, very far away, a new peak will split with a single flat crack like a shot in another country. Then the moss swallows it.

Most of the animals are silent too. Sound travels badly here and echoes lie — the young valleys return your voice from places you haven't been yet, sometimes in altered form — so the abundant fauna has largely given up on calling. They speak in light, in scent-pulses, in vibration through shared root-nets, in changes of color moving herd-wide like weather.

There are engine-grazers in the warm basins, huge, low, six-legged, their backs plated with overlapping keratin and iron-rich scale, lungs that work like bellows with a visible, rhythmic machinery under translucent skin. They breathe steam. They filter metal dust and spores from the fog and precipitate the excess as shining dung that the forests immediately colonize. Silent moths with a three-meter wingspan drift between the chimney trees, wings dusted with conductive powder, trailing faint sparks in the dusk. Herds of glass deer — not glass, but tendon and crystal-growing bone — pick their way through the Knife Fields, chiming only when they stumble. Down in the condensation forests the air itself is crowded: gnats, dragonflies with gear-jointed wings that fold like instruments, frogs that inflate resonant chambers but use them to pump water uphill into bromeliads instead of to sing.

Everything is eating something industrial and being eaten by something green. Caterpillars extrude silk laced with wire. Birds build no nests — they incubate their eggs in the warm, humming hollows that open in cooling lava flows, hollows that grow around the eggs and open when they hatch. Predators hunt by sensing electric fields. Their teeth are replaced continuously from mineral reserves in the jaw, new ones pushing out bright and machined-looking, with edges too even to be believed.

### The Rule Underneath

After a while — days of walking, if walking is the word for climbing, descending, waiting for a ridge to finish happening — a deeper pattern begins to show through the variety, and it is less comforting than any single strangeness.

Form here is a temporary assignment.

A cooling pool of metal-rich melt will begin, as it cools, to crystallize into perfect hexagonal gears, meshed, turning slowly as the pool contracts. For an hour it is unmistakably a mechanism, purposeful, interlocked. Then green filaments thread up through the axles. The gears soften at the edges. Leaves unroll from the teeth. By nightfall it is a plant, and small animals are already living in it.

A fallen engine-grazer does not simply rot. Its bellows-lungs keep working for days after death, then slow, then begin to root. The iron plates of its back crack into something very like basalt columns. Within a month there is a small, warm hill where it died, with ferns on it, and metal condensing on the ferns.

Seeds dropped from the turbine reeds sometimes germinate as seedlings. Sometimes they germinate as tiny, complete pumps, beating wetly in the mud until roots grow out of the intake and anchor them and they learn, over a week, to be reeds. Stone, plant, animal, machine — these are not kingdoms here. They are phases. Matter passes through them the way water passes through steam and rain and ice on the slopes, restlessly, because the place is too hot, too charged, too fast, too *new* to let anything settle into being only one thing.

That is why nothing is built. A wall would be a wall for perhaps a season. Then it would be a hedge, or a herd, or a slow machine walking itself apart into the next valley. Artifacts don't survive their own tendency to become alive, and living things don't survive their tendency to mineralize. The turbulence isn't weather on top of the land. It goes all the way down into what things *are*.

Time misbehaves accordingly. Valleys produce seeds of plants that haven't flowered upstream yet. Machines appear in fresh lava already worn, already repaired once by something with leaves. In the central basin, under the permanent storm, there are terraces where you can watch a landslide run uphill in the dimness, not reversed exactly, but out of order — dust settling first, then the crack, then the fall of bright stone — as if the mountain were trying several versions of the event at once and hadn't yet chosen which one to keep.

Gravity, too, is local opinion. The plateau's own mass, rising and shifting, makes pockets where falling water curves, where the heavy fog flows along ceilings of overhangs, where your inner ear insists the slope is the other way. The vastness has generated its own small physics, valley by valley, none of them quite agreeing.

### The Center, If There Is One

Inland, the forests thin into fields of fresh vents and plating-lakes so saturated with dissolved metal that their surfaces mirror the cloud-lid in copper and oil-rainbow. The fauna grows larger and stranger and quieter. Things move through the steam on many legs with the unhurried confidence of infrastructure. The chimney trees here are enormous, their trunks fluted like cooling towers, and they stand in rings — rings that pulse, heat shimmering in sequence around the circle, power passing from tree to tree in a slow rotation that has no center and no obvious purpose except to continue.

Sometimes, through a tear in the vapor, you can see all the way across the basin to ranges that were not there last week, blue-black teeth against the glowing lid of the sky, already green at their bases, already inhabited, already refining ore into birdsong-less, light-speaking life.

You understand then that you are not looking at a landscape with an ecology and an industry in it.

You are inside one organism of process — mountain, forest, factory, herd, storm — that has not yet decided, and may never decide, which of those words it is. It rises. It rains upward. It smelts itself and grows itself and grazes on itself in warm, dim, nearly perfect silence, abundant in every crack, and it makes new kinds of itself faster than the old kinds can disappear.

Leave a tool on the ground overnight, and in the morning it will be heavier — plated, rooted, faintly warm, beginning, at the joints, to bend toward the light.
