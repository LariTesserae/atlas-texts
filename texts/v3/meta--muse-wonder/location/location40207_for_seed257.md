It is a world that begins only after you go under.

From outside, dosed to the weather on a bare summit far above the tree line, it does not look like water at all. It looks like a low, windowless drum of stone, thirty paces across, squatting in thin wind among other black peaks. No stream reaches it. No lake reflects beside it. The air up here is too cold and too fast and too poor to hold in the lungs, and the ground is all scree and frost-split rock. Rain, when it comes, arrives sideways and leaves again.

Inside the drum, the cold and the wind simply stop.

You enter feet-first down a wooden ladder into a dry throat of air, and then you duck beneath a lintel that was carved to be passed only swimming. On the other side there is no other side. There is only the Cistern, filled to its vaulted roof, warm as a held body, dark as the inside of a mouth.

It is small enough to cross in twenty slow strokes. It takes hours to know at all.

Everything here was made. There is no natural floor to find with your feet, no original cave wall behind the work. Flagstones were laid stone by patient stone over leveled bedrock, then walls of fitted blocks were raised in rings, then partition walls, low baffles, troughs, stairs that now lead obligingly down into deeper water, ladders carved intending dry use that have not been dry for two thousand years. A domed roof closes it. Clay pipes, bundled like reeds, run under the flags and up through the walls. Stone hoppers, stone spouts, stone feeding holes plugged with wooden bungs. Even the irregularities that feel at first like reef or fallen rock resolve under the hand into intention: a sill worn smooth, a niche, the stump of a post, a statue seated against the current that never comes.

Ancient water has unmade most of that intention. Edges that were square have gone to knuckles. Steps are ramps. Inscriptions are long, shallow braids under the fingertips. The mortar joints have swollen out in pale mineral seams, and where fresh water seeps in — a finger's width a season, no more — travertine has grown over the carving in slow, brainlike shelves. Face after face along the lower wall has been reduced to the same featureless, egg-smooth witness. You can tell a hand was intended here, a mouth there. The rest has been taken by time and by mouths.

Because, in the absence of any plant, the place is crowded with animals.

There is no green in here. No algae films the stone, no moss, no weed lifts from the cracks, no root has found its way down from above. The light, what little of it arrives through one cracked overflow slit high in the dome, falls in a single diluted shaft and dies before it has traveled an arm's length. Nothing can grow from it. The colors are all mineral and flesh: limestone grey, iron brown, clay red where a pipe mouths open, and then everywhere the whites, ambers, and translucent pinks of living things that have forgotten the sun.

Blind fish drift through the middle water, no longer than a hand, their eyes covered over with pearl skin, their whiskers tasting ahead of them. They do not startle. At this altitude, in this warmth, nothing has taught them hurry. Glass eels labor slowly along the partition tops. Freshwater mussels have claimed the lower walls in overlapping pavements, opening and closing with a patience indistinguishable from the stone's own settling, and between them a gravel of snails — thousands, tens of thousands — each grazing its own minute territory and laying down, over a life longer than the keeper who dropped its ancestor here, a polished meander you can feel but not see. 

In the niches live pale shrimp, dense as thrown salt when a hand passes too close and they all, together, flick backward into shadow. The vault above your head is not empty either. Fish swim inverted along its underside, grazing mineral crust from the roof as if it were simply another floor. Sponges — animal, though everything in you wants to call them plant — have built soft, fingered colonies around the pipe mouths, filtering a current too slow to feel against the cheek. Small crabs pick through the silt beds that have accumulated on the stairs.

They eat each other, mostly, and what the wind delivers. Insects blunder in through the roof vents and drown on a surface that exists only in the uppermost chambers in summer, when the level drops a hand's breadth. Mineral sweat leaches through the mortar. And twice a year, by custom older than any reason anyone can give for it, the keepers unplug a feeding hole and lower in a mash of pounded grain and dried meat on a wooden tray, sinking it slow so as not to cloud the water. It disappears over days, not minutes. Nothing here feeds in a frenzy.

The keepers themselves are nearly as quiet as their charge. They work with stone hammers, wooden tongs, woven nets of twisted fiber, clay bailers — nothing that would ring, nothing that would spark. They do not speak in the inner drum; whether that is reverence or just the practical fact that a voice underwater, heard through the submerged passage, arrives as a meaningless pressure on the teeth, no one outside the tradition can say. They tap signals on the wall. One tap for halt. Two for rise. A long scrape for turn back, you have gone far enough, the deep cell is for the breeding fish and no hand enters there, ever. The dead are lifted out with tongs when they float. The living are never lifted out at all.

The rules suit the place, and the place enforces the rules without malice. Move fast and you stir the silt from the flagstones, and then even the one thin blade of light is gone and you are swimming in warm brown nothing, tasting limestone, waiting — sometimes an hour, sometimes the rest of a dive measured in held breaths between the air pockets trapped deliberately in the vault's high cells — for the water to forgive you and clear. Speak, and the fish fold away into the walls and the shrimp vanish and the mussels shut, and the apparent emptiness afterward is total. Touch the roof colonies and a slow snow of white particles descends, and settles, and is eaten.

It is warm here improbably, against all the evidence of the peak outside. The black stone of the roof drinks the high sun all day and gives it back all night into a body of water too enclosed to lose it. In deep winter, when the outer walls are rimed and the ladder rungs split with cold, the Cistern holds at blood heat, breathing out a faint mineral steam through its vents. Its tide, if it can be called that, is a seasonal swelling of an inch, a slow overflow down a carved channel beaded with snails. The water that leaves is replaced by seep and rain so gradually that a full turning of the volume may take ten years. Some of what you swim in fell as rain before you were born.

That may be the final strangeness of it, quiet as it is: that a structure built by hand, with hand tools, on a mountaintop where no water should stay — built perhaps as a reservoir, perhaps as a fortification against a drought no one remembers — has outlasted its purpose so completely that it became a sea instead. A sea in a stone drum, above the clouds, unlit, holding its own weather and its own blind nations, eroding its makers' names away at the speed of snails.

Outside, the wind works on the peaks. Inside, nothing is working quickly at all.
