There is no shore here, and no surface in the ordinary sense. The top of the world is ice, packed tight, and the bottom is a drowned valley far below it, and between the two everything is water.

It begins overhead. Sea ice has been driven into this basin and held there until it has nowhere left to go — floe against floe, ridge against ridge, white and blue-grey blocks jammed together for tens of kilometres. From underneath it does not look flat at all. It looks like an inverted mountain range. Pressure keels hang down ten, twenty metres, their undersides scoured smooth, their cracks filled with smaller ice. In the depths of the polar winter there is almost no light through it. At midday, for a little while, a very faint blue-grey dimness leaks down through the thinner pans and along the leads that have frozen over only recently. It is enough to show that there is a ceiling. It is not enough to see by. Fifty metres down, even that is gone.

The water is cold in the specific way seawater under ice is cold: just below the freezing point of fresh water, hovering at the temperature where it would turn if it were not salt, and not still enough to be stable. It comes into the basin through a narrow, shallow sill at one end, forced by the tide, and because it has been dragged along the underside of the ice it arrives supercooled and full of ice of its own making.

This is the packed part. The upper water column is a slurry.

Frazil ice forms there in the turbulent flow — tiny platelets and needles of crystal, millions upon millions of them, born in moving water too cold to let them stay liquid and too restless to let them join into a sheet. They do not fall. Ice wants to rise, but the current keeps taking them, tumbling them down and sideways and back up in slow vortices, so the water is thick with them, a silent underwater snowstorm going in every direction at once. Near the ice ceiling they collect into a soft, spongy layer of platelet ice, metres thick in places, hanging like a loose second roof. Below that they thin, but they never quite disappear. A beam of light, if you brought one, would not travel. It would strike crystal after crystal after suspended silt and stop within an arm's length, in glittering murk.

And yet it is quiet. That is the strange conjunction of the place: it is violent and it makes almost no sound. There is no surf, because the ice keeps the wind off the water. There is no roar from the current, because currents at this scale move with a kind of massive slowness even when they are strong enough to lift sediment off the bottom and carry a diver, or a dead fish, or a lost rope, kilometres from where it went in. What you hear, if you could be there and listen, is small. The tick and faint chime of ice crystals striking each other. A soft hiss where silt is being dragged across silt. Very far away, transmitted through the ice with terrible clarity and then lost, a groan as the pack shifts and a ridge keel grinds against another. Then nothing again. The turbulence is enormous and mostly subsonic, felt in the chest and in the tug on anything tethered, rather than heard.

The basin itself is old beyond any human sense of old. Before it drowned, ice of a different kind — a true glacier, hundreds of metres thick — ground its way down this valley again and again. It took the corners off everything. The bedrock underneath, dark gneiss and basalt, was scoured into long, low, whaleback forms, striated in the direction of flow, and then, after the sea came in, it was buried. What is exposed now is mostly mud: fine grey glacial flour, silt and clay laid down grain by grain until the floor is as near flat as a natural floor can be. It stretches out of the dark in every direction, a town-sized, valley-sized plain with no landmark worth the name. A dropped object does not catch on anything. It sinks into sediment so tightly compacted by pressure and age that the top few centimetres are soft and everything below is like wet stone.

Here and there the mud is interrupted. There are fields of rounded pebbles and cobbles the glacier carried and dropped, packed close as paving in patches, black and green and rust-red, each one worn egg-smooth. There are scattered shells, white and brittle, from animals long dead, half-dissolved. There is sunken kelp, which is almost the only vegetation the place can claim — not growing, because nothing green can grow in this dark, but arrived from brighter water far away, waterlogged at last and sunk, lying in dark olive ribbons across the mud. The cold preserves it obscenely well. It rots over years rather than weeks. Now and then a piece lifts in a bottom eddy, turns over once, and settles again a little farther on. Under the ice far above there is a true, sparse algae that clings to the platelet layer and browns the underside in the brief spring, but most of what reaches the bottom of it arrives as a slow fall of detritus, marine snow mixed with crystal, that the current usually steals before it can accumulate.

Fauna is rare, and what there is has the slowness of cold blood in cold water. A Greenland shark may pass through the basin once in a season, huge and blind-looking and unbothered, its flesh full of the chemistry that keeps it from freezing. A skate ghosts along the mud, stirring up a plume that hangs for an hour. Isopods like woodlice the size of a hand pick over a dead fish for months. Amphipods swarm, briefly, where something has fallen, and then disperse back into scarcity. In the platelet ice overhead, small crustaceans graze the algae and are themselves the reason anything larger bothers to visit at all. Mostly, though, a watch kept here would be a watch kept over emptiness in motion — silt clouds boiling up from the flat floor when the tide turns, frazil streaming sideways through black water, a single feather star anchored to a cobble, opening and closing in the current to filter what passes.

There is nothing built. No wreck has ever made it this far under the pack in one piece; no foundation could be poured, no wall would stand against the moving ice above even if anyone tried. Human presence, such as it is, is primitive because anything more complicated fails. From holes kept open in the ice far overhead, hunters sometimes lower lines on wooden hand-reels — sinew and braided line, bone hooks, a stone sinker, in more recent generations a hook of simple forged iron. The line goes down through the platelet slush and vanishes, and mostly it comes back empty. What gets lost stays lost. Somewhere out on the mud there is a stone weight with its knot still around it. Somewhere else a knife with a wooden handle, the handle swollen and black, the plain iron blade furred with rust, half-buried and then uncovered and then half-buried again as the silt moves. These are not ruins. They are litter, and the basin is slowly digesting them at the pace it digests everything: grinding them down, coating them, taking them in.

The rules here are the rules of pressure, cold, and reversal. Twice a day the tide turns and the entire basin changes its mind; water that flowed in over the sill flows out, eddies that spun up on the flat spin down and re-form turning the other way, clouds of mud that had begun to settle are lifted and carried back over ground they have already crossed. Nothing dropped falls straight. Bubbles do not rise clean and fast; they collect frazil as they go, grow sluggish and spiky, and freeze into the ceiling. Warmth is a rumour from somewhere else — there is no vent, no spring, no sun-warmed shallow. Sound does not travel the way you expect and light does not travel at all. Up is not bright. Up is merely ice, and a hole in it, if you are lucky, is a slightly less dark place in the dark.

It is not a weird place. It obeys everything. That is what makes it so thoroughly itself: ordinary physics, given a flat old drowned valley, a roof of packed ice, a cold turbulent tide, and a winter with no sunrise, will produce this — a world that is submerged to the exclusion of all else, full to saturation with crystal and silt and moving water, ancient and eroded to smoothness at the bottom and ceaselessly, silently unmade and remade at the top.
