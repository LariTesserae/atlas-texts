From the air it does not look like a place at all.

It is high, sharp country — a young plateau still lifting, split by fresh faults. Black granite and pale, unweathered scree run in long unstable slopes. Nothing softens it. There is no soil to speak of, no plant life, only stone in the process of becoming smaller stone: frost-split boulders, knife-edged ridges, fans of talus that shift an inch a year and sometimes a meter in a night. The wind comes thin and clean off the icefields and cuts straight to the bone. At this altitude breathing is work. Your lungs burn. Your eyes water and the tears freeze at the corners.

And it is wet, improbably wet for a place so cold and bare. Water is just under the skin of the mountain. It seeps from every new crack, beads on south-facing rock at noon, runs for an hour in bright threads, then skins over with ice again by three. The stones weep. Mist makes itself out of nothing whenever warm seep meets cold air, hangs low in the hollows, and vanishes. All day there is the sound of melt finding a way down, though you have to stop and hold your breath to hear it.

In one of those hollows, where a fault has opened a slit no wider than a doorway, there is a weather station. Or that is what it looks like. A low ceramic bunker, a dish crusted white, a mast, a red door in the cliff with its paint sanded off by ice. Sparse, mute, half-buried.

The door is the whole exterior of the city.

Beyond the second airlock — there are always two, and never both open at once — the mountain does not end where it should. The rock has been stitched open from inside. Current engineering would call it an expanded volume; the people who live here just call it the Cup. A pocket of inhabitable space folded into the fault, larger within than the mountain could hold without it. They treat this the way other towns treat a bridge or a well: infrastructure, maintained, unremarkable, occasionally closed for repair.

You step out of the lock and the town is below you, descending.

It is built in layers around a central well, a vertical shaft cut — and kept — open to the sky. Real daylight falls down it, bright high-altitude light, blue at noon and hard gold in the late afternoon, thrown deeper by mirror vanes that tilt almost imperceptibly through the day. At the top a transparent membrane breathes. It lets the light in, breaks the wind to a sigh, sheds snow in slow, silent slides. When a gust hits, the whole skin flexes and the light ripples faintly across the streets far below, as if the town were under clear water.

The streets are a single spiral ramp, wide enough for two carts to pass, winding down past seven terraces. Houses, workshops, cistern-halls, schoolrooms, a market arc, a bathhouse, offices for the water board — all of it fitted into the curve of the wall with long gaps between. The density is urban in its organization, street numbers and districts and a night tram that makes the full spiral in forty minutes, but sparse in fact. Between one lit doorway and the next there may be thirty meters of bare wet rock, a shuttered condenser, a niche where someone has set out a bench and a kettle. It gives the place the feeling of a city after curfew, even at midday.

Everything is mineral. There is no wood here — nothing grew to make it, and nothing is imported that can rot. Houses are sintered stone and matte ceramic and a pale alloy that repairs its own hairline cracks overnight, sealing them with a slightly darker seam. Roofs drip into gutters, gutters into runnels, runnels into grates in the ramp. Floors warm themselves just enough to dry bootprints within minutes, then go cold again. Walls sweat. In the morning every interior surface is beaded with clean condensation, and the wipers — small, flat machines that move like patient beetles — go out to collect it before the bells.

Water is the civic fact. It is harvested from the membrane, drawn from the fault seeps, melted from rime scraped off the upper terraces, reclaimed from breath and baths and cooking steam, and stored in the great cistern that occupies the bottom of the Cup. The cistern is the town square, in effect: a black, still reservoir under a vaulted ceiling, with walkways around it and the council hall looking down. Pale blind fish turn very slowly in it, rarely seen, considered good luck when they surface. Thin films of mineral-loving bacteria color the overflow pools a milky turquoise and give the lower levels a faint smell of hot stone and iron. No green thing. The food vats produce protein and fat and something that can be persuaded toward sweetness; meals are hot, salty, and much discussed, precisely because there is so little variety to discuss.

It is cold inside — kept just above freezing by design. The reactors, three silent cylinders sunk below the cistern whose operation the engineers describe with the calm vocabulary of plumbing, run more efficiently cold, and the folded space itself sheds heat poorly. So people dress indoors as out: layered synthetics that never quite dry, hoods up in the market, gloves cut off at the fingers for work. Your breath fogs when you talk. Metal handrails sting. But the cold is wet rather than biting — a damp, encompassing chill that gets into cuffs and collars and stays. Bathhouse hour, when the waste heat is released and the lower terraces fill with steam, is the social center of the day. The whole town seems to be there at once, quiet even then, conversations held low. Voices do not carry far here; the wet baffles, the curved stone, and a lifetime of habit keep it mostly quiet. What you hear instead is process: drip and counter-drip, the subaudible hum of the tram rails, ice ticking as it forms on the membrane struts, the noon melt-pulse when the sun hits the seep-fields outside and for twenty minutes every channel in the city runs loud and bright and the cistern rises by a finger's width.

For all its quiet, the Cup is in constant motion. Mirror vanes track. Condenser shutters open and close in sequence around the rim, so the town inhales in a slow rotation. Maintenance crews in orange shells rappel the wet face of the well, chipping rime, resealing alloy, freeing frozen drains. Loads move up and down on inertialess lifts that make your stomach hesitate a second after you have arrived. Neighborhoods take turns on water-watch, heat-share, membrane duty. A city that must make its own air pressure, catch its own rain from stone, and dispose of its own warmth cannot afford stillness; its activity is not bustle but metabolism.

Fauna is almost an rumor. The fish. A clinic cat, long-haired,ysin lordly, who sleeps on the warmest pipe in the school. And outside, three ravens who have learned the airlock chime and wait, patient as officials, for the outer door to cycle, hoping for vat-scraps. When one appears at the membrane, children are called to look, quickly, before it is gone.

The rules, posted inside every lock in chipped enamel, are simple and absolute: wipe the rime from your boots or the ramp floods by evening. Never prop a door. Leave your outer layer in the wet-room; do not bring the mountain's cold deeper than it has to go. Report new water — a seep where there was none, a trickle with color to it — because new water means new movement in the rock, and the Cup must move with it or be closed. At night, when the membrane goes clear and the stars appear directly overhead, undimmed, crowded down the throat of the well, you are requested not to stand talking in the shaft. Your breath rises, freezes on the skin, and in the morning someone has to climb up and scrape you off, word by word.

People here do not think of it as hidden. They think of it as contained. A town that fits in a crack in a young mountain, kept wet and cold and lit by ordinary sun, holding itself open by means nobody's grandfather fully understood and everybody's daughter can maintain. At noon the melt comes down, the channels brighten, the fish turn once in the dark, and the long spiral fills — briefly — with the sound of a place very much alive.
