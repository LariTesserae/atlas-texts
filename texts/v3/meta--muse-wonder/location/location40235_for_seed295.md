It starts with stairs, and it never quite stops being stairs.

You climb to it from below — from the hot low country, from forest that still grows on actual ground — and at about six thousand feet the forest thins, the air stays warm but loses its weight, and you find the first cut. A step made of fitted stone, wide as a road, green in the joints. Then another. Then a landing faced with stone, a retaining wall faced with stone, a drain running beside the steps faced, at the bottom, with stone slabs so old the water has worn a thumb-deep groove down their centers.

After that, there is no more mountain in the ordinary sense. There is only what was built on it, over it, out of it, so long ago and so completely that the original slope is a kind of rumor underneath.

The valley, if you can still call it that, opens above the cloud line and then immediately closes itself in again. It is maybe four miles long, two miles at its widest, a high bowl tipped toward the morning sun but so deep in its own walls and so thick with growth that it spends most of the day in shadow. Tier follows tier up both sides. Terraces broad enough for houses at the bottom, terraces narrow as shoulders near the top, all of them walled, all of them paved or planted or roofed at some point, all of them now softened into approximate level ground by centuries of leaf-fall, dust, dung, and collapsed thatch settling into the fill behind the stonework. If you dig anywhere — and the animals dig everywhere — you do not hit soil and then rock. You hit rubble fill, then a floor, then another terrace's ceiling, then broken clay, packed earth, charcoal, more stone. Even the streambed is a canal, its banks revetted, its bed laid flat. Even the cliffs at the head of the valley have been faced and niched and stepped for footholds. There is no natural substrate left to stand on. You are walking on a made place that learned how to hold itself up.

And hold itself it does. The erosion here is not dramatic. Nothing is crumbling in any hurry. The walls lean in places, bellied out by tree roots, but the roots then become the wall. Moss stitches the joints. Fig trees that began in a crack have split a corner tower and then grown so thick around it that their trunks are now load-bearing, their canopy the roof. Rain, when it comes, finds a thousand designed ways down: spouts, channels, stepped falls, cistern lips, and where the design has failed, the plants have improvised a slower one. It is settled. It is weathered into balance, a ruin that finished ruining a long time ago and settled into being a landform.

It is damp without ever being properly wet. Rain arrives as interruption — a twenty-minute downpour off the peaks in the afternoon that hammers the leaves and drums the stone hollow, then stops. Between rains, mist sits in the bowl until late morning and condenses on everything: stone sweats, feathers bead, the undersides of broad leaves drip steadily onto roofs that no longer have people under them to care. Water pockets where it was meant to pocket. The cisterns, round stone tanks roofed with living mats of fern, stay half full all year. The canals run an inch deep, or run dry and shine with algae, or carry a sudden brown pulse after a storm and then clear. Frogs live in the plumbing. Every drain mouth has its resident, throat pulsing in the dark opening. It is a riverine place with no river, moisture arriving from the sky and the cloud and the breath of too much vegetation, and staying because the shade keeps it.

For shade is the other condition. At this height the sun above the mist is fierce, but down in the built bowl it rarely lands directly. The eastern rim steals the morning. The western terraces steal the evening. And over the floors and plazas and stepped courts, the second growth has closed in — not wild forest returning, exactly, because it grows from terraces, from wall-tops, from roof-lines, in rows that still remember orchards. Mango and banana gone feral and huge. Cecropia in the plazas. Tree ferns along the canals. Orchids and bromeliads packed into every niche and cornice, holding their own cups of water after rain. Under that, it is a green twilight, humid and close, smelling of wet stone and guano and crushed leaves. Inside the roofless houses it is darker still, and inside the covered galleries and nesting chambers under the terraces it is true dark, warm dark, the kind that breathes.

Because now the population is not human at all.

That is the thing you understand before you even see the whole valley, because you hear it from a mile below, on the stairs, through the mist. The sound is not loud in the way a waterfall is loud, steady and ignorable. It is deafening in layers that keep renewing themselves. Parrots — thousands of them, green and scarlet and blue-headed, roosting in the upper terraces — come off the walls in shrieking sheets at any disturbance and settle again still shrieking. Swifts nest in every drain hole and under every cornice, and their twittering is a continuous high wire of noise that never switches off in daylight. Cicadas take the warm hours and raise a ringing that you feel in your teeth and in the hollow stones underfoot. Toward evening the frogs in the cisterns and canal pockets and bromeliad cups add a lower, pulsing chorus that makes the walls seem to hum, and then the bats begin to pour from the under-terrace chambers in rivers, and the owls and nightjars start, and the frogs get louder to compete, and there is no point in the twenty-four hours when this place is quiet, or even moderately hushed. You shout to a companion six feet away. You sleep, if you sleep, with your ears ringing, half convinced the stone itself is sounding.

And they are everywhere, in densities that stop being countable. Monkeys — capuchins and howlers — travel the wall-tops like roads, which they are, and raid the feral orchards and drop rinds into the courts below where coatis and agoutis and a snuffling, rooting traffic of peccaries work the leaf litter. Tamarins nest in the thatch remnants. Kinkajous sleep rolled in the dark of the roof spaces. Every sunny stone that does get an hour of light has its lizards doing push-ups, its butterflies puddling at the damp mortar. Ants run in permanent highways along the carved gutters, carrying green leaf-flags that make the aqueducts look as if they are flowing uphill with vegetation. Bees have filled whole chambers; you can smell honey near certain doorways and hear the comb-dark interior seething. Wasps paper the undersides of lintels. Termites, not to be outdone in a built place, have built their own architecture atop the human kind, mud towers and sheeted runways over the stone towers, so that some walls wear a second, earthen skin that is also inhabited.

Birds most of all. The terraces are a rookery expanded to the size of a town. Egrets stalk the shallow cisterns. Herons stand in canals that were designed to a heron's depth. Hoatzins clamber, clumsy and prehistoric-looking and entirely unbothered, in the shrubs overhanging the water pockets. Toucans bounce along the fig branches and swallow the fruit whole. In the largest open court — a plaza paved with slabs that ring like bells when the monkeys drop seeds on them — vultures gather in the one reliable patch of midday sun, wings spread, surrounded by a shifting ground-cover of finches and doves pecking at a century's accumulation of seed and droppings between the stones. Nothing is hunted here. Nothing seems to have been hunted for a very long time. The animals do not flee so much as flow around you, annoyed, and resettle at arm's length, watching with the confidence of tenants.

There is no technology in any of this, and there barely was when it was made. Stone fitted without mortar, or with mud that has long since become habitat. Wood, much of it rotted to a sponge that beetles live in, some of it ironwood-hard and still holding a roof-beam's shape after centuries. Thatch collapsed and re-thatched by no one, simply layered now with fallen leaves until it is a soil with a slope. Clay shards underfoot, bone gone soft in the damp, woven fiber preserved only in the driest undercroft niches where the bats roost. No metal. No glass. No wire or wheel or engine or any remnant of one. The builders worked with hands and fire and leverage and time, and then the place passed — slowly, presumably, over generations of dwindling — to builders who worked with beak and claw and saliva and dung, and kept building. Weaverbirds stitch their pendant nests under every surviving eave until some doorways hang with green-and-yellow fruit of them, swaying. Swallows mud their cups onto carved faces. Monkeys drag branches into favored sleeping corners and trample them into platforms. The maintenance of this city is now entirely biological, and it is, in its piecemeal way, continuous.

The cycles are slow. That is the strange counterpart to all that noise and motion: almost nothing here is in a hurry to change. The parrots explode and settle. The bats pour out and pour back. The rain comes and the canals flash and subside. But the terraces subside a centimeter in a decade. A fig that split a wall in your grandfather's hypothetical lifetime would still be splitting it, slightly more, in your grandchildren's. The seasons are a matter of which trees fruit — fig, then mango, then the wild avocados that drop and rot sweetly on the warm stone and bring in clouds of iridescent flies — and of the mist sitting a little lower, the cisterns a little fuller, the frogs a little louder. Growth and decay have matched each other for so long that the place gives an impression not of wilderness reclaiming a ruin, but of a single, very old organism metabolizing at a pace too large to see, its heart audible in the animal chorus, its skin made of worked stone and leaf.

By dusk — early, in that shadowed bowl — the light goes blue and then simply goes. The warm air stays. The surfaces stay damp. Fireflies come out over the lower terraces where the canal water pockets and mirror them, doubling the effect, and the night shift of sound rises until the dark is as full as a physical substance, frog and insect and night bird and the rustle and squeak and thump of ten thousand animals moving through a stone town they have inherited floor by floor, roof by roof, niche by niche.

You can walk for an hour in any direction and not leave it. Stairs to terraces to courts to covered ways to stairs again, all of it made, all of it lived in, the sky narrowed above you to a strip of cloud, the horizon when you finally climb to a high wall — and you should, the view steadies the sense of scale — showing rank after rank of green, animal-crowded terraces filling the whole high valley to its rim, rings of a constructed mountain that long ago stopped distinguishing between building and landscape, and settled, deafening and lush and dark, into being both.
