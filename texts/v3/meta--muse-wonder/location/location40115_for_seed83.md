There is a hatch at the bottom of an alley stair, and then another stair inside it, iron, going down far past where the daylight gives up. The heat meets you before the darkness does. It rises up the shaft in a slow, damp breath, smelling of hot oil and wet iron and stone that is not stone.

Below is one room. That is the whole of it.

Perhaps forty paces long, twelve high where the vaulted ceiling is not lost behind pipework. A cathedral, if a cathedral were built by plumbers and then forgotten by everyone except the pressure in its walls. There is no earth here, no rock face, no natural floor to stand on. Under the grating is another grating. Under that, riveted plates, a sump, more plates. The walls are tile over brick over iron sheeting, patched in places with boiler plate and lead. Even the pillars are hollow — standpipes, cased in chipped green enamel, sweating at the joints. Everything you can touch was carried down here in pieces and bolted to something else.

It is almost completely dark. The hatch, when it shuts, shuts completely. What light remains belongs to the room itself: three pilot flames in glass nipples, blue as deep water; a row of indicator bulbs, most dead, two still holding a dull red; and, in the wettest corner, a faint phosphorescence from the slime on the cold-water inlets, enough to make the falling drops glimmer for an instant before they vanish. Your eyes do not so much adjust as learn a new economy. Shapes stop being shapes and become differences in blackness, in warmth, in the closeness of air.

And it is close. Dense beyond the room's actual size. Pipes run at ankle height, knee height, waist and shoulder and just above the head, in bundles of three and seven, wrapped and unwrapped, crossing, doubling back, descending through the floor only to rise again two meters away wearing a different coat of paint. Between them: valve wheels, chain falls, pressure gauges with cracked faces, junction boxes, catwalks stacked three levels high and connected by ladders with no rails, tanks shouldered together like cattle in a pen. There is nowhere you can stand and see to the far wall. There is no far wall, practically — only intervals. You move crabwise, ducking, turning a shoulder, putting a palm flat against a warm pipe to steady yourself, learning which ones are too hot for skin by the smell of other people's learning baked into them.

The established ones are polished. That is how you know the routes. In the dark, generations of hands have brightened the lagging and worn the rust to a deep, stable mahogany patina. The iron stairs have dished treads. The valve at the center junction is not painted anymore; it is bright all over from turning. Nothing here is new and nothing is quite failing. Lime has built slow stalactites under the leaking flanges, white and orange, an inch a decade. Rust blooms, flakes, blooms again in the same place, kept in balance by heat and handling. It has reached the form it wants to be.

The heat is the true architecture. Forty, forty-five degrees near the floor, hotter as you climb the catwalks toward the trapped air under the vault. It does not fluctuate so much as it breathes on a cycle too slow to wait for — over hours, the pipes tick and expand, a centimeter here, two there, and narrow passages get narrower, and a doorway you squeezed through in the morning will take your shirt buttons by afternoon. The whole room is very slowly flexing. Old-timers, if there were any left, would navigate by it: go left while the system is cool, come back high when it has swollen.

Water is not abundant, but it is inescapable. No stream runs here. Instead, where the one cold inlet crosses the hot chamber, there is a permanent, localized rain — condensation forming on the underside of the pipe, fattening, falling in a curtain no wider than a doorway, pattering onto a tray of perforated steel. Elsewhere, damp. Beads standing on tile. A slick on every horizontal surface. The sump at the low end holds a black mirror three meters across that never quite dries and never quite fills, fed drip by drip, drained sip by sip by a pump that clears its throat once or twice a day and then thinks better of it.

That wet corner is the only garden. A few black ferns, wiry and heat-stunted, grow straight out of the mortar joints, nowhere soil could be. A mat of something pale and leathery spreads on the inside of the drip tray. Nothing flowers. Nothing reaches for light that is not there. It persists the way the lime persists — a side effect of a leak that has lasted longer than most buildings above.

Life otherwise keeps its distance. Silverfish in the insulation. A cockroach, enormous, lacquered, unbothered, crossing a gauge face. In the sump, if you hold perfectly still and let the pilot light's reflection settle, something small and silver turns over once near the surface — a fish, or the idea of one, eyeless, living on what falls in. There is said to be a rat, singular, white around the muzzle with age, who knows the expansion cycle better than anyone and is therefore never seen twice in the same passage. You will hear the fauna more often than you see it: a scratch inside a hollow pillar, abruptly over.

For an industrial place, it is profoundly quiet. The boilers at the far end — two of them, riveted, squat, their firedoors long since wired shut — are not running, or are running so far below capacity they amount to a memory of running. What remains is a low, distributed activity: the tick of contracting metal, long after you think it is finished. The hiss of a seal that is not quite a leak. Water finding a seam. Once in a great while, a flywheel up on the third catwalk, beltless and purposeless, completes a fraction of a turn as if disturbed in sleep. The quiet pools between pipes. Whispering carries strangely and talking feels like a misuse of the air; the room teaches you to move your lips less and listen with your skin.

The rules assemble themselves.

Keep one hand in contact with the warm line — the one with the yellow bands, worn almost to the iron — or you will loop. Do not step off the grating where the paint has worn to a cross; under it, the plates are hot enough to take a boot sole. Drink, if you must, from the condensation tray, never from the sump. And do not be on the low catwalk when the pump finally commits and the room exhales — a slow purge of steam and sump-air that moves through the passages in sequence, erasing the phosphorescence, filling the dark with a white that is hotter than the black was. It lasts nine minutes. It has always lasted nine minutes. Afterwards the ferns are a little flatter, the gauges have crept, the passages have rearranged themselves by a knuckle's width, and the room settles again into the long, patient middle of its cycle, dripping, ticking, holding its heat in the dark under the city, complete.
