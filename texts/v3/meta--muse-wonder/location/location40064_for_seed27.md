It begins with the noise arriving before the place does.

You are still in the ascent shaft, still rising through three, four thousand meters of smoothed stone, the old mountain long since worn to a stub beneath you, and already the floor is humming under your boots. Not a machine hum. A creak, a knock, a long woody groan traveling up through the rock, then a sudden clatter overhead like a million dry hands applauding at once. By the time the doors open you have to raise your voice.

"Welcome to the Sere Table," the keeper shouts, cheerfully. She is not wearing ear protection. Nobody native does.

The Table is a plateau that was a mountain range a billion years ago. Time and roots have planed it down. What is left is a broad, open tableland, twenty kilometers from rim to rim, gently domed in the middle, its edges rounded as river stones. There are no peaks left, no gullies worth naming. Only low swells of dark bedrock showing through in bald patches where even the Plant thins itself, polished, grooved by root-hairs into corduroy patterns that all run one way, toward the center.

For the Plant is singular, and it is also everything.

There is no soil here. There has been no water here, in any free sense, for longer than the city keeps records. The sky is a pale, dim pewter even at noon — the sun small and white through high, permanent haze — the air cold enough to make your knuckles ache, thin enough that the first day you breathe in short, embarrassed sips. And ankle-deep across the entire plateau, unbroken from rim to far rim, is a mat of living tissue.

It crunches.

That is the first wrongness your body learns. Total vegetation, and it is dry as old paper. The mat is made of interwoven rootlets, bark scales, fallen leaf-plates, and living cords the color of rust and ash-purple. It gives slightly underfoot, like walking on cork, then crackles. Every step releases a smell: hot resin, pepper, iron, something electrical. No damp. No rot. Its sap is not sap. Cut a stem — you are forbidden to, everywhere, by law delivered at shouting volume in the shaft — and it weeps a clear oil that beads, hardens in cold air to amber, and never wets your glove. The biochemistry here runs on oils and ionic salts, on hydrogen pried directly from silicate rock by root acids, on carbon snatched from the air. It does not drink. It quarries.

Above the mat, spaced far apart, rise the Trunks.

Sparse is the only word. You can see for miles between them. They are not trees so much as districts of the same body, thick as houses, black-purple, their bark built from overlapping plates the size of doors. Each plate is a dead leaf, retained forever. When the wind comes across the Table — and after ten in the morning it always comes, cold, scouring down from the rim — those plates lift a fraction on their hinges and clap, clap, clap together. Each trunk is a percussion section. Between trunks stretch the canopies, not overhead but hooking sideways at twenty, thirty meters up, reaching for the next trunk: colonnades of stiff branch and resonant leaf. The leaves are metallic, thin, almost black to catch the dim light, edged in pale copper. They do not rustle. They ring, very faintly, each at a slightly different pitch depending on its size.

Stand still on an empty stretch between trunks at midday and the sound is architectural. Wind-drums in the trunks. Leaf bells above. The mat itself talking under the wind pressure, transmitting knocks for enormous distances, faster through root than through thin air. Under it all a subsonic creak as the whole organism flexes toward — not the light, the light is too weak and too diffuse to be worth following — toward the sound. It grows toward noise. Louder places grow faster.

That is why there is a city.

The settlement is called tuning. Officially on maps it is Table City, nine towers and a low sprawl of linked halls hugging the central dome, home to perhaps twelve thousand people, but that shape misleads. It is more like a dam built across a river of growth, or a hand cupped perpetually around a mouth. Its buildings are made from harvested plate-bark and woven root-fiber, sealed in resin, massively thick-walled, all curved because the Plant will push flat walls over in a season if it likes the sound inside. Their interiors are miraculously quiet: cork-lined vestibules, double airlocks not for pressure but for decibels, rooms where for the first time you can hear your companion swallow.

Threaded through every building, through conduits grown rather than drilled, is the other technology. It does not look like engineering. Small silver nodes like beetle eggs sit grafted into living trunks. Hair-fine filaments disappear into the mat and come up kilometers away. Field-lifts carry people up the inside of hollowed Trunk Elevators without cables or contact, just a queasy lessening of weight and a pop in your sinuses. At night the nodes pulse a pattern you can feel in your molars, and the mat around the city answers by slowing its growth by a precise three percent. They are editing it, constantly, at molecular scale — coaxing this one root to turn, poisoning that one bud with a signal molecule that has not yet been invented anywhere downhill, keeping streets open, keeping the canopy from closing over the dim sky entirely. Nobody fully understands the oldest nodes. They were placed by the first tuners and have since been partly overgrown, partly incorporated. The Plant now makes components for them inside its own stems. When a node fails, sometimes a new one extrudes, already working, from a bud, silver shell and all.

Thus the rule that governs life here, printed over every airlock: NO OPEN WATER.

Bring a canteen out uncapped and nothing happens at first. Let a cup spill and the mat does something terrible and beautiful. It remembers water with some deep ancestral pathway the tuners have never been able to delete. Within minutes the spill-site swells. Pale, soft shoots — the only soft, pale things on the Table — force up through the root-cords, splitting bark, splitting resin seals, splitting stone if they must. They grow toward the wet patch at visible speed, drinking it, mad for it, and for two or three deafening hours that spot will flower with white, water-fat blooms that burst and seed the wind. Then the water is gone, bound away, and the shoots desiccate where they stand, becoming more dry plates for the wind to clap. A burst pipe in the old days cracked a habitation tower in half. Now all plumbing is triple-skinned, all human waste freeze-dried before transport, showers are dust and ultrasound and oil. You learn to spit into a sealed cup.

Fauna has made other arrangements and thrives in the loud, dry cold. Herds of mat-grazers wander the open spaces between Trunks — long-legged, big-eared beasts with split hooves that drum their location into the mat, chewing leaf-plates with grinding sideways jaws, swallowing rock dust to help. They are the color of the mat; you notice them mostly by the silence-shaped hole they make, because near a grazing herd the birds stop. The birds — not quite birds, feathered gliders with four membranous wings — ride the wind colonnades and scream constantly, not to be heard over the Plant but to steer it: their nesting colonies scream a trunk into bending away over a generation. Their droppings are dry pellets that the mat absorbs by nightfall. Smaller things live in the bark cities: earless burrowers that speak only by thumping the root interior, iridescent oil-beetles that tap intricate rhythms to ask the Plant to open its resin ducts — and it does, it opens for them, a bargain older than humans. Predators here are almost silent in the air, huge and soft-footed on the mat. In a world this loud, silence is camouflage.

The day has a pace built from wind and growth. Dawn is dim grey and comparatively still, the leaf-plates shut, frost not of water but of carbon dioxide glittering white in every crevice, sublimating by eight with a collective ticking hiss. Mid-morning the wind rises, the city opens its tuned vents, and certain streets become wind-instruments on purpose — public thoroughfares that howl a clean A note, market squares designed to roar so the mat will grow outward, away from the stalls. Afternoon is active: grazers migrate, gliders hunt, maintenance crews in resin-hard suits walk the mat with listening spikes, driving new filaments, pruning back shoots that have begun to lean too lovingly toward a busy kitchen. Their work is gardening and conducting at once. If they keep the city's total noise-shape steady — the tuners have scores, literal musical scores, revised nightly by the node network — the Plant maintains the gentle dome of open space the city needs. If they stopped for a week, the nearest Trunks would lean in to listen. In a month, the leaves would meet overhead. In a year there would be a hill where the market was, and the wind would make it clap.

Evening the wind drops all at once, as if a door closed beyond the rim. This is the loudest moment, paradoxically. Released, every tensed plate and leaf settles, knocks, rings down its stored vibration in a long diminuendo that travels from rim to center through the mat. You feel it in your feet long after your ears give up. People come out of their thick houses then, into the cool, dimming air, and talk at normal volume for an hour in the streets while the Plant ticks and cools around them. Children press their ears to the ground to hear the grazers coming home by footfall through root. Somewhere far out past the last trunk a glider screams once, and a whole branch, very slowly, begins to turn that way.

Nothing here is finished. The mountain eroded to its final form, and then life moved in and made that form sing, and then people moved in and learned, with tools they half-invented and half-were-given, to sing back just enough to live inside the song. Sleep in Table City is sleeping in a cork room inside a drum inside a forest that is one dry, cold, ancient animal made of leaves. You dream, the natives tell you — shouting again, kindly, over the morning wind as the plates begin to lift — of rain, because your body remembers what the Plant is trying to forget.
