It begins long before you can see it. You hear it on the switchback road, still an hour below the plateau, with the windows up and the engine laboring in the thin air — a pressure in the teeth, a roar that is not quite separate from the blood in your ears.

Then the road crests, the heat comes up in a warm, wet breath, and the city opens.

It is high, and it is old in a way that has nothing to do with its factories. The whole town sits in a broad, eroded bowl worn out of pale limestone and basalt, a valley that finished changing a long time ago. Its rim is softened to nothing, its floor terraced by water over centuries. Into those terraces, people have cut a second city: canals, cisterns, sluice gates, millraces, reservoir beds, all following the same patient lines the rain once followed on its own. The factories did not invent the shape here. They moved into it.

Water is never far from the surface. That is the first rule.

The plateau catches cloud. In the morning the mist condenses directly out of the warm air onto cold stone, onto iron railings, onto the copper roofs, and runs down in black streaks. The rock faces weep even in dry weeks, beading along old fault lines. Every street crosses water — a channel under a grate, a pipe sweating inside a wall, a bright canal between houses where the current is made to fall, and fall, and fall again in steps down the slope of the town.

And falling is what makes the noise.

At the north end of the bowl, the river comes in low and brown from the high fields and is taken. Not dammed all at once, but divided, again and again, into a dozen engineered cataracts that drop through the city. Each drop turns something. Turbine halls of riveted steel and wet brick. Stamp mills. A textile works. A foundry that uses water power to run its bellows and electric dynamos in the same vast, echoing room. The falls are beautiful — white, aerated, throwing up a permanent warm spray — and they are absolutely deafening.

Near the Third Drop you have to shout directly into a person's ear to be misunderstood. Near the First, shouting is a kind of pantomime. The roar takes the middle out of every other sound. Tram bells do not ring, they flash. Doors do not slam, they shudder in their frames an hour later in your chest. Conversation has evolved a local economy: short sentences, written slates hung around children's necks, a fluent, elaborate sign language that uses the whole forearm and is spoken even by hearing families at the dinner table, out of habit and tiredness. Newcomers get headaches, nosebleeds, a frantic loneliness in crowds. Old residents go a little deaf in exactly the same frequencies and call that peace. When you have lived here a year, they say, you start to hear the water *underneath* things, like a voice under a floor.

Because the noise is everywhere, the city is built sparse to escape itself.

This is not a dense city, though it is unmistakably a city — electrified, administered, industrial, with tram lines, freight yards, a water board, a turbine exchange, neighborhoods with names and rivalries. It is spread out across the whole bowl in loose clusters, each cluster huddled close to its own power source and then stopping. Between the clusters lie huge emptinesses: flood fields of cracked concrete that are lakes for one month a year, drying yards paved with dark stone, empty plazas big enough to lose the roar in for a moment, reservoirs like flat mirrors reflecting nothing but sky.

A district will be twenty houses, a mill, a cistern tower, a laundry strung with steam — and then half a kilometer of scrub and bare wet rock before the next. The trams run long, lonely distances between stops, sparking blue where their poles cross the mist.

The houses turn their backs to their falls. That is the second rule.

No windows on the water side. Walls there are a meter thick, limestone faced with a second skin of terracotta tile, hollow in between and filled with sand. All the living happens in interior courtyards on the lee side, where the sound drops from a roar to merely a loud, constant rushing and you can hear a spoon against a cup. Kitchens open onto courtyards. Bedrooms open onto corridors that open onto courtyards. Balconies are glassed and gasketed like ship cabins. Over generations, the vibration has taught the builders what will fail: no tall chimneys, no loose slate, no unbraced glass. Everything is bolted, wedged, mortared, and then bolted again. In the older quarters, you can see door frames that have walked two centimeters out of true over fifty years, shimmed and shimmed again with copper coins.

Materials go soft and dark with damp. Limestone blackens. Iron rusts to a deep, almost purple brown but never quite flakes away, polished by spray on its windward edge. Wood swells and is replaced by more wood, pre-swollen in tanks so it will fit. The electric lines run in ceramic conduits that tick with condensation. Everywhere smells of hot wet stone, machine oil, algae warmed by the sun, and the faint ozone crackle of the dynamo halls.

Vegetation has made only a sparse peace with this. On the open rock between districts there is little: wiry, silver-leafed scrub, a kind of high-altitude sage that grows sideways, agaves planted in rows on parapets to hold the soil with their roots, their leaves cupping water. Nothing tall can stand the constant shaking of the air near the falls, and nothing delicate can stand the spray-then-baking cycle of the warm days. But in the cracks of the sluices, where no one can quite scrape, there are ferns of an improbable green, and black moss, and mats of tiny white flowers that close when the mills change shift and the current slackens for twelve minutes. The reservoir margins grow reeds in disciplined lines where the water board allows them. Trees exist mostly in courtyards, protected: one fig, one stunted olive, grown in the shape of the wall that shelters them.

Animals are rare, and the ones that stay are altered. There are mules with leather ear hoods, who work the towpaths and sleep in padded stalls. There are courtyard dogs who learn signs faster than children. Above the bowl, huge, silent swifts carve circles in the clear air above the mist — they nest in the dry rim cliffs, outside the noise, and never land in the city at all. Sometimes at dusk you see them dropping through the spray rainbows, hunting insects dazed by the vibration, and gone again. People look up when that happens. It is one of the few moving things here that is fast.

Because despite the roar, despite the industry, the pace of the place is profoundly slow.

The heat at altitude does it, and the damp, and the sheer neurological cost of living inside a sound that never stops. Work in the mills is done in long, unhurried rotations. The water itself moves on a schedule set by snowmelt two valleys away, weeks ago. Goods cross those empty distances between districts at walking pace. Afternoons empty out. Between two and five the trams run almost vacant, the courtyards fill with people sitting very still with their hands in their laps, letting the vibration pass through them like weather. Repairs are never finished, only maintained — a gate is being re-seated, a channel re-lined with basalt blocks cut to fit a curve first laid out three hundred years before there was electricity to light the work. Everything erodes at exactly the rate it is repaired, and has for a long time.

Once a year, for the re-lining, they stop the First Drop.

They do it in stages, at night, with a week of posted notices and flashing beacons, because the silence is considered a public hazard. For about four hours the greatest of the cataracts is diverted into its flood field, and that whole quarter of the city goes, by degrees, quiet.

People come out of their courtyard houses and stand in the streets. Dogs bark, astonished at themselves. You can hear insects, which exist after all, in the scrub. You can hear a baby two streets away. You can hear the small falls, the drips, the secondary wheels still turning in other districts, suddenly distinct as individual voices. Old people weep without quite knowing why. Teenagers dare each other to speak in normal voices and then fall to whispering, frightened by how far it carries.

And then, in the small hours, the gates open again. The water finds its old, polished channel. The white fall re-establishes itself step by step, the turbine takes up its note, the windows begin again their almost invisible trembling, and the city exhales and goes back inside itself — sparse, warm, wet, industrious, held together across its empty spaces by wire and canal and that enormous, continuous sound, which is not so much heard anymore as inhabited, like altitude, like gravity, like a hand pressed forever, gently, against the sternum.
