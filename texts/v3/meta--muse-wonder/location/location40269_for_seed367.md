From above, there is nothing there.

For hours the plateau goes on without changing — high, warm, wind-polished, old beyond old. The mountains that must once have stood here have long since been worn down to their own roots: low domes of pink and grey rock, broad saddles, plains of grit where every pebble is rounded and varnished dark by sun. Nothing sharp remains. Even the dust has been sorted by the wind into ripples so fine they look combed.

The air is thin and dry enough to split the lips. There are no rivers, no streams, no green lines to follow. A few thorn bushes hold their ground far apart, each in its own circle of bare earth. At noon the light up here is merciless. At the edges of the day, it is empty.

And then the ground, quite without warning, dips.

It is not a valley. It is a pocket. A shallow, steep-sided bowl sunk into the plateau, perhaps eighty paces across and a third as deep, invisible until you are standing on its rim. Its upper walls are natural, smooth-worn rock. Its lower walls have been built for so long that building and rock can no longer be told apart.

This is where everything has gathered.

### The Bowl

You go down by a stair cut into stone, then worn by feet and hooves into a polished trough. It is narrow enough that you have to turn sideways for a donkey coming up laden with brushwood, and it makes a gate without needing a door. Voices rise to meet you before you see anyone — not one voice but a layered roar in miniature.

The bowl is a town, complete, though it could be hidden in a single city square somewhere below.

Houses of rough stone and mud-brick are terraced in rings around the slope, each roof serving as the yard for the house above. Walls are shared. Alleys drop between them in steps, roofed here and there by grapevine, fig branches, woven reed mats, until much of the lower town lives in a permanent, greenish dimness. Direct sun reaches the central floor for only a few hours around midday. The rest of the time light comes reflected — off a pale wall, through leaves, up from dust — soft and brown and close.

It is warm down here in a way the open plateau is not. The rock holds the day. The wind that scrapes above passes over the rim and seldom descends. At evening, when the plain turns quickly cold, the bowl breathes out stored heat from its walls, its packed earth, its bodies.

The building materials tell the economy at a glance: stone cleared from the terraces, mud mixed with straw and dung, timber so scarce and precious that a roof beam is patched, spliced, and inherited. Doors are low. Lintels are worn glossy where heads and horns and hands have brushed them for generations. There is no metal to speak of beyond a few iron-shod tools, knives, and bells. Everything else is hand-work — clay pots sealed with resin, rope of twisted fiber, baskets, hide buckets, wooden ploughs, stone hand-mills sunk into floors.

Districts exist even at this scale. The upper sunny ring is granaries and drying floors. The middle ring is houses. The lowest, shadiest level is stables, communal ovens, the cistern mouth, and the threshing floor that doubles as market, meeting-place, and dance ground. Paths have purposes and names. A turn no wider than a man's shoulders is a street because generations have treated it as one.

### No Water, Much Life

There is no well in any ordinary sense. At the lowest point of the bowl, under a roof of flat stones, a seep weeps from a crack in the rock — not a spring, a dampness. It fills a clay-lined basin a few cupfuls at a time, clearer in the cold months, barely at all in the hot ones. Above it, stone channels — no wider than a finger — lead from every roof and terrace to cisterns sealed against evaporation. On still mornings, dew is harvested from angled slabs of rock propped around the rim, each one licked by condensation and draining, drop by drop, into a pot.

No water is ever thrown away. Wash water goes to the vines. Water from rinsing grain goes to the animals. Urine, human and animal, is collected for the gardens and for tanning and for making the mud plaster set hard. A spill is not a mess here; it is a loss, and children are taught to wipe one up with dust and squeeze the dust out over a plant.

And yet, impossibly, the bowl is lush.

Not lush as a riverbank is lush — there are no reeds, no moss, no broad wet leaves. This is a dry luxuriance: dense, waxy, small-leafed, deep-rooted, and overwhelmingly green against the stone. Fig trees have forced their roots twenty feet down into fissures and spread canopies over whole alleys. Grape vines climb house to house in ropes as thick as a wrist, making tunnels of shade. Pomegranate, prickly pear hedges with paddles stacked like green hands, rosemary and sage and thyme in every wall crack, beans and squash and herbs in terraced beds no bigger than blankets.

Each plant is fed the way the town feeds itself — constantly, and from waste. Dung goes to the beds. Ash goes to the beds. Old thatch, feathers, ground bone, sweepings from the threshing floor. In return the gardens produce fodder cut daily by the handful: vine prunings for goats, fallen figs for pigs, bean stalks for donkeys. Nothing leaves the loop, because the bowl cannot afford for anything to leave it.

The gardens smell rich and close — warm earth, crushed herbs, fermenting manure, ripening fruit — even while your throat stays dry.

### More Animals Than Ground

The people live here, but it is the animals that make the place feel full to bursting.

Goats occupy the roofs, picking their way along parapets with bells at their throats, reaching for vine leaves allowed to hang just within reach. Sheep are folded at night into ground-floor rooms whose warmth rises into the sleeping quarters above. Donkeys bray from the stair and set off every other donkey in sympathetic outrage. Chickens are underfoot continuously — in alleys, in houses, on the millstones the instant a back is turned. Dogs, lean and dusty, hold hereditary sleeping spots in particular patches of shade and defend them like property. Cats slip along the wall-tops.

The air belongs to birds. Doves and pigeons nest in holes deliberately left in house walls, hundreds of them, their cooing a constant under-note and their droppings harvested from ledges. Swallows build mud nests under every eave and arrow through the alleys at head height, turning so sharply they brush sleeves. Sparrows bathe in dust in such numbers that a disturbed patch seems to explode. In the warm months the whole bowl hums with bees from hives built into a south-facing wall — clay tubes in a bank, each with its own small flight-storm in front of it — and flies gather in shining clouds wherever animals stand, and lizards, bronze and green and sand-coloured, own every sunlit stone, doing their push-ups over the script of the town.

At feeding times, the sound becomes genuinely loud. Bells, hooves on stone, roosters, bleating, cooing, children shouting messages from roof to roof because it is quicker than climbing down — all of it caught and held by the bowl's walls, amplified, returned. A conversation at the cistern can be heard, word for word, on the topmost terrace. Privacy, here, is a matter of manners, not of distance. Everyone knows whose goat is coughing.

This is the central rule of the place: every presence is near every other, and so everything must be negotiated. Whose goats may graze which roof. Who gets the shade of the great fig between the third and fourth hour. Who sweeps this stretch of alley, and therefore who claims its dung. Which family takes seep-water first on which day, measured not by the jar but by the jar's marked notch. Rights here are tiny, precise, and ancient, and most disputes are not about land — there is almost none — but about droppings, shadow, and sound.

### The Day in Motion

The bowl never settles. Before dawn the animals begin, and the town is up the stair in the blue dimness with the herds, out onto the empty plateau to browse the thorn scrub while the air is still cool. The bowl empties of hooves and fills again with smoke from the ovens, the rhythmic knock of the hand-mills, the shriek of a saw of wood on wood.

Midday brings the herds back to shade and the one hour when sun stands full on the threshing floor and everyone retreats to the vine-tunnels and low rooms; even the lizards vanish into cracks, and the only movement is bees and the slow drip at the seep. Then the shadow of the western rim climbs down, the stone begins to give back its heat, and the second working day begins — fodder cut, water carried up in skins notch by notch, repairs to walls that are never finished being repaired, bread baked, animals milked into wooden bowls, birds coming in to roost in a vast settling rustle in the fig trees.

At night the plateau above goes black and star-crowded and cold. Down in the pocket the dark is warmer, closer, and full of breathing. Goat bells tick as heads shift in sleep. A donkey sighs through its nose. The cistern ticks with a single falling drop. The rock ticks, cooling.

From the rim, looking down after dark, you see almost nothing — a few lamp-glows in doorways, sunk below the level of the world. From the plain, a traveller could pass within a stone's throw and hear, perhaps, a faint impossible murmur of doves and bells coming out of bare rock, and find nothing.

That is how it survives. The ancient plateau offers it nothing but shelter and stone and sun. Everything else — the green, the noise, the town, the herds — the bowl makes out of itself, cycling the same water, the same dung, the same heat, the same handful of fertile dust around and around, tighter and richer with every generation, until a hidden dip in empty ground has become one of the most crowded and alive places for a hundred miles.
