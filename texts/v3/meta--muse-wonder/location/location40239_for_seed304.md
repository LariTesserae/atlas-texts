It is a town built where there should only have been water.

From a distance it does not look like a town at all. The land is low and flat for as far as you can walk it — marsh, reedbed gone thin and patchy, black pools reflecting a normal grey sky. Then the boardwalk you've been following, slick planks laid over mud, begins to dip. The reeds stop. The ground falls away in a fresh, raw scar, and inside the scar everything is packed together.

The hollow is not large. You could throw a stone across it, and on a clear morning you can see every roof at once. It is a steep-sided cup of pale, newly-broken stone and blue clay, maybe sixty paces wide at the bottom and hardly deeper than a church is tall, but a whole settlement has been forced into it, wall against wall, roof under roof, until there is no unclaimed ground left.

It happened recently, in geological terms, and in human terms too. An older marsh pool drained, a bank of clay slipped, the earth underneath — soft chalk and fractured flint — gave way and settled. What is left is sharp. Nothing here has been rounded by weather yet. The stone edges will cut a careless hand. Scree still ticks and settles on wet nights. After rain, a new white seam will be showing somewhere on the pit wall where water has prized a slab loose. The people live with that youthfulness as a daily fact: you do not lean ladders against the outer cliffs, you wedge them, and children are taught which faces weep clear and which weep brown and are about to fall.

Water is the second builder here. It is everywhere, but it is never quite a river. It weeps.

The walls of the hollow sweat even when it has not rained for a week. Beads form on the chalk in the shade, gather, and run in slow, bright threads down to a system of gutters — some cut into the rock, some made of split wood and bark, some simply a line of flat stones set with obsessive care. In the lower streets the air condenses on anything cool. Pots left out overnight are wet by morning. Iron tools bloom with dew inside closed boxes. In houses where a cooking fire has been lit, a fine indoor rain sometimes falls from the ceiling beams, warm on one side and cold on the other.

The town is arranged to catch, channel, and endure this. There are no wells. There are weep-cloths, strips of woven fibre and hide hung against favoured faces of rock in the communal alcoves, heavy by noon, wrung into jars. There are roof-catchers, inverted pyramids of oiled canvas. And under everything there is the bottom drain, a covered channel as wide as a man that runs the circumference of the lowest terrace and out, with a groaning, patient slowness, through a timber-lined cut to the marsh. Boys and old women walk it at dawn with wooden rakes. If it blocks, the lower lane floods before supper. Everyone knows this. Everyone, wordlessly, takes their turn when the warning slate is hung at the meeting hall.

Because they cannot spread out, they have built up and in.

The houses are small, stone below and timber above, sharing walls so thoroughly that it can be hard to tell where one family ends and the next begins. The stone is the pit's own — angular, pale grey and cream, set without much mortar and chinked with clay, moss, and crushed shell from the marsh edge. Upper stories are wattle, hide, and plank, darkened by smoke and damp. Roofs overlap like scales. Where a roof ends, a neighbour's yard begins: a few square feet of planks where pots are kept, washing is strung, a goat is tethered in the busiest season. Bridges of two planks and a rope join houses across lanes too narrow below for a loaded basket to pass, so much of the town's traffic happens at eave-height, ducking under lines, stepping from sill to sill.

The lanes below are not so much streets as stairs and sluices. Some are stepped, each step hollowed by feet and doubling as a drain. Some are just ramps of packed gravel that must be re-laid after every heavy wetting. There is a market square, if you can call it that — a widening the size of a modest room, paved with the flattest stones they have, where five stalls is a crowd and goods must be hung on walls rather than laid out. There is a hall, barely larger, with a roof high enough to stand a spear upright, used for storing grain in raised bins, for settling disputes, and for drying things that must not mould. There is a shrine at the wettest alcove, not much more than a niche black with algae, where people leave a first cup of clear weep-water. No one explains it at length.

Vegetation has not been invited, and mostly it has stayed away. The pit gets light — normal daylight, when the sky is open — but its floor is gravel and clay and trodden wet, too disturbed and too starved to root much. A pale sorrel pushes up in wall cracks. A black-green film makes the north stones slick. Reeds and marsh grass make a rough fringe beyond the causeway and along the outfall drain, but inside, if you want green you must climb out. One old woman keeps beans in a broken pot on a roof; they grow leggy and suspicious and are a matter of local pride. Everything vegetable that matters — grain, roots, fibre — comes in over the boardwalk, in exchange for what the pit gives: cut stone, good blue clay, lime burned in a kiln built into the warmest part of the wall, and a hard, clear water that travellers claim keeps longer in a skin than marsh water should.

Animals are correspondingly few, and therefore noticed. Eels find their way up the drain in the warm months and are trapped in baskets set where the channel narrows; a heron will sometimes stand, absurdly tall, at the pit rim at first light and be watched by everyone until it leaves. Swifts have colonised the high eaves and a colony of tiny brown snails lives only, as far as anyone can tell, on the constantly damp lintels of the lowest houses — when they cluster thick, old people say, the pressure of water in the rock is changing. Goats are kept two or three at a time, never more, because there is nowhere for them to be. Dogs are not kept at all. There is not room for an animal that does not earn its floor-space.

That is perhaps the governing rule of the place: every surface earns. Ground floors, over years, are gradually surrendered. As the town builds a hand's breadth upward, and the pit floor compacts and sinks a finger's breadth downward, the lowest rooms grow damper, cooler, harder to keep a fire in. First they become stores, then cisterns, then sealed wet cellars with a hatch and a bucket on a rope. Families live above the rooms their grandparents slept in. You can read a family's age here by how many drowned doorways are stacked beneath their living floor. The richest house is not the widest — no house is wide — but the one with three dry floors and a roof-yard that catches the afternoon sun.

It is a cool place even in summer. The stone holds the night. Breath shows in the lanes an hour after sunrise. Voices do not carry far, or rather they carry too well through shared walls, and so the town has grown quiet by custom. People speak low, knock on wood rather than stone, and pad the feet of stools with felt. Market bargaining is done close and almost confidentially. What sound there is, is water and work: the irregular percussion of dripping into a hundred vessels, never quite in rhythm; the squeak of a wooden pump by the lower cistern; bucket-chains at morning and evening, hand over hand, a soft chorus of effort; the tap of stone hammers where someone is always — always — repairing a wall, resetting a gutter, cutting a new step before the old one becomes a slide.

For there is no finished state to aim at. That is the other rule, and newcomers learn it by getting wet. A roof is a provisional arrangement with the sky. A lane is a truce with a slope. Houses lean very slightly inward after a wet winter and are jacked upright with timber wedges and good humour in the spring. The kiln is rebuilt. The outfall is cleared. A slab falls where no one was standing — they are careful, mostly, about where people stand — and by the next week it has been split for lintels. The town is primitive in its tools, wooden wedges and stone mallets and iron used sparingly for the few edges that must hold, but it is urban in its interdependence. No one household can keep its own drain clear, its own wall weeping in the right direction, its own roof from shedding onto a neighbour's fire. Privacy is a luxury of the roof-yards at dusk. Everything else is negotiation.

At night, lamps are few and shutters are fitted tight against the damp. The town in the hollow gives back almost no light to the marsh. You might pass within fifty paces on the boardwalk, hear for a moment the faint ticking of water into jars and a murmured conversation three walls away, smell woodsmoke and wet clay and the faint iron tang of the cellars, see one lit window high in the pit wall like an eye half-open — and then the flat land closes over it again, and it is only pools and reeds and the wide, ordinary sky.

By morning the weeping will have filled the jars. The lanes will be running clear. Someone will be up on a roof, re-setting a stone while the mortar-clay is still cool, and the swift, silt-grey work of staying dry will have begun again.
