You have to go down to find it, and the going down takes longer than the place itself should warrant.

From above it is nothing: a flat, pale waste of cracked salt and sun-bleached stone, heat shivering off it in silence. Then the ground breaks. Not erodes — breaks. A round mouth, perhaps two hundred paces across, with edges still sharp enough to cut a boot. Forty years ago the roof of something gave way here. The rock inside the pit is still fresh, black and ochre and unsoftened, fractured in clean sheets. Rain has not had time to round it. Wind has not had time to fill it. It is a young wound, and the town has moved into the wound before it could heal.

It is called Karal Sump.

Sixty meters below the surface the air changes. The white glare narrows to a single, dim column falling from the opening, and then loses itself in mist. What reaches the bottom is not daylight exactly, but a greenish, underwater dusk. Everything below that shaft lives in permanent late afternoon. Oil lamps burn amber behind wet glass. Pale algae makes a faint second light on damp stone. Your eyes adjust, and then they keep adjusting, because nothing down here is ever quite fully lit.

And it is hot. Not the dry, clean heat of the waste above, but a packed, breathless heat that presses on the chest. Geothermal warmth seeps up through the floor of the pit. Factory heat adds to it. Bodies add to it. The walls sweat. Your skin sweats instantly and does not dry. Iron is warm to the touch at midnight. Wood never fully cures; it stays a little soft, a little swollen, and after a few years it begins to smell sweet, like fruit left in a closed room.

The town fills the pit completely.

There is no empty ground. Terraced houses are bolted into the pit walls in rings, three and four stories leaning out over one another on iron brackets and fresh-cut stone, their roofs serving as someone else's garden, walkway, and cistern. Staircases double as market stalls. Pipes double as handrails, then as bridges, then — where a pipe runs wide and flat above the lower roofs — as streets. Laundry hangs across alleys so narrow that opposite neighbors can mend each other's awnings without leaning. A loom-shop on the third ring uses its waste steam to cook the rice for the families on the fourth. The tannery's runoff waters the ferns on the smithy wall. Every surface is claimed, layered, shared, disputed, and used twice.

At the center, taking up what would have been the plaza, stands the Condenser.

It is the reason the Sump can exist. A vast industrial tower, built upside-down by surface standards: a broad, finned crown of blackened copper spreads out near the top of the pit where the air is coolest, and a thick trunk of riveted iron descends into a shallow, steaming sump-pond at the bottom. Slow flywheels turn at its base, geared so far down that you can watch a tooth move if you are patient. Pistons sigh once every few minutes. It does not roar. Nothing down here roars. The hot, wet air swallows sound, felt and distance do the rest, and the loudest things in Karal are a drip striking a bucket, the tick of cooling metal, a wooden shutter eased shut against the mist.

The Condenser breathes, on a cycle of about nine days.

For the first days — the Dry Days — it only gathers. The copper fins go cold and dark. Moisture is drawn out of the air, out of the pond's steam, out of the inhabitants' own breath and cooking and washing, and holds somewhere inside the great body of the machine. The town feels, impossibly, slightly less damp. Lips crack. The ferns curl.

Then, without announcement, the Drip begins. Condensation beads on the fins and runs, not straight down — this is the Sump's private strangeness, learned and never explained by the engineers who maintain it — but sideways, following the cold, crawling along horizontal pipes, pooling on the undersides of beams, raining gently upward from the warm pond to the cool metal above it on the worst days. Old people call it the water remembering the sky. Children stand under cold pipes with their mouths open. Householders set out every cup, pan, basin, and hollowed leaf they own. For a day and a night the whole town is a soft percussion of dripping, and no one raises their voice over it, partly from custom and partly because loud speech, for reasons nobody tests anymore, makes the nearest broadleaf plants fold shut for hours.

Water here is never a river. It is pockets and films and sudden mercies: the sump-pond, black and warm as broth at the bottom; the seep-holes in the east wall that weep after surface rains nobody below ever sees; the perpetual condensation beading on pipe-joints and window glass. You wipe a wall in the morning and wring the cloth into your house-pot. By law — and this law is older than the magistrate's house — a wall belongs to whoever keeps it dry. Roofs are inherited. Drip-rights are married into.

And despite the dimness, despite the fact that no direct sun has ever touched the lower rings, the place is lush.

Vegetation has taken the town the way damp takes paper. Great ferns unfurl from the mortar, their fronds beaded and dark green, growing not toward the light shaft but toward warmth — toward kitchen flues, toward the Condenser's trunk, toward sleeping bodies behind thin walls. Vines with leaves like wet hands grip the ironwork and must be cut back from the flywheel daily, in a slow, ceremonial armistice. Moss grows in precise copper-green lines where pipes leak mineral water. On the Condenser's fins a metallic, rust-red lichen spreads in maps, harvested once a season and boiled into a dye that never washes out. Roof-gardens crowd with heat-loving greens, tubers grown in ash and sump-mud, small bitter melons hanging over alleys and brushing the heads of passersby. Roots have learned the buildings. In the oldest houses — forty years being old, here — you cannot say where beam ends and root begins, and no one tries to separate them, because the root is now holding up the beam.

Fauna, by contrast, has mostly declined the invitation. There is too much heat, too little sky. Now and then a blind white moth blunders in the lamp-glow and is treated with unreasonable ceremony. Small, translucent frogs live in the sump-pond's reeds and are heard perhaps twice a year, a clear glass note after a pipe bursts. A single species of black lizard suns itself, absurdly, on the hottest pipe in town, motionless for days. It is considered bad luck to count them, because there may only ever be one. The surface world sends down nothing but dust, the occasional lost bird that cannot find the angle to leave, and new settlers, who arrive blinking and overdressed and are quiet within a week.

Because everything here moves slowly. The heat forbids hurry and the machinery forbids it twice over. The cage elevator that connects the rings makes its descent in just under an hour, creaking past kitchens and workshops and sleeping alcoves, stopping for goats that no longer exist out of a schedule no one will revise. Meals cook all day. Repairs proceed by accretion. A crack in the pit wall widens a finger's width in a decade and is measured against grandmothers' marks cut beside it. Even argument is slow; grievances are left, like tools, in a damp place to soften. People speak low and finish each other's sentences not from intimacy — though there is that, unavoidably, in a place with no privacy — but because starting a new sentence costs breath.

At night the mist thickens until the lamp of your nearest neighbor is a drowned star six feet away. The Condenser ticks as it cools. Water crawls to where it is coldest. Somewhere a fern, disturbed by a sleeper's warmth through a wall, uncurls against the plaster with a sound like a page turning.

Above, unseen, the waste lies silent and blazing and empty, unaware of the dense, green, iron-bound life sweating patiently in the dark beneath it — a town the size of a cathedral, still young, still sharp-edged, still deciding, very slowly, whether it is a settlement built around a machine, or a single warm organism that has learned to dream it is both.
