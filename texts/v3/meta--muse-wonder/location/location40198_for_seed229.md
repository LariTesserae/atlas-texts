It is called the Ear.

You come to it from above, because there is no other way. For a half-day the track has been climbing through country with nothing on it but stone the color of old teeth, and then the path narrows between two leaning slabs and you are on the rim, looking down into a bowl no wider than a village green.

It should be empty. High places like this usually are.

Instead it is full of green.

Not green the way a wet place is green. There is no water in the Ear — no spring, no seep, no tarn cupped in the rock, not even dew in the morning. Your lips split on the climb. Your tongue sticks. Any sweat that rises on your skin is taken instantly by the wind and leaves a faint white map of salt behind. And yet below you thirty or forty great plants are growing, spaced far apart on the bare floor, each one lush to the point of excess, each one throwing out more leaf and plume and sail than it could possibly need.

They are, in a sense, one plant. That becomes clear once your eyes adjust to the dimness.

The bowl is maybe a hundred and fifty paces across, scooped smooth out of black and rust-red volcanic rock. Its walls are fluted vertically, worn into long organ-pipe grooves by ten thousand years of the same wind doing the same work, patiently. They curve inward near the top. For most of the day the sun never reaches the floor directly; it strikes the eastern rim, it blazes on the peaks beyond, but in here the light is filtered through dust and moving leaf until it is a brownish, underwater gloom. Warm gloom. The black rock holds the heat of what sun it gets and gives it back through the soles of your feet.

And the wind never stops being made.

Cold air slides off the high snowfields somewhere out of sight, pours down the outer mountain, and catches on the rim. Hot air lifts off the warmed floor. In the bowl the two meet and cannot settle. They turn. The Ear has a permanent, standing weather of its own — not quite a whirlwind, not quite a storm, a slow, grinding vortex that occupies the center of the floor and keeps everything loose in the place in circulation.

In the middle, where no plant grows, there is a pillar of lifted things turning in the air: grit, husks, shed feathers, dead beetles, live beetles, seeds like small hot-air balloons, scraps of woven fiber torn from old nets. It thickens and thins. Sometimes it leans and brushes the wall and the wall answers with a low stone note. Sometimes the updraft strengthens without warning and the whole column shoots twenty feet higher and bursts, raining dry debris over the grove, and every leaf shivers and every animal in the place cries out at once.

That is the first sound. There are no quiet ones after it.

The plants make most of the noise. Their trunks — if they are trunks — rise as thick, jointed columns of a pale, waxy wood, ringed like bamboo, hollow. Wind worries at the slits in their sides and they hum, each at a different pitch according to its height. From those trunks they put out sails: enormous, ribbed leaves, stiff as stretched hide, green-black on top and copper underneath, coated not with moisture but with oil. Crush a fallen fragment and nothing wet comes out. It smears. It smells of resin and hot pepper and old incense, and it will burn, sullenly, for an hour.

Higher still the sails give way to plumes — feathery, extravagant fronds that do not so much photosynthesize, in the dim, as sieve. They comb the turning air for what it carries: dust, pollen, ash, insect wings, mineral powder ground off the rim. The roots below do the rest, cracking the volcanic floor so slowly you can find fresh white splits radiating from old trees, drinking stone. Their sap is not sap. It is a clear, viscous oil that beads but never flows like water, and in cold snaps — brief, at this altitude, even here — it sets in the hollow trunk and makes the whole tree ring more purely when struck by gusts.

Between the great hollow trees, set well apart in the empty reaches of pumice and glassy sand, are the cushions. They look at first like mossy boulders, densely leafed, almost obscene in their softness in so dry a place. Get close — if the wind lets you — and you see they are bladders. They inflate through the warm part of the day, small pores sighing air in, until their overlapping scale-leaves lift and flutter. Then a gust will press them and they deflate all at once with a sigh like a bellows, ejecting a cloud of spores and a smell so sweet it makes your teeth ache. Ground birds nest in the lee of them and are periodically blown, protesting, straight upward.

For the fauna live mostly in the air here, because the ground is unreliable.

There are gliding lizards with ribs they can spread into fans, who launch from the rim and ride the vortex for hours, snapping up the insects in the debris column. There are finches, or things shaped like finches, with no interest in landing at all; they build their nests inside the hollow tree-trunks, in the resonating chambers, and their chicks grow up deaf to everything but vibration felt through their feet. At dusk they drum the trunk from inside with their beaks — tok, tok-tok, tok — to shake pollen loose from the plumes above, and the tree answers through its whole height.

Lower down, armored beetles the size of fists climb the reed-like side shoots and position themselves as living hammers, lifting and dropping their weighted abdomens. Moths with powdery, oil-proof wings cling upside-down under the sail-leaves during the worst gusts and come out in the brief lulls in such numbers that the dim air goes briefly silver. Hairy, hand-sized spiders do not build webs; they trail a single line and kite, letting the vortex carry them from tree to tree, reeling themselves in only when they have crossed the bowl. Everything calls, drums, whistles, clicks, not occasionally but constantly, because in the Ear scent blows away before it can mean anything and sight is cut by dust and moving shadow. Sound is the only reliable country. It is also a treacherous one.

Sounds do not leave cleanly here. The curved, fluted walls gather them and send them around again. A shout is taken, flattened, carried around the rim, and returned to you a minute later from behind, lower and stretched. A dropped stone may tick its way audibly around the bowl three times. By afternoon the place is full of the day's earlier noises still circulating — bird alarms from morning, a beetle's drumming amplified in a trunk, your own footsteps on grit returning as a stranger's. The tenders say not to speak your name in the Ear. Not from superstition, exactly. You will just have to hear it called back, wrong, for hours.

People do come here. You can see their work once you know to look for what does not blow away: low crescents of stacked stone on the windward side of young shoots, built without mortar, heavy enough to stay. Woven nets of gut and plant fiber lashed between trunks, not to catch birds but to add a bass buzz that the plants, apparently, grow toward. Pruning cuts sealed with black resin. Notches carved at intervals up certain trunks — a scale, literally, cut by someone with a chipped obsidian blade who wanted that tree to sound a different note next season.

In the lee of the north wall there is a single hut, if hut is the word: a half-buried ring of slabs roofed with layered sail-leaves, weighted with stones, its doorway baffled twice so the wind cannot get a straight run at the interior. Beside it a cistern, capped with a fitted stone, has stood empty as long as anyone remembers. Cracked clay cups are arranged around it. Dry offerings — salt bread, bundles of feathers, a little carved lizard with its fan-spread ribs inlaid in shell — are left on the rim. No one pours water here. When rain threatens, which it rarely does at this height and in this rain-shadow, the tenders climb up and cover the youngest cushions with hides. A few drops will hiss on the oil-rich leaves, pop the heated seed-pods prematurely, startle the whole grove into a premature chorus. A true rain, they believe, would not feed the Ear. It would detune it.

They harvest sparingly, with hand tools: copper knives gone green at the edge, bone saws, stone weights. Resin to waterproof cloth that will never meet rain anywhere else either, but will not rot. Hollow trunk-sections for instruments that can be heard from one valley to the next. Sail-leaf fiber for rope that sings faintly in any strong wind, which shepherds down below claim lets them find lost animals, and lost children, by ear.

Nothing here is still for long except the rooted trunks and the stonework. Everything else is in the process of being lifted, turned, dropped somewhere unexpected, and lifted again. A tool left on the ground will travel. A hat will join the central column and be seen, companionably, for two days, passing at head height every few minutes, before it snags in a plume and is incorporated into a nest. Even the plants are not truly still — they sway without cease, sails trimming themselves to the gusts, plumes reorienting, cushions breathing, trunks flexing at their joints with a creaking, musical complaint. Put your palm flat to one and you feel not a tremor but a continuous low conversation running up from the roots, which are themselves growing through warm rock toward pockets of sulfur and broken feldspar, and out — tenders insist, and the pattern of the trunks supports them — toward the buried body of the one original tree that ringed this bowl before there were people to hear it.

When you leave, climbing back into the thin, clean silence of the rim, the noise does not so much stop as lose its walls. It pours after you for a surprising distance, leaking out of the notch in the rock. Your ears ring. Your clothes are full of grit and hooked seeds and one small, determined spider. For the rest of the day ordinary sounds will seem to arrive too directly, from where they were made, only once.

Below you, invisible now, the Ear keeps turning in its warm, shadowed cup: one dry, oil-blooded, many-voiced plant and all the animals who have learned to live by shouting in its wind, patiently making a lush place out of air and stone and noise, with nothing to drink at all.
