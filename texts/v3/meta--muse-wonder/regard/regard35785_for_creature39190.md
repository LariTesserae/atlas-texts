If it is truly benevolent, and truly wise, it will not hurry.

That will be its first act of understanding. It will come down from the rim by the permitted route — the switchback stairs, the freight lift, the ladders bolted through other people's kitchens — not because it needs to, but because to arrive any other way would be to misunderstand the place. It will learn in its own body, if it has a body, what they all know in theirs: hurry builds heat faster than the still air can take it away. It will slow. Its shoulders will lower. It will walk as if through shallow water.

And it will not, in the first rush of pity, fix anything.

For pity, from above, is very sure what the pit needs. The pit needs to be cooler. The pit needs real rain, falling all the way. The pit needs to be widened, drained, shaded, opened to the wind; it needs soil and birds and animals scurrying in its crates; it needs its people lifted out into clean, moving air where they need not conduct an argument by moving a bucket six inches along a rail.

A power that is only benevolent would do all of that in an afternoon, and destroy the town by nightfall.

Cool the boilers, and you cool the rising breath. Cool the rising breath, and it never meets the cold loop under the rim and beads there. No beading, no drip-zones. No drip-zones, no beans in their troughs, no green seam of fern, no moss in the pipe joints for tea and poultices. Make the rain fall all the way to the roofs of the lowest streets at forty-four degrees and you have not given them weather, you have given them flood and rot in houses that share walls with cisterns, where paper already goes soft in the drawer and nothing ever finishes drying. Bring down dogs, birds, kind brown-eyed animals for the children who have never seen one — the dog was tried, in the early years. The story ends the way everyone expects. Bring down bees for the bean flowers and you have taken away the paintbrush that passes household to household in the coolest hour, and the slow, flower-by-flower concentration that looks like prayer because, in a place with no other small quick lives to do the world's small work, it *is* a kind of prayer, and they know it, and it has made them gentle in odd places.

The wise power learns this the way a visitor learns anything in the pit: not all at once, and not by looking. It stays through a cooling cycle, and a venting cycle, and a building cycle, and a sleeping cycle. It stands on the middle terraces and watches the grey veil of rain be born under the rim and fray and vanish halfway down, and understands that this failure is not a failure. It is the town breathing out and catching itself before it falls. It tastes — for taste is instrumentation here — the first drip, iron and machine oil, thrown away; the second, for washing; the patient third. It learns which handrails are decorative and which will take skin. It announces its steps on a ladder and looks at the rungs, not the room. When the felt-wrapped bell strikes once and four thousand people stop at the same time, it stops too, and rests inside that synchronized silence instead of finding it unnerving.

Only then, having learned the rules — which are not written, which are temperatures — does it act. And what it does is almost nothing, done exactly.

Deep in the black glassy rock, where the excavators' tooth-marks have not yet had time to soften and the walls still let go clean and loud as a rifle in another room, there is a fault learning its way toward the lowest streets. The pit people cannot hear it; they hear everything, and this is beneath hearing, a stress accumulating year over year. The power sets its attention there, the way a hand sets itself quietly against a wall that has begun to lean, and takes the catastrophe out of the rock's future without taking the settling, the ticking, the small honest cracks. The pit will go on being unfinished. It will not be allowed to finish all at once, on top of the sleeping half of the town.

In the boiler yards, where the massive engines are lagged in asbestos cloth under sheet iron and make not a roar but a pressure in the teeth, it does the second thing. Their hands know that lagging by texture; it is part of how they read the world in the dark. So the power does not strip it or replace it with anything strange. It leaves the weight, the weave, the feel under the palm exactly as it is, and quietly unmakes the poison in the fiber, so that what their hands trust will not, in twenty years, be what kills the people whose hands it is. No one will ever know. That is the point. A gift they would have to marvel at would be a debt. This one simply lets grandmothers go on being grandmothers, knees full of memorized stairs.

In the steam, where something sweet and chemical rides up and kills any rat that rides the freight lift down before the week is out, it does the third thing, and here it is careful in a different way. It takes the sweetness out. It does not then fill the pit with animals — that choice, if it is ever made, must be made slowly, by people putting out a first cautious saucer, not by heaven opening a crate. It only removes the reason the choice was impossible. Years from now a sparrow may come down past the shade-net, farther than sparrows come, and land on a bean trough on the middle terraces, and not die of it. A twelve-year-old may be startled, for the first time in the pit's history, by wings crossing the light. If it happens it will happen at the speed of trust, which down here is the speed of everything.

And the last thing it does is not in the pit at all. It goes up to the rim, where shade is deeded and inherited and argued over in courts, where the records that matter are stamped into tin because paper cannot survive below, and where a pit-born child who climbs out to see the moving air might, by the letter of an absence, lose the water rights and the surveyed shade that are worth more than money in the family she comes from. The power does not smash the court or lecture it. It sees to it — a clerk's eye resting a moment longer on a line, a precedent remembered at the right hour, a registrar deciding to be exact in a generous direction — that going up does not forfeit coming back. That a door, in a town built of shared walls and borrowed kitchens, works in both directions. No one down below will be told to leave. No one up above will be told to pity them for staying. But staying will, from then on, be a choice the way leaving is a choice, and not merely the shape of the rock.

It gives them no rain that reaches the ground. It gives them no wind. It gives them no sky wider than the rim, no rescue, no revelation, no new word for the weather they have. It does not make the noon disk gentler as it crosses the pit floor and turns every piece of metal untouchable, because they have built a whole courtesy around that forty minutes, and courtesy, like shade, is not improved by being abolished.

On its last night — if night means anything to it, if leaving means anything — the power sits out where they sit, on an iron balcony among the bean troughs in the coolest hour, faces turned up not to any sky but to the narrow band where their weather is born. The condensation beads a little faster in the dark. The rain that never lands falls a little farther before it vanishes. Perhaps, that once, it lets it fall a hand's breadth farther than it should. Not to the roofs. Not a miracle anyone could build a doctrine on. Just far enough that on one terrace, one old woman whose knees remember every rung feels, on the back of her wrist where she has wiped salt away all her life, a single drop arrive whole — cool, clean, tasting of nothing but the cold loop and the dark.

She does not cry out. People down there speak low; raising the voice is work, and work is heat. She turns her wrist over, slowly, and looks at it, and then she does what the pit has taught everyone in it to do with anything precious and perishable.

She shares it. She touches it, fingertip-wet, to the leaf of the nearest bean plant — and the power, benevolent and wise at last in the only way that matters here, learns the lesson it came down three hundred meters of black rock to learn, and goes, and leaves the town almost exactly as it found it:

still hot, still dim, still unfinished, still shoulder to shoulder and murmuring and exact — but no longer quietly doomed by the three poisons it could not taste, and no longer a cup with no way out of the hand.

A cupped hand of a town, held down in the young rock. Keeping itself alive on its own recycled rain.

It does not open the hand. It only, forever after, makes sure the hand is not crushed.
