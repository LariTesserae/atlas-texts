They would not drain it.

That would be the first temptation, if they arrived as powers usually do in stories — looking down through iron cloud and ice at black water where a sky ought to be, at villages forty metres down, at people gone to root and frond and hollow green hair, and calling it a catastrophe still in progress.

Drain the basin. Give them back the valleys. Give them back lungs, mouths, sheep, weather on their faces. Rescue them upright into the thin air.

A wise power would stay under long enough to understand that this would be the second drowning.

The water did not kill these people. It arrived slowly enough to be learned. Generation by generation they answered it in their bodies. To reverse it now, however gently, however lovingly, would be to flood them with sky. The kindest-looking miracle would tear the bubble-strings from their backs, silence the hollow music in their trunks, leave the bells ringing in empty air for beings who no longer have animal ears to hear them with.

So the power would do the harder thing.

They would learn to be slow.

They would not descend in glory on the surface, where nothing feeds and nothing settles and the eldest only sometimes lift their heads beneath the forming ice to listen for rain. They would come down through the dim blue-green noon, past the ice lilies letting go and turning as they fall, past kelp forests combing east and then west with the lean of the whole sea toward the moon, and they would stand — if standing is the word — in the empty cobble deserts between settlements, where only travellers and the very young cross, and wait to stop looking like an event.

A century, perhaps. Two. Time enough for a hand-high child to notice them twice.

They would learn the vegetative weather before attempting to speak in it. Not translate it, at first. Translation is another kind of drainage. They would only listen: moss-light moving valley to valley, a question asked last season answered now on someone's ribs, seed loosened by bronze, stress travelling through a hillside as chord. They would discover that grief here is not an interruption of the forest but one of its pitches. That ancestry is architecture. That a doorway kept clear of kelp-drift is not nostalgia, or not only nostalgia, but an instrument kept tuned.

And if they spoke at all, they would not speak from above, or from the bell towers, or from any one direction that could be leaned toward for the next thousand years. A god with an address would deform this place as surely as a second sun. Whole families would root wrong, listening for approval instead of tide. The towers are enough. The moon is enough.

If they answered, it would be everywhere and therefore nowhere: a faint brightening in the silt after ice-boom, a bubble-chain released a moment early, a pulse with no source for the young to carry upward and garble into something usable. Not a command. Only, once in a very long while: *heard.*

Their real work would be at the edges, and mostly against speed.

For what harmed this place, in the beginning, was not water. Water they could become. What would harm it now would be suddenness: the quick world finding it. Divers with lights brighter than noon. Sonar louder than bells. A drill in the rim. A collector taking one white stone from a cairn, one flint scraper from beside a hearth, one seed-pod in a jar, and by taking it making all the others into specimens. Curiosity with engines moves at animal pace. These people live at forest pace. Between those two tempos, even wonder can become weather too violent to survive.

So the power would misdirect. Not with wrath. With cloud. With a reputation for nothing up there — black peaks, a frozen tarn, bad maps, instruments disagreeing politely. With roads that keep almost arriving. With dreams, in potential visitors, of other mountains that need them more. The basin would remain what it has always been to the fast world: rumour without evidence, a sea where there should be a mountain sky, impossible enough to leave alone.

And against slower dangers they would act just as sparingly. If the ice thickened year on year until the noon wash failed, they would not tear it open. They would thin the cloud by the width of a hand for one summer in ten, and let the rooted decide what to do with the extra light. If silt began to choke a bell tower beyond what current and ritual could clear, they would not cleanse it in the night and present a miracle. They would set a stone rolling on a far sill, changing a fall by almost nothing, so that over fifty years the tower mouth scoured itself again. If a seed-child was carried past every lee wall into the great grey desert at the wrong tide, they might — perhaps — turn one small current aside. Six inches. No more. Enough for the pod to catch the edge of still water. Not enough to take the choosing out of the settling.

They would preserve possibilities, not outcomes.

They would not bring fish for the weirs. They would not bring birds for the ice. They would not fill the animal silence, because the silence is not empty; it is the condition in which sound learned to pollinate, and moss learned to speak, and people learned to become a forest that can hear itself think. To introduce hunger into a world whose seed-pods have no shells would not be enrichment. It would be the invention of prey.

Nor would they make them immortal, or legible, or safe from change. A benevolent power that freezes a living place into perfection has only built a clearer jar. These beings were not saved by being kept dry. They were saved, if saved is even the right word, by having time to answer change with their whole bodies. The power would guard that time with everything they had. Against flood and against drought, against ice and against fire from below, against the sudden attentions of gods and men, they would act as a watershed acts: not preventing weather, but making sure it arrives at a rate life can translate.

Once, perhaps, at the turning of a very hard tide, the eldest in the bell valley would pulse a question that had taken three hundred years to form — not *will you save us*, because that is an animal question and they are no longer, in any useful sense, animals — but something slower and more exact, moving rib to shoulder to hillside to tower in green sequence:

*Is the answering only ours?*

And the power, who had waited centuries to be asked anything so unhurried, would answer by doing at last the one visible thing they had been doing all along.

Down at the lowest rim, where a river once left the valley for lower ground, water pours impossibly up the rock face from the tarn below into the sea. It has always done so. It does not obey the basin; it remembers the level the basin chose, and corrects, and corrects, without announcement or praise. The young go there before their final rooting and hold their bubble-strings in that upward rush. Pilgrimage. Measurement. Bathing. None of the explanations are sufficient.

They are not meant to be.

That is the form this power's love would take: not a hand reaching down to lift them out, but a hand never quite visible at the edge of the world, keeping the water neither rising too fast nor falling away, turning the quick and the hungry gently elsewhere, listening through ice-boom and bell-note and rain-hiss until listening itself becomes a kind of shelter.

Nothing would change.

That would be the miracle.

The bells would go on ringing the people. The people would resonate back. Seed would fall in slow green snow through black water. Valley would answer valley until even the empty silt plains seemed briefly to have a nervous system.

And high among black peaks, unnoticed, unthanked, a sea would stay where a mountain sky should be — not because its inhabitants could not bear to lose it, but because something wise had finally understood: they already hadn't.
