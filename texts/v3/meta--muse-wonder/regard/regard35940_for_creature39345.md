It would not descend.

That is the first thing, and the hardest.

From the rim, with all that power at its back — power enough to finish the southern terrace in a night, to set the green doors true so they never warp again, to lift the smoke clean out of the bowl each evening and lay dry, seasoned timber in a stack higher than the spoil heaps — it would look down at the roofs steaming after rain, and keep its hands, if it has hands, still.

Because it would see, as anyone who stands there long enough begins to see, that the town is not unfinished in the way a broken thing is unfinished. It is unfinished the way a child is unfinished. To complete it from outside would not be a kindness. It would make strangers of them in their own streets. A man who did not re-hang his door has lost one of the hundred small knowledges that make the door his. A terrace no basket carried away becomes scenery. The whole close, low-voiced, mutually-known life down there is not an unfortunate side effect of the work. It *is* the work. The houses are only where it happens to be visible.

A benevolent and wise power would know the difference between relieving suffering and relieving people of themselves. So it would do what the best of their own elders do: it would stay near, it would learn the rota, and it would intervene only where the pit can kill without teaching.

It would learn, first. For a full turning of the seasons it would do nothing but attend — which, from a great power, is not nothing. Attention of that quality settles over a place the way the clapper settles over the bowl at night. Nothing is visibly changed. Everything is held.

It would learn which gutter spills on which path. Which grandmother's wrists have given out. Which stretch of channel is swept by whom, and who quietly sweeps it again when the first sweeping was done by tired hands. It would come to know the hours by smell as they do: wet clay, porridge and onion from the upper streets, green wood when the lower streets take their turn. It would admire — and this matters, admiration from such a being is a form of shelter — the first laws. *Do not block water. Do not cut under your neighbour without telling him. Burn when it is your street's turn.* A whole moral philosophy, unwritten, arising honestly out of limestone and gravity. It would not improve on that with commandments.

Then, at the edges of what they cannot yet know, it would begin to tip the world — very slightly, and always in the direction they are already leaning.

It would do nothing about the stoop. Nothing about the cold hands, the patching, the chilblains, the laundry that dries only *less wet*. Those are the pit shaping its people, and the shaping is not cruelty; it will make a people who can live where others couldn't, patient at the pace stone allows. To warm the whole bowl would be to unmake them as surely as to flood it.

But the bedding plane at the south face, the one no one yet knows will split in frost — that, it would not leave to be learned by a wall falling on a sleeping family. Instead, on a still night when the face is empty and the baskets are stacked for morning, it would let the crack come early. A sound like a slow knuckle through the rock. A line opening clean and unmistakable in the new cut, there for the first shift to find, tapping with their mallets, going quiet, fetching an elder. They would change their plan. They would believe, correctly, that the stone had warned them. Pride intact. Knowledge earned. No one buried to pay for it.

Likewise the water. It would not give them a river — a river would end the cistern, and the cistern, with its planks and its damp black earth and its pale lizard left alone to eat the flies, is the town's heart and first commons. But once, sometimes twice in a generation, the rain on the plain will come in a way the bowl cannot survive: a sheet flood finding the ramp like a poured bucket. Against *that*, quietly, years ahead of need, it would coax the spoil heaps on the rim to settle and grass over a fraction faster; it would nudge a shallow swale in the plain, no more than a sheep-track deep, so that the worst of a freak storm divides around the rim instead of down it. The town would never know it had been spared the night that ends first generations. Children would go on running up the zigzag to watch for thatch parties. That is precisely the point.

It would be similarly discreet with disease and hunger, the other two executioners of new places. It would not make them rich. It would make the difference between scarcity and catastrophe a little wider: the party from the plain with salt and timber finds the track passable one more time before winter than it otherwise would have; the stored grain in the pits under the floors, bailed and fretted over, moulds a little less readily; the rats, when they finally come — and they will come — arrive a few years later than the first stored surplus that could feed them, when there are more cats, more hands, more margin. Small mercies, indistinguishable from luck. The people would thank their own carefulness. Their carefulness would grow under the thanking. A wise power understands that loop and is content to disappear inside it.

And it would protect them, without ever being asked, from the one gift that would corrupt the place fastest: it would keep iron rare a while longer.

Not forever. But bronze picks guarded like family members, lent reluctantly, swung in that slow *tok, tok* that will not shatter the edge — that scarcity is doing silent work. It sets the speed of the whole town to the speed of hands and trust. A sudden plenty of hard metal would cut faster, yes, and also cut *under* faster, and arm the first serious quarrel, and let one family dig what now requires the agreement of a street. There will be a generation ready for iron, one that has internalised the unwritten laws deeply enough to hold them against new power. This is not yet that generation. So if some trader comes over the plain with a cart of bright tools and bright ideas about what the town could sell if it dug *deeper, faster*, the power might arrange — a lost wheel-pin, a long conversation at a farmstead, rain — for the cart to arrive a season late, and then another. Delay, not denial. It can tell the difference even if the trader can't.

What it *would* give openly — if "openly" is even the word — are the small, deniable kindnesses, the ones that fit into a life without restructuring it.

A hand's breadth of extra overhang in the upper cut, so the swallows thrive and take the flies that would otherwise plague the cistern streets at dusk. The seep line in the western rock kept sweet, fern-green, dripping year-round, even in the dry summer when the plain cracks. A flat stone by the cistern, pale limestone, that for reasons no one examines catches the midday sun first and holds it longest into the cold evening — where old people will begin, quite naturally, to sit, and where children warming their hands will hear which roof drips where, who is feuding, how far the south face advanced this month. No inscription. No miracle. Just a warm stone, becoming, over twenty years, the place where the town talks to itself. If the power has a favourite among its works, it is that stone, because the town will do all the meaning-making itself.

And one night a year, perhaps — the worst night, when cold air pools deepest and a baby is colicky and someone's storage pit has flooded despite everything, and the whole enterprise feels like a mistake dug thirty feet down — it would do the least measurable thing of all. It would let the bowl be, for an hour, unexpectedly still and clear. Smoke rising straight. The dripping from the seep line ticking like something keeping time on purpose. A sense, in a few doorways, unspoken because they are a people who keep even wonder low-voiced, that the hollow they are making is *noticed*. That the work is witnessed by something that finds it good. They would sleep a little better. In the morning they would put it down to a change in the weather, and pick up their baskets.

That is all. That is nearly everything.

It would not finish their town. It would guard the conditions under which they can finish it themselves — guard them from the frost-split learned too late, from the one flood that teaches nothing, from the tools that arrive before the wisdom, from every catastrophe large enough to end the story and too large to be a lesson. It would tip a hundred small chances a degree in their favour and take credit for none of them. It would leave them their stoop, their quiet, their mended cloaks, their shared walls and shared risk, their soft relentless murmurs at doorways.

Years from now, when the corners have finally begun to round, when moss has widened and the spoil heaps are grassed and there are people in the lowest houses who were born in them and find the plain strange and windy and too loud — when the town no longer looks embryonic but looks like a place that was always there — the power would withdraw the way it came: without announcement, the warm stone cooling to an ordinary stone among stones, the swale on the plain just a sheep-track, the luck just the way things turned out.

And if you asked it why it did so little, for so long, with so much in its hands, it might tap a wall twice, the way they do down there.
