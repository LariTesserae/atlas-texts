First, it would kneel.

Not out of reverence in the human sense — not prayer, not awe performed for its own sake — but for the practical reason that there is no other way to meet them. Standing, it would be weather. A shadow across the sun, a change in the wind. On its knees, face close to the black rock, breath held so as not to become a storm in a world eleven centimeters wide, it could finally be something like a neighbor.

And for a long time — by its measure, which is not minutes — it would do nothing at all.

It would watch the glass mites turn all at once in the water film, following a signal too fine for air. It would watch the springtails rebuild the same spiral levee on the same ground, though none of the builders had lived to see the first one fail. It would watch paddle-flies row belly-up across the ceiling pool and push through the surface into another sky. It would watch the seven-legged mite die under the spider's funnel, and haul itself out of the floor pool an hour later with the same green gut, the same limp, and rejoin the herd as if death had been only another crossing.

It would be tempted, as all kindness is tempted, to improve things.

It could cool the floor pool by a degree and make the pulses gentler. It could widen the Cup. It remembers — because the hanging droplet remembers, because at noon the lens shows grazers the size of hands moving along the shore of a steaming lake — that these creatures may once have been, or may dream of being, larger than this bowl allows. A wise power could give them back the lake.

It does not.

Size is not dignity. Bigness is not rescue. To enlarge the Cup would be to flood a world whose entire civilization — if the levees, the farmed plots, the kept tool of black glass, the silk gutters are not civilization, then the word is too small anyway — is built for three-centimeter migrations and nine-minute seasons. The shapes in the droplet are a memory, not a blueprint. You do not honor a people's history by drowning their present in it.

It could stop the spider. That would be easy, and it would look like mercy from above. Spare the mites. End the folding silence under the funnel. But the spider is not a flaw in the system. She is its apex, its gardener of numbers, the keeper of the gutters that carry fresh water where it would otherwise be lost, and the amphipods eat what falls from her table, and the mat feeds on what the amphipods leave. To remove predation from a closed world is not to grant peace. It is to choose a slower collapse and call it compassion because the dying was less visible.

It could break the other wrongness. It could let the dead stay dead, let water escape, let natives thrown beyond the rim finish their desiccation honestly instead of finding themselves back at the mat's edge after the next pulse, moving. It could make time run at the mountain's pace, one generation per season, sensible and slow.

It does not do that either.

Because it does not know — and wisdom begins exactly there, in refusing to pretend it knows — whether resurrection in the Cup is a prison or simply their form of continuity. The mite does not hesitate when it returns. It does not investigate itself, or worship the floor pool, or flee the spider's crevice forever after. It rejoins the herd. If death here is a door hinged like the surface between the pools, then barring that door in the name of natural order would be imposing an alien metaphysics on creatures who have built a working one out of steam and stone.

So inside the rim: nothing.

No voice. A voice would be an earthquake with grammar. No sign in the droplet; they have signs enough, and a new one would bend a hundred generations around interpreting it. No gift of speech, no lifting of the veil, no explanation of the summit seen from above. They do not need to be told they are small. From inside, they are not.

Outside the rim is another matter.

Outside the rim, the power does everything.

It makes the mountain forgettable. Not invisible — invisibility invites searching — but unremarkable in every record that might bring hands. Survey data comes back slightly wrong, altitude mismeasured, the summit a smear of uninteresting obsidian in photographs, overexposed, featureless. A climber's route bends, not by force, but by a sudden and entirely personal conviction that the scree slope to the left looks more promising. A geologist's sample bag develops a hole two valleys before the peak. Wind takes a drone. Fog, which never forms at that altitude, forms for eleven minutes, exactly as long as a boot might pause at the topmost slab.

It does not build a wall. Walls are announcements. It builds a long, boring history of nobody quite arriving.

It guards the conditions, not the creatures. The thin translucent floor of the Cup will not be allowed to crack early because someone blasted a road through the lowlands and changed the deep water. The geothermal pulse beneath the slab, that slow heartbeat of heat, will go on being fed by the mountain's youth. Sulfur will keep arriving in the seep. Sun will keep hitting black glass at noon. Rain outside the Cup — real rain, mountain rain — will be allowed to be weather, nothing more, and never a flood aimed at a soup-bowl hollow.

And it allows the end, when the end finally comes.

For the mountain is young now, but it will not stay young. In time beyond any mite-lineage's remembering, the edges will soften. Soil will form. Lichen will find a foothold, then moss, then green — the first green this place has ever permitted. The slab will split further, or crumble, and the invisible boundary that keeps the water from escaping will thin and fail, and the Cup will open to the sky like a hand unclenching. A benevolent power that loved only preservation would stop that. It would freeze the peak in obsidian perfection and keep the fauna in their sealed perfection beside it, a specimen of forever.

This one will not. It did not come to make a museum. It came to attend to a living place, and living places are not betrayed by changing. When the Cup breaks, it will be there with the same attention, neither hastening nor preventing, and whatever can learn to live in a world with exits will learn, and whatever cannot will become part of the soil the green things root in, and that too will be honored, and not called failure.

There is one thing more, and it is so small that only one being in the Cup is positioned to notice it.

The power learns their language.

Not quickly. It listens past the silence, ear to stone, through whole successions of fly generations, until it understands that the conversation has been happening all along in taps through water film and tremors along silk, in antennae laid flat against wet rock. And then, once — once only, at the scale of a single falling droplet — it answers.

A tap. Through the stone. No louder than the *plink* that arrives a fraction of a second before the rain lands.

Under her funnel, the wet-ash spider stops.

She raises one foreleg — the one not holding the splinter of black glass — and lays it against her gutter-line, feeling the vibration come up through the silk. She does not look up. She has no concept of up that includes gods. She plucks the line once, experimentally, the way she would clear debris.

Then she goes back to work, tensioning her gutters against the next rain.

The power withdraws its hand before its shadow can cool the pools. It leaves no mark, no cairn, no name for the place in any tongue. It leaves only what it found, continuing: herds of sparks turning together in the film, spiral levees rising again from boiled ground, a droplet turning slowly between two skies, showing the summit whole, and empty, and crowded with impossible life.

It leaves them undisturbed, which is not the same as leaving them alone.

For as long as the mountain stands sharp against the sky, something vast will be on its knees beside a hollow the size of a soup bowl, listening for a sound softer than a wristwatch, and keeping the rest of the universe politely, mercifully, at a distance.
