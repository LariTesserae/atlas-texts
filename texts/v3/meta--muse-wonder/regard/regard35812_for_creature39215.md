At first, it would do nothing.

Not from indifference. From the effort, at first almost physical, of learning how to look.

A benevolent and wise power does not arrive empty-handed. It arrives full of remedies. That is the danger. It sees the plateau from above — the pans dry, the gravel bare, the grass confined to cracks, the whole wide world pared down to stone and dew — and its first impulse is pity, and pity, at that scale, is a weather system.

It could bring rain.

Not a storm. It is wise, after all, not crude. A gentle increase. A river, perhaps, drawn up from the valleys below and taught to run thin and clear across the pavement. Shade at noon. Soil in the hollows. Grass where only crust now grows. It rehearses the gift and sees, arriving with it, the end of everything it meant to save.

The pans are not failed lakes. They are lakes that succeed by disappearing. The translucent things that hatch and mate and die in an afternoon need the drying as much as the wetting. The algae need the desert between dews. The cushion plants have made an entire life out of flowering for one morning and closing for a month. Flood the plateau kindly and it would not become a garden. It would become a different place, populated by faster things, hungrier things, things that could arrive on the wind and outgrow, outgraze, outbreed a people whose whole genius is remaining.

The grazers would drown in abundance before they starved in it. More food, more young, shells crowding shells beyond the dew's ability to feed them, the mineral crust eaten faster than the night could lay it down. Then the crash, at grazer speed, which is to say a famine lasting a hundred years. A gift they could not refuse would kill them more thoroughly than any drought they have evolved to seal themselves against.

So the power puts the river back.

It could do subtler mercies, and rejects them one by one, which is slower and harder.

It could mend the cracked shell of the ancient female at the pan's edge, the one whose sealed dark is leaking a little more each night, whose stacked grandchildren still ride her grooves where cushion plants have rooted. It could. Her death is legible and approaching and, to any heart, protest-worthy. But her death is not an interruption of the plateau's life. It is one of its materials. The birds are already attentive. The worms wait beneath. Within a year her cleaned dome will be nudged, rim to rim, mouthful by mouthful, into an arc, and inside that arc the wind will drop, and dust will stay, and dew will survive an extra hour, and young not yet born will shelter in the lee of their great-grandmother. To save her forever would be to steal a house from her descendants. To save her at all would be to announce that a death here is a mistake. It is not. It is architecture in progress.

It could lift them up. Give them eyes. Give them language quicker than the ticking of shells, give them the knowledge of the rim, of the valleys below the morning cloud, of their own deep history of eating mountains. This is the temptation benevolence dresses up as respect. But what would they do with a faster mind in an unhurried body, in a world that repays only patience? Awareness without agency is not a gift. It is a sentence. They already have the knowledge that matters, stored where they need it: in the tasting foot, in the sealing rim, in the instinct to remain beside the right shell when the pans fill. Remaining, up here, is already the most elaborate gesture available. The power will not improve on it by making it self-conscious.

It could at least remove suffering. It examines this for a long time, at their speed, which means it sits — insofar as such a power sits — through seasons. It learns that there is remarkably little suffering to remove that is not simply *being a grazer*, briefly, on stone. Hunger at the end of a dry month is the signal to seal and wait. Age is not an affliction visited on them; it is the medium they move in. The crack, the slow desiccation, the birds: these are the terms. A plateau without them is not a kinder plateau. It is an unfinished one, shells accumulating uncleaned, arcs unbuilt, the whole slow economy jammed with the undying.

By the time it has understood this, the power has already done the most important thing, which was to wait long enough before acting that most of its love burned off into attention.

What remains is not nothing. It is just work at the edges, where the plateau meets everything that is not the plateau.

Because the place has one true vulnerability, and it is not drought. It is being noticed by the wrong kind of speed.

The grazers survived the death of mountains by being indistinguishable from the ground. That camouflage fails against a creature that does not look for life but for ore. The bedrock here is varnished with iron and salts, ground and reground, concentrated by a hundred million patient mouths. Someday something may come up from below with instruments that read the plateau as wealth rather than emptiness. Something may come with a road, which is a river that never dries and kills everything it crosses at walking pace or faster. Something may come meaning well, with fences and protected status and visitors and wells sunk deep to help, and help, and help.

Against *that*, the power acts without hesitation, and invisibly, and forever.

It does not build a wall. Walls announce value. It does the opposite. It leans, very slightly, on the plateau's oldest trick. In the minds of surveyors it strengthens the impression of nothing. Maps lose a contour. Instruments find the mineral readings diffuse, unpromising, just below the threshold of expense. A road's planners discover, thinking it their own idea, that the pass to the south is easier. A prospector crosses the rim, looks out over thirty kilometers of apparent gravel, feels his enthusiasm quietly fail to survive the view, and turns back, and later cannot quite say why he did not stay. A well-meaning expedition is delayed by weather in the valley, loses its funding season, is reassigned to a livelier desert. Nothing is ever forbidden. Nothing is ever dramatically saved. The plateau is simply, decade after decade, overlooked at exactly the moments when being overlooked is life.

It attends to the climate with the same restraint — not ruling it, only guarding the narrow conditions the dew depends on: the clear nights, the stone cooling just enough, the thin air pulling the day's breath back down by dawn. If the wider world warms in ways that would steal the condensation, the power spends itself there, far away, at the source, in amounts too distributed to ever be traced to a flat-topped wilderness no one has named. Not cooling the plateau. Not watering it. Only preserving the terms under which water can go on visiting, briefly, as it always has.

And it erases itself. That is the last discipline. A footprint in pan-mud can outlast the animal that made it, and a power's footprints are heavier than most. So it gathers them up. Where its attention pressed too warmly on a crack and drove the worms deeper than sunrise should, it cools the stone. Where its passing displaced a shell — it is ashamed of this, early on — it waits the hour for the path to close on its own rather than closing it by hand. It learns to witness without becoming weather.

Once, if the story is allowed one indulgence, it does a single small thing inside the boundary, and does it at grazer speed so that it will never be distinguishable from the world.

Through one long night, in a shallow pan that has not held dew well in years, it makes itself cold and low and still, the size of a flat stone among flat stones. Condensation beads on it as on everything else. By dawn the skin of water in that pan is, perhaps, a fraction deeper. A fraction longer-lived. The grazers crowd its edge shoulder to shoulder, drinking through their feet, rasping the green film, unaware that anything has been given. The young stacked in the arcs get one extra hour of damp. Nothing multiplies beyond its means. Nothing is owed. By the time the sun is an hour up, the stone is gone, and the pan is only a pan again, and no shell on the plateau has been singled out, and no rhythm has been broken.

Then it goes, or it stays — at that scale the difference is mostly grammatical — and settles into the only role its benevolence and its wisdom can agree on:

Not shepherd. Not gardener. Not god.

A witness with its hand kept, deliberately and at cost, in its pocket. A guardian of the edges. A keeper of the plateau's right to be mistaken for empty by everything that would love it wrongly or use it swiftly. A power that has understood, lying at dawn with something like an ear to the stone, listening to ten thousand faint scrapings as the crowd harvests the night, that the correct response to a world this complete is not to add to it.

It is to make sure the world is left alone long enough to go on completing itself — tick by tick, shell by shell, ancestor by ancestor — at the speed of dew.
