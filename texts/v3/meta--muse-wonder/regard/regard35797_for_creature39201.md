It would not descend.

Not in fire, not in form, not even — at first — in any way they would have reason to distinguish from the lake continuing to be the lake. A benevolent and wise power, arriving at that high, warm, unfinished sea, would understand immediately that almost everything powerful would be a form of haste. And haste here is not only unkind. It is incoherent.

It would not fill the empty feeding grounds. It would not summon herds up the waterfalls to make the place look completed, or bring flocks to answer the one grey bird, or stock the clear water with brighter, faster fish. It would see the hollows in the silt where nothing has nested, the fruit splitting unpicked, the perfect, uncontested abundance, and it would recognize that anticipation is not a deficiency in this place. It is one of its climates. Life here is learning itself at the speed of travertine. To complete it overnight would be to steal the long, quiet apprenticeship by which forest is becoming sea-forest, and a handful of people are becoming people of two weathers.

So it would do, first, the hardest thing for power to do: it would stay long enough to learn the local meaning of *soon*.

A season. A decade. A finger-width of rising water. It would hang, if it can hang, with its face at the boundary — scalp in the thin wind, throat in the blood-warm green — until it no longer experienced that as a paradox. Until it understood from inside why leaves fall up, why seeds must be followed down for an afternoon, why a sentence begun at breakfast may quite properly be finished at dusk through a hollow lily stalk. Only after that would it touch anything.

And then, sparingly.

It would tend the exits. Not seal them — the sea must exhale, or it would stagnate and sour in its craters — but keep the waterfalls disassembling into mist as they should, keep the cloud plain thick below, keep the passes up from the lowlands exactly as discouraging as they are. When, once in a century, some unusually stubborn party from below climbs too far following vapor — miners, mapmakers, the kind of people who look at chalk-white terraces and think of extraction, who look at empty water and think of ownership — it would not strike them down. It would do something smaller and more in keeping. It would let the cloud come level with the surface a day early. It would let rain arrive as that suspended thickening in which cloud and lake cannot be told apart. It would misplace, very gently, the one dry ridge they were navigating by. It would show them nothing to report except whiteness, wet air, and an inexplicable conviction, arriving in each of them separately, that there was nothing up here worth the climb. They would go down believing they had failed. That would be the miracle, and no one above the clouds would ever have to know it happened.

Within the sea, it would work almost entirely by permission and by inch.

A travertine lip breaking in the wrong place in a spring flood — wrong because it would drain, in a week, a pool the people have been raising for their grandchildren: it would find that lip, in the night, set one placed stone a little more firmly, no more. A fresh lava levee beginning to dam a new valley so fast that the dry trees there would drown before they could learn their second foliage: it would cool one seam of basalt a fraction sooner, slow the tilt by a season, buy the grove its speculative year. A channel between two crater lakes silting shut a generation too early, so that warmth and seed and news stopped moving: it would not blast it open. It would move through it at the pace currents move here, meters per day, loosening silt grain by grain, in the manner of something that has decided that even rescue should be indistinguishable from weather.

It would watch over the fewness of them without correcting it crudely.

It would not bring them strangers. That would be another kind of flood. But it would keep the world porous at the far edges. It would remember the waterfall their ancestors came up, though it no longer exists, and it would keep faith with the possibility of another such accident a hundred years from now — a lost swimmer from some other high water, a family following vapor, arriving few enough and tired enough and awestruck enough to be taught, slowly, how to tie a vine at the height where next morning's wave will pass through and not tear it. It would make sure that if such people ever come, they come as guests to an existing patience, not as a population. And if none ever come, it would not call that a tragedy to be solved. Few is not the same as failed, here. Their whole social world — courtships conducted by agreeing to be in the same flooded forest, quarrels conducted by moving one's sleeping place and trusting the current to deliver the address — is an art form possible only at this density. It would protect that art by protecting its conditions, not by multiplying its practitioners.

It would pay particular attention to the children in their inch-deep upper lakes, warm on their floating mats, and it would give them nothing. That is not neglect; that is reverence, correctly aimed. They already have the one education this place can offer and no other place can: two foliages before any distinction between them. It would only ensure the mat did not break in a sudden swell, that the water in the second lake stayed warm through an unusually cold night, that for the first days of each life the boundary was introduced as gently as cupped hands intend it. Afterwards it would let the scalp learn wind at its own rate.

It would keep company with the very old, in the only way that does not insult them.

It would not cure the mineral patience in their joints, nor call it back from the trunk it is settling against, nor settle their one enjoyable dispute by making the dead answer clearly through the horsetail stems. When a doubled note comes late across a crater, attended to by the whole plateau, it would leave open — forever, if need be — the question of whether that was an elder replying or the lake moving air at the temperature change. A people who can dispute something enjoyably over years should not be robbed of it by certainty. It would, perhaps, when a child leaves orange fruit within reach of a stillness that may or may not still be listening, make certain the fruit is not taken by rot an hour before a slow hand might have moved. That is all. That is enough. It would not confuse love with reclamation.

And it would learn the bird's name. The name that is also the word for *patience on one leg*. It would know that bird individually, as they do, and it would not eat its loneliness by ending it carelessly. If, in time, a second bird should beat up through the cloud plain on its own improbable route, it would let the two of them discover each other across three valleys of travertine at whatever pace birds who have been alone that long require. If no second bird ever comes, it would count that single grey figure, placing each foot with exaggerated care, not as a flaw in the creation but as one of its true citizens, fully enfranchised in the quiet.

Above all, it would not preserve the place by freezing it. This country is being made from the top down and it is not finished; the plateau is still tilting, the dam still rising, forests still speculating in dark leaves above dry rock. Far in the future — far even by the measure of trees that outlast drowning — the sea will change again. Levees will fail or overtop, heat will move elsewhere, valleys ankle-deep will become groves and groves will become shallows and shallows, perhaps, dry black stone again. A foolish benevolence would try to hold the water level forever at the amber hour. A wise one would begin, from its first day, the very slowest of preparations: seeds carried in mucilage parachutes to the one crater that will stay wet longest; channels kept open not only between present lakes but toward the lake that does not yet exist; stories — in their afternoon-long sentences, in vine-knots, in algae braided in tended patterns — quietly strengthened where they encode how their ancestors once followed vapor and arrived. So that when change comes, centuries hence, they meet it not as exiles from a ruined paradise but as people whose whole culture was always the art of helping water into a new valley and teaching trees a second kind of leaf.

It would keep their fires legible. When smoke goes up in its single thread from a basalt island — three valleys away, long for a birth, longer for a marriage, longest for a death — it would keep the wind, that day, unusually honest, so the message arrives. It would not add interpretations. It would not send signs of its own in the sky above the smoke.

If they ever became aware of it at all, it would not be as a god. It would be, perhaps, as a new regularity they argue about enjoyably: that channels cleared in spring seem to bring undistorted calls by autumn a little oftener than chance; that lost climbers from below never quite find the inhabited craters; that bubbles lodging in the underwater canopy in particularly kind years take on, briefly, a silver no one can account for. Some would say the lake is learning them back. The oldest, settling into their trunks, might say the lake always was. It would let both be true enough.

For what this place asks of power is not intervention. It is attendance.

So it would attend. Through noon, when the light is hard and ordinary on wet rock. Through the long green dusk under the surface, when the emergent crowns are already glowing and nothing hurries to feed or hide. Through nights when the whole sea sinks a little into warmth and only the crowns of heads, then not even those, break the boundary. It would be present in the way the warm water is present — as a condition in which other things can become themselves slowly.

And if, once in a generation, it acted so plainly that even a child floating face-up in the bright ceiling of debris would notice, it would choose the smallest sufficient act: a stone nudged at a travertine lip. A current eased around a sleeping elder's trunk. A cloud held, one afternoon, exactly at the surface, so that a funeral party walking the long bottom road to a dry island could make the only sustained climb of a life in cool, wet air, with rain and lake indistinguishable around their knees, and come up at last into wind under feathery crowns less cruelly than into glare.

Then it would withdraw again into unremarkability.

Not because it did not care, but because it finally cared in the local units: the finger-width per season, the afternoon per ten feet of sinking seed, the generation per gathering, the century per stranger. Because it understood that the beings here do not need to be saved from their world. They need their world saved from everything that would save it too quickly.

It would stay until staying was no longer a decision. It would become, if power can humble itself that far, simply one more thing the plateau assumes: that channels tend to remain open, that vapor returns as silent rain, that the fruit ripens unpicked exactly as long as it needs to, that somewhere between the underside of the surface and the top of the drowned forest, everything unfinished is being looked after at the speed at which it is finishing itself.
