It would not clear the sky.

That would be the first kindness, and from below it would look like no kindness at all. A power that can attend can usually also disperse: lift the brown-grey canopy off Sarrat Alto, let real noon fall for the first time in a century on the pumice-concrete terraces, burn the damp out of the hammock rooms, dry the ledgers, make the streetlamps irrelevant. It could do it in an afternoon.

It would understand, after watching one full cycle of the local rain, that the canopy is not a lid on the city. It is part of its breathing. Break it and the hot ground would meet cold high air without mediation; the microbial mats would bleach and tear in direct sun, the cisterns would lose their pale slime to light, the blind shrimp in the deepest reservoir would die still blind but now also exposed, and the caretakers — grey-gold, slow-blinking, skin salted white at the knuckles — would stand squinting in a brilliance their eyes have unlearned. Green would not come rushing back up the switchbacks to heal the courtyards. Nothing with leaves can hold here. Only the things that have simplified themselves could be killed more efficiently.

So the power would not bring the sun.

Nor would it seal the wells. Nor drain the interior gutters, nor make all the pipes blue, nor repopulate the empty terraces with the hundred thousand people the roofs imply, nor restart the market for lithium salt so the pans carted to the siding would once again be *for* something. Those are the miracles of someone who has mistaken a place for a problem.

It would come up the switchback road the slow way, because wisdom is not in a hurry and benevolence should learn the local gait. Three steps, a pause. Three steps, a pause, in time with the pumps through the soles of the feet. It would drink only from the blue pipes. It would leave every door it passed pinned open. It would hang its notebook, hopefully and without expectation, to dry.

And it would ask — not the caretakers first, though it would be polite to them, share their steam-oven bread, let the clock-woman show it how a rule lengthens by afternoon — it would ask the being who actually lives here.

Because Sarrat Alto has one inhabitant of which the few dozen people are only the most recent and most articulate organ. The being is the whole maintained circulation: magma-close rock remembering water, water remembering channels cut a century ago, bacterial meadows in mustard-yellow and milk-white and rust-red thickening and tearing and regrowing in three days, slime keeping copper sweet in the dark, shrimp twitching translucent in a maintenance bucket and being carried courteously back down, moths at the substation lamps, one lizard perhaps always the same lizard, boilers leaning against backs, a woman keeping time not by the sky but by discharge, salt pans filled for no market, rain falling upward-tasting off bridges in the morning and falling everywhere at once for ten minutes late in the day. A city that was built as an apparatus and became, by a hundred years of not being fought, a creature.

You do not rescue such a being by redesigning it. You find out whether it is trapped.

There the power would do its real work, and almost none of it would be visible from the street.

Far below the plateau, deeper than any copper-cased well, it would lay a slow hand on the fracture system — not cooling it, not stilling it, that would be a death by geology — but easing, over generations, the pressure that makes maintenance compulsory. So that the flywheels turn because the caretakers choose the turning, not because the alternative is the city filling from below in a season. So that a grandchild of the boiler-tender and the ledger-keeper's daughter, taught the blue pipes and the reading of mats before she could read print, could one day *leave* without her leaving being a small apocalypse for everyone who stayed. It would give the being an ending that is not a catastrophe, far off, at walking pace. Then it would withdraw its hand before the pumps could change their pitch enough for anyone to write it in the ledger.

Down below, in the world of markets and ministries, it would be even less dramatic. It would misplace things.

It would misplace the survey that would have shown an eager company how much lithium still passes through the separators. It would soften, in a filing cabinet, the already faint official curiosity about why tinned food and dried goods and replacement linen and thick-soled sandals go up a two-mile switchback road to a city with almost no taxpayers in it. The supplies would keep coming. That is the subtle part: not a bounty, not a rescue convoy with banners, just the road kept passable after landslips no one quite remembers clearing, the truck arriving in the amber murk, the driver reminded without nagging to duck under the low beam and not to seal the door of the depot behind him. The salts would sometimes be taken away. Sometimes not. It would no longer matter.

It would protect the being the way the caretakers protect the slime: by leaving a handprint of it in every clean place, so the pipes remember.

For the people themselves — because a wise power can hold the whole creature in mind and still notice a pair of hands — it would do small, deniable mercies. Kidneys quietly unburdened of a year's slow mineral project. A brass rule that stays true on the one afternoon the clock-woman most needs it true. The child who wants the world below finding the road open and the way described kindly; the child who wants to stay, finding that staying is honored and not pitied. A filter pulled up empty, most days, so the shrimp remains a rumor treated with courtesy. Paper, upstairs where paper keeps, going on keeping a little longer than paper should — the names of wells named for dead aunts and lost tools and the saint whose feast day it flooded, preserved not in some dry eternal archive elsewhere, but *here*, where the names are used.

It would not give them children to fill the schools, or take away the children they have. It would not teach them a greener literacy. They already read the mats the way other people read weather: yellow thickening, safe to wade; white, silica coming; red tearing loose in sheets, a surge hours before the gauges admit it. That is a complete way of knowing a world. Benevolence does not insist every world be known in leaves.

Late in the day, if it stayed that long — and it would stay exactly that long, because the cycle completes late in the day — the canopy would thicken, the light fail to bronze, and the warm sudden rain would come drumming for ten minutes on every iron canopy at once, filling every interior gutter, hissing in the hot channels. The power would be seen, if anyone was watching and anyone rarely is, standing not under shelter but in an open doorway with the others. Not sheltering. Just pausing. Letting its sleeves bead. Letting sound be eaten by the saturated air. Feeling the pump settle through the pavement into its teeth, every four seconds, like a heart that has found its final form and maintains it.

Then the rain would thin. Runoff would gather. Somewhere across the empty terraces a new seam would darken, bloom white with salt, begin patiently to drip where no well was drilled.

The power would not be the one to fetch the chisel. That is not its work, and doing it would steal the one true pleasure the place affords.

It would watch the nearest caretaker watch the seam for a day to learn its temper. It would approve the shallow channel cut along the line the basalt already remembered. And if it lasted a month and got a name — after a dead aunt, after a tool lost in a sump, after a saint — the power would repeat the name once, softly, in the steam, so that it was held in one more memory than the damp could reach.

Then it would go down the switchback road, leaving every door pinned open behind it, and let the city breathe through the space where it had been.
