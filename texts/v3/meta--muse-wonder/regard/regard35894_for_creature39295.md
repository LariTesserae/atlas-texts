They would not arrive at noon.

Noon would be too much like an announcement — the town at its greenest, the pools standing black in the plazas, every doorway occupied with its own voice. Anything powerful enough to be noticed there would become, instantly, another obstruction in the chamber. The moss would swell around it. The notes would bend. The small gardeners underneath would go still in their runs and whistle, once, from everywhere at once.

A wise power would know better than to be an event in a place like that.

They would come instead in the dawn quiet. Those twenty frozen minutes when the bowl holds its breath, when every surface is furred white and even the low gate has not yet begun to speak in the molars. They would come from below, because everything that belongs here does, and they would stop outside the lip for a long time.

Not to study it. Study is only a politer kind of taking. To listen.

They would listen for a year, at least. Probably longer. Up here a day is not a sample of anything; it is one note in a chord that takes four seasons to resolve. They would hear the town sharpen in the freeze and soften in the summer swell. They would hear frost wedge the stones by hairbreadths and hear the moss answer, patient as weather. They would hear the frogs throw their voices into the cisterns and count the water by echoes. They would hear, under the noon roar, a tunnel mouth sigh shut. A fern rank shiver in windless air. The distributed breathing, at midnight, in the courtyard whose basin never quite freezes through.

And understanding would arrive the way everything arrives here: not by sight.

There is nothing broken.

That would be the first hard mercy: to resist the tenderness that wants to repair.

It would be easy, for a benevolent power, to do ruinous kindnesses. To lay roofs back over the houses and give the cold green dark back its shelter. But a roof would silence a chamber. To bring fire up the mountain in any form — hearth, warmth, a coal that never dies — and dry the moss, crack the condensation stones, change by a few degrees the exact difference between stone and air on which all the drinking water depends. To give the gardeners sight, as if blind was a word for lacking, in a town that turns to white river every evening. To give them speech, in a place already so articulate that a loose lintel can alter the meaning of a street.

To lift them up, as people say when they mean *make them more like us*, would be to evict them.

So they would do nothing inside the bowl. Nothing at all. They would leave the screaming alleys to scream, because the scream is part of the map. They would leave the fallen houses fallen and the retired courtyards slumped into their grassed mounds. They would leave the frogs mostly uneaten and entirely useful, and the caches of lichen scraped absurdly neat in dry alcoves, and the spiral-tamped moss in the chimney throat of whatever room has been chosen for resting. The work is being done. It has been being done since before the hands that stacked the high stones went back down into the clouds.

But outside the lip, the world is almost empty. Grey-pink rock. Scree that has settled. That is where a power could put its strength without putting its weight on anything alive.

Because there are things too large for loaf-sized bodies, and wisdom is partly a matter of scale.

Far below the bowl, on the outer slope where no tunnel runs and no whistle carries, there is a fracture beginning in the bedrock — not this century, not the next, but certainly, patiently, on the mountain's own schedule. Water works into it each thaw. Ice argues it wider each winter. One year, a thousand years from now, a section of the lip will go down the mountainside in the slow, smoothed way everything here falls, and the wind will find a new mouth, and the cloud-river will pour through the gap instead of over the eastern houses, and the town will lose a note it cannot replace with moss.

The gardeners cannot mend bedrock. They can feel it coming — of course they can; they will hear that section of wall change pitch a full season before it moves — but feeling is not the same as reaching.

So the power would work there. Outside. Where the stone is bare and the wind takes all voices entirely.

It would not look like building. Nothing they did would be allowed to look like building. Stone set against stone, yes, but fitted to geology rather than to intention, braced deep where only roots of mountains go, then left rough for the weather to worry into anonymity. No seam for a future climber to marvel at. No unnatural straightness. Just a slope that, over the following centuries, fails to fail. Frost would get in and find no purchase. The melt would arrive and be turned aside by a fraction of a degree. The lip would hold.

If the colony ever noticed, it would be as music. One spring, the western gate-note a hair deeper than expected. A courtyard gone subtly more resonant. Whistling from four sides at once. Investigation. Then the patient work of incorporation — moss packed here, lichen scraped there, a throat darkened to meet the new chord — until the rescue had been entirely translated into the town's own language and was no longer a rescue at all. Just this year's tuning.

That is how a wise power would give a gift: in a form the receivers could refuse, rework, and finally own.

The rest of the work would be even less visible, and would happen mostly below the clouds.

They would not wall the mountain off. Walls attract the wall-minded. They would do something gentler and much more thorough: they would let the mountain be forgotten, slightly, at the edges. A pass that never quite repays the effort of finding it. A path that delivers travellers, unharmed and a little puzzled, to a pleasant valley one range over. Maps that disagree in ways no one finds worth correcting. Names that slide off the tongue. The occasional expedition that comes home with excellent photographs of somewhere else and a conviction, which it cannot defend at dinner parties, that there was nothing up there.

Not malice. Not even concealment, exactly. Privacy. Which is a thing powerful beings owe to small ones who cannot request it.

And they would tend, distantly, the conditions no tunneller can reach: the forests far below where the cloud-sea gathers itself, the springs that feed the fog, the slow traffic of wet air up the slopes. Not commanding the weather — the weather up here would notice being commanded, and so would everything that navigates by it — but keeping faith with it. Turning aside the one drought in ten thousand years that would keep the cloud-river from the lip for a whole season. Not by rain sent dramatically to a mountaintop, but by a degree of coolness, weeks earlier and valleys away, in a place where no one was listening for a miracle.

Almost nothing. For almost as long as the mountain lasts.

Only once, perhaps, would they cross the lip.

It would be because of the visitor. Visitors leave things even when they take care: a seed worked deep in a boot-tread, of a lowland grass with opinions; a bright thread; a spore that does not belong at altitude and would find the damp courts delicious. Small apocalypses arrive exactly that size. The power would move through the town in the fog, when presence is hardest to localize, and lift those few foreign things out of the moss without disturbing a single cushion of it, the way you remove a splinter from a sleeper who does not wake.

They would smooth nothing else. Not the visitor's shape in the sleeping-moss — that lifts on its own by morning. Not the visitor's memory. A witness is not a harm. Somebody should be able to say, if only once and badly believed, that the empty town was not empty.

Then they would go back outside the lip and be weather again. A watchfulness indistinguishable from the ordinary patience of the place.

And in the night courtyard, under a hand's breadth of emerald cushion, a dozen small backs — or one long one; the distinction means less and less, with the years — would lift and fall in their slow, distributed breathing, frost white at the tips, faintly warm against the cold. Somewhere west, very deep in the stone, the gate would sound a fraction truer than it had the year before.

A whistle would go up from under a wall. Not alarm this time.

Locating. Answered from the cistern mouth. Answered again from the high side, relaying down a street full of fog.

The power would hear it, and understand it was not for them, and be glad of exactly that.
