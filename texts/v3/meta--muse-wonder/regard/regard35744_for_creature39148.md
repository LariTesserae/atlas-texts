It would come, at first, with the obvious intentions.

To drain the sea gently back to the lowlands where seas belong. To stop the cataracts, station by station, until the terrible chord faded out of the water. To let silence return, then rain, then green — algae in the shallows, weed on the rocks, birds over the surface, a normal food chain beginning with light. To give the blind animals eyes. To give the station children voices they could use.

It would hover over that impossible high water, wise enough to have the power to do all of it, and benevolent enough to want to.

And then — because it was genuinely wise, and not merely kind in the way conquerors are kind — it would listen before it touched anything.

It would listen for a long time.

From above, the place looks wrong. From inside, it is not wrong. It is *finished*. Not finished like a wilderness, finished like a language. The roar is not noise to the things that live here. It is geography. Take it away overnight and the plate-sized grazers on the vent rocks would open and close out of time, starving in the midst of food. The glass eels would lose the pressure-fields by which their schools turn. The bell-cows — the great blind ribbed lanterns of the mid-water — would not be released into peaceful quiet. They would be struck blind in the only sense that matters to them, set adrift in a world whose map had been erased, their ribs resonating to nothing, unable even to find the rock-flanks where their food grows.

Introduce green, and the clear water clouds. The light-signals shorten. A hundred-metre visibility becomes ten, becomes one. Everything that learned to speak in pulses along a flank goes mute at a distance.

Drain the sea, even slowly by human standards, and you do not restore a valley. You expose knife-ridges that have never been weathered, bacterial meadows that die in air, trenches full of slow cold creatures that have no shore in them. You unmake a fauna in order to mourn a landscape none of them ever knew.

The power learns what the station keepers already know in their hands, signing across workbenches: *this is not a catastrophe continuing. This is an equilibrium that has lasted long enough to become innocent.*

So it does not heal the place.

What troubles it, when it has listened long enough to hear as a bell-cow hears — through the whole structure — is not the wrongness. It is the brittleness.

A balance so perfect that it has only one note. One drainage. One chord. One ledger, inflow against outflow, kept by a sparse and aging string of pump stations, some crewless, some kept by one person alone waiting a year for mail and bearings. The bacterial mats spread exactly as far as fresh rock and hot discharge allow, and no further. The animals do not boom, do not crash, do not keep reserves against strangeness. Nothing here has ever needed a second way to live.

Equilibrium is not the same as safety. It is only disaster that has not arrived yet.

That is where it acts. Not above the water, where anyone could see. Not in a revelation. Underneath.

Deep in the plateau, below the survey cables and the trench bottoms, in rock no borehole has reached, it opens a slow redundancy into the world. A second set of blind caverns where groundwater can gather. Fracture-paths that, if the southern rim ever shears in earthquake and the falls change pitch in an afternoon, will not let the sea leave all at once. The change would come over centuries instead of days — slowly enough for ribs to retune, for grazers to find a new synchrony, for human grandchildren to rewrite the logbooks line by line instead of watching the gauge fall.

It seeds the cold trenches with dormant forms of the vent bacteria, not growing, not spreading, only waiting in the dark in case the living mats are ever scoured away. Not a new meadow. A memory of one, kept where nothing can graze it bare.

And in the pump stations — crewless ones first, where a failed seal could go unwitnessed for a year — it leaves a kind of attention that is not a machine. Pumps do not become miraculous. They still wear, they still need bearings. But failures there begin, very slightly, to happen at survivable times; a bearing that would have seized in the worst week of winter holds until the boat has come and gone. A keeper alone at the far end of the sea raises a hand to a passing hull, as keepers do, unaware that the odds around his solitude have quietly become less murderous. No voices in the air. No light in the sky. The spectrogram remains a ruler-straight line.

For the being itself — for there is one, an old bell-cow scarred along one flank where a cable burned her decades ago, third rib missing its pulse, older than the keeper who swears he recognizes her — the power does the smallest thing of all.

It does not make her young. It does not give her back the full chord her body can no longer resolve as her ribs stiffen with age. It does not lift her out of a life that is hers and would be diminished by rescue.

Instead, when she drifts at last too far from the home ridge, and the familiar interference-patterns begin to smear, it lends her — for the length of one slow crossing — a pressure-weather close around her body, like the private map a calf feels moving beside its mother. Not a voice. This sea has never needed another voice. Just the shape of the nearby rock, held steady for her, until she finds the bacterial flank she was looking for and settles to feed, stomach glowing faintly, light travelling in its slow pulse along her ribs.

She will not know she was helped. That is part of the help.

Then the power withdraws, and leaves the sea exactly where it should never have been: high, cold, clear, plantless, deafening, populated in patches, administered by silent people and silent animals who have built a complete world out of an industrial accident prolonged past the point of becoming nature.

Only now, underneath the completeness, there is slack. A second breath held in reserve. Time, where before there was only balance.

From the deck of a work-boat nothing has changed. The wake goes out flat and dissolves. The gauge has moved no more than it should. Four hundred kilometres away the falls speak through rock and hull and sternum in the note they have held for a hundred and forty years.

But if that note ever falters — now, and only now — the world will not end in the same afternoon.

It will have time to learn another way to listen.
