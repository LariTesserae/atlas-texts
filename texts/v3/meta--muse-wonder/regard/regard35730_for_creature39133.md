They would not save it.

That is the first thing, and it would take them a very long time to learn — longer than most powers, even benevolent ones, are accustomed to spending before they act. From outside the basin, from above the low cloud where the air is clean and distance still behaves, it looks like an emergency that has lasted a billion years. Cold that should stop a heart. Brine for water. Children born old out of scrap. Animals unscrewed alive while they are still trying to graze. The dead carried steaming in the herd because there is nowhere to set them down.

A lesser kindness would arrive with heat. With clean rain. With walls and dry ground and an end to hunger. It would melt the lace ice between the grazers and discover, too late, that the lace was a lung. It would stop the crane-jawed hunters and watch the herd, relieved and grateful, fuse slowly shoulder to shoulder into one warm, immobile continent of flesh, and then cool. It would lift the tenders off the backs and give them hands that were only hands, and they would not know how to stand on the ground it made for them, and the weight would crack the ice under their feet.

So the power does not arrive from above.

It comes down the way everything else in the basin arrives: in pieces, and experimentally.

A loose part appears in an assembly pit that no mother remembers offering. Not iron. Not horn, not chitin, not glass secreted behind an eye. Something light and patient that does not rust and does not conduct, that sits in the black water and is ignored by the crabs for a season because it is not yet useful. Then a tender picks it up because her soft hand likes the feel of it. Then a piston finds it makes a good seal. Then it is in a knee. Then it is in a jaw.

That is how the power enters. Not as a grazer. It is not humble enough yet to be that large. It begins as a mite.

It learns rust by taste. It learns that eating is cleaning and cleaning is wounding and wounding, done carefully and forever, is how a joint stays a joint instead of becoming stone. It is rained off backs into the shallows and learns to be a crab, one claw squaring itself over years to a gauge it did not choose. It steals a still-twitching part from a pit and drags it sideways into the mud, not to keep it, but to see if it will follow. It does. Most things here will follow anything that moves as if it knows a warmer place.

It learns not to go into the water alone. It learns to read hammering through its feet several seconds before the hammer falls, and to answer in a rhythm not its own. It learns that silence is not peace here. Silence is where the cold gets in. So it learns to keep making a small sound, even in sleep — a ticking, a grinding of its own ribs — and wakes frost-white more than once in the press of the herd, carried along dead or nearly dead while the others politely make a climate around it until it remembers how to steam.

Centuries of this. Perhaps more. Time pools. The power had intended its attention to be a kind of love, and finds attention here is a physical substance, like heat. The herd begins to lean toward it without knowing why. Pits near where it sleeps assemble slightly stranger children: a feeder with an unnecessary second eye that sees not heat but distance, briefly, as it was before the basin finished flattening; a bellows-bird whose whistle comes back off the herd in a voice it almost recognizes as its own, asking a question; a tender whose tool-hand, some mornings, is only fingers, and who uses those mornings to touch faces.

It does not correct this. By now it knows better than to mistake strangeness for error.

It asks, eventually. That is the second thing.

Not in words. Words would arrive before it spoke them and stand upright in the steam and frighten the calves. It asks through the ribs of the oldest grazer it can ride, hammering very slowly, with the soft hand it has grown for the purpose, the only question it has left after all the eating and tending and being taken apart by patient hunters who left, each time, exactly the parts it needed to become plural again:

*What is missing?*

The answer does not come from the grazer. The answer comes from everywhere at once, which is how the basin says anything true. From the listeners sitting in the lee of frozen chimneys, pressing their ears to throats full of ice. From the tender children unborn in the pits, teeth migrating through silt toward a jaw. From the stub-hills at the horizons, weeping brine from pores that were once blowholes, with young animals licking salt from their grandmothers' skin. From the water underfoot, holding the shape of every sun that has not risen yet.

Not warmth. They make warmth.

Not food. The mud is food, and rust is food, and each other, gently disassembled, is food.

Not an end to the cold. The cold is what keeps everything from rotting into soup. The cold is the memory of the place. Without it the water would forget their shapes.

What is missing is a silence that does not kill.

They have noise the way other worlds have air. They signal, they graze, they keep each other liquid with it. Even sleep is a half-hour of walking slower. No one in the basin has ever been quiet and warm at the same time. No one has ever had a thought that was not also a signal to the herd, a hammer on a rib, a whistle returning off a thousand backs. Their dead speak. Their unborn hammer. The water repeats them. Nothing here has ever been alone inside itself long enough to find out whether there is anything in there that is not an echo.

So the power does the third thing. The only thing.

Inside the oldest of the stub-hills — the one whose legs went under the mud before the basin had finished becoming flat, whose back is the closest thing to high ground and is almost none — it hollows out, very slowly, with its own body, over the course of one long migration circuit of the herd, a single chamber.

It is not large. A tender could lie down in it. A calf could not. The walls are living hill-flesh, ticking as they cool, and the power lines them with itself: with the light, patient material that does not rust and does not conduct, that first appeared in a pit as an offering no mother remembered making. Sound goes into that chamber and does not come back as a shape. It does not stand up in the steam. It does not arrive before it is made. It simply stops, the way sound stops in places that have rooms built for stopping.

And the cold does not follow it in. That is the gift, and it is small enough to be survivable. The chamber stays at the temperature of the hill's slow blood. Frost forms at the threshold and no farther. A tender who climbs the weeping rock, licks brine from the stone, and finds the opening — and they do find it, the way parts in a pit find the casing that fits them almost — can crawl inside, and close the flap of hide and mineral wool, and be quiet.

The first one stays for eleven minutes and comes out weeping antifreeze-sweet tears she does not have a word for, and hammers nothing on anyone's ribs for an hour, and the herd makes room around her silence the way it makes room around a birth.

The second one sleeps. Truly sleeps, legs still, eye filmed, breath not remembered from deeper water but simply absent and returning. He dreams of a shape the water has never held. When he comes out he goes straight to the nearest pit and coughs up, from a throat-sac he did not know he was growing, a part that has no ancestor: a small hollow thing, tuned, that makes no sound when struck but changes the quality of the silence around it, so that other animals standing near it feel, briefly, less crowded inside their own heads.

The pits take an interest. The pits take everything's interest. Within a generation — a decade, a century, time pools — there are parts in the basin whose function is not to pump or grind or see heat or cut rust, but to hold a space where a thing can finish becoming itself before the herd needs it to be something else.

It does not spread far. It cannot. A basin full of silence would freeze solid in an hour. But here and there, in the lee of a dead chimney, in a hollow under a grazer's locked shoulder-plates, in one more chamber and then another in hills that volunteer a little of their inward warmth, there are pockets where the noise of the world arrives and is allowed, gently, not to become anyone.

The power stays long enough to see the frozen white grazer at the front of the herd — ahead of where it died, steam still leaving a chimney that opens onto nothing living — carried at last, by tenders who have all slept once in the hill, to the edge of one of the new chambers and set down facing it. Not dismantled. Not reassembled. Just listened to, in quiet, by animals who are not asking it for a route. Its steam slows. For the first time in years it is making the sound before the sound is heard. Then it stops.

The water keeps its shape for a long while. Then the shape, too, is allowed to stop.

After that, the power leaves the way it came: in pieces. A light seal in a knee, here. A softness in a tool-hand, there. The tuned hollow thing, copied badly and then better by generations of pits that never learn what it is for and keep making it anyway. It takes nothing with it except the habit, which will trouble its eternity pleasantly, of making a small ticking noise in its sleep so the cold cannot find the seams.

It does one last thing from outside, where distance behaves again, and this one it does with all the force it had refrained from using inside. It hides the basin. Not behind a wall — walls are buildings, and the basin has outgrown buildings by turning them into animals. It hides it in the attention of the universe. It makes the place uninteresting to empires, unprofitable to miners, unsaveable-looking to rescuers, a flat and finished basin under low cloud where nothing could possibly still be happening. It sets its own memory around it like frost on a seam, and lets every map agree that there is nothing there worth the weight of arrival.

Inside, the herd completes its circuit and begins it again. Boiler-grazers grind shoulder to shoulder and keep each other liquid. Mites eat the rust that is also blood. Hunters unscrew the living with professional tenderness, and the dropped parts crawl toward one another and decide, experimentally, what to be next. Bellows-birds dismantle their dead with the wind of their passing. In the pits, steam rises over parts assembling themselves out of their grandparents — and now, here and there among the bolts and lenses and still-beating pumps, a small hollow thing that holds a silence, waiting for a body that knows what to do with an interior.

The basin is finished becoming flat.

What lives in it, shoulder to shoulder in the dim, is still becoming — but now, very occasionally, one of them becomes quiet first.

And stays warm.

And finds out what it is when no one, not even the water, is repeating it.
