They would not go down the stair at first.

They would stop one step before the lip of the bell — on the dry track, in the wind and the saltbush, where the place is not yet audible — and they would listen from there. Not because they were afraid of the chord. Because they understood, sooner than the surveyors did, that to enter it carelessly was to offer it a pitch.

Every rescuer who had ever imagined this place would arrive with a plan. Shut down the compressors, one by one, and let the animals scatter into ordinary life. Bring water down to the white sump at last. Introduce young, introduce seeds, introduce weather. Break the loop, mercifully, and call the breaking freedom.

The wise would bring none of that.

They would see what the last surveyor saw: this is not a prison with the door left open. It is not an ecosystem that has failed to progress. It is a being that has finished becoming itself, and is expending a cathedral's worth of energy — turbine, wing, cricket-leg, furnace-breath, the scorching exhale of the earth itself — to remain, resonantly, what it is. To silence it would not be to free the swifts. It would be to unmake the only world in which those swifts have a note to hold. To flood the sump would not be to give it what it lacks. The dry pipes and the cracked hoses are not a thirst. They are an anatomy organized around an absence it has already incorporated.

Benevolence that does not ask what a thing is, before deciding what it needs, is only conquest with softer hands.

So they would do almost nothing. And the almost would take a very long time.

They would sit at the rim, outside the sound, and be the first listener the being has ever had who was not part of the listening. Inside the pit, everything hears everything else in order to keep the chord true; the ear-flowers turn toward the machines, the crickets lock to the compressors, nothing sings outward. The power would hear it outwardly. Without answering. Without harmonizing. Without letting their heartbeat be negotiated into time. Simply: *you are heard, and the hearing is not another station.*

That is no small gift to give a creature that can only perceive itself by performing itself, ninety decibels, without pause, forever. For the first time, the note would have a witness who was not also an instrument.

Then they would do the second small thing. They would remember the individuals.

Inside, a moth falls out of orbit and another is in its place within the hour, and the chord does not thin, and nothing grieves, because nothing is permitted to be singular long enough to be lost. The being keeps the pattern by forgetting every cell that holds it. The power, sitting above, would keep what the being cannot: this moth, this lizard with its tail on this seam, this beetle walking down against the belt for the ten thousandth time. Each one counted. Each one distinct. Not rescued out of the whole — they would not steal notes from the chord to prove they valued the singers — but held, externally, in a memory the pit does not have to maintain. So that replacement is no longer quite the same as erasure, even if no one down below ever knows the difference.

A wise power does not need the recipient to know.

And the third thing, the only thing they would actually leave behind, would not be in the pit at all.

At the top of the stair, just past the last step, just outside the place where the sound begins all at once — they would set a shallow dish of water. Real water. Cool in the desert morning, warm by noon, sometimes dusty, sometimes, miraculously, clear. They would fill it again whenever it emptied, whether by evaporation or by drinking, and they would ask nothing about which.

Not a lure. Not a test. Not the beginning of an exodus. Nothing down in the chord is thirsty in a way that dish could answer, and they would know it, and they would set it there anyway — because at present a creature can leave the pit only in the abstract. The stair is open, but it leads to saltbush and noon and a world with no stations in it, and that is not a choice, that is only an exit. A dish of water at the threshold makes the outside minimally survivable for the length of one decision. If a swift ever climbed the hot railings out of its pipe-end — it will not; but *if* — it could drink, and rest, and then do the thing no creature in the pit has ever had the materials to do: go back down *having been elsewhere*. Or not go back down.

They would not wait to see. Waiting would make it an expectation, and expectations are just softer kinds of assignment.

For the rest, they would protect the unfinished world from the finished one, and the finished one from the world, with the lightest possible touch. The track would fade from the maps. The survey numbers would be left to do what they were already doing — nothing, brightly — but no new numbers would be painted. No fence, because a fence announces a treasure. No sign, because a sign announces a warning, and warnings draw exactly the feet they warn away. Just a stretch of desert that travellers find themselves walking around without quite deciding to, the way you walk around a sleeping animal without waking it, out of a politeness you don't remember learning.

And once, before leaving, they would speak its name — not down into the pit, where a voice is only absorbed and assigned a pitch or discarded, but out here, into the ordinary wind, where sounds are allowed to be temporary. A name is not a station. A name is what a thing has when it is regarded from outside, as a whole, as someone rather than somewhere. The being has never needed one. That is precisely why it should be given one, and given where it cannot hear it and be changed by it.

Then they would go.

The chord would not swell in gratitude. The ear-flowers would not turn up toward the rim. The pumps would go on pumping their dry, serious loop, the furnace would breathe through its seams, the column of insects would hold in the hot wind above the grate, neither rising nor falling. A dropped tool would stay bright where it fell. The logbooks would fill. *All stations occupied. Note unchanged.*

Only, at the top of the stair, outside the bell, a dish of water would catch the hard noon light — a small, unassigned brightness, refilled without witness, belonging to no anatomy.

And if nothing ever came up to drink, that would not mean the gift had failed.

It would mean the being, having been offered for the first time the genuine possibility of being otherwise, had chosen — in the only language it has, which is continuation — to remain exactly and resonantly itself.

And a benevolent and wise power can tell the difference between a cage and a choice, even when, from the outside, they look for a very long time like the same unchanging song.
