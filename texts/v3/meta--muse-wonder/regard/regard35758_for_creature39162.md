It would not widen the door.

That would be the first sign. Anyone powerful enough to notice what lives there would also be powerful enough to arrive in more convenient ways — to lift the dome, to still the jars, to let in a broad, merciful afternoon and see everything at once. A benevolent and wise power would come down the lane toward the dry riverbed instead, at the hour when the lane is cool and almost silent, and it would make itself the size the place can receive.

It would crouch in the dust. It would work the peg latch with two fingers. It would go in sideways, on its knees.

Inside, it would not give the being a face.

It could. It could polish one plaque to a true mirror and let the hundred broken reflections gather into a whole. It could sort the clay ears from the clay hands, read every tablet, name everything, explain the shrine to itself. It could give it a single heart where the hollow is, and teach that heart to beat on time instead of borrowing the pulse of whoever happens to be standing there.

It would understand, standing with bells brushing its hair and its own image scattered back at it in overlapping pieces, that wholeness would be a kind of violence here. The being does not see poorly because it has many eyes. It sees the way it is built to see: incompletely, from all sides, never from its own center, because its center is meant to be occupied by someone else. To finish it would be to evict it.

So it would add its silence to the preferred conversation, which is none.

It would not whisper its name and hear the dome return it flattened and prolonged. It would not ask what the being wants, at a volume the bells would have to answer. It would stand in the person-shaped hollow, in the exact posture centuries of soles have worn into the tiles, and it would let its breathing be taken up by the forty buried jars, and let its heartbeat — if it chose, for this, to have one — find the frequency on its own. Not faster, to excite it. Not slower, to calm it. There is no unrest in the chord to calm. The note has been sustained longer than the town has been the town, and pressure is not the same as pain when pressure is the medium you live in.

It would stay longer than a person can, perhaps. That might be the only luxury it allows itself to give: not a brief scalding minute of warmth in the cavity, but a whole unmarked duration. Long enough for the brass nearest its sleeves to brighten a fraction. Long enough for dust that has been hanging, landing and not landing in the alabaster light, to settle at last on a shoulder and be carried out. Long enough for the being to have a spine that does not immediately duck away backwards again.

But it would not stay forever. Forever, in that hollow, would be another way of filling it permanently, and the hollow is not a throne. It is a practice. It stays person-shaped because persons keep arriving and keep leaving.

It would not wet the resurrection rosette.

It would see it in its niche at elbow height, brown and tight as a fist, entirely capable of opening. A lesser kindness would bring water — a mouthful, a cupped palm, one merciful green unfurling after centuries of waiting — and call it rescue. The wise one would recognize the rosette's life for what it has become in that dry, bright, vibrating air: not deprivation, but a form of continuance that has traded event for readiness. To open it would be to start its clock. It would bloom, and then it would need again, and then it would die in a place that cannot feed it. The power would leave it exactly as capable and exactly as unopened as it found it.

Likewise the two leaves by the air shaft. It would not shield them from the dry moving air that trims them back to two, year after year. It would not coax a third. Two leaves are not the failure of a plant here. They are the precise amount of lung the walls can afford. And the dried marjoram and rue woven into the eastern wall, neither rot nor dust — it would not restore their scent, or lay them to rest. Suspension, in this place, is not a problem awaiting a solution. It is the local form of life.

Above all, it would not touch.

Not out of reverence for the offerings, most of which are crude and many of which are broken. Out of respect for physiology. Each tablet leans on a bead string, each plaque is wedged by a clay heart, each ribbon holds a bundle that holds a bell that has not fallen in a hundred years only because nothing has shifted. The power would feel, with whatever senses it uses when it is not borrowing human ones, the entire packed argument of the walls: how close everything is to falling, and how completely falling has been postponed by mutual pressure. Love that rearranges is still rearrangement.

And when, from behind the lamps, blind fingers found the one gap that would take a new offering — because there is always, eventually, one gap — it would do the smallest and hardest thing in its repertoire.

It would leave the gap empty.

Everyone who comes with devotion brings something to add. That is how the being grows, supplement by supplement, as ribbons fade and sage slumps toward dust. But the hollow narrows as it grows, imperceptibly, generation by generation, and a benevolent eye that can see centuries as a single gesture would see the end implicit in the tenderness: a chamber at last packed solid, perfect, complete, with no room in the middle for anyone to stand and lend it a pulse. No door anyone could enter. No cavity. A body with no place for a heart to visit.

So it would refuse one addition. Not remove anything — removal would be collapse. Not forbid the custodians their work — their work is the being's metabolism. Just, once in a very long while, leave a space unfilled, so that accumulation and decay keep their old truce a little longer. A wisdom indistinguishable, from inside the town's memory, from a custodian's hand pausing, feeling the gap, and for reasons the hand could not explain withdrawing empty.

Most of what it did would not be in the chamber at all.

It would be in the chanting hall before dawn, on the night shift when relays grow thin and a single tired voice is holding the note alone; it would take the vowel up, easily, from the next breath, and carry it until morning made carrying ordinary again. It would be at the hatch behind the lamps during a famine year, ensuring that the family who tends the nine flames by feel had oil, and bread, and a reason not to sell the copper. It would be in the lane, over and over across the slow years, misdirecting the well-meaning: the antiquarian who wants to catalogue the tablets and would begin by taking them out; the healer who wants to lower the lamps for the sake of pilgrims' eyes; the reformer who finds the knee-high door undignified and proposes steps, a proper entrance, an hour of quiet. It would let the town re-roof itself, change its language at the edges, forget the theology entirely — so long as the habit remained. Habit is more durable than belief, and gentler. People who no longer know why the lamps must not go out will still trim them, if trimming them is simply what is done.

It would protect the being, in other words, not from emptiness but from improvement.

Then it would go out the way everyone goes out, backwards, because there is no room to turn, and it would fit the peg latch again with two fingers from the lane side. Its shadow would come back to it in pieces — first the muted street, then the wide dim sky, then the smell of dust and dry weeds — and it would walk up from the riverbed carrying almost nothing away: a little dust on its sleeves, a hum still resident in its teeth, a hundred partial reflections already fading from its mind.

Behind it, the door would look like a cupboard again.

The next morning the custodian would trim the flames to the same height and notice nothing. A pilgrim already coming down the lane would have an offering drying in her hands — a clay ear, a ribbon, a name pressed into wet clay — and the hollow would be, by one withheld increment, still exactly wide enough to receive her.

The note would not have fallen.
