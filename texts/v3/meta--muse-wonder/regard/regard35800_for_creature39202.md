It would come up the stair slowly.

That would be the first sign of the wisdom. Not appearing in the central peat pans in light, not arriving in the western districts at sunset with its shadow falling correctly away from it. It would climb, on foot, for days, through the cold ordinary daylight, learning the roots of mountains with its own feet, arriving at the Table footsore and wind-burned and properly tired, the way guests are supposed to arrive.

And for a long time — a long time by even the Table's reckoning — it would do nothing that looked like power.

It would sleep in the guest-chambers grown inside rooted citizens, and speak quietly in them, the way you do in a place someone is still living in. It would drink from the condensation cisterns and not improve them. It would learn to tell a citizen from a wild fern-tree, then learn that it still mostly couldn't, and apologize, in scent, over several seasons, to the ones it had mistaken. It would stand in old districts and read the lean of a hundred canopies over a plaza the way other visitors read archives, and it would not ask anyone to hurry their opinion for its benefit.

It would be tempted, at first. Benevolence is tempted the way water on the Table is tempted: toward the remembered route, not necessarily the right one.

It could finish things. That is obvious, and that is the danger. It could set the distances and make them stay set — could speak once, in whatever language the plain underneath the city speaks, and fix the road between the plazas at three days forever. It could raise the aqueduct arches and keep them raised. It could stop the gentle persuasion that bows every tower flat, could give the citizens voices that worked at visitor-speed, could bring animals up the stair in its pockets — bees for the pollen, birds for the silence, small quick living things to do in an afternoon what the golden weather does in two years.

It would understand, standing there with its hand on a trunk that was also a house that was also a person politely pretending this was normal, that every one of those gifts would be a demolition.

Fix the roads, and you abolish the bell-ringers. The road-keepers are not a workaround for a broken geography. They are a clergy, a youth, a way for the walking phase of life to be *for* something. Fix the flattening, and you declare war on the Table's one law on behalf of people who have spent ten thousand years building a civilization out of obeying it gracefully. Give them quick voices and you take the creak-language, the scent, the slow public thinking-through-roots that makes a neighborhood dispute resolve itself underground before the canopies have finished being polite about it. Bring bees, and in fifty years there are hives, and in a hundred there is honey, and in two hundred there is something with a throat, and then something that eats the thing with a throat, and the great textured silences are gone and the moss has something in it that bites.

A wise power does not cure a place of being itself.

So it would ask, instead. Properly. At root-speed.

It would plant the question — not in words. It would find the oldest rooted citizen it could be introduced to, someone whose memories came in strata, house beneath house beneath house, and it would settle nearby, not too near, and it would lean. The way canopies lean on a proposal. It would hold the lean, through winters, while the question worked its way down through graft and root and the slow consensus of the soil. *I have noticed you. May I be of use?*

This would take a generation or two. It would wait. Waiting, done correctly, is not an interval before the action. On the Table it is most of the action there is.

While it waited, it would work.

Not great work. Municipal work. It would take employment with the city in the only capacity a stranger can honestly fill: it would walk. Somewhere in the interior, where the districts are sixty kilometers apart and getting farther, there is always a road that has gone a winter too long unattended — a road-keeper planted mid-route and become a milestone-ancestor, the replacement cutting still being debated two districts away, the measuring chain hanging in a longhouse that is also a widow. The distance there is quietly inserting more of itself, season by season. Left alone another century, it will insert enough that the two plazas forget they share street names.

The power would take up the chain. It would take up the wooden bell.

It would walk the road, and ring the bell once a day so the road could hear itself, and insist, on foot, at root-speed, in the company of no one, that the way between these two places is the length it was last year. It would do this for as long as the debate about the replacement lasted. A hundred years. Four hundred. It would grow very good at the work. It would learn where the fog prefers to cross, where the dew-lakes evaporate in shapes, which plate-lichens keep a warm pocket above themselves in the worst of the cold. Fast walkers would pass it, once a decade or so, carrying messages into yesterday evening, and would navigate by it the way they navigate by married groves, and none of them would think it was anything but a road-keeper of unusual persistence.

That is what benevolence looks like at this scale: not rescue. Attendance.

Below the Table, quietly, it would do the only other thing — and it would do it without ever telling the citizens, because telling them would make them responsible for a gratitude they never asked for.

It would forget the Table on behalf of the lower world.

Not destroy the stair. The stair is worn concave by feet that are no longer anywhere, and some of those feet may still be owed a way home. But atlases would lose their certainty at the last mountain range. Expeditions would set out well-funded and come back with excellent maps of somewhere slightly else, vaguely dissatisfied, unable to say what had bored them. An empire with opinions about timber would find, generation after generation, that the paperwork for the harvest survey had been filed under a district that did not quite exist. Anyone climbing with an army, or a market, or a menagerie, or a hurry, would find the daylight on the stair cold and thin and ordinary for a very long time, and would turn back, most of them, convinced the turning was their own idea.

Anyone climbing alone, slowly, prepared to sleep inside a person and call it a guest-chamber, would arrive.

That is the whole of the intervention: a gate kept at the speed of welcome, and a road kept at the length of memory.

And in time — in a great deal of time — the answer to its question would come back up through the roots, the way all real answers do on the Table, arriving everywhere in a district at once and taking a while to be noticed. Not in words. In shade. A hundred canopies over a plaza, leaning, very slightly, in a pattern no visitor would read as language:

*Stay if you like. Work if you stay. Do not finish us.*

Then the power would know it had been accepted, because nothing would change. It would keep its chain and its bell. A festival that began before it arrived would still be going on, at growth-speed, somewhere behind it. Sometime in the next century a cutting would be carried past it in a moss-lined bronze pot, on its way to being debated, and the carriers would hold the pot a moment over the road where the bell had just rung, so the child could learn the distance by listening to it.

And when, eventually, the replacement road-keeper was finally planted and grown and ready to take up the chain, the power would hand over the bell without ceremony, and walk on — perhaps to another unattended road. Perhaps, at last, to a plot of its own, if one were offered, and debated, and agreed.

It would not leave the Table perfected. It would leave it exactly as unfinished as it found it, one road no longer than it should be, one stair no easier for the impatient, one old citizen's question answered in shade.

Nothing here is finished.

It would make sure it stayed that way, one day, one bell-ring, one walked mile at a time — flat enough, for now, and for a very long now, to live on.
