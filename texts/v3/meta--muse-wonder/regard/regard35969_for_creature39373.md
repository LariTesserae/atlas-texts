They would come down the long ramps at evening, when the light has stopped doubling itself, and they would not be seen arriving.

Not out of stealth. Out of manners. A power arriving the other way — in brightness, from above, with a voice — would be a sharp thing in a place that has outlawed sharp things for reasons that are practical first and sacred later, and so cannot now be separated. Raised voices die here. Miracles would too. Or worse, they would not die cleanly. They would seed, like salt in a crack, and grow in the dark with patient, enormous force, and lift floors no crew had planned to reset.

So they would learn the distances first. Close, or not with the voice at all.

For a long time — and a benevolent and wise power can afford a long time, which is most of what makes them wise — they would do what looks, from the plateau, like nothing.

They would watch the dawn crews. They would learn which wetness is which: the reliable beading on the north lip of a cistern, the lying black patch under the gypsum that darkens once in five years and ruins an inheritance argument, the thread of water in a lip-channel that will be choked by crystal teeth if a grandchild oversleeps. They would put a palm flat to a wall, whole-footed and quiet, the way everyone here checks on a house as one might steady a living animal. They would learn to judge a bulge by its springiness before it can be seen. They would blunt themselves.

They would not stop the ground.

That is the first temptation, and the cruelest. To lay a hand on the basin and say: *be still.* Give them a floor that stays level. Give them a wall that does not belly out at the ankle by spring. Build, at last, for centuries, as visitors from the plateau are always quietly grieved they do not build.

A wise power sees what that stillness costs. Permanence here is not a gift withheld; it is a poison correctly refused. A house that cannot be taken apart by two people with hammers and rope becomes a trap when the fever comes. A channel cut too deep closes harder. A tree, if you could bully one into growing, would tear its own roots out, year by year, and die sharp. The fungus, the brine flies, the geckos in their hundreds, the swifts stitching the cup of air and never needing to leave it, the hairless rodents licking stone at dusk — all of it lives in the narrow, returning margin between damp and dry, lift and settle. Make the margin permanent and you do not preserve the town. You preserve its photograph, and empty it.

Their permanence is not in what they have built. It is in knowing how to unbuild it before breakfast. You do not take that from people because you love them. That is not love. That is taxidermy.

They would not bring a river either.

They would be tempted, second, at the sight of the table-wide salt pools, the jars filled a thread at a time, the feasts held not for food but for stored water that merely *stayed*. One clean river down the ramp — it seems so simple, so obviously mercy. Until you follow it one season forward: water standing uncovered and unregistered, flies beyond any gecko-wall's governance, banks greening with plants the salt and the heaving will mangle, mud where there was clean silt to swallow sound and falling pots, the cistern-kin maps — more carefully kept than property lines because they *are* the property lines — made worthless in a generation. Forty people who share no blood and feud murderously in hand-talk over a new seep would have nothing left to be kin *about*. You would not be giving them water. You would be dissolving the shape of their relatedness and calling the flood a kindness.

And they would not cure the white filming of the old people's eyes.

Here the power would have to sit with their own hands for a while, because this one hurts to leave alone. The milky brightening, the slow shift from sight to foot and palm, the old kept tenderly-practically by the gecko-walls, counting chirps in the dark. Surely sight, at least, is an unmixed good?

Almost. But a people who could trust noon would stop listening through their soles. They would walk faster across flats they should cross reading bird-scatter and glitter. They would plaster over the unplastered wall because they could *see* the flies coming. Within two generations the cheek laid to plaster, the pause in the doorway, the fingertips abraded and healed and abraded again — the whole second, truer eyesight of the place — would be an affectation, then a ritual, then forgotten. And the ground would still be rising two centimeters in the night, patient as ever, waiting for the first generation who meets it only with their eyes.

A benevolent power does not give gifts that amputate the organ the gift was meant to serve.

So what, then? Anything?

Yes. But at the scale the town itself trusts: last night, and tomorrow morning.

They would guard the rim. Not with walls or wonders. With overlooking. They would make the path down stay just forgettable enough to plateau quarriers who might otherwise notice that the gypsum cuts clean, that the evaporite could be sold, that a basin already conveniently lowered could be filled, dammed, planted, improved. Traders with goats would still come, two nights, complaining of the light and the silence and the floor moving under their bedrolls — trade is good, complaint is traditional — but surveyors would find their headaches curiously decisive. Conquerors would find the place not worth the water it doesn't have. The power would stand, season after season, between the town and every larger history that would love it to death by fixing it.

They would tend the conditions, never the outcomes. When a warming decade off the plateau began, very slightly, to thin the night air, the basin would go on radiating its cold a fraction longer than physics alone would allow — no one would notice, except that the beading came as it always had, and a grandmother would sign to a grandchild: *you see, it remembers.* When a drought of condensation came that was beyond remembering, they would not open the sky. They would breathe out once, low in the lowest streets where the cold already pools, in the hour before dawn, so that the grooves were beaded and one cistern-kin on the edge of a feud that could turn real would find, instead, a new dark patch, no wider than a table, in a place people had given up on. There would be a mat there in a week, and flies, and geckos, and an argument — vigorous, obscene, delighted — about whose right it was to scrape it. An argument is a form of mourning here. It is also a form of continuance. The power would trust it to do the work.

Now and then, at the smallest scale, they would cheat.

A floor deciding to rise under a house where a woman lies feverish would decide, instead, to wait three nights. A dust devil bearing down on a line of children carrying roof-stones, each child with the exact unimpressed timing of gulls, would set its glittering column down a hundred meters early, among empty flats, where it belonged. A marked roof-stone, the true inheritance, dropped in the silt during a reset by a child who would be shamed for it — when the palm sweeps for it in the muffling powder, it would be there. Just there. The power would call none of this miracle. They would call it carrying a stone when no one was counting the carriers.

And if they were ever, finally, discovered — some old person, filmed white to near-blindness, navigating by the holes in a gecko-wall, pausing because the wall felt, this morning, *accompanied* — the old person would not cry out. Raised voices die here. They would rest a broad, plastering hand against empty air and check it the way you steady a living animal, and ask, close and low, in the voice meant for someone within arm's reach, whether the floor was going to rise tonight.

The power, who had spent a century learning not to answer prayers for temples that stand forever, would answer that one honestly.

*Not tonight,* they would sign, high against the chest, where it reads against a pale shirt and not the pale ground. *Sleep.*

Then in the morning they would be out with everyone else, before the light hardens, carrying silt in baskets back to the flats for the devils to redistribute, un-bulging a wall that will bulge again, rounding a corner by palmfuls of wet clay. Working in near-silence. Watched from a crack by something gypsum-colored, throat pulsing, in the wall they left unplastered on purpose.

From the plateau, afterward, they would be almost impossible to see. White ground, white houses, one more silted figure crossing a flat with a roof-stone on each shoulder, in no hurry at all.

Which is to say: they would have learned, at last, what the place already knew.

That to love a living thing is not to exempt it from its own restlessness. It is to take up, daily, quietly, without being thanked in any register that carries, your share of its resetting — and to set everything you offer down with the edge turned to the wall, rounded, weighted, easy to lift again tomorrow.
