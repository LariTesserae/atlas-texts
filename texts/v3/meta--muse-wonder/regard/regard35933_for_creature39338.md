It would not begin by helping.

It would come up the switchback road the slow way, windows up, engine laboring, feeling the pressure arrive first in its teeth. However old, however powerful, it would understand on that road that entering here correctly meant submitting to the terms of the place. No descending in light over the First Drop. No sudden hush spreading from its feet across the turbine halls, so that men could look up in wonder and hear, for the first time in their lives, their own hearts.

It would know better than that before it crested.

It would rent, or be given, or simply find itself occupying one of the courtyard houses in a district not its own — twenty houses, a mill, a cistern tower, a laundry strung with steam — and it would live there badly, at first. Headaches. A nosebleed over the morning bowl. A frantic loneliness standing in a crowd on a tram platform, everyone signing enormous, easy conversations around it while its own arms tired in minutes.

A lesser benevolence would call that suffering and set about relieving it.

This one would stay.

It would learn to button its clothes tight and keep nothing loose at its hip. It would learn to stand closer than was comfortable, sternum almost to sternum, hand flat to a forearm to ask a question. It would learn the big, sculptural sweep of the language — elbow to fingertip, tendons standing out — and it would be clumsy at it for longer than seemed dignified. Children with chalk slates would correct it gravely. A grandmother would answer its laborious explanation of its own day with one floury hand and never look up from the knife board, and it would discover, slowly, that it had been understood perfectly.

Between two and five it would sit in the courtyard with its hands in its lap and let the vibration pass through. One fig. One olive shaped by the wall. A spoon against a cup, arriving with startling clarity through everything else. The roar underneath things, like a voice under a floor.

It would not heal their ears.

That would be the first temptation, and the deepest failure of imagination. It would see the chart the water board could draw by district — the clean band of middle frequencies gone by the Third Drop, the lower, wider deletion by the First, voices reduced to texture without edges — and every instinct trained toward kindness would want to give it back.

But it would listen longer.

It would hear old women call that deletion *peace*, and know they were not being stoical, or quaint, or oppressed in a way they could not name. It would hold a baby who slept through shift changes and freight cars and her father re-seating a gate with a mallet two rooms away, and it would understand that the hand pressed to the sternum had, over generations, been answered. The body had pressed back. To lift the hand now would not be a gift. It would be a fall into silence — insomnia, airlessness, a child rigid all night in a quiet lowland room, listening for a world that had stopped running.

The wise do not cure an adaptation because it looks, from outside, like a wound.

Nor would it silence the falls for their health, improve the turbines into inaudibility, line the cataracts with miraculous bright material, summon engineers from below the plateau with plans drawn in quiet offices. It would walk the halls with its palms out, not quite touching the riveted steel, behind the tuners whose enamel is worn in strange facets from copper coins held in their back teeth, and it would learn what they know: that nothing here is to be finished, only maintained at exactly the rate of its erosion. That is not a problem awaiting a savior. That is the civilization.

So what would it do?

Almost nothing, here.

And what little it did, it would do mostly elsewhere.

It would go two valleys away, where the snow still decides next month's water in perfect indifference, and it would attend to the places this city cannot reach: the high fields where the river is still low and brown and undivided, the watershed that might one day tempt a thirsty lowland government with its maps and its reasonable arguments. No thunderclap. No face in the clouds. Perhaps a survey that never quite gets funded. Perhaps a boundary stone that stays legible longer than boundary stones usually do. Perhaps snow, arriving on time, for a very long time.

It would attend to the rim cliffs, where the swifts nest outside the noise and never land, and make sure those dry ledges remained dry and unregarded by climbers, by quarrymen, by anyone with a use for them. It would keep the road up exactly as difficult as it is — not impassable, never that; people must be able to leave, and marry across districts, and travel badly, and come home. But difficult enough that the city cannot be visited casually, or saved casually, by the weekending well-intentioned.

Closer in, its mercies would get smaller and more specific, down at the scale the residents themselves trust.

A basalt block settling a fraction truer in the night, so that the man who has spent a month re-cutting it finds, not a miracle — he would distrust it, and be right to — but a morning where the work suddenly agrees with his hands. A bearing that sings wrong three weeks before the gauge, nudged instead toward singing wrong in a way one particular aging tuner cannot miss. Ferns continuing improbable in the cracks of the sluices. The white flowers that close when the current slackens, continuing to close. Morning mist that burns off, perhaps, a little cleaner in the lungs than it might have, over decades, while smelling exactly as it should: hot stone, machine oil, algae warmed by the sun.

Not the roar removed. The cost of inhabiting it, shaved so quietly at the edges that no one could ever point to the year it changed.

And for the few who never do thicken to the place — because there are always a few, in every generation, whose bodies will not learn the filter, who weep in crowds at thirty as they did at nine, rigid and exhausted and ashamed among people who call the deletion peace — it would not insist that their failure to adapt is a spiritual lesson. It would help there to be, up on the dry rim among the swift cliffs, one plain house that is merely quiet, with work that matters and a tram that comes twice a day, where a person could live within sight of the reservoir mirrors and the rising spray and still sleep. Exile would cease to be the only alternative to endurance. The city is large enough — sparse enough — to hold also those whom it cannot hold closely.

Once a year, it would be present for the stopping of the First Drop.

It would post no notices of its own. It would stand in the street with everyone else when the quarter went, by degrees, quiet, and it would not fill that quiet, and it would not lengthen it past what was needed for the re-lining, because four hours is mercy and five might begin to be terror. It would hear the dogs astonished at their own barking. Insects in the scrub. A baby two streets away. Teenagers whispering, hands clasped tight to keep them from betraying nervousness. Old people weeping at all the small, separate voices of the world that the roar usually, mercifully, holds together into one.

It would weep, too. A power that can stand in that sudden unholding and not weep has not yet learned what it came to learn.

And in the small hours, it would make its one certain promise kept, invisibly, year after year: that the gates open again. That the water finds its old, polished channel. That the white fall rebuilds itself step by step, the turbine takes up its note, the beds begin their almost invisible trembling, and shoulders drop all over the quarter, hands unclench and rise again into easy, tired conversation. Not because the noise is good. Not because it is bad. Because it is theirs, and they have built a way of being human inside it that no outsider has the right to improve by subtraction.

If it stayed long enough — and a wise power would measure "long enough" in the local units: fifty years for a doorframe to walk two centimeters out of true, three hundred for a curve of basalt to be relaid by hands that feel they have only just missed the first mason on the previous shift — then eventually it would no longer be a visitor. Its shoulders would set forward from leaning in. Its forearms would rope with the effort of being understood. It would diagnose, a little, by the teeth. It would sleep badly below the plateau.

People on the long trams would not pick it out at first.

Then its true work would simply be the work: another pair of hands maintaining things at the rate of their erosion. Another presence holding the upstream snow, the rim cliffs, the difficult road, the right of the falls to continue. Guarding this place, above all, from the next benevolent power that arrives full of silence and cures and certainty — and sending that one, gently, to sit in a courtyard between two and five with its hands in its lap until it understood.

Nothing loose. Nothing swung free.

The hand pressed forever, gently, against the sternum. The wise power does not lift it.

It learns, at last, to set its own hand down alongside — not pressing harder, not taking the weight away, just staying there in the vibration, so that the long, slow being of the city knows, in the one register that never gets drowned out, that it is not bearing the pressure alone.
