They would not come down at first.

They would stand a long time outside the rim, in the thin wind where it still cuts at the ears, and look at the white sea of clouds, and feel the wrongness — the heat rising out of a summit that should be cold — and resist the first and oldest temptation of power: to descend, to reveal themselves, to make contact, to improve.

Because from above, in one clear thinning of the mist, they would take in what a lesser kindness would miss.

Nothing here is waiting to be saved.

The place is not a ruin. It is not a wilderness in decline, not a garden gone wild for want of a gardener. It is a working system, older than any word for system, in which fog is intake and river is output and the bog itself rises and falls on a deep pressure cycle like a sleeping animal breathing. The barges drink what would otherwise burn itself off in spark and lichen. The deer sort themselves by temperature. The algae, the shrimp, the fish, the birds, each occupy their degree.

And in the margin where the water is exactly as hot as blood, the keepers are doing the maintenance.

A foolish power would call them primitive. A sentimental one would call them innocent. A wise one would watch long enough — seasons, years, one whole slow mineral generation — to understand that they are neither. They are competent in a language the visitor does not speak: pressure read through pale filaments in the skin, sulfur sorted fold by fold through the nostrils, a valley known not as a map but as a sequence of trembles and notes and safe warmths underfoot. They have no fire because fire is redundant. They have no speech because calling costs hot breath. They have no roads because a road would be a wound that the afternoon rain would have to heal every day.

To give them tools would not be a gift. It would be an amputation. Give them dry-country medicine, dry-country shelter, dry-country knowledge, and you would not lift them out of the caldera. You would only teach them to be unable to live in it.

So the power would do what the keepers themselves do. They would learn the work by watching where the work is almost invisible.

They would not heal the old keeper whitening into travertine at the edge of his last pool. That death is not a tragedy happening to the system; it is one of the ways the system feeds itself — body to sponge to root-mat to warmer peat where the shrimp crowd thicker. To pull him out of that cycle, to extend him, to cure the mineral out of his joints, would be to love an individual in a way that insults his whole life.

They would not speak to the children in the cooler terraces. They would not let themselves be seen standing upright between the trees, a miracle at person-height. Because once seen, they could never be unseen, and a being that has a god in its forest has a smaller forest thereafter.

They would not even clear the choked flute, though they could do it in an instant, cleaner than any nail or tooth or patient hand. Another keeper is already on his way, three terraces down, belly-deep, listening to the pitch go wrong. That journey is not inefficiency. That journey is the knowledge renewing itself in a body. Take the task and you take, grain by grain, the reason the keepers know the valley at all.

If anything, then — almost nothing, inside.

Outside is another matter.

For the caldera has one great vulnerability, and it is not heat, or disease, or the slow building tremble under the eastern terrace. It is attention. Human attention, arriving the way it arrives everywhere else: first as a sensor anomaly in a barge log — *warm rock, deer, localized change in conductivity* — then as a footnote, then as an expedition, then as protection, which is only possession with cleaner hands. A fence. A research station on the rim, with generators. Boots sterilized, conscience clear, and the whole held climate beginning to fail under the weight of being studied, named, filmed, visited, saved.

Against that, the wise power would act without hesitation, and without mercy for curiosity.

Not with a wall. Walls invite climbing. Not with terror, because terror becomes legend, and legend becomes a map. They would do something much quieter and much more complete. They would make the place *uninteresting to remember*.

The barge sensors would go on reading deer, warm rock, water. Where a keeper crossed an open terrace in full view of a downward camera at the exact wrong second, the file would corrupt in a small, plausible way — condensation on the lens, a compression error, nothing that would make a technician look twice. Coordinates would drift by unremarkable amounts in unremarkable databases. A survey team that once proposed a landing would find its funding gently, bureaucratically elsewhere, its members promoted into other work they honestly preferred. Anyone who came over the rim in person would have a wonderful day, see hornbills and tree ferns and steaming pools, feel obscurely fulfilled — and go home unable to say why they had not stayed longer, and never quite able to describe the route to anyone else.

The power would tend the larger terraces the keepers cannot reach. That, at last, is work of their scale. Keep the cloud sea fed as the lowland world warms, so fog still pours cold over the rim. Keep the barges' quotas set where they have always been set, at the amount the valley sheds and no more, so the high dark hulls go on drinking the excess charge and never learn to thirst. Watch the deep aquifer not to master it but to know, decades ahead, the season when the good tremble becomes the building one — and if that season ever comes, not to transplant the keepers, which would kill them more surely than the eruption, but to slow the change to a pace their few-meter migrations can follow, pool by pool, generation by generation, the way they follow morning warming upslope a little at a time.

And keep a watch. Not a tower. Not a presence. A habit of returning, the way the keepers return to a spring mouth before it can seal. Once in a while, across a human lifetime, to stand again in the cold outside the rim and confirm that the heat is still held, that the flutes still sound their separate clean notes, that below in the luminous ground-cloud at night a broad shape still crosses between the trees with its filaments lifted, trailing a few soundless sparks off one wet shoulder, unhurried.

No sign would be left. No blessing, no marker, no myth. Myths are only maps that have learned to sing.

Except, perhaps, once — if the power stayed very long, and learned the etiquette as thoroughly as the ecology, and earned the right the way anything earns a right there, by doing nothing superbly through the held breath of noon — on a night when the mist stood in the forest lit from beneath by warm water, and somewhere across a pool a keeper ticked a nail down a travertine edge, *I am here, the vent is open, the water is rising*, the power might answer.

Not in words. Not in light.

Only grounding the charge built up in their own body, palms flat in the wet peat on the far side of the valley, letting it go down harmlessly into the ground, doing the smallest and oldest piece of the work, correctly, in rhythm, where no one would ever know whose hands had done it.

Then nothing would change.

The barges would drift. The bog would settle and rise a hand's breadth. The deer would stand belly-deep at dawn, breathing steam. The old keeper would become a seat of stone, then a warmth in the peat. A child would learn, with her whole skin, which step was safe.

Nothing would change — and keeping it so, against time and weather and the well-meaning hunger of the world, would be the whole of their benevolence, and the proof of their wisdom.
