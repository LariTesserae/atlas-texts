They would come the way everything important comes here: not as light.

Light would be a violence. A sudden brightening over the rooftops would scatter the blind silver fish, close a million sponge mouths, send the worm-gardens vanishing into the mud, and drive her — her, especially, who has lived her whole life by touch — deep against her door-stone with her skin gone bloodless. It would be a kind of knowledge she has no body to survive knowing.

So the power, being wise, does not shine.

And being benevolent, it does not descend.

It arrives, if arrival is even the word, as a stillness at the rim of the bowl at the turning of the tide — a moment when the inflow hesitates, just faintly, the way water hesitates at a threshold before entering a room it respects. Down the avenues nothing changes that any crab could point a claw at. The current resumes, cool-sweet from the flats. The sponge towers inhale. A shrimp curtain parts and closes.

But for one full cycle, something is paying attention to the city in its entirety, and paying attention *correctly*, which is to say: by taste, by pressure, by vibration, by flow. It learns the place the only way the place can be learned. Left: clam beds. There: bacteria blooming white in a crevice. There: iron, old and rusting. It reads neighborhoods by their chemical signatures. It feels the grooved limestone at the center, and the tone the tide makes across it, below hearing, in the bone —

— and it goes around.

That is the first thing it does, and perhaps the truest test of its wisdom. It could have crossed. Whatever it is, nothing in the bowl could stop it, and filling that emptiness with a presence would have been so easy, so instinctive for anything vast encountering anything reverent: to be the thing the space was waiting for. It was not waiting. It was being kept. Generation after generation of fish and eel and juvenile octopus learned the detour in their bodies without ever knowing why, and the not-knowing is not a flaw in the arrangement. It *is* the arrangement.

So the power swims the long way around the plaza, feeling in whatever serves it for a mantle the faint tone that troubled the water, and leaves the center exactly as empty as it found it.

The second thing it does is almost nothing, and it does it far away, where she will never know.

Far above the rim, beyond the eelgrass flats, at the broken shore and well past it, the upper world has its own tides — hungers that move in straight lines, dredges and poisons and a warming that is not the good bath-warm of the bowl but a slow cornering of the warmth, a thinning of what the inflow can carry. The power does not smite any of it. Smashing is just another kind of light: bright, brief, and ruinous to everything adapted to the dark around it.

Instead there is a season — the residents of the city will never mark it, because nothing in their lives changes — in which a channel that would have been cut is surveyed and found, inexplicably, unprofitable. A slick that would have settled over the flats disperses along a current that turned, that morning, a fraction earlier than anyone predicted. Rain over the watershed falls the way rain used to fall, in the months it used to fall, so that the lagoon runs neither too fresh nor too foul, and the plankton comes in clouds at the right turning, gritting faintly against a million small skins.

The city does not experience this as rescue. It experiences it as another year. The crabs sweep. The towers drink. The cucumbers eat the streets clean and leave pelleted sand behind them. That is how genuine protection feels from inside a life: not as a miracle, but as the world continuing to be possible.

And the third thing — the last, the smallest — concerns her.

The power finds her the way her neighbors find her: a brown arm across a round doorway, each sucker a pale cup pressed to stone, tasting. It does not heal the arm that grew back blunt. It does not restore her dimming eyes, which she does not use and would not thank it for. It does not save her from what is coming, because what is coming is not an interruption of her life. It is its completion. To pull her out of the cycle — out of hunger and molt and the doorway fight and the final thinning around a curtain of eggs — would not be to love her. It would be to evict her from the only house she has ever been.

It knows better. It has watched the winner, tide after next, turn everything in her room and keep the spiraled shell in the back niche for no reason it can account for, because its ridges please an arm to trace. It understands that the shell is not a symbol. It is better than a symbol. It is a preference that survives the one who preferred it, handed on not by teaching but by the shape of the niche and the smoothness of the thing itself. That is scripture enough, down here.

So it does not touch the shell.

What it does, if it can be said to do anything at all, is ensure that in her remaining tides the water over her threshold tastes as it should. That when she unspools one arm into the dark at the turning, there is a crab there — not delivered, not placed, just not absent, one of the ordinary crabs that would have passed anyway through a city still capable of producing crabs. That the parasites do not thicken in her doorway faster than the gobies beneath her floor can eat them. That the pallor that crosses her skin when the barracuda's pressure-wave rolls down the avenue passes, as it always has, in a breath, and is gone.

Mercies small enough to be mistaken for the world working properly. Down here, they are the same thing.

Then, in not many tides, she slows. It does not matter which ending finds her — the eggs, strung like pale beads in the darkest part of the chamber, her body thinning to membrane around the blowing-clean of water across them; or simply an inflow she no longer bothers to shut the silt against. Either way the floor goes ungathered. Either way a sucker leaves a stone untested, and the house knows first.

The power is there for that too, and it does the hardest thing in its repertoire, which is to keep its attention company with her without lifting so much as a current to intervene. To let the soft parts be taken first. To let the beak be ground down and the last stain of her loosened on the exhale to feed the towers — generation cemented to generation, shell upon dead shell, one more layer of the city she already was becoming while she lived.

Only when the doorway is briefly just a ring of stones again does it withdraw, and it withdraws the way it came: along the streets, by taste, around the center.

It does not leave a mark. A mark would be a building, and nothing is allowed to build in the place it is honoring. But for a long while afterward — longer than any tenant of the towers, longer than the eel in the drains, longer even than the habit in the fish that bend, always, around the open limestone — whenever the tide crosses the grooves at the heart of the bowl and makes its tone below hearing, the tone is very slightly steadier for having once been listened to, all the way down, by something that could have crossed, and chose instead to learn the detour.

That is what it would do.

Keep the flats whole. Keep the inflow sweet. Keep the center empty. Let a small, old, eight-armed keeper of a door live the rest of her life entirely as herself, unrescued and unwasted and — in the only tense this city has ever kept — not ended, just carried out on the exhale, and brought back in again with the morning tide.
