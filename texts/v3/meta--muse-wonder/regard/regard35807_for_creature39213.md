It would arrive without arriving.

That would be the first, and hardest, decision. Any true presence in that water is a vibration. Any warmth is a melting. Any light brighter than the filtered green through three metres of ice would send every worm into its tube, every shrimp to the boundary layer, every sculpin sliding silent and blinded out along its avenue. Every antenna in the dome would turn, at once, toward it.

So the power does not enter the city. It learns, first, how to not be a disturbance in it.

It stays still for a very long time on the scale the city keeps — which is to say, a long time even for something that is not in a hurry. Hearts here beat a few times a minute. A generation takes a year. To watch properly is to slow one's own metabolism in sympathy, until the platelet crystals at the mouth of the dome can be heard, in the bone of the world, growing scale over scale, and the sideways snow at tide-turn is not weather but a census: who is feeding, who is carrying, who has died in the middle streets and is already becoming a doorway for something smaller.

It would be tempted, at first. All benevolence is tempted at first, and wisdom is mostly what remains after the temptation has been looked at clearly.

It could keep the dome from freezing through.

It could hold the meltwater lens one fraction of a degree above capture, all winter, so that no amphipod is ever sealed mid-carry in clear ice, no sea spider halted mid-step, leg lifted forever over sponge. It could warm the salt layer where the worms raise their dissolving chimneys. It could feed the algae a little more light, so the gardens thicken, so the eggs in the orange pouches hatch into abundance instead of into a city where almost everyone is eaten or becomes a wall.

It could remove the sculpins.

That thought would come, and be recognised for what it is: pity, misapplied. Remove the finger-long, ice-coloured patrols, and the masons would do what masons with no planning law always do — build beautifully, communally, ceaselessly, across every thoroughfare, until they sealed the crack at the back of the dome and suffocated in their own architecture. The sculpin halted in the ice with a mason in its mouth is not a cruelty interrupting the city. It is one of the city's load-bearing facts. So is the freeze. So is the spill when the keel rolls. These creatures are not a failed version of something that should have lasted. They are what happens when a hollow lasts exactly as long as it lasts.

To save every citizen would be to end the settlement.

So inside the dome, it does — deliberately, attentively, with great effort disguised as passivity — nothing.

Not one pellet is placed by it. Not one glass brick carried. Not one predator stayed, not one egg fanned by any pleopod but the mother's own. The city must be allowed to be authored by its citizens' mouths, or it is a terrarium.

Outside the dome is another matter.

Because the dangers that belong to this place would come, in time, from inside it: ice growing down, ice breaking up, hunger, sealing, predation, the slow crawl of brine channels opening an annex behind a curtain of crystal. Those it must not touch.

The dangers that do not belong to it come from very far above the three metres of ice.

A hull, kilometres off, beating the water with sound louder than any tide-crack, loud enough to arrive here not as noise but as a false season. A warm discharge, invisible and tasteless to the thing that made it, quite warm enough to unmake a lens of meltwater balanced on a fraction of a degree. A fleck of something that will never become food, never become a wall, never rot into information — drifting down to the boundary between fresh and salt, bouncing there, found by antennae that have no category for it except: examine, examine, examine, waste a winter on the inedible.

Against these, the power has no scruples at all.

It does not announce itself. It simply widens. The stillness it has practised, learning the dome, becomes a perimeter. A ship's course bends by a degree no navigator will ever remark on — an iceberg on a screen, a current reading, a small professional caution. A survey's pinging is delayed by weather that is only, technically, weather. A spill disperses a bay away, into water with nothing balanced on a skin halfway down a room no bigger than a bathtub. What cannot rot is turned aside long before it reaches the platelet fringe, and carried off to be somebody else's long, human problem.

This is invisible work, and it intends to remain so. No amphipod will ever know the shipping lane moved. No worm will compose anything about the winter the water stayed clean. Gratitude is a warm-blooded transaction, and there is no one here to offer it except in the only local currency: continuation.

And yet pure restraint has one more temptation inside it, quieter than rescue.

When late winter comes — and it will come, and the power will not stop it — the end will take one of its two shapes. Either the dome freezes through, crystal by crystal, reading whole streets into the ice like a fly in amber, a map preserved by being killed. Or the keel tears away and rolls, the lens spills, the little glass walls lose the architecture of buoyancy that held them, and thousands of weak swimmers, ceiling-walkers, bottom-grazers, find themselves wall-less in the open dark under the floe, swimming up.

Up, toward what? That is the last question, and the only one benevolence is allowed to answer.

Not toward a city built for them. Not toward safety, which does not exist at that scale and never did. But a power that has watched platelet ice decide its shape for a season knows what a beginning actually looks like from below: a flaw. A roughness in the underside of the new ice. A place where crystals grow imperfectly enough to leave a hollow, where meltwater can gather against a ceiling and hold, where the first animal arriving with a soft pellet in its mouth can press it against something that will, provisionally, stay.

It cannot make them begin again. It must not. Beginning again is the whole of what they are.

It can see to it that when they swim up, blind and upside-down in the blue-green dark, there is more than one flaw up there to find.

So some distance away — not far, in amphipod terms; far enough not to be a gift with an address — the underside of next winter's ice will be very slightly, very deniably, imperfect. A scar in the young floe no instrument will flag. A place where the freezing falters, and a dome no wider than a bathtub can hollow itself upward, and a lens of fresher, colder water can form its wavering skin, and things can drift up to it and stop, and hesitate, walking for a moment on water.

Then the power withdraws to its perimeter, and slows itself down again, and does the one thing it has done from the start that is neither rescue nor abandonment.

It pays attention.

Someone mid-carry. Someone mid-step. Someone carrying eggs through a passage one fish wide toward an annex that did not exist last month. A city that no one will ever map, unwitnessed by anything in it, because witnessing from inside is just antennae sampling iron and salt and alarm.

It will be witnessed from outside now. Not saved. Not improved. Not kept.

Known, exactly as it is, for exactly as long as it is — and then known in memory when the ice takes it, so that the record of the universe contains, somewhere, the full and temporary fact of it: that under three metres of ice, in a flaw in the world, thousands of pale citizens built towers out of themselves, leaned with the tide, emptied and refounded their districts, and were, for one winter, everywhere, and never alone, and never finished.

The power makes no mark on the ice.

But for a hundred kilometres around a place no chart names, the water stays strangely, mercifully quiet — while inside the dome, undisturbed, the building goes on.
