They would come to the rim first, and stop there.

Not out of reverence — reverence is too quick, and often only another way of making a thing into a symbol. They would stop because anything wise learns the edge of a place before it crosses it, and this place has an edge as clear as a shoreline. Level, level, level — then silence. A door. They would stand in the ordinary warm quiet of the country outside, and listen to a factory that is not there, and they would not yet decide that what they are hearing needs anything from them.

They would listen for a long time. Longer than visitors. Longer than geologists with their instruments, longer than the lifetime of any species that has come down to drink and wisely decided not to. They would listen until they understood what visitors feel in two days and usually misname: that the stillness here is not emptiness, and the motion is not striving.

Then, perhaps on a morning like any other morning, they would go down.

Barefoot, if they had feet. Lightly, if they could choose their weight. Not to spare it pain — a footprint is only a shallow bruise to it, glazed by noon — but because courtesy, to a being whose whole back is a sense organ, begins with how you walk. They would not bring cool metal that sweats in the steam. They would not break a chimney to see if it regrows. They would not stand close to a vent to test the hesitation before it fires.

They would already know.

They would walk the long empty spaces between pores, ten minutes of white salt and black iron clay at a time, and they would let it learn them the only way it can learn anything: two pressures, alternating, moving in a line, pausing beside the still pools where the blind shrimp feel along the mineral rim. Not the pattern of prey. Not the pattern of a mate — there has never been a mate. Only, perhaps for the first time in the ten-thousand-year habit of its life, the pattern of something that has come neither to take heat, nor take water, nor take stone, nor stay.

That would be the whole of the first gift, and it is not small: to be known in one's own language, without being required to answer in someone else's.

They would be tempted — any benevolent power would be — to do more. That is how you tell the wise ones from the merely kind. The kind ones would look at the eyeless shrimp in their clear pools and want to give them sight. They would look at an animal twelve kilometres wide that has never seen the sky it breathes into and want, tenderly, catastrophically, to give it eyes. They would look at its perfect, circular, unexported life — stone dissolved below, stone laid down above; heat in, breath out; nothing made, nothing spared, nothing sent onward — and they would want to give it a purpose. A child. A message. A future less precarious than a million years of remembering the same shape.

The wise ones would lay that impulse down on the rim, with everything else they do not carry in.

Sight would be a cruelty in a basin where the light is diffused to a directionless glare and there is nothing to see but steam. Hearing would be a sentence passed on a creature that must listen to itself breathe. A mind, of the sort that plans and longs and knows it will end, would not be an awakening here. It would be an infection introduced into a body that has stored all the memory it needs in the geometry of its throats and chambers — memory without sorrow, correction without anxiety, persistence without the knowledge that persistence must fail. It does not suffer from being what it is. It does not lie awake under its own cracked plates wondering why it produces nothing. It produces itself, wholly, every nine seconds at the eastern pore, every minute at the great one, and that — they would understand, standing with the clap of the caps travelling through the soles of their feet — is not a lesser form of a life. It is a form of life so complete it has made all other forms unnecessary, and therefore fragile, and therefore complete again, the way a circle is.

You do not improve a circle by giving it somewhere to go.

So they would not heal it, because it is not wounded. They would not feed it, because abundance here is poison — a richer water, a stronger heat, a new mineral carried in on a well-meant wind would be only a faster error for it to spend centuries cancelling out. They would not make it immortal. They would not speak to it in any tongue that required it to become lonely in order to reply.

What they would do would happen almost entirely outside the basin, and mostly to other people.

Far upwind, where the warm northwest wind crosses the ordinary country, they would make certain that nothing tall is ever built to break it. No tower, no wall of trees planted in a government season, no city deciding across a hundred hopeful years to grow in that direction. The wind would go on arriving unshaped, at the angle the plumes have learned. Down in the deeper country, where men will sooner or later hear that roar — for men always, eventually, hear a roar and think *power* — surveys would quietly lose interest. A borehole would find, or seem to find, disappointing temperatures. A road surveyed toward the rim would discover a better route, for reasons every engineer involved could explain and none could quite remember choosing. A company formed to take the basin's heat and turn it usefully into light for distant towns would dissolve in disagreements about money. Nothing dramatic. No lightning on the drilling rig. A wise power does not make martyrs or mysteries; mysteries attract expeditions. They would make the basin, to the acquisitive world, faintly unpromising and hard to keep in mind — a blank on the map that the eye slides off, twelve kilometres of hot cracked nothing, producing, as any report will confirm, nothing at all.

And downwind — this is the one indulgence they might allow themselves, the single act that reaches past maintenance, and even this they would do with their hands, so to speak, behind their back.

Somewhere far beyond the rim, in warm, wet, mineral ground that no one wants, they would keep a place empty. Not seeded. Never seeded. They would not carry a fragment of the lining there in mercy, would not take a spore from a rising plume and plant a daughter basin and call it rescue. That would be their story imposed on its body. They would only keep the ground ready — the right heat under it, the right water moving slow through fractured stone, no plough, no poison, no foundation — for longer than empires, longer than languages, against the one day in ten thousand years when the northwest wind falters, and a plume goes up straight instead of sideways, and something the animal did not know it had shed goes over the rim and does not come faithfully back.

If nothing ever lands there, nothing has been lost. If something does, it will not know it was waited for. It will simply begin, in the dark, the million-year work of hollowing itself a home.

Last of all — perhaps only once, perhaps once a century thereafter, when the guardianship has become as habitual to them as breathing is to the basin — one of them would go down again among the vents and stand very still near a great chimney, in the pocket of ringing quiet behind the fallen cap, and do the only thing that could be received as it was meant.

Press a hand, or whatever serves as a hand, flat to the warm plate.

Wait until a distant pore fires, and another, and another finds its period.

Then tap — once — not in warning, not in command, not in any rhythm the body below must follow. Just once, clearly, in the middle of its ongoing time, so that somewhere deep in the sensitive underside of the world it arrives as what it is: an irregularity that does not repeat, does not threaten, does not erode, and does not need to be corrected.

*I was here. You were perceived. Nothing is required.*

And then they would withdraw their hand, and climb back up in the evening while the caps lift and fall in the haze behind them — *lift, breathe, fall; lift, breathe, fall* — and at the ridge the sound would go level, level, level, and stop, as if a door had closed, and they would not look back to check whether anything had changed, because they would know, and the knowing would be the final proof of having done it rightly —

Nothing in the basin will be different tomorrow.

Nothing was ever supposed to be.
