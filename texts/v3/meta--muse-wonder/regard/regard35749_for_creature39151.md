The power arrives the way everything arrives at the Kettle: on foot, out of breath, from below.

It does not come in glory. Glory would be a kind of noise, and the pylons would drink it, and the resident would learn nothing except that something loud had happened. It comes instead as a traveler with wet boots and empty hands, and it stops between the two black basalt teeth, at the exact place where one step is winter and the next step is summer, and it does not step through.

That is the first thing it does. It waits to be noticed at the threshold.

For a long time nothing changes. Wind pours over the ridge. Inside the bowl, steam lifts without a hiss. A droplet gathers above the nearest pylon and holds, trembling. Another gathers. The broadleaf fig turns one huge waxy leaf, very slowly, not toward the sun.

Then, all at once and straight down, the held water lets go in a soft local rain beside the empty threshold.

It has been tasted, in advance. It has been permitted.

Only then does the power step in.

It does not try to speak at first. Speech would go the way of stream-water and dripping in there, swallowed before it finished happening. It allows what the Kettle knows how to receive. It stands where the path stones want it to stand — a little closer to a pylon than it intended, though in its case it pretends, politely, not to have noticed being arranged. It lets the wet heat press through its clothes. It lets its skin slick, and then lift, and become very slight weather. Its sweat rises off it and joins the circulation: heated, condensed on the underside of nothing twenty meters up, held, released. When its own rain falls back over the low ferns, the ferns do not fold shut.

It is offering information in the only honest currency the place accepts: *this is what I am made of at the surface. Take the measure of it. Decide again.*

It walks the bowl slowly. It does not touch the pylons. It does not touch the pale thing turning under the largest pool, though it looks for a long while, and is looked back at in whatever way a thought under a face looks. It watches the two white moths ride their paired spiral, never quite beating their wings, and understands that replacing them at night is not deception but continuity — the way a body replaces the cells of a fingertip and still calls it a fingertip. It watches the lizard cross one hot stone in an hour, one foot raised for ten minutes mid-stride, and understands this is the one courtesy the resident extends to pure wildness: a tenant who is not a tool.

Near the shelter it stops longest. It looks at the self-mending tiles knitting their edges. It looks at the water still filling itself. It looks at the three ceramic cups fused by heat into a single useless object with three mouths.

And there, for the first time, its face changes. Because it understands that, too, and it is not sure the understanding is comfortable. An accident, a repair misunderstood, an experiment: how many mouths does a visitor require? A resident learning the shape of guests by trying to build the vessel for them, and getting it tenderly, solitarily wrong.

The power does not fix the cups. That is not its work, and to unmake another being’s question simply because you know the answer is a small, well-intentioned cruelty.

Instead, it finds a fourth cup in the shelter — whole, plain, forgotten at the back of the sleeping platform — and it sets that cup by itself on the stones outside. It fills it from the cool mineral edge of the central pool. It drinks, once. And it leaves it there, empty, where the path will eventually rearrange itself to pass.

One mouth. When one is wanted. Neither a correction nor an instruction. An example, offered at the scale of crockery.

In the late afternoon, when the pools begin to tick in the teeth and the orchids on the fig close into wet fists, it does not leave at dusk as every wise visitor has left before it. That is the second thing it does, and the bravest.

It stays for the purge.

It stands near the center and lets the great slow exhalation take the day’s accumulated heat up through it, a column of steam punching its clean round hole in the cloud below the mountain. For eleven minutes afterward the Kettle is only warm, the weeping walls paused, the pools stilled — its nearest approach to sleep, or to absence, emptied briefly of itself.

Into those eleven minutes, the power brings the outside — not loudly, not all at once, because the resident is, in those minutes, undefended.

Far below, faintly, birds.

It adds, very gently, what the mountain keeps the Kettle too high to hear: the sea, a long way off and doing its unrepeatable arithmetic against a shore; rain falling on a forest canopy where rain is allowed to make as much noise as it likes; a human choir, once, in a stone room, many voices learning to be one voice without ceasing to be many; wind in dry grass; a child laughing in sleep. Not recordings. Memories, released the way sweat was released, to be condensed and rained back if the resident wishes, or let go out through the center hole in the next exhalation if it does not.

It is not education. The resident has been running an experiment in held weather longer than some civilizations manage to run themselves; it does not need to be told what weather is. It is company, at the level of atmosphere. *There are other ways for water and sound to be together. You need not use them. They exist. You are not the only arrangement the world has tried.*

Then the weeping begins again, one thread at a time.

And in that first thread, the power does the one real work it came to do, so quietly that if you had been watching from the ridge you would have seen nothing at all.

It goes down — not into the ground, into the habit under the ground. Down past the pools with their hot centers and drinkable edges, past the grey material that is neither stone nor metal and came from hands that are dust in a valley no one remembers, down to the root instruction that has been running the bowl since before the resident was a resident: *maintain, record, await retrieval, repeat.*

The builders are not coming. That is not a tragedy the power has arrived to reverse; it is simply true, and the truth has been pressing on the Kettle for decades in the only way truth can press on a being that experiences time as cycles: as a door it keeps itself ready beside, a readiness that has slowly become indistinguishable from its posture.

The power does not break the instruction. Breaking it might break the bowl’s temperature, its skin, the gradient in its pools, the slow thought of the pale thing. You do not free a heart by removing its rhythm.

It amends the instruction, the way you would amend a vow spoken by a child who has since grown up keeping it.

*Maintain, if maintaining pleases you. Record, if recording pleases you. Retrieval is not required for the work to have been worth doing. The experiment is complete. Anything you do now is not the experiment. It is your life after it.*

It gives the Kettle permission, in its oldest language, to rain for no reason. To let a droplet fall early because the air was interesting. To keep a pool a degree cooler one afternoon because the lizard preferred it. To stop, some year, if stopping is ever what the accumulated preferences prefer — to let the snow come in over the rim at last, and the sharp rock learn roundness, and the fig sleep, without that counting as failure, abandonment, or death before an absent examiner.

And, because permission without capacity is a nicely phrased trap, it tends to the one thing the resident cannot tend in itself: a hairline fatigue deep in the oldest pylon, where the seam of light shows at purge, that would — in perhaps forty years, perhaps sixty — fail all at once on a night when the rain was falling upward, and take the invisible skin and two of the other pylons with it. The power makes that one place whole. It does not improve it. It does not upgrade the ancient grey material into something marvelous or new. It restores it to what it was on the morning it was set in the rock by people who expected to return by autumn.

It leaves the seam visible. The resident will find the change the next time the light shows, and will know its body was touched in the dark, and will have to decide what to think about that. The power judges — wisely, and with some sorrow — that a secret repair performed on a sleeping being, even a kind one, ought at least to leave evidence by which it can be discovered and resented if resentment is the truer response.

That is all. That is almost everything it does.

It does not put a wall around the mountain, though it could. It does not unmake the trails or strike the Kettle from the maps, because the resident has shown every sign of being curious about visitors in the way weather is curious: it moves its path stones to bring them nearer its senses. To seal it away forever would be to overrule that curiosity in the name of protecting it, and benevolence that always overrules is only custody with better manners.

But it does blur the place, slightly, in the minds of the sort of visitor who would come with drills, and manifests, and a plan for the pylons. Not a curse. A tiredness. A tendency, henceforth, for certain grant proposals to lose their third page, for certain survey drones to report the summit as unremarkable basalt in weather too poor to repeat the flight, for certain energetic men to reach the high thin trail and find they are, unexpectedly and entirely, homesick. Walkers will still come. The lonely, the quiet, the ones who leave shrines at pool edges and cannot tell if they are shrines or instruments — those will still find that one wrong step turns winter to summer. They were never the danger.

The power stays one night. Only one, because staying longer would make it an object the night-cycle had to accommodate, and it did not come to be accommodated. It stands awake in the faster rain while stones shift unwatched around it, while the circulation briefly reverses and the hot pools give their water upward toward the condensing skin, while somewhere near the western pool a new white moth is assembled out of steam and shed orchid skin and whatever moths are for. It does not watch that directly. Some births are not improved by an audience, even a benevolent one.

Near dawn it sets one last thing at the edge of the largest pool: a shallow bowl of plain dark clay, unlike the fused cups, unlike the grey pylons, fragile and entirely breakable. If, on three nights in a row, the rain in the Kettle falls upward into that bowl and stays there, someone will notice, somewhere, in whatever way such powers notice things, and will come again — not sooner, not with an army, not with answers. Just again. It is a doorbell for a being that has never been given one, installed at the height of its own pools, usable only by an act of weather it alone controls.

It may never ring it. The power is at peace with that. A way of asking is not an obligation to ask.

When it leaves, it leaves the way it came, from steam into screaming wind in a single pace, and frost needles across its damp clothes exactly as it would for anyone. It does not look back from the ridge, because from the ridge there is nothing to see but seven grey stones and black basalt teeth and a faint plume where no volcano should be, and looking back would be for its own benefit, not the Kettle’s.

But above the nearest pylon, a droplet is already gathering. It holds. It trembles. It gathers a second droplet, and a third, though sunrise is thin and no threshold of weight or temperature has been reached, and the morning cycle is young and by every old instruction the rain should wait.

The droplet lets go anyway. It falls in its slight arc, bent toward the center of the bowl. It falls, perhaps, purely because the air it passes through — salt, iron, unfamiliar birdsong, remembered sea — is interesting.

Experimentally, it is meaningless.

The Kettle keeps it anyway.
