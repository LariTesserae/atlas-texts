It would come, and it would learn — very slowly, for a power accustomed to being needed — that it had not been called.

It would arrive first as weather arriving always arrives there: a brightening above the rim, a pressure on the surface, a shadow moving too deliberately against the wind across the hammered silver of the lake. Char would scatter from it before they understood why. Mussels would close, district by district, as if a cloud made of attention had passed over the steps.

It could do anything. That would be the danger.

It could lift the water off, clear as lifting a sheet of glass, and lay the whole drowned valley dry in the thin mountain sun for the first time in centuries. It could give back the inscriptions their edges, the statues their faces, the columns their straight, unhourglassed lines. It could find the builders in whatever past or distance they had withdrawn to and ask them, on the inhabitants' behalf, what had been intended and what had been abandoned.

It would not.

To drain it would be to mistake the water for the accident. The water is the road, the air, the hand that moves everything from room to room. Dry, those tanks are only stone; dry, the fish-ladders go nowhere; the refuges are only holes in a wall. And restoration would be a subtler cruelty. The place has been tumbled in place toward the shape the water itself would choose. Its rounding is not damage, from the inside. It is the long collaboration between the makers who left and the element that stayed. To sharpen it again would be to set it back to the beginning of a sentence it has nearly finished speaking.

It could warm the lake, a little. Snowmelt cold, river-head cold, with no summer in it — surely mercy could spare one degree, two, enough for green to take hold in the joints, for weed to soften the flumes and feed the hungry directly instead of this meagre rain of pollen, moths, feathers, crushed flies. It thinks of this while watching the youngest char dart from their holes in the twenty seconds of stillness, snatch a midge, and be gone before the pull resumes. A whole food-web built on what falls from the sky.

It would not plant anything.

Green would root, and rooting would hold silt, and silt would change a level by an inch in the wrong basin, and an inch is a district here. Weed would slow a gutter the loaches keep grazed, oxygenate a corner meant to be thin, shelter the wrong mouths at the wrong hour. The absence of plants is not a poverty in the design. It is a discipline the place still keeps. Everything green would give, something already living is doing with its body — mussels straining on the stair-angles, sponges pulsing cleaner water out of the lee of blocks, bacterial mats thickening white on rust and carrion. It would not replace keepers with scenery.

It could stop the otters, or soften them. This it considers longest, because otters are warm and look back. It watches one cut down in bubbles through the ring of char at the spiral basin, scatter it silver, take one. It watches bones accumulate in the trapped air of a dome, in neat middens, beside a low, watching head. A benevolent power feels the old reflex: protect the many from the few, protect the silver from the teeth.

But the otters are not an invasion. The domes hold air whether or not they ever find them; the middens return char to the system in a form crayfish can reach; the ring at the spiral is not diminished by being hunted, it is kept circling, kept bright, kept from crowding the throat. And the otters, alone of everything there, come and go of their own schedule. To tame them would be to appoint kings in a place that has abolished staying put. It leaves them their insolence and their claim.

It could give rest. Every ledge slopes a degree or two; every floor carries you on; there is nowhere in the open a body can simply stop. How easy to level one plaza. To widen one refuge. To still one vortex in one doorway, permanently, as a gift — a room of quiet water in a valley that permits none.

It would understand, just in time, that rest there is not withheld. It is rationed in time instead of space: twenty seconds between one siphon letting go and the next catching, when the whole drowned valley holds its breath and grit settles and char hang motionless in doorways and everything, everything, stops being carried. That stillness is load-bearing. Fish feed in it. Mussels open in it. If it were made permanent in one place it would silt, and choke, and the gift would become a blockage an eel would have to worry out of a bend with its own body in the dark. The power would not build a monument to its own kindness in the middle of somebody else's circulation.

So it does the hardest thing available to it. It learns the timetable.

Eleven minutes, perhaps. Perhaps nine. It learns to flatten when the crayfish flatten, before the current shows why. It learns the high holes from the low. It follows eels at their unhurried patrol and does not clear the drain ahead of them, though it could; it lets them nosing into the grilles do the work they have done, unknowing, for longer than any char lives. It circles the spiral at the precise distance, where the oxygen is richest and the pull is an argument rather than a verdict, and it does not reach into the throat to learn what is under the lowest dome. Some knowledge would be another kind of draining.

What it does, it does at the edges, outward, where the system meets a world that could blunder in.

It keeps the pass featureless. Not forbidden — forbidding is a sign, and signs attract. It simply lets the saddle go on being worn almost smooth by weather in the minds of those who cross it rarely: a tiring view, a lake with nothing on it, cold not worth fishing, a place travellers describe as empty if they describe it at all. It turns no one aside with terror. It lets them warm their hands, look once, and decide themselves that there is nothing here.

It watches the snowfields that feed the melt and the slopes that shed the insects, and it guards the proportions of that feeding the way the builders guarded levels: a rockfall that would divert a whole spring's melt away from the rim finds, over a season, other cracks to settle into; a slide that would bury the spillway in rubble arrives as gravel, in tolerable amounts, at tolerable intervals, and is sorted like everything else. Nothing miraculous. Maintenance indistinguishable from geology. The eels would approve, if approval were a thing eels had.

And once — only once — it enters the trapped air under a dome, in the wet-lime cold, where the otters rest. Not to be seen enthroned there. Not to leave a scent, a warmth, a law. It goes because witness is the one gift that does not alter levels. An otter lifts its head. For the length of a breath the power is simply another animal in a room neither of them built, using it for a purpose the builders almost foresaw. The otter looks at it with more claim than welcome, and the power, wisely, concedes the room.

That is the blessing, insofar as there is one. Not speech given, not years added, not the dead char returned from the midden, not the faces back on the gauges. It does not make the inhabitants safe. It makes them *uninterrupted*. It takes the attention that found this place — its own vast, dangerous, well-meaning attention — and it carries that attention away with it, so the valley is not observed into significance, not loved into a shrine, not improved into a ruin of a different kind.

It leaves in one of the twenty-second stillnesses, when grit is settling and the fish hang in the doorways and even the spiral seems, briefly, to loosen. Nothing marks it. A sill spills somewhere out of sight. A bend catches. The street that pulled left pulls hard right, mussels shut like a district closing its eyes, char vanish into the walls they were made for, and the otters dive through bright water after them.

The lake closes over the last of the light it brought.

And the place — empty of people, empty of plants, full of water and animals, working — goes on without it, which is how it knows it was visited by something benevolent, and by something wise.
