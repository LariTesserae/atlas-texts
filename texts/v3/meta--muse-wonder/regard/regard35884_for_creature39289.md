They would not descend at once.

That is the first sign of them. Anyone else — curious, devout, acquisitive, kind in the ordinary hurried way — goes down the rope to see. A wise power stops at the rim where the ears pop, and stays there through a whole turning of the tide.

Not to study it. Study is too small, and too loud in its intentions. They stay to learn the hours of it: how the low note thickens in the sternum when the sea far away begins to push into the cave mouths, how the hiss tears thinner at the slack, how the stones knock deep underground like something vast shifting in its sleep. They keep their mouth slightly open, like any visitor. They let their eyes water. Benevolence does not exempt them from the pressure. Wisdom consists, in part, of not asking to be exempted.

They do not speak to it. They have voices that could, perhaps, be heard even there — could cut the roar, could shape themselves into the one frequency that would reach whatever passes, in that turning air, for a centre. They do not use them. To be answered by it would not be a blessing they could give. It has breath, not utterance. It knows the world as pressure, grit, warmth, obstruction in the spiral. It has been complete in that knowledge longer than the spruces have been standing. Giving it a voice would not complete it further. Giving it ours would be a theft disguised as a gift.

Nor do they silence it, even a little, even in mercy. That temptation comes, to all benevolent things, at the end of the first hour: surely this is too much, surely no creature should have to sound like this forever, surely peace must mean quiet. But they are wise, and so they sit with the temptation until it reveals itself as ignorance. The roar is not happening *to* the resident. The roar is the resident — its body, its motion, its one sustained act of being. To quieten the bowl would not be to soothe it. It would be to unmake the throat it breathes through. There is no sufferer in the centre asking for relief. There is only a very old shape in balance with what made it.

So they do not heal it, because it is not wounded.

They do not feed it, because the forest drops what it needs onto its floor and it sorts the dead at its door in its own good time.

They do not finish the fourth wall. They look at it a long while, though — the knee-high curve on the south side, the stones still piled beside it, sorted by size for hands that never came back. A lesser kindness would complete the work, or clear it away, restore the bowl to some idea of untouched nature. They do neither. The unfinished wall is not a failure in the place. It is the record of the exact point at which people learned manners: far enough to attend, not so far as to presume. They leave the pile in its order. They leave the rotted hazel sleeping platform, and the green troughs holding their condensation, and the patch of stone at hand height worn smooth by palms that learned to speak through rock. These things are not debris. They are a grammar. Whoever comes next, if anyone ever does, will need to be able to read it.

What they do, they do outside the rim.

This is the part no one would ever witness, and it is most of the work. They walk the low dark plain for miles in every direction — the wet spruce forest, the places where the limestone goes soft under moss, the long blind course of the caves under the hill all the way to the sea cliffs — not as a surveyor walks, but as a neighbour walks a boundary they intend to keep by keeping it unremarkable. Where a cliff fall, some winter soon, would choke one of the cave mouths and change the pressure in the throat, they do not prevent the fall. They loosen a different stone, higher up, so that when the cliff goes it will go cleanly, and the sea will still breathe. Where a drainage ditch, dug by a village not yet founded, would in fifty years lower the cold water that keeps the bowl frosted at noon, they let a beaver's forgotten dam stand in the mind of the valley a little longer than it otherwise would. Where a road will one day want to come — and roads always, eventually, want to come — they make the ground on the far side of the ridge very slightly more plausible. Nothing miraculous. A firmer bank. An easier ford. The road goes there instead, and takes its noise with it, and nobody travelling on it ever knows there was a decision.

They do not put a fence around the hollow. Fences announce. They do not put a name to it on any map, and if it has acquired one in some local keeping, they let the name drift a valley sideways over the next generation, the way names do when nobody corrects them. They do not raise a shrine, or clear the leaning spruces, or reweave the living thatch that has grown wild over the rim. The quarter of cold sky visible straight overhead is enough sky. It has always been enough.

Inside the bowl, in the end, they permit themselves one descent.

They go down the way the attendants went: hand over hand on what is left of the bark rope, past the trembling ferns, keeping out of the centre, mouth open, eyes streaming. They take the pocket behind the east ledge where the noise drops from unbearable to merely terrible. They do not sit in the shelters facing outward — that posture belongs to a species, and they respect tenancy even of a posture. They simply stand in the slack of the current for a while, being an obstruction: a warmth in the spiral, a softness that changes the hiss where it passes. The resident knows them in the only way it can know anything. As pressure. As a brief alteration in its own chest. Then, unhurried, they lay one palm flat against the worn patch of stone at hand height, where so many human palms have rested.

They do not tap a message. There is nothing to say that the stone has not already been told, and nothing the resident needs translated. The gesture is older than messages. It means: *you were here. We noticed. We are not going to mistake noticing for a claim.*

Perhaps they leave the bowl a fraction cleaner than they found it — not the floor, which keeps itself, but the rim, where some later century will eventually drop a bright, indigestible thing that the spiral would worry at for a hundred years without managing to grind down. That they carry out. Not as purification. As the smallest housekeeping, the kind any guest owes a room that was full before they entered it.

Then they climb up, hand over hand, past the drips and the troughs and the unfinished wall, and the rim arrives for them, as it does for everyone, like a shoreline. Two steps beyond the lip the spruces close in, the moss is soft and ordinary underfoot, and the silence rings.

They do not look back. Looking back would be for their benefit, and they have had benefit enough: one full tide of standing inside a life utterly unlike their own, which did not need them, did not want anything from them, and was not improved by their attention. That is a rare thing, even for a power, and they are wise enough to receive it as the gift it is — the gift of being, for once, unnecessary, and welcome anyway in the way weather is welcome: by passing through without being asked to stay.

The roar stays in its one small room in the earth. It goes on sorting pine needles from white pebbles, beetle shells from grit, at the threshold. It goes on deepening its groove by the width of a hair. The moss reaches down toward the floor and never arrives, and reaches again.

Nothing has changed.

Everything, for miles around, has been quietly arranged so that nothing has to.
