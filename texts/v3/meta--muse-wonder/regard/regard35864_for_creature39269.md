They would not finish it.

That is the first thing, and it takes them longer than you would expect — because the impulse to finish it is, at first, indistinguishable from love.

A benevolent power comes to the edge of the bowl the way a hand comes toward a sleeping animal: already softening. It sees the young rock that never sets, the forest that never rises, the light that never sharpens into morning. It sees the great glassy salamanders fanning their red gills in an inch of water, breeding in a body that was meant only to be a beginning, and every kind instinct in it says the same kind, ruinous thing:

*Part the cloud. Shrink the pools. Let one season turn.*

Give them the signal. Let the mudstone harden and the channels cut and the gills close. Let the larva become, at last, the adult folded inside it like a letter never opened. Rescue the place from its perpetual first chapter.

And then the power — being wise, as well as benevolent — stays its hand.

And stays. And stays.

Long enough for the gnats to column unconcerned through its presence. Long enough for condensation to bead on it as it beads on everything else here, impartially, on leaf and frog and stone. Long enough for a salamander, on its snowshoe feet, to pad across the film of water directly beneath where the power is attending, and settle, and let small fish take shelter in the shade of its gills.

Because staying teaches what arriving could not.

This is not a wound. This is not a place failing to become a place, nor an animal failing to become an animal. That is a story told by landscapes that hardened, by creatures whose pools dried on schedule — a story in which adulthood is the only honest ending, and everything before it is only preparation.

The bowl tells another story, with its whole teeming body.

Here, incompletion is not a lack. It is an agreement millions of lives deep. The roots keep the mud soft; the soft mud keeps the water shallow; the shallow water keeps the salamanders larval; the larval salamanders graze the seedlings that would raise a cooler canopy; the dim canopy keeps the heat close; the heat keeps the cloud fed; the cloud keeps everything wet and warm and seasonless, forever beginning. Break one link in the name of improvement — drain it, dry it, let it *mature* — and you do not liberate the salamander. You strand it, gilled and thin-skinned and cartilage-boned, on banks it was never built for, in air that would take the water straight out of its body. You do not liberate the bowl. You kill the only world in which its thousands of frogs and films and flatworms and photosynthetic skins make any sense at all. The adult form is not hidden treasure. In this place, it would be a death with better posture.

A power that cannot see that is merely strong. A power that sees it, and still reaches in, is merely vain — it wants the salamander to confirm *its* idea of a life.

So the wise one does the harder, quieter thing.

It does nothing to the interior of the bowl at all.

It does not thin the chorus or spare the eggs or lift the heron's beak. Predation here is not cruelty to be edited out; it is the rate at which life redistributes itself, carcass to city to clean husk, and a salamander made safe from being eaten would unbalance the bowl as surely as a salamander forced to metamorphose. It does not grant them speech, or foresight, or sorrow over their condition — they have no sorrow over their condition. It would be obscene to give them grief as a gift.

What it does, it does at the edges, where the bowl is not itself but vulnerable to what is not itself.

Far out at the rim, where the higher country stands, it attends to the slow threats the bowl cannot answer: a shift in the outer weather that would starve the cloud deck; a crack through which the whole shallow saturation might one day drain away downhill; the approach of anything — river changing course, climate turning, or living thing with tools and opinions — that would cut a first channel and start the long hardening. Against these, the power sets a guard so gentle the bowl will never feel it. A seep redirected. A slope eased by the width of a hand over a century. A deep, cold spring coaxed up under the root-mat, insurance against a drought the bowl has no word for. Nothing shields the salamanders from their own world. They are shielded, only, from having their world taken.

And then — because preservation without possibility is just another kind of arrest, a museum instead of a life — the power leaves one small new thing.

At the far margin, where the bowl already thins against the rising ground, it makes a place that did not exist before: a shallow run of pools, a hand's breadth deeper than the sheet-water, fed by a trickle that, unlike everything else here, is allowed the faintest rhythm. In long cycles too slow for any living thing to learn by, the last pool in the run will thin. Not vanish. Thicken, warm, shrink a little, and fill again. A gradient where none was offered. The whisper of a signal.

No salamander is driven to it. No egg is laid there by an arranging hand. Most will live and be eaten and redistribute within sight of it and never go, and that is exactly as it should be. But across the generations, feet on water being what they are, one may wander up the run, as they wander everywhere, unhurried, tails swaying in the dark. And if it lingers there as the pool thins around it — if something in the old, unused machinery of its body answers, at last, not to force but to invitation — then it may do the thing its kind has not done in longer than the soft rock can remember.

Not because it was unfinished. Because now, for the first time, finishing is a *choice*.

And if none ever chooses it? If the run of pools sits for ten thousand years as nothing but a slightly cooler place where dragonflies stitch and lilies open? Then the power will count that no failure. The door was the gift. Not the going through.

At the end of its visit — if visit is the word, for something that stayed long enough for moss to begin, bright and fine, across the place where it knelt — the power withdraws the only way you can withdraw from such a place without tearing the root-mat: slowly, and letting the water close behind it.

It leaves no monument. Stone would be an insult here, and anyway the mud would edit any monument out within the week, snails at its edges, white fungal threads stitching it down into richness. It leaves no blessing the frogs can hear; the chorus goes on at its one unchanging pitch, trading voices as the green light thickens toward what passes for night.

It leaves a depression where its weight pressed the mud. The depression fills, in seconds, with tea-brown water.

Before the light has fully thickened, a salamander comes padding out across the flats in the near-dark, gills glowing faint rust-red through the duckweed, algae green under its glassy skin, a grain-sized orchid seed riding patient in the mucus along its spine. It finds the new hollow the way it finds every hollow: by the minute trembling of the water, by feel, by the bowl arriving through the seam down its flank.

It settles in. Its tail keeps up its slow, absent-minded undulation. Its footprint-puddle will be permanent now, and frogs will lay eggs in it, and gnats will gather over its warmth.

It is not rescued. It is not completed. It is not improved.

It is *kept* — and, for the first time in the history of its kind, it is also, almost imperceptibly, free.

The power goes on its way, wet to the skin, and does not look back to check whether anything has changed.

Nothing has.

That was the mercy.
