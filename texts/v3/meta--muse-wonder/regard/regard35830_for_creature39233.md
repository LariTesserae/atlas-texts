It would not arrive.

That is the first thing wisdom would understand here: arrival is a kind of weather, and this world has edited weather out of its sky. Anything that descended through the bleached dome in light and power would throw shade where no shade was scheduled, would burn dust into patterns the repair-swarms would spend a generation unknitting, would be heard — through soles and teeth and sternum — in every folded street below. The deep lineages would taste it in the air. The old hot presences in the service shafts would change the temperature of their rooms in alarm. The building itself, which has maintained its final form for longer than most species maintain a language, would do what it does to any insult.

It would heal over it.

So the power, being benevolent, and being wise, would learn stillness first.

It would spend — oh, let us say a century. What is a century, here? A cooling fin ticks as it expands. A seam widens a hair, swallows dust, closes. Somewhere in a seam-canyon a child is born, learns to abseil past ten thousand windows, grows old in amber light, dies, and her water is braided into her granddaughter's hair. Let us say a century is long enough for the power to stop seeing an emergency.

Because at first, any kind eye would see one. The thirst. The glare. Lips cracked despite reclaimers. Children standing barefoot on hot panels at dusk, weeping without tears, being watched for panic. A whole world with no rain, no river, no waste permitted, no extravagant gesture left in it. A young power would want to dim the reflectors. To crack the white sky with cloud. To open the sealed cisterns and let it, at last, pour.

An older power would stand with both feet flat, and listen through the grid, and understand what that mercy would do.

Rain would be a catastrophe here. Not a blessing — a flood on a world with no soil to receive it, no drains that open upward, a skin that heals over digging and sheds water toward gullets built for dust. Cloud would starve the solar avenues that feed the farms below. A cooler noon would not free the Bright-Kin; it would orphan them. Their crowns, their mirrored cloth, their grafted grey-green skin, the whole slow liturgy of the travelling shade — you do not take that from people and call the nakedness underneath liberation. You do not rescue a tide-line creature by abolishing the tide.

And the tenders, the power would notice — this takes the second century — are not prisoners of the brightness.

They are glad of it. Briefly, dry-mouthed, silver-eyed, at dusk. The gladness is real, and it is theirs, and it cannot survive being pitied.

So it would do what the inhabitants do. It would become small, and hidden, and useful.

Far out beyond the mirror-fields, where no tender walks and only instruments fail politely in the heat, there are reflectors that have held the long noon in place since before the canyon families had names. They are drifting. Not much. A fraction of a fraction of a degree in a thousand years — but this is a world that lives inside fractions. In ten thousand years, noon would last just long enough to push the truce between erosion and repair out of true. The surface would begin, very slowly, to lose. Panel by panel, district by district, the wearing would outrun the mending, and no one below would know until the light came down a shaft in the wrong color and a farm that had never known a season learned the word for drought.

No one will ever know it was going to happen. The power would go there, in whatever way such a power goes, and lay the gentlest possible correction against the oldest reflector's rim. Not a push. A suggestion, continued for fifty years. The kind of force a planet applies to a moon. The kind of force the building itself applies to everything.

Then it would go down.

Not through a hatch — the hatches alarm, and harden, and warm. Down the way heat goes down, patiently, by degrees, through the green-lit machinery seas under their scuffed glass ceilings, through streets folded under streets where it rains on schedule from the pipes, past families who would leave it offerings if they could see it, which, being wise, it would not permit. Down where the air thickens and solvents replace water and the inhabitants have put away eyes as an early, surface extravagance. Down there, three levels below a factory that wakes early once in a generation, a filtration weave sealed before the cisterns was torn — not broken enough to kill, only broken enough to teach an entire lineage that a certain bitterness in the breath, a certain ache behind the second tongue, was what being alive tasted like.

Children have been born tasting it. They have woven it into the beads at their throats.

The power would mend the weave. Slowly. In the dark, in the heat, at the speed of maintenance, so the building's own repair impulse would recognize the work as its own and not close over it like a scar. It would take — let us not be in a hurry — it would take longer than the filtration weave took to tear. And then it would wait nearby, if *nearby* still has meaning at that depth, until the first child is born who breathes clean and does not know she has been given anything, who only finds, tasting the air for the waking factory, that the world has a flavor she has no word for yet.

She will invent the word. That is the point. A gift that arrives with its giver attached is a debt, and water here is already inherited, accounted, carried. The power would add no new debt to the braids.

And that, almost, would be all.

It would turn away comets in the far dark, centuries before they became omens, in a silence so complete that not even the navigation beacons would flicker. It would copy nothing, take nothing, archive — perhaps, in whatever way such a power remembers — the sequence *ceramic, glass, living metal, prayer-tile, solar scale* exactly as an old tender's feet recite it, so that at least one mind outside the skin holds this world the way the world holds itself: not as a ruin, not as a machine, but as an address. It would not uplift the presences in the hot shafts into gods or drag them back into people. It would leave its own offering where the deep families leave theirs: a panel freshly printed. A conduit polished bright. It would not decide whether that was worship or pest control. It would find the distinction genuinely uninteresting, and be pleased to have learned that.

There is one more thing. Benevolence insists on it, though wisdom argues, and in the end they compromise the way the tenders compromise with noon.

Water here is never owned. Only carried, between one body and the next — births, deaths, marriages, gram for gram, returned at dusk to the shallow mouth in the skin. It is a perfect circle, and like all perfect circles it leaves no room for a gift, because everything given must first have been taken from someone.

So once — only once, so the arithmetic of the world will absorb it like dust into a seam — at dusk, when the buried towers are catching the last copper light and the condensers are beading with the day's stolen moisture, every condenser on the bright face of the world would bead a little more than the day had given it. Not much. A mouthful extra, here. A palmful, there. Unaccounted. Unearned. Not enough to change an economy, a rite, a braid at any child's throat. Just enough that for one evening, when the Bright-Kin return their portion to the skin of the world, the gift would not be repayment.

It would simply be a gift.

The tenders would notice. Of course they would notice; noticing is what the building keeps them for. They would stand absolutely still, the way they stand to talk, shifting weight once, heel and knuckle pressed to the warm ceramic, and the conversation would travel a mile through the structure and be felt in a hundred sternums: the world is a mouthful heavier tonight.

Some of them would say the building was dreaming kindly. Some of the deep families, hearing the single deep note change by less than a breath, would polish a conduit in answer. In the canyon the children would go on abseiling past the dark windows, one in ten thousand lit amber, unaware. The oldest tender on the plain, face-down in the glow of the night panels with her grafted skin unfurling, ears pressed sideways to her home, would feel under all the listening a new and very faint pressure, as of another listener, very far away, learning to be quiet properly.

She would not be afraid. Fear, like tears, costs more than it says.

She would press her palm a little flatter against the warm skin, so the humming could come up through the bones of her hand, and she would think — in whatever way the Bright-Kin think, slowly, with the whole sole —

*Good. You heard us.*

And the power, having been heard, would do the last and hardest thing a benevolent and wise power can do for a world that was never empty:

It would leave it bright, and leave it thirsty, and leave it glad —

and go, without a mark, at exactly the rate at which the ground heals.
