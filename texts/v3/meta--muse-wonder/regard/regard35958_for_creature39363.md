They would not rescue it.

That is the first thing, and the hardest. A power that was only benevolent, without the wisdom, would arrive at the bowl already grieving — for the fissure too narrow now, for the eyes sealed under mineral, for the dark chamber and the body that cannot turn in it freely. They would want to open the rock. They would call it mercy.

A wise power comes sideways through the cleft, feels the roar close behind them like a door, and understands at once that the enclosure is not a prison added to the creature. It is the creature, after this long. The wall is skin and ear and memory. To crack it open would not be to free the resident. It would be to tear the organ it thinks with.

So they do not touch the back wall.

They do not touch the pool, either. They do not clean it, or deepen the narrow neck of water between the worlds, or try to hurry the rings. They do not give it back its eyes. They do not bring it a companion — God, no. A second great soft body in that chamber, a second breathing, would be a lifelong shout in the one place it has made perfectly attuned to a falling drop. They do not heal the old dent on its left side where the rockfall still arrives, century after century, in the resonance. It has grown around that wound the way the fern trunks grew around theirs. It is part of how the place hears now.

They sit down on the dryish ledge at the rim, where the roar resolves into a pressure in the sternum, and they make themselves small and separate, in the manner of everything that is permitted to stay.

And they stay.

Not for an afternoon. Not for a night outside the cleft, like the people with the hammerstone, speaking the names of the dead and hoping. They stay at the creature's own tempo, which means the mist thickens and thins around them ten thousand times. Their clothes rot and are replaced by damp. The snails write silver over their stillness and it dries to nothing. The sunbird of one decade is replaced, furious and iridescent, by its descendants, and the power learns not to turn their head at its coming. A fern unrolls. Another fern unrolls. The lichen on the rim adds a fraction of a ring.

They are learning the bowl before they presume to speak into it. Anything said too soon would just be one more voice taken down into the layered dark, crossing the others without interference, to be returned softened a lifetime later. They have no wish to add noise to that archive.

From the ledge, over years, they come to see the two or three true dangers, and they are none of them in the bowl.

One is far upslope and still three hundred years away: a hairline fracture, opening grain by grain in the mountain's shoulder, which will one wet season steal a third of the seep before it ever reaches the back wall and send it out through a new, useless spring on the outer face. One is in the valley, further still in time — the slow shift of warm winds that will one day bring the clouds a little lower, a little less often, so the sunbirds' corridor of flowering trees thins and breaks. One is human, and nearest: not malice, only notice. A path beaten a little too clear. A name on a map. People arriving in numbers, arriving loud, arriving with the wish to be healed *now*, who would stand at the pool and shout their questions into it and wait an impatient hour and leave disappointed, having taught the grove nothing but shouting.

Against these, the power acts — elsewhere, almost invisibly, and mostly in advance.

They set a hand, once, deep inside the mountain where no living thing will ever see, and persuade the hairline fracture to close along a different grain. Not sealed forever; nothing here is forever. Just redirected. Given another thousand years to become a problem, by which time the resident will have become something else and may not mind. They go down into the warm valleys in other seasons, in other shapes, and see to it that a certain flowering tree is not the last of itself on a certain ridge. They do nothing so crude as a fence or a curse at the cleft. They do something subtler and more durable: they make the way up forgettable. The turning off the ridge path slides out of the memory of those who came looking for a wonder to use. It stays perfectly clear, perfectly easy, in the memory of those who came with almost nothing and intended to leave almost nothing. The mountain keeps its own door policy. The power only reminds it how.

Inside the bowl itself they change nothing.

Except, at last, they speak.

By then they know how. Close to the stone, mouth almost to ear, the way conversation has always had to happen at the center. Not a command. Not a blessing — a blessing from a great power can land on an old thing like a rockfall, an event it must spend centuries growing around. They simply tell it, carefully, one true thing from outside: what the world has become beyond the ridge, in the time since it slipped through the fissure. The seas. The cities like brief, bright fevers of sound. The wars. The musics. The species that have gone silent while it was listening, and the new ones, furious and iridescent, that have come up singing in their place. They make of their voice a carrying thing, and they lay the whole of it, not thrown, but placed, at the edge of the pool where it can be received.

Then they wait for the answer.

This is the real offering, and it is not the words. It is the waiting. No living person has ever waited out the full interval — the hours the old petitioners allowed, sitting outside the cleft, were only the bowl clearing its throat. The power waits years. They grow proficient at stillness. Moss takes the print of them and holds it and slowly swells it away, and takes it again. Rings begin in the middle of the black pool with no drop seen to arrive, and cross, and cross each other, each in its own thin layer: the rockfall still arriving on the left side; a woman of forty generations ago describing a sickness, carefully, close to the stone; the *thoom* of the resident shifting in its sleep, turning some older sound over in the folds where it keeps them.

And then, one dusk — perhaps the dusk of a year that had a name, once, in a valley now empty — their own voice comes back.

Slower. Softer. The consonants worn off it like water off rock. The emphasis moved, so that the part they had thought was the grief has quieted, and a small, plain fact they mentioned in passing — that the clouds still move under the ridge, that strangers still sometimes climb a long way to hear themselves understood — has become the center of the sentence, and rings in the bowl long after the rest has faded.

They receive it the way the pool receives a drop. They do not correct it. They do not ask for more.

Only then do they understand if anything else is wanted, and it is almost nothing: the resident is very slowly becoming stone. Not dying. There is no hurry and no tragedy in it; the mineral fans on its skin are the same fans drying on the wall, rust, pale green, a blind white. In another age the chamber will hold a shape that is mostly rock and a hearing that is mostly the mountain's own, and the distinction, already administrative, will quietly lapse. The power does not arrest this. To keep it forever soft, forever itself, would be another kind of fissure to trap it in. They only make sure the becoming will not be interrupted at its most vulnerable point — that when the pool's narrow neck finally silts and closes, as one day it must, the last sounds taken down will be good ones, arrived at in the right order. A drop. The sunbird. Their own returned voice, still arriving. Nothing startled. Nothing shouted.

When they finally rise, the ledge is damp where they sat. They leave by turning sideways in the cleft. They take nothing away except the changed sentence, which they will carry down the mountain and deliver, not to the dead who could have used it centuries ago, but to the living air of the world — spoken once, in passing, in the hearing of people who do not know why it steadies them.

Three paces out, the roar is cut clean. The warm wind is ordinary wind. Anyone meeting them on the ridge would see nothing at all had happened.

Behind them, unseen, the bowl goes on. The ferns lean a fraction toward a thread of water finding a new flute in the stone. The frog blinks. The pool lifts in a slow ring that travels, unhurried, all the way to its stone margin before vanishing — the power's voice, taken down into the flooded dark, kept separate from every other voice, to be turned over very slowly and given back, softened and changed, in an interval already longer than any human life.

The power has arranged only this: that when it is finally returned, to no one in particular, the grove will still be there to receive it, the water still weeping down the wall, the sunbird still coming furiously to the orchid, the way up still passable — sideways, with almost nothing — to whoever next needs to be heard at that tempo.
