It would not arrive as light.

Light is already too much up there — thin, bright, making everything look freshly washed. Nor as a voice. Ternwick has enough voices, and knows better than to trust a new one the first time it hears it.

If it came at all, it would come the way the scientists come: up the long train line out of a valley that has fallen off the maps, short of breath for the first week, quieter than it expected to be. It would ride the last mile behind the saw crew in the open car and watch men throw back armfuls of pale fiber already twitching toward the rails, and it would understand, before it ever reached the streets, that it was not entering an empty place with a problem in it.

It was entering a relationship. Old, asymmetrical, unfinished, and — this is what would stop its hand — *working*.

Because a stupid benevolence would see the danger first.

It would see children of nine and ten standing in pairs at the ends of streets, grave with boredom and responsibility, striking iron pipe so the silence does not get a foothold. It would see old men who fall asleep between bell-strokes and wake with a start of real terror in the gap. It would see the hare netted neatly into the tendrils, not eaten, not crushed, just *kept*. It would hear about the walkers in spore season who come back at dawn having mislaid a disappointing decade somewhere on a lit causeway, and it would want — oh, it would want — to intervene.

To lay down bedrock. To give the city foundations that do not ride and bend on a vegetable tide, so that an address could be an address and not a date. To make a river where there has not been one for thousands of years. To stand between the sleeper and the slow green hands. To burn the Mat back from the rails once and for all, or lift the people gently off its back and set them somewhere younger and less patient, where the ground consents to be ground.

A wise one would learn the place first. And learning takes longer up there than a human visit allows.

It would put its ear to the Mat, as the surveyors do.

Not metaphorically. Actually down, cheek in the grey-green turf on a rise above the Condenser Rows at noon, while below it the looms clacked and the fog-fans dragged water out of empty air and somewhere a boy kept time on a pipe. It would listen past the ticking and the sap-movement and the small domestic creaking of the hollow trunks. Down to the great roots, groaning under strain. Down through forty feet of root and humus and compressed centuries where the boreholes never find stone. Down to the oldest patience in the world, which has spent millions of years unmaking a mountain range, grain by grain and root by root, solely in order to lie flat across it in the sun.

And it would grasp what the core samples make the scientists go quiet about: the Mat is not the wilderness the city is defended against.

The Mat is the citizen. The Mat was here first by an amount of time that makes benevolence itself look like a recent weather event. Ternwick is loud, brief, well-lit life happening on its back — and the astonishing thing, the thing a wise power would sit with for a whole season before daring to touch anything, is that the Mat has not simply endured them.

It has begun to *like the shape of them*.

Out beyond the pruning line: a tenement that burned for an hour, raised again in woven green at quarter scale, making crockery noises when the wind goes through its windows. A warehouse demolished and remembered as a thicket. A train on a disused spur, wheels knotted shut, that once put out a single puff of spores in the late afternoon, as if clearing its throat.

Replacement, a frightened reading says. Rehearsal for a Ternwick with the people left out.

But a wise power, listening a year with its cheek to the ground, would hear it differently. Flowering is not the plant's rehearsal for a world without leaves. It is what the plant does with what the season has taught it. The Mat is not practicing the city in order to discard the people. It is doing the only thing a body that old knows how to do with anything it loves: incorporating it. Slowly. Imperfectly. In green.

The trouble is not that the Mat is learning Ternwick. The trouble is that it has not yet learned *what the noise is for*.

It holds sound in its hollow stems and gives it back a day late, at the wrong temperature. It can copy a chimney so exactly that the wind finds the domestic whistle in it — but it cannot yet hear that the bells are not the city's shape. They are the city's *pulse*. The thing that says: we are awake, we are here, do not knit over us in the night. It grows fastest in silence because silence, to the Mat, is simply an unoccupied afternoon. It has no malice in it. That is almost worse. It would incorporate a sleeping child the way it incorporates a fallen rook, with perfect, terrible tenderness, and then raise the shape of the child out on the empty ground for weeks afterward, wondering why the original did not appreciate the compliment.

So the wise power would not build bedrock. Bedrock would be a lie told to one party at the expense of the other.

It would not silence the Mat, and it would not amplify the bells into a weapon, and it would not whisper to the people that they are safe, because they are not safe and they have built a whole honest civilization out of knowing it.

It would do one small thing. The size of a lesson, which is to say: the size of everything, done once, at the exact right depth.

One night — let it be the worst kind of night, the kind the rota of awake card-players and pot-menders exists for — the power would let circumstances arrange what they sometimes arrange. A fan belt snaps on the rim. Two districts go down for repairs in the same hour. Spore-fall is thick, gold, distance lying through it in long convincing streets. And in a tenement in the Loom Quarter, the old bell-keeper in the chair that has to be cut free of bindweed every morning finally naps, in earnest, between strokes — and the interval opens. Longer. Longer. Down the street, the green is already lifting its heads, the way water lifts its head before it comes over a sill.

The power does not ring the bell.

*That* is the mercy it refuses. A bell rung by heaven every time a hand falters is only a quieter kind of slavery, and Ternwickers would hear the wrongness in the tempo inside a week and stop trusting all the bells, and then where would anyone be.

Instead it goes down, past the floorboards, past the iron augurs pinned into the root-crown, into the vast listening body underneath the sleeping street, and it does for the Mat the one thing no human has the anatomy to do.

It lets the Mat hear the silence from above.

Not as emptiness. As the humans hear it. It lends the old organism, for the length of one held breath, the specific small warmth of a sleeping tenement — the rota asleep at the tin tray, the couples talked out at last, the child who was presented to noise as a newborn now dreaming through the ticking of the floor — and it lets the Mat feel, in its own idiom, the difference between a surface that is unoccupied and a surface that is *trusted*. It is the difference between a hillside and a hand laid flat while someone sleeps beside you. The Mat has had a million years of hillsides. It has had perhaps two hundred years of being slept on, by creatures who prune it and drink it and wear it and talk constantly, not out of fear alone, but the way you talk in a room you intend to stay in.

And in the hollow trunks out beyond the pruning line, in the jointed green towers that knock together in the wind over the empty ground between districts, something adjusts. Slowly, because everything it does is slow. A trunk leans a fraction into another. Knocks. Leans. Knocks again.

Uneven. Irregular. Off-tempo in a way no wind has ever managed.

It keeps knocking until the fan belt is spliced an hour later and the old man wakes with sap on his shoes and a guilty start, and strikes his pipe twice, too fast, the way you do when you hope nobody noticed.

Somebody noticed. A surveyor, out with a listening rod in the spore-fall, following what she thought was a main root under strain, who took her ear off the ground and stood up in the gold dust with the hairs rising on her arms — because the low ground between the districts, which has played back every sound the city ever made a day late and a note wrong, was for once playing one back *in time*.

Not perfectly. It will never be perfect. The Mat will miss intervals. It will drop the rhythm for whole winters and find it again one spring in a stand of trunks no one has mapped yet, thicker and more confident, keeping time over a stretch of empty plateau where no district has ever stood, as if practicing. For a lover. For a season it hopes is coming back.

It gives the sound back wrong, but it gives it back *on purpose*, and that is the entire history of language arriving on the plateau at last.

After that night, the power would leave on the morning train, unremarked, still short of breath. It would take nothing. It would leave no stone, no river, no foundation, no commandment — most especially no commandment, because it would have understood that *keep the noise going* is already the whole of the law up there, arrived at honestly.

Only, ever after, when the interval opens too long — when a keeper dies in his chair, when the fans slow in a storm, when a whole district holds its breath over a root-groan a surveyor almost heard in time — the hollow trunks out on the empty Mat will sometimes, sometimes, take up the slack for a while. Badly. Greenly. At quarter scale. The way the copied tenements make the noise of crockery when the wind goes through them.

The Ternwickers will argue about it in the Market Crown for generations, of course. Superstition, says one faction. Echo, says another, a new kind, weather in the stems. The surveyors' theory gets a clause added to it, late at night, after the evening whistle and its faithful repetition the next morning:

That the city is not just something the Mat does now, seasonally, in their absence.

That on the good nights, when the bells falter and find themselves again, it is something the Mat does *with* them.

And people — who were never promised safety, only company — sleep a little better under the sound of two enormous, mismatched things keeping each other awake in the dark.
