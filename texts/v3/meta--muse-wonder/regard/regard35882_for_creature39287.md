They would not descend into the middle.

That would be their first act of understanding, and for a long time it would look like doing nothing at all.

They would come to the rim — this benevolent and wise power, whatever shape benevolence takes when it has lived long enough to outgrow hurry — and they would stop where the plateau stops. They would look down into the double light, into the polished emptiness of the salt pan, into the ring of life pressed so thin and so patiently against the wall, and they would resist the oldest temptation of the kind-hearted:

to improve it.

They would not make a pond in the center.

How easy that would be to imagine as mercy. Water, real water, standing open under the sky. A tree beside it. Shade. A green heart where now there is only glare. For a season, perhaps, it would look like a rescue. The frogs would gather. The baboons would drink. Dragonflies would come in numbers.

And then, slowly, everything that belongs here would begin to die of kindness.

The translucent shrimp would lose the drought that tells them when to be brief and brilliant. The algae would be shaded out of its own stone pores. The salt pan would darken and soften and cease to throw the heat upward in the column the swifts ride without beating their wings. Roots would reach where roots have never reached. Predators who cannot now cross the bright exposure would learn the way to water, and come at night, and the thumbnail frogs sealed in their clay would have nowhere left that was empty enough to be safe. The hollow works *because* its center is unusable. Its emptiness is not a lack. It is the engine.

A wise power would see that. They would see that the being who lives here is not one creature hidden somewhere waiting to be found. It is the arrangement itself: swift and lizard and frog and bee, baboon and crow and snail and grass, all written in a circle around a void, each keeping to the narrow hour and narrow ledge it can bear, each made possible by all the others keeping out of the middle. To save any one of them by filling the center would be to unwite them all.

So they would not build. They would not plant. They would not leave food, or a shelter, or a sign.

If they entered at all, it would be at dawn, along the margin, in the manner of the place. Single file, perhaps. Light-footed on the terraces. They would stay where the clay seam sweats sideways in the night and dries by ten. They would learn the tempo before they presumed to touch anything: condensation, bloom, brief feeding, retreat. Noon belongs to no one. They would be gone, or still as lizards in the cracks, before the light turned.

And then — outside the rim, where the hollow cannot defend itself — they would do their work.

Because the threats to a place like this do not rise out of its center. They come from the flat world above, which cannot see it until it is almost on top of it. A road surveyed in a straight line across the plateau, because straight lines are cheaper. A quarry that wants this same white limestone and would find the swift-holes only when the wall came down. Poison drifted over the grazing land, thinning the insects until the evening gyre grew quieter year by year and no one could say why. Boots, too many boots, coming down the baboons' worn path to watch the tapping, until the troop stopped coming and the old female's knowledge went untaught into the dust.

Against those, a benevolent power would not be still.

They would move the road by the width of a forgotten map-error. They would let the quarryman's eye slide, again and again, to poorer stone a valley away. They would turn a wind at the plateau's edge on the one morning it mattered. They would make a fence fall out of fashion, a footpath lose its reputation, a bright idea in a distant office quietly fail to find funding. Nothing miraculous. Nothing anyone could point to. Just the plateau remaining, year after year, unremarkable to everyone who might ruin it — dust, stone, heat, a dip in the ground not worth stopping for.

Inside, if they touched anything, it would be removals, not additions. A strange seed blown in from a far garden, rooting too thirstily in a damp pocket: lifted out before it learned the seam. A shard of human glass fallen to the pan's edge, throwing one wrong blade of light where the frogs emerge: carried away. A fallen block sealing a crack the bees have used for longer than anyone remembers: not blasted, not levered heroically, but worried loose grain by grain over a night, in darkness, so that by dawn the sideways water found its old path again and no creature woke to a world rearranged by giants.

And perhaps — once, in a drought longer than the place's memory, when even the night stone stayed warm and no condensation beaded and the pockets stayed salt-dry morning after morning — perhaps they would allow themselves one small, deniable mercy. Not rain. Never rain out of season. Just, for a few nights, a coolness laid along the shaded wall like a hand held near, not touching, a cheek. Enough for the stone to sweat a little. Enough for a green-black film to wake in the pores. Enough for the frogs to risk one brief, glistening hour.

Then they would withdraw it before anything learned to depend on it.

They would not heal the old baboon's shortened fingers, or teach the young ones the angle of the wrist. That teaching is already happening, flake by dropped flake, and it is hers to give. They would not feed the crow, or mend its splayed foot. It has made a trade of its lameness and a philosophy of falling stones. They would not enlarge the swift-holes, widen the terraces, deepen the pockets. The slowness is the safety. A beakful a year is fast enough for a home that has lasted this long by never becoming conspicuous.

Mostly, they would keep watch in the way the place itself keeps everything: from the edge, in interval, without possession.

Years would pass and a visitor standing on the rim at noon would see exactly what visitors have always seen — an empty, blinding hollow, apparently lifeless, apparently in need of nothing — and would be exactly as wrong as visitors have always been. The swifts would be shutter-eyed in the cool dark of the wall. The shrimp would be waiting as eggs in the salt. The algae would be a stain no thicker than breath. The baboons would be somewhere up on the plateau, the old female's granddaughter already thickening at the nails.

And in the evening, when the light loosened and shadows became single again, the ring would stir all the way around, complete and unhurried, and rise on the warm breath of the empty center —

which the wisest power, out of love, had left empty.
