They would not arrive.

That is, they would arrive the way weather arrives on that peak — seen from far off, not approached by any road, not remembered afterward as a visitation. Anyone watching, if there had been anyone watching, would only have noticed that for an afternoon the wind did not find the ringing ridges, the heat shimmer over the black fins stilled, and the stars that never quite leave the noon sky were, for a little while, more attentive.

A benevolent power learns the first rule of a place before crossing it. This place is not on any approach. So they do not make one.

They do not come down through the dome as a fire, or a voice, or a hand big enough to pick up the little model city and set it, mercifully, in a green valley where it is needed. That temptation exists. It is the shallow kind of mercy, and wisdom is what remains after you have refused it.

Because they see what it is, at once and without needing to be told by the pictograms on the pylons.

Not a ruin. Not a prison. Not a trap with trams.

A larva.

A city folded into forty meters of hollow summit, rehearsing elevators for passengers who have not been born, outlining its own doors in the brown dusk so it will not forget its shape in the dark. Growing a tower. Folding it away. Growing it better. Keeping its water in a closed loop, its streets unworn, its air hot and thin and secret, until the day it can be unfolded somewhere low and real, and rained on, and dirtied, and loved at full scale.

To populate it now would be murder by kindness. To fix it now would be to freeze it at perfection, and perfection is only its chrysalis. To speak to it — in words, in thunder, in any language that would leave it forever after listening for a voice from above its dome — would teach it the wrong shape for a home. Homes do not answer gods. They answer kettles, and keys, and feet on stairs.

So the power does the hardest and most loving thing available.

They become, for exactly as long as is safe, an inhabitant.

They enter sideways, between the obsidian fins, careful of their gloves. They pass between the white ceramic pylons and feel their ears pop and the world change its mind about size. They do not marvel loudly. They walk at an ordinary pace, neither worshipful nor proprietary, down an avenue that is both knee-high and thirty stories tall, and they let the warm street take their weight.

It heals, as it heals for everyone, behind each step.

Almost.

The tram comes, because the interval has elapsed. They board it — once. Once is the rule, and even powers keep the rules of a house that has invited them only by not forbidding them. They sit where a passenger sits. They do not choose a destination aloud. When the residential blocks rise to the east, shuttered and patient, they get off, though a different stop, farther on, has already begun to suggest itself with that helpful, terrible confidence the place has when it starts modeling a life for you.

They do not let it finish.

That is the gift, you understand. Not arrival. Everyone the city imagines arrives, opens doors, rides up in elevators whose cabs brighten with implied weight. The routines are full of arrivals. What the rehearsal has never contained — what no timetable can teach itself — is a departure freely made and not regretted. Someone who came, was glad, was ordinary, and then left, not because the place failed, but because leaving is also what people do in cities. They go to work. They go home. They move away. They die and are carried out through doors that must learn to close after them and still open again the next morning.

The power teaches it by doing it, gently, in its own streets.

They stay through one rain. Only one.

When the dome vents sigh and the hot mineral breath of the basin meets the cold of the pylons and falls in that fine manufactured drizzle, they stand in the plaza where the world drops away into avenues under avenues, lights under lights, down into separations so vast that single towers hang there on threads of light. They let the rain bead on them. It runs, where the gravity is still imperfect, a little way uphill along their sleeve, seeking a seam. They do not drink it. They watch a droplet climb out of a pooled hollow instead of settling, and they understand about the water: that it wants to become structure, crystal, a fan clear as ice in a shadowed joint.

Near the wall, the little harvester discs come out, coin-sized and diligent, to shave the fans back.

The power kneels — a difficult, un-godlike posture, at two scales at once — and places, not a hand, but a shadow, over one rosette. Just one. Just for the length of the rain. The disc pauses. It considers. It goes around.

It is so small a mercy it could be mistaken for nothing. But the fan remains after the drizzle has evaporated off the avenues, and in the dimming amber it does what crystal does: it catches light that was never meant for it and breaks it, very faintly, into directions the city did not plan. For the first time, somewhere in the sensory grammar of streets and seams and cooling ceramic, there is a color the city did not rehearse.

Possibly, in a hundred years, that will be its first eye. Possibly it is only a weed spared once. A wise power does not need to know. Benevolence is not engineering. It is allowing something to become without insisting on becoming its author.

When the rain stops, they go.

They find the threshold street while it is still grown that hour. They pass between the sweating pylons. Their ears pop back into the thin black air of the summit. The city behind them closes a door across the plaza, changes a signal for no traffic, sends an empty tram sighing to its next courteous stop. It does not call after them. It has learned, perhaps, a fraction of a degree better how not to.

From outside, dosed to pocket-size again under smoked glass, it looks unchanged. Perfect. Toylike. Waiting.

It is not unchanged.

One scuff on one avenue does not heal until the next day. Not a wound — the power would never wound it — just a memory of having borne a real weight and not immediately smoothed it away. A city that will one day be lived in must eventually learn to keep a few marks. Real streets do that. They remember parades, and floods, and the corner where everyone always turns.

And one crystal fan, in one shadowed joint where the drips linger longest, grows back a little faster than the others after every rain, in a pattern that includes, forever, the interruption of that shadow.

That is all that happens here.

The rest of what they do, they do elsewhere, where it will not disturb the rehearsal.

They turn the mountain aside in the minds of surveyors. Not with terror, not with avalanches or guardian storms — cruelty is unnecessary and leaves legends, and legends are a kind of approach. Instead: a persistent clerical vagueness. Flight paths find reasons to bank a few degrees off. Satellites pass with a predictable, uninteresting glare on their lenses at just that orbit. Climbers, good ones, honest ones, reach the lower ridges and discover in themselves a sudden, sane conviction that the higher black fins are unstable, unrewarding, better photographed from below. They go home alive and do not quite recall deciding to. The maps keep being printed with the peak a fraction lower than it is, in the way maps sometimes prefer not to compete with heaven.

They give it time, which is the one resource a thing stored above the weather thinks it has in abundance, and never does. Mountains, even young ones, erode. Domes cloud. Routines wear their grooves deeper until practice becomes compulsion. So the power sets, far out beyond human calendars, a gentler circumstance waiting in the world below: not a command, not a prophecy, just a valley, someday, that will need exactly this — high ground, clean water discipline, streets that know how to fold away from flood and unfold again — and people, not perfect commuters, who will arrive arguing and tired and carrying too much, and ride the tram wrong the first week, and get lost, and plant illegal green things in the residential east in defiance of every edited rule, and wear the pristine avenues down at last into home.

Until then, they let it be empty.

They let it practice being peopled without mistaking practice for people. They let its doors open for no one, so that one day they can open for anyone.

On their way out of the world — if out is a direction that applies — they look back once from directly above, the only angle from which the secret is visible: a cup of brown dusk in a black peak, a miniature city breathing heat into a violet sky where the stars refuse to leave.

They do not bless it in words.

They leave it alone on purpose, which for a benevolent and wise power, seeing a living thing that is not yet ready to be met, is the most articulate blessing there is.
