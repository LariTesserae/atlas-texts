It would not come through the door.

A power that was truly benevolent, and truly wise, would know better than to arrive in that courtyard as a presence with weight. The ground is too new. It would sigh under anything that did not first learn how to step.

So it would come, if it came at all, the way the damp comes. From underneath. From the edges. In the hour before first light, when the bricks are lightening by a shade and the condensation is still beaded on the undersides of the fig leaves, it would settle itself into the twelve paces of shaded earth and do the hardest thing a power can do.

It would wait with them.

It would not finish the waist-high room on the north side. That would be the first temptation, and the cruelest kindness. The roof poles are cut, the sap is beading green, the kin have not yet arrived and the room is waiting for them. It could raise the walls in a night. It could dry the silt by morning, straighten the slight belly in that one forgiven course, lay the terracotta tiles true and level across a courtyard that has not yet settled.

It would do none of that.

Because it would see what they are actually building, and it is not the room. The room is only where the lesson becomes visible. What they are building is a way of living on ground that cannot be hurried — feet that put their weight down slowly and are ready to take it back, hands that press a brick again when it slumps instead of cursing it, eyes trained to a vine gaining a knuckle's length in an afternoon. If the walls went up without the waiting, the house would stand and the people would be homeless inside it.

So it would leave the wall waist-high. It would leave the belly in the other wall, slight and instructive. It would leave the spider to rebuild the web the swallows broke at dawn, and the dragonflies to patrol an empty depression as faithfully as a pool. To intervene there would not be help. It would be theft.

What it would do is work at the scale the place itself has taught it: a finger's width here, a hand's breadth there, in the things the inhabitants cannot yet reach with bare feet and fingertips.

Deep below the courtyard, where the old river still remembers being a river under the drained marsh, it would attend to the water. Not take it away — the seep in the low corner, the frogs no longer than a fingernail, the single reed, the elephant ear with its three enormous leaves, all of them are information, and the builders are right to listen. And not let it return all at once in the first heavy season, to unmake the new walls and prove that land was a lie. It would keep the balance they cannot yet keep: damp every morning, giving underfoot, but never rising. A dampness that teaches, not drowns.

By the east wall, where the young fig is already disproportionately alive and beginning to lift the nearest stepping stone, it would turn one root — one only — a fraction downward in the dark. Not stop it. The lifting is honest; they are putting down roots fast and shallow just like it, and the stone rocking underfoot reminds them of that kinship every day. But a root that only spreads will one day tear the courtyard apart with love. A root that also goes down will hold. The fig would never know it had been touched. It would simply, in ten years, be the tree that shades the whole east side and has not cracked the kitchen wall.

And outside — because a courtyard is never only defended from within — it would do the smallest possible work in the town that is still being twined out over packed earth. A lane that might have been marked to cut straight past the passage would curve, for no reason anyone could later name, a little to the south, so carts would not thunder by and shake the drying courses. The neighbor on the roof side, planning a higher house, would find himself content with a lower one, and the patch of sun would go on travelling from the west wall to the center near noon for another generation. Nothing compelled. Nothing broken. Just the world, nudged at its soft points, the way silt is nudged into a mold.

Above all, it would give them the one gift they cannot make with their hands and cannot ask for without ceasing to be who they are becoming:

Time at the right speed.

Not more time — that is a different and sadder gift, and usually a false one. Just time that is not snatched. A dry season that arrives slowly enough for them to learn what the damp corner wants to be in it. A wet season that does not test the unfinished walls before the course below has dried. Years in which no one with authority looks at the empty earth between the stones and sees waste, no one looks at the unpaved low corner and sees a floor unfinished. Years for the child who thinks a house grows a wall at a time to be right.

And on one night — perhaps only one — it might do something even smaller, and entirely for love rather than wisdom.

On a night when the building had gone badly, when a brick pressed too wet had slumped for the third time and a builder sat in the greenish dimness with silt under their nails and wondered, without words, whether they had mistaken stubbornness for patience, it would let the jars sweat a little cooler against their wrists. It would let the basil smell a little stronger as they passed. It would let sleep come, when it came, without the dream of leaning walls.

They would wake believing they had simply rested. That is correct. They had.

In the morning they would make their circuit barefoot, as always, feeling where the damp had risen and where the fig had pushed. The stone by the fig would still rock, but a fraction less. The pool would have shrunk a finger's width, no more. The wall would not have leaned in the night. The vine would have gained, along its string, exactly the length of a knuckle.

"It held," one of them would say, quietly, to no one in particular.

And the power, already withdrawing the way damp withdraws — from the surface first, leaving the dark earth underneath still cool — would let them believe the place had done it.

That, too, would be correct.

Because in the end it would understand what the courtyard had been trying to say to everyone who entered it barefoot: that to love an unfinished thing wisely is not to complete it, and not to leave it alone, but to guard the conditions under which it can complete itself — slowly, at body pace, one considered step at a time — and then to accept, with something very close to gratitude, that no footprint you leave in it should be permanent.
