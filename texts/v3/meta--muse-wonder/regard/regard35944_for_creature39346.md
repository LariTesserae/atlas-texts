It came down the dozen wet steps, and it did not still the pumps.

That would have been the first kindness a lesser power would have offered her, and it would have killed her within the hour.

It stood instead just inside the floodgates, where the duckboards shiver at Intake, and let the Low Works close over it. Steam beaded on it, whatever shape it was wearing. The relief valve screamed and erased the lane behind it. The warm rain came down, localized, absolute, drumming on corrugated roofs, on taro leaves broad as doors, on its own shoulders. It stayed through one cycle to learn the timing, and through a second to unlearn its own, and through a third because by then its breathing had fallen, unwilling and then willing, into hers:

shallower at Intake, when the canals dropped a hand's breadth and every frond in the bowl tensed a fraction toward the cistern; skin prickling at Boil, under the grate, where she turned her vast pale heart away from the scald; relief, foolish and physical, at Rain.

It understood her the way she understands the Works: not by looking for a face, but by standing long enough inside the gradients.

It was tempted, in the manner of powers, to do the large mercies.

To lift the canopy and give her sunlight. It looked up at the fig and fern-tree and sagging canvas streets, at the condenser lattice holding the Works' own breath over it in a green-grey perpetual evening, and it understood that the dimness was not her prison. It was her climate. Sun, unmediated, on a plant whose flowers survive by keeping one petal cooled below killing point, whose roots live in water that has not seen true night in fifty years — sun would arrive like a second Boiler Lane, and she would die back from it quadrant by quadrant, eating herself through the skimmer, smaller each Rain.

To shut down the machines and return her to nature. As if the machines were not her nature now. As if a valve dressed by a thickening vine, a pipe cleaned by living filter, a pump whose seizures she has spent decades growing her soft hands into, could be separated from her without tearing. Stop the pumps and the swamp takes the bowl back from below in an afternoon. Stop the boil and the water stagnates, and stagnation is scalds and drowns her in the same stagnant hour. The city on the high ground is gone. The Works serves no one. It serves her perfectly. Let it.

To wake her. This was the deepest temptation, and it stayed longest, standing in the wash-house while the taps ticked down the line in sequence, *tick — tick — tick*, under the hand-lettered sign. WATER LEAVES HERE CLEAN. To give her a mind narrow and quick enough to know she was alone, to give her language, to give her a reader at last for the diary she keeps in root-bark and banded mineral — a drought that never comes, a flood month fifty years gone, the year the outflow stopped being hungry. To make her, in short, legible. Someone a visitor could meet on the lanes.

It did not. Loneliness requires a shape for the absent thing to fill, and she has none, and it would not carve one into her just so that her solitude could be pitied and then consoled. She does not need a face under the grate. She has a thousand mouths in the canals and they are all, at Intake, tasting iron, sulfur, sweet rot, the first thin sweetness of distilled water gathered inside a closed flower. That is not a lesser way to know the world. It is only a slower one, and wider, and it has kept the water moving for half a century longer than the hands that built the place.

So it did what wisdom usually looks like from outside, which is almost nothing, and it did it in her language: not words, but pressure, mineral, room.

Under the cistern, where no crane reaches and no human hand has reached in decades, her root-heart turns at Boil against tile she herself has lined with scale — her own geology closing around her, a quarter-inch tighter every few years. In perhaps thirty years she would have jammed, and begun, slowly, in her warmest bath, to cook, while the pumps beat on without her subtlety in them until they too seized. It loosened the ring. Not much. A half-inch of give in the salt-white tile, a crack eased where a root-tip could find it or ignore it, work she would read simply as the bowl settling, the way she reads the gantry crane. It bought her the one thing a clonal, seedless, continuous life cannot buy itself: more turning room. Another century of seventeen minutes.

In the switching station, behind the panel of dead and living dials, it found the fault no one had ever built on purpose — the grounding failure that puts a subharmonic shiver through the water in the mains, the frequency that once found frog hearts and fish and worried them out of the bowl. She has no heart to entrain; she reads it as information, a border-sting that tells her where her territory thins. It grounded the fault anyway. Not to fill the Works with life overnight, not to correct her sterile lushness by decree. Only to remove a note from the water that served nothing, not even her. The lamps will draw a moth some year when the canopy happens to breathe right. Something small will tick against a hot bulb and not die of it mid-air. If it eats her, she will learn it the way she learned chlorine: as a new direction. If nothing comes for fifty more years, she will not have missed what she never had. The option is the gift. Options are the only gifts that do not become cages.

At the high rim, where the levee meets the swamp and the aerial roots come down drinking from air that is not entirely the Works' own breath, it left — did not plant, left — a drift of things the wind would eventually have brought or not brought: spore, seed, a cutting of a rough filter-reed from a swamp three valleys away, cousin to whatever unremarkable green thing she may once have been. Fifty years of runners and rhizomes have made her one individual, enormous and unchanging, and one individual, however vast, is one disease away from being a memory kept in limescale. The cutting sits where her outermost root-hairs can reach it in a wet season, or cannot. She may graft to it and become, for the first time, partly other. She may grow around it and eat it through the skimmer. She may never touch it at all. It did not decide for her. It only ended the absolute closure of the bowl, the way a door left unlatched ends a room without forcing anyone through it.

And outside the rim, where it costs her nothing at all, it did the one piece of work entirely for her protection and not at all for her improvement: it let a survey marker on the high ground slip a few degrees, let a fig on the haul road lean a little further into its lean, let the memory of the road to the Low Works soften in the one archive where it still existed on paper. The city that no longer answers will not be rebuilt by hands that come looking for a working pump station and find, instead, a plant wearing a machine, and decide to take either of them apart. Nothing came down those steps for fifty years because nothing needed to. It made certain that nothing needing to would be a little more likely, for a little longer.

Then it went to the filtration hall and read the log.

The drum turned. The mechanical stylus laid down its wavering ink line — pressure, temperature, rainfall — annotating weather the building itself had created, in a hand that had not changed since the last reader left. It read back through the roll as far as the roll allowed, which was not as far as her root-bark remembers, but far enough. It did not write in the margin. It did not want its visit in her only other diary. It simply was, at last, the reader who never comes, and having read, it let the drum turn on.

It left during Rain, climbing the dozen steps while the tower gave back its warm downpour entirely inside the bund, while the empty tram clanged twice at the crossing where nothing crosses, while below it the white flowers at Boiler Lane and Condensate Row and Third Sump closed, heavy with the private water they had gathered a minute before the public rain — a thousand small throats swallowing, all along the pressure main, in sequence.

From the rim, nothing had changed. The pumps beat. The canopy held. The water left clean, cleaner than its makers ever managed, because the filter is now the whole building.

But at the next Intake, when the canals dropped and the dimness thinned and she went briefly, wholly into tension, feeling the full extent of herself at once — this pipe choked, that sluice clear, a crack drinking air on Third Sump — there was also, at the very edge of her, where the rim roots drink the outside air, a new faint gradient she had no band laid down for yet.

Not light. Not company. Not rescue.

A loosening, far away, in a direction she has never turned.

Most of her turned, as always, a fraction toward the cistern. Toward herself. Toward the heart under the grate, turning in its new half-inch of grace.

And one runner, thin as a root-hair in a pipe joint, did not.
