If she found them — and finding is the right word, for first there would only be the sound, the fall on the north rim, frogsong, a bell-note coming up through water that does not let sound go — she would not go down at once.

A wise power learns to wait at the rim.

From above, there is almost nothing to save. Dark water. Lily pads with upturned rims. A few reed chimneys smoking sideways in the wind. It would be easy, being benevolent, being hasty, to mistake this for poverty, for a drowning. Easy to drain the lake a little. To give them back their dry market, their stone kitchens. To cut a channel in the caldera lip and let them walk upright again in the sun.

She would put her face under the water first. And she would understand.

This town was not ruined by the lake. It was negotiated with, fingertip by fingertip, generation by generation. The houses are alive because they were never rescued from growing. The people breathe because the town breathes. To lower the water now, even in kindness, would be to hang every wall in the air to die roots-first. To make them fully water-breathers would be just as violent — it would end the bells, the heads together in warm plant-smelling air, the loud evening talk, the babies born with one breath in each world. They are not a failed version of something else. They are the precise, unlikely answer to this place.

So she would not give them gills. She would not give them years. She would not clear the outlet forever, or make the horsetail grow fast, or teach the trout to come when called. Speed is not a gift here. It is the one weather they have never learned to survive.

What she can give them is what they cannot see from inside the caldera: the outside of their own fragility.

She would walk, once, very slowly, around the watershed. Up past the snow patches that never quite melt, that feed the fall in August when every other high tarn goes to mud. She would put her hand to those and — not make more snow, not hold back the warming years by force — coax the stone beneath them colder, deeper-shaded, so melt comes later and steadier. A thumb's width, in a lifetime. Nothing they would name a miracle. Only that in the dry year, and the dry year after that, the fall does not fail.

She would find the slope above the inlet where the black rock is loosening, winter by winter, toward a fall that would not kill anyone — that is not how this town dies — but would choke the inflow with rubble and silt the terraces and cloud the clear streets for a decade. She would not hold the mountain up. She would encourage one stone, the key stone, to settle more truly into its socket. The rest will come down someday, in pieces, at a rate the sluice-keepers can clear by hand in the mornings. As they expect the world to come.

Down in the lake, moving the way a shadow of cloud moves, she would look for the slow dangers, the ones too gradual even for a patient people to notice: a sameness creeping into the seed-rice of the terraces, saved from the same good heads too many years running; a blight sleeping in the rhizomes of the oldest houses, house after related house grown from cuttings of cuttings. She would not heal it. She would stir the bottom of the old deep rooms where their dead are laid into the roots, and nudge a drifting, wild strain from the inlet reeds to cross, one summer, with the tame. A little strange pollen on a sluice-keeper's wet forearm, carried house to house. No one will know. In twenty years a wall will grow with a tougher skin. They will say that child was lucky in her house, and begin one like it for a grandchild.

And people — she would watch the people most carefully of all, and do the least.

There are not very many of them. There never can be; the lake will hold what it will hold. She knows what a closed valley does over ten generations, however barrel-chested, however bright-eyed underwater. But she will not send them a stranger in a boat. That story has ended badly in every lake and island ever sung. Instead, once in a while — once in more than a lifetime — a hill shepherd will lose his way in fog on the caldera in late summer, and come down thirsty to a shore that is not on his way home, and hear drumming under the water. He will be received, because they are not unkind, in the market bell over the drowned market circle, head to head with them in the din, given water-chestnuts and raw mussels and laughed at for the face he makes. Most such men go home again.

Some don't. Some, years later, do not go home the second time. It will be their own choosing, and the fog will only have been fog.

What she will do, reliably, is keep the way in hard to find. Not sealed — sealing is a kind of cutting — but unmemorable. The path over the rim will keep slipping a little where it slipped last year. The lake will keep looking, from above, like a place with nothing in it but pads and frogs. Travellers with instruments, with nets, with plans for the water, will find the climb longer than they expected, the evening coming on, a heron standing in the shallows looking at them with an elder's complete lack of alarm, and most will turn back of their own sensible accord. Those who persist in good faith can be endured. Those who persist to drain, to dam, to take — they will simply, persistently, be unlucky in small, plausible ways, until their luck becomes their decision to leave.

Once, perhaps, she would let herself be almost seen.

Late on a still evening, when the bells are full, heads in golden circles, bodies trailing into green shadow, a child too young for the sluice but old enough to pearl-dive after shells on the market stones would surface into her family's bell and say there was someone walking the old causeway, not swimming, not weighted, the bubbles going *through* her. The grandmother, green along the forearms from the lower terraces, would not correct her. She would shell another water-chestnut into the water for the fish and say that the town has old rooms, and some of them are occupied, and it is polite not to point.

That night the drumming would go on a little longer than usual. Not in worship. In acknowledgement, which here is the same as hospitality: another being in the water, keeping to the paths, not cutting anything.

And by morning there would be nothing changed that anyone could prove, except that the sluice was clear, the fall ran white and steady over the north rim, a new leaf had furled itself — a week early, but who counts a week in a house that takes twenty years? — over the sleeping nook of the newest child, and the herons had all, for no reason anyone could name, chosen to stand along the inlet side, exactly where the bank would want watching after the next storm.

She would leave them wet, cold, slow, loud in their bells, mortal, leech-bitten, beloved of ducks and trout. She would leave them exactly themselves.

Only watched over, at the edges they cannot watch, by someone who has finally learned that the deepest benevolence is not to life them out of the lake.

It is to make sure the lake, and they, can go on refusing to be separated — a fingertip, a thumb's width, a lifetime at a time.
