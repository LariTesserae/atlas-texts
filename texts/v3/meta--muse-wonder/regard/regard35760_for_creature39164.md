It would arrive without announcing itself.

That would be the first sign of its wisdom, and also of its benevolence. A louder arrival — a voice from the rim, a light over the plaza, a wonder in the cisterns — would make a dam in the life of the town. Within a generation people would be listening for it instead of listening to their channels. They would begin, for the first time, to wait.

And it would not want them waiting. It would stand for a long while at the edge of the plateau, where the waterfalls fall straight and the rock has not yet softened, and it would do what no merely powerful thing finds easy to do.

It would look carefully enough to be tempted, and then carefully enough not to act on the temptation.

The temptations would be obvious, because from outside the absences are obvious.

It could bring bees.

It could fill the warm mornings with a low, working hum, lift the feather-brushes out of the children's hands, let fruit set itself in abundance beyond what any household had deliberately chosen. It could bring birds next, to eat what the bees had multiplied, and fill the roofs with song. It could bring small burrowing things to turn the wet soil, fish to clear the cisterns, beasts with patient shoulders to stand between the cart-shafts where pairs of neighbors now lean together in fiber harness. It could bring milk, wool, leather, horn, eggs, honey, manure — whole rooms of substance the town has never had.

It could heal the slow closing of the lungs that takes the old. It could enrich the diet, strengthen the children, make the grain stand thicker, make the springs proof against any change in the rock.

Each gift, considered alone, would look like mercy.

But a wise power does not consider gifts alone. It considers them downstream.

Bees would not merely pollinate. They would take the deliberate setting of fruit out of human hands, and with it a season of common work by which children learn the rows, the households learn their intentions, and the town decides, flower by flower, how much it means to eat. Abundance would arrive unasked, and then hunger of a new kind after it: pressure, waste, storage, guarding, more people than the terraces had agreed to feed, more gardens than the channels had agreed to water.

Birds would not merely sing. They would hunger, nest, scatter seed, die in cisterns, bring a beauty that reorganizes love and a wildness that reorganizes fear. Burden-beasts would not merely pull. They would need fodder cut from human plots, pens on ground now given to water, care in sickness, breeding in season, slaughter or grief at the end. For the first time there would be lives in town whose good was not the town's good, and whose suffering people had created by keeping them and could relieve only by use or killing. New tools would follow. New appetites. New words. New cruelties, at first bewildered and well-meant, then customary.

In a dry place, perhaps that price would be survival. Here it is only change dressed as improvement.

The power would see that the town is not incomplete because it lacks animals. It is complete in the way its fountain is complete: because inflow and outflow have agreed over a long time, at cost, down to the grooves cut in steep paths and the resin rubbed into skin and the marriages arranged partly by spillway. You cannot pour a new species into such a basin and call only the added water good. The level is the good.

So it would not give them animals. It would not give them health that outruns their food, or food that outruns their water, or knowledge of the low country that would make this high, warm, wet town seem poor in their own eyes. It would not make itself an upstream god whose guttering they must forever maintain with prayer.

It would learn their rule from them, and obey it: widen, do not dam.

There is, deep under the plateau, a place where the new rock is still remembering its uplift. Stresses move there along a joint no mason can inspect, no ear at an eave can hear blockage gathering. Left entirely alone, in some grandchild's lifetime or some later lifetime still, it might one day slip all at once and do in a single night what all their wise widening could not answer: cut half the springs sideways into a lower country, turn balance into emergency, stewardship into desperate invention.

Against slow change, the town needs no rescue. Slow change is the element they are made for. Sudden change is another matter.

So there, and only there, the power would put its hand — not to stop the mountain from ever softening, not to freeze the springs at their present rate forever, not to make the town eternal. It would ease the locked place by almost nothing. It would spread one night's possible violence across two hundred years of creep, so that springs, when they alter, alter at the speed of a channel silting: noticeable to old people, arguable at noon in a courtyard, answerable by moving a garden, raising a platform, marrying uphill, digging no deeper than a wooden spade can go. It would turn catastrophe back into weather.

No one would hear it do this. No stone would ring. The aqueduct would tick on under its eaves of moss. The fountain would neither rise nor fall.

Afterward, it would remain a while longer — not as a ruler, not even as a guest, since a guest must be fed and housed and explained — in the posture of a good upstream neighbor whose existence the people below never need to know about. It would keep less careful powers from blundering up the rim road with their songs and beasts and cures. It would keep the place from being discovered as an example, a warning, a garden, a cure for other people's loneliness. It would let the town's quiet stay unremarkable to the town.

And then it would go away without taking so much as gratitude, which would be only another new crop requiring tending.

If there is any trace at all, it will be negative and therefore perfect: a disaster that never quite happens in one generation; a spring that fails so gradually that great-grandchildren disagree about whether it has changed; children still going down the crop rows with plant-down brushes in the still weeks, touching flower to flower, bored perhaps, competent certainly, unaware that the absence in their hands was ever seen from outside as a lack to be pitied.

On the plaza at noon the sandals will go on crossing the damp pale stone. The water will go on finding its level.

The power, having loved the place, will have honored it in the hardest way available to power.

It will have left it finished.
