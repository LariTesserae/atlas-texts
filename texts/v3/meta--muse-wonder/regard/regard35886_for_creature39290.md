It would not descend in light.

No light is needed here, and extra light would only make the shadows colder by contrast. It would come, if it came at all, as a guest comes: on foot, in the late afternoon, with empty hands, and it would ask — in a normal voice, not raised across the square — if there was room.

Room, here, is a number of cups a day. The power would know that, and respect it. It would let itself be counted. It would drink what was offered and no more, wash in a basin and see the basin carried to the garden, sleep under a roof whose catchment it had silently, appreciatively, calculated. Sixty or seventy roofs. Two hundred and fifty people. So much dew off the north slabs in a dry month. So much held. So much used. It would lie still at dawn and listen to the dripping, and judge the week, and find the judgment good.

It would not bring a spring.

That is the first temptation of benevolence, and wisdom is mostly the refusal of it. A spring on the saddle would not be a gift here; it would be a rewriting. A spring means a new house can be raised, and then another. It means marriages no longer wait for a widow to move in with her sister. It means trees in the town proper, and shade where beans were grown for the sun, and roots in the cistern walls, and more grain to be brought up from below, and a road made wider for bringing it, and the road bringing other things besides grain. It means the gaps between the houses filling, because empty stone, with water underneath it, begins to look like waste. Within three generations the town would be larger, richer, louder — and the knowledge that holds it together, roof by roof, cup by cup, would be a quaint old arithmetic nobody needed anymore.

And then, one dry year, when the spring ran lower than springs in stories are supposed to run, they would discover that they had forgotten how to live on dew.

A wise power does not give a people more than their wisdom is built to hold. It does not confuse abundance with mercy.

So it would walk the lanes instead. Seven minutes end to end, much longer if it stopped, which it would. It would stand in the square where the flagstones are worn concave. It would put its hand, if it had a hand, into the cold damp air of the cistern-house and feel the true center of the town against its skin. It would watch the lizards take the noon walls and vacate them at a shadow. It would note the two pines by the lower gate, bent all one way, and not straighten them.

There is very little here that is broken. That is rarer, among the worlds a power might attend to, than suffering. Suffering is everywhere and asks obvious things. This place asks the nearly impossible thing: to be seen completely, appreciated completely, and left essentially alone.

Nearly alone. Not quite.

Because balance is not the same as safety. The town can hold itself against everything it was built to expect — lean weeks, a failed rain, a hard frost, a house-line ending. It cannot hold itself against being *noticed*. One road surveyor with an eye for a pass. One valley lord counting roofs for a tax. One well-meaning story carried down about a pure, quiet people in the high stone who want nothing — and wanting nothing is, to certain kinds of minds, an invitation. Pilgrims drink more than guests. Admirers arrive with gifts that must be stored, questions that must be answered, a wish to be taught the secret, as if proportion were a secret rather than a practice renewed each morning before the sun is up.

Against that, the power would act. Quietly, in the town's own manner: a repair made to be invisible.

It would let the pass slip, very slightly, in the memory of the valley. Not erased — erasure leaves a shaped hole, and shaped holes attract attention. Just softened, the way a corner of wall is rounded and then stays that way. The road, when it is finally built, would go by the other valley, where there is a river, where building makes sense. The tax-collector's list would add up satisfyingly without a cold saddle of sixty roofs at the end of a climb his mules dislike. The traveler's tale would come home as a tale of some empty stone, some pines, nothing much up there, not worth the water it takes to reach. No curse. No wall of mist. People would remain perfectly free to come. They would simply, most of them, think of somewhere else first.

Inside the walls, it would do even less.

Perhaps, in the night, it would clear one channel that everyone believed someone else had cleared. Perhaps it would reset a single dew-slab by the width of a thumb — not to a new angle, to its *old* angle, the one a grandfather set and a winter's frost had since argued with, grain by grain. Perhaps it would sit a while with the oldest woman, the one who knows which beam only looks like oak and which cistern cracked two generations back, and listen to the whole archive without once suggesting it be written down. Writing it down would change it. Listening would not. And if fire, or sickness, or a fall of rock — the calamities that are not part of any proportion, that no amount of good maintenance can price in — if one of those ever came to the saddle, then there would be one memory besides hers that held the true map of the town, and could give it back, house by house, slate by same-thickness slate.

That is the covenant it would make, and it would not announce it. An announced covenant becomes a shrine. A shrine needs a keeper. A keeper needs a house, and the house needs a roof, and the roof— the arithmetic fails. So it would make the covenant the way the town makes everything: by doing the maintenance and not speaking of it.

And on the last morning it would leave before the light was fully up, while the roofs were still beading and the barrels still ticking. It would take its counted cups out of the town's stores — in its own departure, which is the politest thing a guest can do here. It would not leave the cisterns fuller than the night had made them. It would not leave them emptier. It would close the lower gate exactly as it found it, between the pines.

The people would notice, in the days that followed, almost nothing. That a hinge swung easier. That a channel ran clear at first rain without anyone quite remembering the clearing of it. That the slab behind the cistern-house gathered, perhaps, a little more faithfully — though on reflection, no, it had always been the best slab; the man whose grandfather set it would say so, and be right.

Someone in the bakehouse queue might mention the guest. Quiet, they'd say. Knew how to stand in the warm part of a lane. Didn't ask for anything extra. Dry, brief laughter. Then the bread, dense and counted, handed over. Then work.

And high and held in place, under a normal, clear sun, the town would go on being itself exactly one more day — which is all it has ever asked of heaven, and all that heaven, being wise, would finally have the discipline to give.
