If they are truly benevolent, and truly wise, the first thing they will do is not arrive.

They will come up the ridge the slow way, stone to stone, and feel in their own footing why the rules are what they are. They will not flatten the scree for an easier path. They will not dull the quartzite, or widen the way, or put up a sign to say that anything remarkable is ahead. Signage is a kind of shouting. It brings the wrong kind of feet.

At the dip they will stop.

A lesser power would descend full of plans. It would see the thin water and think: *deeper pans*. It would see the nightly freeze and think: *shelter*. It would see the frog, thumb-sized, letting the ice take the water in her own skin, and it would want — oh, with the best and most ruinous intentions — to warm her.

It would lengthen the summer of noon by an hour. It would keep the pans from skinning over quite so hard. It would make the moss softer, the season kinder, the caracara less patient.

And in ten years there would be no place left. Only a pleasant wet garden where a hard, precise, necessary thing used to live. The rosettes bloom because the cold prunes them. The cushions hold water because they grow almost too slowly to live. The tadpoles race because the ice is coming. The frog survives the night not in spite of surrendering almost everything, but *by* it. To take away the freeze would be to take away the shape of her heart.

So the wise power does not improve the bowl.

It learns it.

It learns which pan holds longest after cloudless days. It learns which cushions are hollow and which are solid to the core. It learns the herder's careful step and does not mistake it for ownership, and the caracara's stillness and does not mistake it for cruelty. It learns to tell the difference — the difference everything here depends on — between loneliness and privacy.

Then, having learned, it does its work almost entirely elsewhere.

Down-mountain, where work can be done without breaking anything. In the valleys where trails are decided. It lets another pass look easier on the map. It lets the lower grazing seem, that year, a little better. It lets the road crew's eye slide past this ridge toward a more promising one. When boots come up anyway — because some always will, the herder, the wanderer, the one who heard something once — it attends to the soles of those boots long before the ridge: the mud of other valleys worked free, the foreign seed shaken loose, the invisible fungus that rides from warm wet places and could empty every pan here in a single season of silence. That it stops. Quietly, at a distance, the way you would cup a hand around a candle without touching the flame.

It attends to the weather not by commanding it but by guarding the conditions that make it. The cloud that pours over the western rim at two is born far away, over green valleys and warm earth rising. The wise power keeps faith with those lower places too, so the cloud still comes, still snags on the cold rock, still gives up its breath of water — never much, and never all at once, because that measure is the measure of the bowl.

Inside the rim, it touches almost nothing.

Almost.

On the afternoon when the cloud tears early and a boot has gone, carelessly, through fifty years of cushion — when cold black water is running out of a wound that will not heal in one lifetime — it does not grow the cushion back. That would be a lie told in green. It holds the water there a little longer while the torn edges settle. No more than a wet week would do, in a good year. Indistinguishable from luck.

In a dry spell that runs longer than dry spells should, when the pan under the great rosette is shrinking to a black mirror and the tadpoles are still all tail and hunger, it does not fill the pan. It lets the cloud linger ten minutes more over the rim before the wind reasserts. Ten minutes. The sort of mercy the place can absorb without learning to depend on miracles.

And when the freeze comes down one night harder than the heart under the ice expected — it does not thaw her.

It waits with her.

This is the hardest part of benevolence, and most powers fail at it. To watch the frost feather visibly across the moss. To watch the pan tick shut. To know the exact slowed beat — once, then nothing, then, in its own good time, once again — and to keep your hands, your warmth, your enormous pity entirely to yourself, because intervening would save tonight and cost the ten thousand nights that made her.

Instead its attention becomes a kind of shelter that changes nothing physical at all. The bowl has always rearranged itself inside boundaries that do not move. Rim. Lip. Wall. The sudden drop. The power joins itself to the boundaries. It becomes one more thing that holds, so that everything inside can go on freezing and thawing, blooming and closing, filling with cloud and emptying into light, unwitnessed by anything that wants to keep it.

It leaves no offering in the cairn. The cairn already has a feather, a braid of red wool, things carried up by hands that knew to carry them back down in spirit. It adds no stone. Adding a stone would be signing the work.

If it speaks at all, it does so after the caracara has gone, at the hour the frogs speak, and only in the grammar the place allows.

*Tok.*

One note. Not a chorus. Not a rescue. An acknowledgment, across twenty paces of deep moss, of where the water is this week.

And from the black pan, after a long pause — not because she knows, not because she needs to know — an answer.

*Tok.*

That is all it does.

That is not nothing.
