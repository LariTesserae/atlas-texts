It would not come down out of the sky.

That is the first act of wisdom, and to almost everyone on the plateau it would look like nothing happening at all.

A power that is merely benevolent might arrive with rain. It could look at the white mornings, the leaf-cups, the children's hair combed wet for drinking-water, and think: *here is the wound, let me fill it.* It could set a river walking across the Floor. It could open the stomata pits and make them wells that never closed. It could soften the noon heat, quiet the roar so that no child need ever shout to be heard by her mother six feet away, give the old back the frog-register, the bird-register, all the bright upper floors of the world they have been politely evicted from, one frequency at a time.

A wise power would stand at the top of the switchback stair, with the lowlands hazed behind it, and not do any of that.

Because it would hear, first, what the place is actually saying. Which is not: *we are failing.* The plateau is not a mistake waiting for correction. The scarcity of water is not an accident that befell it; it is the architect of everything in it — the swollen trunks, the folded leaves, the herd migrations, the dawn industry of skins and cloths, the well-rings hauled up stone by stone over centuries, the body itself, lean in the limbs and stored at the middle. Take away the thirst and you do not get the same world made comfortable. You get another world, and the first one drowned.

The roar is not noise pollution. It is roads, sex, weather reports, maps, lullabies, law. Silence the cicadas and the frogs go unanswered, the frequency-tuned flowers do not open, the cats go blind in the only sense that matters, the people lose the deafening privacy in which lovers bellow endearments and all the real language migrates into the hands. Cure the patterned deafness of age and you have not returned the elders to the camp. You have removed them from the buttress root where they sit, filtered at last to the lowest register, hearing the tree rather than its tenants, able to say *move east tomorrow, this one is finishing* — because someone in every generation must be able to hear only the deep things, and the price of that office is the frogs.

Benevolence without that understanding is just conquest with better manners. Every foreign axeman believed he was improving the view.

So the power would learn the etiquette.

It is, after all, already written, older than any current language: *take what is shed, given, dead-and-dry, or grown for taking. Do not wound the Floor. Do not axe a Pillar.*

It would take it literally, and more seriously than the axemen ever took their own laws. It would not land. It would walk, if walking is the word, the way the herds walk at dawn — low, in the mist rivers, feeling through whatever serves it for soles for the hollow note and the full note, for last month's rain held green and sour in the sponge below. It would learn the place the way everything there learns it: by foot, by ear, over time. It would not hurry. The Pillars think in centuries and the Floor thinks in longer units than that, in root-graft and slow chemical conversation, and a visitor who cannot match that tempo is not listening, only inspecting.

Within the plateau, it would do almost nothing.

Almost.

It would sit, once, beside an elder whose hearing has narrowed to the infrasonic and the thunder, both of them with their backs to a buttress fin, doing nothing for an afternoon while the full roar spends itself above them. It would not speak into her silence. It would not restore her. It would accompany her in the register she has left, which is also, not coincidentally, the register in which the Floor itself can be addressed. If any conversation happened — between a visiting power and a continent-sized root-mat that has been one being longer than the cliffs have held their shape — it would happen down there, below the frogs, below the birds, in the deep hum through resonant chamber and grafted root, and it would not be in words. It would be two large attentions discovering each other. *You are heard* is not an offer of help. It is older and rarer than help.

When the great cessation came — midday, midnight, ten miles holding its breath at once, the Pillar-hum cut like a string — it would not fill the quiet. It would not mistake the held breath for a request. It would do what the people do, climber frozen on bark, child signed gently still against a breast: nothing, completely, with its whole attention. And in that minute or two, when you can hear sap ticking in the ground and blood in your own ears, the Floor would register, perhaps, one thing that was not there before. Not a voice. A listener. An extra weight of regard in the silence, like a hand set flat on a table by someone who has decided not to drum their fingers.

That is, inside the boundary, nearly all. No new tools. The green metal blade goes on being passed down six generations, resharpened on nothing, treasured out of all proportion to its edge — as it should be, since it is ancestry that happens to cut, and no gift of better steel could fail to be an insult to the dead who kept it. No granaries against the year the wave fails; the people have already survived that year, and made a story out of it, and made a sign for the sound the herds made standing in the mist, and a people that can make a sign for an unprecedented thing is not fragile in the way benefactors hope. No medicine for the storied dead laid at the pit's lip with a skin of water and a handful of seed-heads. By morning the pit has done what it does. The gesture for it is the gesture for returning a borrowed cup. A benevolent power does not refill a cup that was offered, emptied, and withdrawn in gratitude. It receives it.

What it would do, it would do at the edges, where the harm does not belong to the system.

For the plateau has one true vulnerability, and it is not thirst, heat, hunger, cats, or silence. It is that the world below does not keep the etiquette. The axemen came once. They will come again in other costumes, with other reasons — survey, salvage, medicine in the bark, the gene in the resin, the view — and each of them sincere, and each of them certain that one Pillar, one mile of Floor, one spiral cut a little wider will not matter. A mile-wide black spiral near the southern rim, where nothing has rooted since, is what *one* looks like.

Against that, restraint ends. Consent is the whole question, and the lowlands have never asked.

So the power would become, invisibly and without announcement, a peculiarity of the lower world. Maps of the rim country would keep being almost right. The switchback stair would remain maintained and somehow never quite findable by expeditions with equipment. A survey party would have an excellent season, take careful readings of everything except a location, and come home with a mild, unshakable conviction that the ascent was further east. Fevers that the plateau peoples have no history with, carried up in lowland lungs and lowland insects, would spend themselves in the foothills like weather that could not make the altitude. When the great lowland climate begins to lean on the plateau from outside — heat that is not the Floor's own heat, dryness that is not the dry-bright season but a theft of the mist itself — the power would lean back, not to hold the world still, but to give back what was taken: the baseline, the ordinary conditions under which this place invented itself. Not a garden kept. A theft refused.

And the scar? The black spiral, a mile wide, five hundred years unrooted, kept in story the way other cultures keep an eclipse?

It would ask.

That is perhaps the whole difference between a god and a gardener: a gardener would heal it on sight, tidy the lesson away. The power would go down into the low register and put the question to the being who was wounded, in the slow root-language, over however long the question takes — years, a generation of people, the time between two flowerings of a crown. *Do you want this closed?* And it would abide by the answer, even if the answer is the one the stories already suggest: that the Floor keeps the scar the way the fallen trunks keep the handprints, a child's hand set inside an ancestor's so the fingers touch — not because it cannot heal, but because the district remembers, in its body, the season it went quiet, and the memory is load-bearing. If the answer ever changes, the healing would happen just as slowly, from the edges in, root-tip by root-tip, no faster than forgiveness usually goes.

In the end — and the end would be indistinguishable, to any survey, from the beginning — it would leave the way the etiquette requires everything to leave: taking nothing that was not shed or given, leaving nothing that was not asked for.

Except, perhaps, one thing, offered in the only register where an offering could be understood.

Generations later, long after the camp at the foot of the Pillar has moved west with the wave a hundred times, an elder sits with her back to the buttress, filtered down to the last band of the world, and hears the deep hum come up through sixty feet of trunk and into her spine — and within it, very low, very steady, a note that was not in her grandmother's telling of the tree. Not louder. Not a message. Just a second thread in the hum, the way a chord is a promise that no single note is sounding alone. She signs to the camp, without emphasis, something for which there is already a sign, evolved over ten thousand years of cups and leaf-cups and borrowed things gladly returned:

Flattened hand, offered.

Withdrawn empty.

*It is listening back.*
