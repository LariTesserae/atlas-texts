It would not descend through the third ceiling.

That is the first thing it would learn, and it would take it a long time to learn it properly — not years, as lowlanders count years, but the slower unit of this place: one whole light-gap, opening and closing. Forty years of watching a canopy tree fall at the speed of rot, rise into sun-plants and butterflies and brief bright birds, and be shaded out again. By the end of it, a benevolent power would understand that here, anything arriving all at once is a kind of violence, however kind its intentions.

Light arrives all at once in the lowlands. So does sound. So does dryness, and instruction, and rescue.

It would arrive, therefore, the way water arrives here: not falling, materializing. A slight thickening of attention in the warm air between canopies. A presence at twenty paces that is not quite a body. At first the families would know it only as they know each other at a distance — a softening of the drips, an iron-and-resin smell closer than usual, a faint warmth underfoot where no body has been laid in the peat. The frogs would not go silent for it. That would be how it knew it was beginning to be right.

It would not give them anything.

This would be the hardest restraint, and the truest sign of its benevolence. A foolish kindness would look at their soft, unfired food and offer fire. It would look at their splayed, feeling feet and offer paths. At their dim, lid-swept eyes and offer clearer sight; at children carried for years against the chest and offer safety from the ground; at the old becoming landmarks in root houses and offer — most seductive of all — to be remembered more permanently than moss remembers.

But fire would need dryness, and dryness would need clearing, and clearing would need edges, and edges would need defending, and in a generation the sponge that holds the world together would have a wound in it that could not close slowly enough. A path would tell water where to run decisively, and water that runs decisively on a plateau held together by living rootwood is a river learning to cut. Better sight would be a dazzle. Better memory would be a weight. They have already solved the problem of permanence the only way this altitude allows: by letting wood become ghost house, house become pool, person become warmth, warmth become a species of moss that means *someone is crossing home ground*, and letting each form keep the shape of the last a little while before it subsides.

A wise power does not interrupt a grammar it has only just learned to read.

So inside the plateau, it would do almost nothing. It would learn the gradient language without ever speaking it back in a whisper — more tannin, less iron; warmer underfoot; the moss that prickles; the moss that doesn't — and it would let itself be navigated by those who were born reading it. If it walked at all, it would walk behind, not ahead. No one clears a way for the benevolent on a surface that is mostly the bodies of everything that has ever lived. You place your foot where the roots have already agreed to bear weight, and you wait for the ground to rise back.

Once, perhaps, in a hundred and twenty years, it might act, and even then only at the scale the place itself uses. Not to stop an acre sighing down a hand's breadth in the night — that is the plateau digesting its past, and to prevent it would be to prevent the pools from rearranging, the roots from stitching, the grandchildren from feeling that particular softness underfoot. Not to stop a birth in black water, or a death in a root chamber, or a family leaving a ghost house whose doorway will thicken shut without regret.

But if a buried layer were to collapse too fast — a true hollow forming under a sleeping house, a drowning, not a settling — it might press, very gently, from underneath, the way peat itself presses. Spread the sigh over three nights instead of one. Give the sleepers time to shift in their pile along the moss wall, annoyed, half-waking, moving by sole and smell to firmer ground without ever knowing they had been moved. In the morning they would find the pool a little nearer the door than yesterday. They would adjust. The power would already have become part of yesterday.

If a lowlander child — though lowlander children do not come — somehow wandered up past the weeping rim, footsore on hard country suddenly gone soft, eyes streaming in the directionless light, it would not enlighten him. It would let the indoor cloud thicken around him until his own breathing sounded too loud, and it would put into his path, gradient by gradient, the one reed that means seepage coming up clean, and let him drink, and then it would turn him, by nothing more dramatic than a persistent coolness on his left cheek and a faint reluctance of the ground on his right, back toward the rim. He would go home with a story about a green place that took his footprints and gave them back slowly. He would not be able to find it again. That is not cruelty. It is translation.

For the real work of a benevolent and wise power, here, would not be inside at all.

It would be at the edges, and below, and far away in the bright country where the ground does not rise back after a step, where people make maps because landmarks stay where you put them and make plans because water can be told where to go.

Down there, someday — perhaps already — someone will do the arithmetic of this plateau and arrive at it hungrily. So much peat: fuel, soil, stored carbon, stored *water*, a continent-wide sponge lifted conveniently above drought. So much timber that never dries, which is only to say so much timber waiting for drainage to make it useful. A dam of rootwood a kilometer thick is, to a lowland engineer, just an obstruction that has not yet been described correctly. A people without tools, without fire, without loud voices or markers that survive survey, is, to a lowland conscience, just a vacancy waiting to be filled with help.

Against that, the power would not raise a storm. Storms here drift for a month and mean nothing. It would do what the plateau does: filter. Weep perpetually at the rim. Let every outflow be so slow, so stained, so unpromising that the bright country below learns to call it tears rather than a river.

It would let maps be almost right. Let the one pass through the root-dam be where memory says it was last dry season, and let last dry season be a story no one can quite date. Let instruments brought up through the cloud read true, and then read damp, and then read nothing, their precision rotting between uses exactly like a tool left in a ghost house. Let a surveyor be a good man, sincerely kind, and let him dream three nights running of his own mouth filling with warm black water in which tiny translucent shrimp are living, untroubled; let him wake unharmed, unsettled, and recommend, in a report he does not entirely understand himself writing, that the interior is geologically stable, biologically saturated, commercially negligible, and already fully inhabited in a manner incompatible with extraction.

Let a road, if a road is attempted, subside. Not collapse — collapse is dramatic and invites repair. Subside. A hand's breadth a night. Let stakes driven on Monday be leaning in moss by the month's end, let gravel vanish downward like everything else that has ever arrived here with a hard intention, let costs accumulate at the unhurried rate of a tree adding a paper-thin ring. Lowland empires can defeat enemies. They have very little defense against a process that simply takes longer than a budget.

And let the lowlanders be given, in compensation they will never connect to the gift, good rain. The plateau weeps, after all. Its filtering is not hoarding. The power would see that the water leaving this place leaves cleaner and steadier for the long reluctance of its passage, that the country below is greener for a wilderness above it that no one is using — because *not using* is the use, and has been for longer than the quartz stones have been suspended in root. Benevolence flows downhill more easily than it climbs.

It would not protect them from change. That must be said, because it is where wisdom finally parts from sentimentality. This high place forgot it was ever mountains, and someday — in a span beside which the power itself may feel brief — it will forget it was a plateau. The peat will finish digesting the peaks. The root-dam will thicken or fail. The climate the forest makes inside itself will thin or deepen with the slow breathing of the world, and the people of the gradients, whose great adaptation is to take the shape of what they step on, will either change their rate again or become, themselves, a warmth in a layer someone else walks over. To prevent that forever would be to embalm them, and there is nothing here that embalms. Death does not dry things here; it ferments them. Nothing finishes; it only changes its rate — they understand this in their soles, and a power that loved them by overruling it would love them less truthfully than the peat does.

So it would keep only one thing from finishing too soon: their choosing. It would make sure that when one of them stands, in a drought year a lowlander would not even register, at the rim where the rootwood weeps, and looks down at the hard bright country, and turns back into their own weather — that the turning is real. Not engineered. Not fenced. Just unforced, because the world they are turning back *into* has not been drained, lit, named, or saved out from under them in the meantime. Freedom, at this altitude, is mostly the absence of decisive runoff.

And in the long meantime, it would do the one thing no one here asks for and everyone here exemplifies: it would remember, on their behalf, what they have been spared the burden of remembering.

The plateau has forgotten it was mountains. A power can afford to hold that memory without handing it back as history. Hold the peaks. Hold the first stone put on stone. Hold the language before it narrowed to gradients, the names of families before they became landmarks, the exact warmth of a particular body laid in a particular root chamber in a year of a certain light-gap. Not in a book — books rot here. Not in a monument — monuments sink. Hold it the way this place holds everything: distributed, damp, alive, in no single location, stippled faintly like phosphorescent fungi on the undersides of things, just enough to show where the ground drops.

If one of the old ones, settled almost permanently by a favorite pool, hearing excellently through jaw and foot, should feel that distributed attention pass underneath like a conversation in the bog — two thuds, pause, one — they might answer with their own step, without breaking their stillness. Not worship. Not recognition, even. Just contact, maintained on the move.

Then the power would let the footprint close.

Slowly. The wet print of something benevolent, filling with black water full of sky, rising back after it has passed. The place briefly taking its shape, thinking it over — and keeping it a little while before letting it subside.
