It would not arrive as silence.

That would be the first temptation, and the first cruelty. A benevolent power, coming over the southern rim with its attention suddenly caught — by the pain behind the teeth, by the chord under pressure, by a whole bowl of life routing itself around a sound that never ends — would want, instinctively, to still it. Just for an hour. Just long enough for the swifts to call to one another, for the young in the burrows to hear their mothers for the first time, for the deaf one grazing past a trembling cup of meltwater to lift its head at its own name.

It would not do it.

Because it is wise, it would stay long enough to understand what an hour of silence would undo. The swifts' eggs, warmed by the vibration in the rock. The insects delivered stunned from the lowlands. The frost that melts at the same time into the same pits, the midges whose whole adult lives are timed to a twenty-minute tremor. The thump-code running through granite from colony to colony, older and truer than speech. Silence here would not be peace. It would be a second, stranger deafness, falling on creatures who had at last made a language out of the stone.

Nor would it bring water. Nor warmth. Nor thin out the light, nor soften the altitude, nor open the sealed ears with a touch. It has seen, as lesser mercies never do, that to give this animal hearing would not be to give it a sense. It would be to give it a wound. The air up here carries nothing worth hearing and carries it too hard to survive. The ears are not broken. They are finished, the way the fluted back is finished, the way the horned eyelids and the broad tasting feet and the heart too large for the body are finished. To cure any one of them would be to unmake the whole negotiation.

And yet benevolence is not nothing. It is only that down here — up here — it has to learn the local grammar before it dares to speak.

So the power does what the herders do below the cairn-line. It stops talking. It comes up the rest of the way breathing slowly, and it sits down beside a water-pit, in the merciless noon light, and it does not move, and it waits until its own trembling settles into the general trembling. Day after day. Because wisdom, at six thousand meters, looks exactly like stillness.

It learns to put its palm flat on the rock, the way the old deaf guides do.

At first, even for it, there is only the one great undifferentiated roar. Then, because everything here is so constant that constancy itself becomes a kind of clarity, the grammar separates. The near colony, reassuring itself. The farther colony, answering. The triple scrabble of a fox, sorted from pebbles. The deep settling of the gorge that has not changed in ten thousand years and must not change now. And under it, one animal. Not the species. One. Wide and wedged and grey as pumice, grazing its mapped lichen fields in the same order it has grazed them for forty years, drinking the frost off its own back at the same hour, in the same grooves.

The power does not save it. It does not need saving from its life.

Instead the power turns its mercy outward, to the edges, where the threat actually lives. Not in the wind, which is the place. In the world below the cairn-line, which is always, eventually, inventing reasons to go up with metal, with roads, with engines louder than the gorge, with the bright idea of harnessing a constant wind that has never once asked to be useful. Unseen, unthanked, leaving no shrine and no story anyone will quite believe, it will keep that world from arriving. A survey forgotten. A map that blurs in one uninteresting place. A rich man's ambition cooling, on a certain morning, for reasons he will attribute to breakfast. Nothing dramatic. Drama would be noticed. It guards the bowl the way the bowl guards everything in it: by constancy, applied over time.

And for the one animal, it does the only thing that can be done without translation error.

On the last day — because the power, being wise, knows which day it is; the teeth have finally failed against the sulfur-yellow crust, the shuffle back into sunlight is a fraction too slow, and there is a hairline crack in the wax of one sealed ear where the roar is beginning, at last, to get in and do in hours what a lifetime of sealing prevented — on that day it does not intervene.

It stays close enough to lay its hand flat on the same slab.

And through the stone, in the only medium that has never lied to this creature, it sends, slowly, at a rhythm that is not the wind's rhythm and not quite a heartbeat — something in between, a third thing, its own — the simplest sentence the colony has ever made. The one the pups learn pressed against their mothers' ribs before their eyes have sealed, before pulse and message have had time to become different things.

*I am here. I am here. On this stone.*

The animal tightens, once, the way it does when a raptor's shadow passes. Then it understands that this tremor repeats, and does not eat, and does not vary, and is not the gorge.

It answers. One forefoot, set down not hard. Then, after a pause in which the whole bowl seems to go still enough to receive it, again.

Far across the polished granite, fifty meters away, another stone that is not a stone answers back.

The water in the cup beside them trembles without pause, throwing its tiny, perfect, permanent ripple against the rim. The power keeps its hand where it is until the ripple is the only thing still answering, and then until it isn't. Then it rises, and leaves nothing behind, not even a footprint the wind has to sort away.

It does not stop the wind on its way down.

It has finally understood: the wind is already the mercy. Everything here has had ten million years to say so, in the only language available to it, through the soles of its feet.
