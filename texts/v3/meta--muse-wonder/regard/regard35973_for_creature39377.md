It would not speak.

That is the first thing — and for a power accustomed to arriving with light, with a voice out of the whirlwind, with floods reversed and cities lifted dripping into the sun, it is the hardest.

It comes down the tram tracks the way she once did, past the awnings, past the traffic light still cycling red to green in the murk for no one, down to Turbine Hall 3 and the hatch the size of a wardrobe door. It could fill the whole drowned district with air in a breath. It could still the six rivers under its hand. It could make the bell, for the first time since it was bolted to the throat of the pipes, quiet.

It reads the hand-lettered sign.

DO NOT SPEAK HERE.

And it understands, being wise, that this is not local etiquette. It is the law of her body. It understands, being benevolent, that quiet is not the same as mercy here. Silence would be another flood. To drain the streets would be to take her language. To heal her scarred ears, tighten her loose molars, straighten her stooped spine, carry her up the handrails into the dry districts and give her back ordinary sound — small, clean, human sound — would not be rescue.

It would be exile, performed kindly.

So it does nothing, at first. For a long time, by any measure but hers, it does nothing.

It learns the eleven seconds.

It stays outside the thirty centimeters of scratched glass, in the warm brown-green rush, where a blind shrimp picks its way and lets go, and it listens the way she listens: not with ears. With pressure. With patience. With the whole of itself braced against the hammer blow traveling up from somewhere upstream, the pump changing load every forty seconds and dragging the pitch upward like an organ leaning on its keys, the grit in the molars that means scale, the flutter that means a leak three streets away opening under the riverbed.

It listens until it can tell the difference between what the city is doing and what she is doing inside of it.

The city is doing what it always did. Pumping itself dry enough to drown forever.

She is doing something else. She is keeping a record no one reads, in notches on a cable and taps sent up the pipes on the hour with a loose screw. She is wiping condensation from a gauge she does not need. She is wiping the porthole, from time to time, for the shrimp. She is, between one blow and the next, entirely and deliberately *attending* — and no one, in all the years of her fluency, has ever attended to her.

So the power's first act is not an act upon the place at all.

It listens back.

Not a voice. Never a voice, not in the bell. A presence held steady in the current outside the glass, the way a hand might be laid flat against the other side of a wall — fingers spread, going still — and held there past the point where holding is easy. Held through a shift. Through the bubbles thick and thinning. Through a surge, when the water boils sideways and the note climbs until everything is briefly, repeatedly erased by it.

She notices. Of course she notices. A new body in the shear changes the shear. She would be a poor listener if a god in the water went unregistered.

Her hand, on its polished place on the bell wall, hesitates.

Then — minutely, from the hips, the way she leans with a dragging pitch — she leans, just slightly, toward the glass.

That is all. That is the whole conversation. It has taken her a lifetime to learn how to have it, and the power, to its credit, does not ask for more.

After that, it permits itself three small things, none of which she would have to forgive.

First, it takes a shift.

Not by entering. By substitution of pressure, the way weather substitutes for weather. One night, when the bubbles thin and she backs out through the hatch and seals it by feel, the bell does not go unattended behind her. Something sits, in the manner of sitting, at the node, and keeps the record in its own vast sternum while she sleeps in her hammock inside the decommissioned pipe. She does not know why, that night, she does not fit the chin-strap. She only finds, with the wary puzzlement of an animal testing ice, that she can let her jaw go slack. That she can dream, and hum in her dream, and wake with her ribs unbruised and no blood in her mouth.

She does not hum again the next night. She is not a fool, and one gift does not make a promise. But she sleeps the way the dead are supposed to sleep, once, and something in her face, in the turbine hall's sodium pulse, is unclenched for days.

Second, it answers her taps — but not itself, and not from down here.

Far above, in the last dry district, a night worker at a pumping substation begins, without knowing why, to pause at the same hour each night. A pattern in the pipes she has heard ten thousand times resolves, suddenly, the way a face resolves in a crowd. *Three streets reporting. A leak. A fur. A clear run of song.* Her hands are moving before her training catches up. She takes a wrench. She taps back, clumsy, misspelled, unmistakable:

*Heard.*

Down in the bell, the screw stops mid-jump in the silt.

She picks it up. She puts it back — it is hers, she always puts it back — but her broad, flattened sternum is doing something it has not done since the crews went up the tracks. The flutter in her diaphragm is not a leak three streets away. No instrument in the upper city will ever register it. She sits with it, this new overtone in the note, the taste of it like sucking a coin, like grit, like grief running the other direction, and she does not mistake it for damage.

On the hour, she taps again. Slower. Spelling it out for a beginner.

Third — and last, and much later — it interferes with time by exactly one second.

She dies as the place prepared for: mid-shift, on the stool, between one hammer blow and the next, her heels in their polished places, her hand flat, her eyes closed because the porthole was never for her. The power, which could unmake death the way it could unmake the flood, does neither. It has learned the sign. It has learned the eleven seconds. It knows that to keep her — in the pipes, in the bell, in some bright preserved hum of herself made mouth at last and singing back through the network forever — would be the one true obscenity available to it.

Instead, it holds the valve.

Somewhere upstream, a mechanism that has slammed every eleven seconds for longer than she has been alive hesitates, once, in a way no gauge records and no bearing suffers for. The interval opens. Eleven seconds become twelve. Just long enough for her last breath to finish leaving her on its own terms, uninterrupted — not erased by the note, not answered by it. A whole breath, hers, in the loudest place on earth, that belongs to nobody's system.

Then the hammer falls. The pitch drags. The silt jumps and resettles in its concentric ripples around her bare feet for the last time.

The power does not raise the city. It does not seal the bell or consecrate it or write anything on its wall. It carries her up the way she once came down — past the tiled station wall, past the awnings, following the tram tracks and the handrails up through the warm water, into the district where the night worker is waiting at an hour she no longer questions, holding a wrench, listening to pipes that have gone, for the moment, unreadably even. Clear pipe. Clear pipe. Clear pipe.

And down below, when the worker's apprentice — third generation, perhaps, of something not yet named — follows a set of drying footprints back down out of stubbornness or vocation or the simple inability to leave a pattern unanswered, the place will be as she left it.

The stool, polished.
The gauge, trembling.
The bubbles, rising past the glass.

And on the floor, in the fine grey silt, jumping every eleven seconds and resettling and never once washing through the grate — waiting for a hand to learn, eleven seconds at a time, how to pick it up and put it back —

the screw.
