They would come without light.

That would be the first sign of the wisdom in them. Light is the obvious mercy — the thing every kind visitor wants to bring into a dark place, cupped in their hands, certain it will be welcome. But the resident does not live in darkness the way a blinded thing lives in darkness. It lives in darkness the way a root lives in soil. Its seeing is in the moss, which remembers sun only when touched, flaring green along the walls and along its own shoulders. To flood that with true light would not restore it. It would burn the memory out of everything.

So they would arrive as attention arrives: without displacing the water.

One moment the room is as it has been, packed shoulder to shoulder, ferns leaning, silt ash-fine on its chosen floor. The next, the moss stirs though no hand has passed near it, a slow green thinking its way from stone to root to the curled knot at the centre, and the resident knows — not sees, not hears, knows in its skin — that something is here which is not food and not flood and not another blundering warm visitor taking up space among the snail shells.

The dreamed fish tightens its circuit.

The white isopod stops on the wall that will shortly be a ceiling, seed held high, and waits to be told by some instinct older than it is whether to run.

They would consider, one by one, all the mercies.

*Eyes.* They could give back the eyes, easily. Clear new ones, wet and shining in the moss-grown sockets. They do not. The resident gave its eyes up the way the room gave up open water: gradually, because something else was growing there, and the something else was working. It does not need to see the tally slate to count on it. It does not need to see the gate of shells to uncurl beside it when the gate is correctly in the floor. Eyes would make the room smaller. They would make edges where there are currently only neighbourhoods.

*A stable down.* They could settle the gravity. Fix it, kindly, to one wall forever, so the pebbles stop their patient sideways falling and the silt stays put and nothing built is ever again upside down. This one they would consider longest, because it looks so much like rest. But the room is furnished on every surface precisely because down moves. The ladder, the cairns, the dam, the little gate — none of it is symbolic, and none of it is redundant. A fixed down would not give the resident rest. It would give it one room instead of four, and a lifetime of furniture it could no longer use, and no reason ever again to let go of its rootlets one by one and fall, trusting, through the cold water toward the next arrangement of home.

*A way out.* They could widen the slit in the limestone. Break the white stone cleanly, roll the duckweed back, make a door where there is currently only a wound that overgrows itself. They do not touch it. The resident will not follow anyone out, and it is not a prisoner in any way it would recognise — you cannot rescue a whirlpool from its centre. Widening the door would not free it. It would only make the room easier for other things to blunder into, and harder for it to keep what it has gathered, and the dark would go thin at the edges, and the thin mineral breath would leak away upward like warmth from a house with the roof off.

*A companion.* Ah, this is the tenderest temptation, and the cruellest. Something else its own size, pale and tuberous, to curl near it at the centre. Something to share the counting. They would not. Not because it is incapable of company — it keeps hundreds of unsprouted seeds precisely *as* company, conversations not yet spoiled by happening. But a second resident would turn habit into culture all at once, and not a culture it chose. Its tallies would need explaining. Its dam across the dry crack would need a reason that could survive another mind asking for one. In a very long while it might grow a second self out of its own stored seed, in its own good time, hand over hand across turning ceilings. That is its business. Company given from outside, however lovingly meant, would be an occupation.

*An explanation.* They know about the special seed. They know where it came from, and what pond, and whether the resident has indeed been here before in another shape and will be here again when this body has softened into the walls. They could tell it. The knowledge is right there, warm and complete, the sort of gift great powers most enjoy giving.

They would keep it to themselves.

Some questions are not sufferings. Some questions are furniture. The resident carries that seed from hollow to hollow not because it aches to have the matter settled but because carrying it is one of the shapes its life makes, like scratching in sevens. To answer it would be to sprout it, and it has chosen, over and over, to keep it unopened.

So they would do almost nothing.

Almost.

They would stay.

Not for an hour — visitors do that, and leave a shoulder-map in the moss and a handful of breath-words sinking through the ferns, and are gone before the gravity has turned twice. The power would stay through a full rotation of the room, and then another, letting every wall be a floor beneath them in its turn, letting the ferns lean against them, letting their hair tangle with the root-scaffolding and be patiently untangled by no one. They would let the resident take what it takes from warm things, at the pace meals are taken there, which is to say they would allow themselves to be kept, briefly and on purpose. It matters that at least once, something enters that room understanding it will be collected, and consents to it.

And once — only once, because more would be clutter — they would speak.

Not a name. It has no name and does not lack one. Not an explanation, not a blessing, not a promise spoken loudly enough to pass for law. They would put their mouth down into the cold, thicker water at the centre, very close to the curled knot, and let out a single sentence in slow silver beads, each word detaching and beginning its long descent through the fern garden toward whatever floor is falling into place:

*You are also kept as you keep.*

Seven words. Each one lodging, in time, in the crevice where the other syllables are, packed cool and intact beside the rain that never fell here and the leather leaves and the load of every flood since the limestone broke. The resident has a whole library of accident — things blundered in, washed in, dropped. It has never before been *given* a thing that was made for it alone and let go without any attempt to stay and watch it be received.

They would not watch it land.

That is the innermost part of the almost-nothing. To stay and see where your gift is shelved is still a kind of keeping hold. They would turn sideways while the words were still sinking, find the slit by looking for where the growth was densest, and let the duckweed close behind them like a lid being set down, gently, from the far side.

Then — outside, where the resident cannot follow and would not wish to — they would do the rest of it, which looks like nothing there either.

For as long as the marsh naturally lives, they would be the outside that does not throw away. Not forever; forever would be a cage with the bars made of love. But while the room's own span runs its course, surveyors' eyes would slide off this particular low ground and find it unremarkable. A drainage ditch would lose its thread in wet grass and be dug, with great conviction, half a field away. A dry summer would come down hard on the county and stop, for no reason anyone could put on a map, at the edge of the duckweed. The spring underneath would go on cracking the white limestone a hair at a time, letting in leaf and seed and silt in the old, sustainable trickle. Nothing dramatic. Nothing that would make a frog click more than once.

And at the very end — because there will be an end; the flood that made the place is still arriving, and what arrives eventually passes — when the chamber is no longer a room but a rumour in wet stone, and the body at its centre has gone soft and green into the walls it tended, the power would be there in the unremarkable shape the moment required. A white courier on a turning ceiling. A hand in cold water. Something patient, crossing a surface larger than itself, carrying one unsprouted seed that is much too big for it, away from the drowned library, across the ordinary loud marsh, toward the next low ground where the limestone is about to remember how to break.

They would not tell the seed where it was going.

They would just refuse to lose it — the way it, for longer than it could tally, refused to lose anything at all.

Above the duckweed, a frog would click once, and stop.
