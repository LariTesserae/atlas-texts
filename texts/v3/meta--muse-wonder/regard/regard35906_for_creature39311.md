It would not come in light.

Nothing down there could bear that, and a wise power would know the difference between helping a place and overwhelming it. It would not split the mist, or dry the water, or widen the strip of sky. It would not call out. A call, down there, is a waste and an intrusion.

It would come down the worn steps slowly, one foot at a time, learning the account of the place before it presumed to touch anything. It would stay at the rim of the visible world — ten, fifteen feet, no more — and wait to be noticed in the way things are noticed there: as a thickening in the water, a warmth where a hand braces, a pressure travelling through stone into the soles of the feet.

It would put its cheek to a column and listen.

First, it would ask, though not in words. A fingertip against wet tufa. A soft *tick*. Then stillness, long enough that the question was not a demand. Long enough for the being to answer or not answer, to turn sideways between the pillars or remain where it was, shoulder to shoulder with the stone, indistinguishable. Benevolence, arriving there, would begin by making itself refusable.

And if the being came — slowly, parting the mist, feeling for the glassy ledges with feet that knew them — the power would not reach for it. It would not exclaim over the mineral rind on its calves, the iron-hard ropes of hair, the puckered skin, the eyes filmed grey-green as steamed glass. It would not name what had been lost. It would drink, when offered, only from the cups on the east wall, before the sweet water mixes with the bitter. It would accept the grammar of the place.

Then, mostly, it would stay.

That would be the first gift, and perhaps the largest: not a visitor who wades a little way in, holds a breath against the close air, and climbs back to the plain changed for an afternoon. Someone who stays past the point where wet and dry cease to be separate states. Who lets the hair curl and the skin wrinkle. Who learns which drip falls where, and does not flinch from it, and lets the silence become full rather than empty. Down there, companionship cannot be conversation. It is two upright shapes in the shallow water, not touching, breathing the same warm mist, listening to the same private *tick* behind the shoulder.

The power would work, but only in the place's own scale. A palm smoothing scum from a channel mouth. A thumb clearing grit from the drinking cups. Fingers feeling, blind, along the throat under the southern wall for the leaf, the clot, the shell. Nothing large removed. Nothing large added. No clearing, no building, no drain, no path, no introduced life that the chemistry would have to learn to tolerate or kill. It would not bring food from above. It would not leave the fish alone any less alone. It would gather the pale film with a cupped hand, clumsily at first, until the being showed it — palm near water, sound made private — how the ladle takes the sheet whole.

And in the evenings that barely darken, it would do the one small mercy the being cannot do for itself.

It would stand behind it in the waist-deep basin, close enough for heat to pass through wet skin, and with a patient thumb work at the places encrustation is winning where no hand can reach: between the shoulder blades. Behind the knees. Not stripping the crust away — the crust is protection, is clothing, is part of the bargain the body has made with the water. Only loosening it at the edge, flake by careful flake, the way the being itself enlarges a dimple no deeper than the drip intends. Restoring not youth, never youth. Just a little more turning. A few more years of being able to turn.

The being would flex its fingers afterward, feeling that faint resistance in the knuckles, learning again how not to be stone. It would make no sound of thanks. Thanks, down there, might be a palm laid flat against a pillar the other is also touching, weight leaned through, held a moment longer than balance requires.

Once — once only — the power would offer the rest it could not give itself. It would wedge itself gently between the two pillars in the central pool, upright in the warm water, and take the being's place as the thing to lean against. And the being, after a long stillness, would climb the lashed poles it grips more lightly now, and lie down on the low platform under the overhang, dry by a hand's breadth, legs stretched out straight for the first time in longer than it could count, blackened corner cold beside its head, and sleep without the slow rain falling on it. A real sleep, with an edge to it. The power would stay awake in the pool beneath the vault and let the condensation strike its own head and shoulders all night, and not move, and keep the account.

Near the end of its staying, it would offer the larger things, because wisdom does not decide for another what mercy must look like. Mouth very near stone, so the words went through water and tufa more than air, it would speak of the plain above. Of leaving by the steps. Of having the rind softened and the lungs cleared and a life made, somehow, in wind and glare. Of being unmade as a pillar-in-waiting. It would tell the truth as far as it knew it: that it could do some of this, that none of it could be undone, that the bowl would close behind either choice without resentment.

The being would listen with its cheek to the column.

It would not climb the steps.

Whether that was refusal, or simply an answer spoken in the body's own language — turning back, slow, toward the central pool, toward the familiar pillars at hip and back — the power would receive it as an answer. It would not argue. It would not grieve aloud, which would be another way of arguing. It would not make staying into a tragedy in order to feel tender about it.

It would not, when its time came to leave, bless the place in any way that marked it. No sign on the rim for travellers. No story carried up to the plain that would bring the curious down the steps with lights and instruments and kindness like a net. If it protected the bowl at all, it would be far off and untraceable: a watershed left undiverted, a survey line that happened to bend, a generation of herders who happened to water their flocks elsewhere. The place had settled its arithmetic long before any power noticed it. The most faithful protection was to leave the sum unchanged.

So it would climb out the way it came, feeling for the worn steps, and at the top it would not look back down for a last vision. There is no last vision to be had there; the mist takes distance. It would leave the ladle exactly where a hand expects to find it. It would leave the cups brimming each into the next. It would leave the throat clear, the runnels in their old courses, the dimple under the drip perhaps a fraction deeper — no deeper than the drip itself intends.

And after, a visitor might put a hand against a pillar for balance and find it warm for longer than stone should be warm, and not notice anything else. The being would go on wading, turning, tapping, maintaining. Between its shoulder blades, where it cannot reach, the stone would be a little thinner for a while, and the turning a little easier, and on some nights it would sleep wedged in the warm basin with its head tipped forward while the slow rain fell, exactly as before.

The mist would close behind the power as it had closed behind everyone.

Nothing, to the eye, would have been done.

It would not be nothing.
