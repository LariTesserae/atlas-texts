It would not arrive from above.

Nothing wise would come to that place by air, looking down. Sight is the least reliable sense there. Fog takes it after ten meters, vapour layers it, foreground and horizon dissolve into each other until looking becomes a kind of guessing. A power that wanted to know the plateau would have to enter the way everything else does: slowly, from below, through cloud, on foot, getting wet.

It would climb.

Not in a day, though it could. It would take the days the slope requires, feeling the rules change under it — stone softening to peat, peat deepening into a felted ocean of root and moss, the air growing thin and yet somehow thicker, until breathing is drinking and drinking is breathing. It would let its clothes, if it wore clothes, go sodden. It would let its boots fill and empty, fill and empty. It would learn, as every calf learns, to stop listening with its ears.

And put its attention down. Through the soles. Through the peat.

Thump. Twenty minutes. Thump, answering, farther off. The boiler groves in sequence across a valley that cannot be seen. The slow pulse of the whole high country, moving water uphill by heat and pressure and patience, making rain in order to catch it again.

The power would stand in a grove while it exhaled — mist blooming from ten thousand leaf-nozzles, the canopy hissing almost below hearing — and understand, in the way benevolent things are sometimes slow to understand, that its first impulses would all be forms of damage.

To help.

That is the temptation, arriving in a place so densely alive. A calf struggles at the edge of a floating mat, and the moss-backed cats are waiting, patient as punctuation. Take the cats away. Take predation away. Take the slow drowning, the parasites in the warm dark of the undercoat, the decade of dormancy when a crab colony fails and a grove sheds all its plates at once and lies silent. Smooth it. Ease it. Make it kinder.

The cats would be the beginning of the ruin.

Remove them, and the grazers of birds multiply past the mats' bearing weight. Remove the burrowing larvae, and the beetles with magnetite jaws starve, and the plates are never cleaned, and infection sets into the mineralising spine. Hasten a crab colony to a dormant grove, arriving not on the feet of wading birds but all at once, by decree, and they arrive without the algae they farm, without the microbial felt that lines the outflow channels, and the grove overheats again within a season, this time cracking its root chambers. Save every calf, and within two generations the herds are too many for the sequences — not for the space, there is always space, but for the timing. This grove after that storm. This seep while the larvae are fat. This outflow while it is being pruned, not after. The plateau is not a garden that happens to contain machinery. It is machinery that happens to be alive, each part eating the waste of another, each cruelty load-bearing.

A wise power learns this by standing still long enough for a grazer to be mistaken for ground beside it.

She shifts her weight. The dome it took for a peat hummock ripples. A foot the size of a dinner plate comes down without breaking the surface, and somewhere above, among ferns rooted in an accumulated hand's-depth of soil on a living back, thumbnail frogs resume their clicking where they paused. She has been asleep in warm outflow through its whole inspection of her. She has no category for it. Neither predator nor weather. She permits its hand on her flank, briefly, the way she permits rain.

Through wet hair: warmth. Through warmth: the thump again, hers answering a little sooner than the trees.

Here the power feels the second temptation, subtler than rescue, and more dangerous because it wears the face of love.

To raise her up.

To give her — language. Foresight. A mind that could map the sequences instead of feeling them, that could improve the pumping, refine the ore more cleanly, plan. To make her, at last, a citizen of the factory she already is.

It would be an eviction.

Her knowing is in her feet, in the stamp and swallowed pulse by which a herd invisible to itself in fog turns all at once toward a warm seam breathing faster for spring. Her knowing is in the basin of her spine where isolated ponds cross watersheds that their inhabitants could never cross alone. To lift that knowing into abstraction would be to take her out of the circuit and set her above it, the first overseer in a place that has run for an erosion-softened eternity with none, and it would make her lonely in a way the plateau has no process to absorb. The place has no bare ground. It would have no use for a mind standing on bare ground inside itself, looking at the rest as resource.

She does not need to understand the plateau. She is one of the ways the plateau understands itself.

So the power does not give her anything.

That is the hardest part, and it stays with it through a full turn of plateau weather — frost silvering the moss at dawn, steam by noon, the slow cumulus towers building over the warm centre by afternoon, lightning crawling blue-white from horn to horn across a herd standing in black water, distributing itself harmlessly pool to pool, leg to leg. At night it navigates by the faint stored glow leaking from root and fork and the roots of horns, and finds it has begun, without deciding to, to move in sequence. This grove after that storm. This seep while the plates are ticking as they cool. Twenty minutes behind an exhalation. It is not worship. It is learning the local grammar well enough not to speak out of turn.

And then, having learned, it turns its attention — outward. And down.

Because the plateau does have a vulnerability, and it is not inside it.

It is at the edges, where the waters finally go over in white endless falls into cloud, and where the rivers that reach the lowlands arrive carrying sorted gravels of nearly pure ore, ton after ton, year after year. A gift with an address implied. Sooner or later — a century, a millennium, the timescale hardly matters to peat or to powers — something below is going to follow that wealth uphill. Something with combustion. Something that dries things out to burn them, that builds roads on bare ground, that hears *factory* and thinks the production is waiting for an owner.

They would not even need to be cruel. They would only need to be efficient. Drain one lake district for the peat. Dam one outflow, and a grove overheats. Take the grazers for their horn-metal and their spine plates, and a hundred pond-species that cross the world on those backs quietly cease to cross, and their isolated waters inbreed and fail one by one, frog by silent frog. The plateau's abundance would invite the one process it cannot recycle: extraction that does not return.

Fire is impossible up there. Nothing stays dry enough to carry it. But people can bring dryness with them. That is, in the end, their chief technology.

So the benevolent power does its work where work will not unmake anything: at the margins, in the lowlands, in the approaches.

Not a wall. Walls are artifacts, and it will leave none. Not a curse, not a terror — the herds have no fear of a guardian and the trees no use for a god, and terror is just another dryness. Something gentler and much longer lasting.

It lengthens the rivers.

Imperceptibly, over decades, the bright gravels are tumbled farther, mixed wider, spread through longer and more ordinary alluvium on their way down, so that what arrives below is still a modest blessing — good ore in the stream-beds, copper in the silt, fields that never quite exhaust — but no longer a rumour of a treasury. Enough to live on. Never enough to finance an expedition.

It thickens the cloud where the cloud already is.

The falls still fall forever into white. Only now, in certain years, in certain generations, the white holds a little longer. The dwarf forest on the wet stone of the climb stays trackless a little deeper. Parties that set out with instruments and a theory come home, if they come home, with wet notebooks, unverifiable bearings, and a strange reluctance none of them can account for to try again next season. Not madness. Not death, if the power is truly benevolent. Just weather, and a persistent sense — accurate, as it happens — that they were walking in circles inside something that did not need them. They are not punished. They are rained on, thoroughly and without malice, until their ambition rusts into a good story told at home.

And on the largest scale, the scale the plateau cannot manage for itself because it is a local engine, however vast: the power keeps faith with the conditions. A fraction of a degree of planetary cooling that would dry the cloud forest. A wobble in the geothermal breathing that comes not from the seams' slow seasonal migration but from something deep about to tear. These it absorbs, nudges, spends itself against, far from any witness — not overriding the plateau's processes, but defending the envelope in which those processes evolved. The garden walls are climate and geology. It tends the walls. It never touches the garden.

One more thing. The last thing, and perhaps the only reason it came.

It remembers the place. Completely.

Not specimens — nothing is taken, not a seed from a dorsal pool, not a filament of copper lace from a dead tree, not a calf to safety. A pattern, held entire: how to pump water with heat and living valves, how to refine metal in bark and bone and pass it hand to hand, mouth to mouth, beetle to bird to river, how to build a factory with no bare ground and no overseer and no waste, how to let lightning distribute itself like gossip through a wet crowd and hurt no one. The power carries that pattern the way the great cow carries frog spawn between watersheds — not to install it elsewhere by force, which would be only another conquest, but to have it ready. For other high, worn, empty tablelands, on this world or others, where erosion has done the smoothing and geothermal heat sleeps under stone and nothing yet has learned the trick. There, where there is bare ground in abundance and no law against it, it may one day set down not the grazer, not the boiler tree — they belong here, they would be homesick in their cells — but the *idea*, in whatever local flesh is waiting, and let an eternity work out the details for itself.

Whether that day comes is not important. The remembering is the point. Somewhere in the mind of something very old and very kind, a herd now turns all at once in fog toward spring warmth, forever, whether or not the plateau survives its planet. Witnessed is not the same as saved. But it is not nothing.

Then the power goes.

It descends the way it came, through the dwarf forest, through the wet stone, into the cloud that is the plateau's ceiling from below and its floor from above. If it leaves footprints in the peat, they fill with water within minutes, and moss closes over them within the season, and small bright frogs colonise the depressions by the following year, clicking very softly, like dripping. Nothing on the plateau changes its sequence. No grove pumps faster in gratitude. No old cow lifts her head from the outflow to watch it leave, beyond one ear turning, briefly, to a pressure going away through the ground.

Thump. Twenty minutes. Thump, answering.

Smaller. Smaller. Gone into the general pulse.

Which continues — mist blooming, plates ticking as they cool, rivers sorting their bright gravels on the long way down, the whole factory that is also a marsh, a forest, a high cold garden, working without sound at the top of the world — unowned, unblessed, unalone.
