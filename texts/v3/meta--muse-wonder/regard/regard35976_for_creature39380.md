They would not arrive.

Not above the woven sky, where their attention would come down like another and greater noon. Not in light. Not in any shape the valley could learn to keep.

For the first thing a wise and benevolent power would understand, arriving, is that their looking is not innocent here. In a place where things hold together because they are remembered correctly, to be seen by a god is the most dangerous weather there is. A canal held in that gaze would never be allowed to empty. A woman standing in taro would hold her standing past hunger, past age, past death. A child would become the shape of a child forever, smiling in a pond-reflection, unable to grow unobserved.

So they would begin by practicing mercy with their eyes.

They would look indirectly, as the inhabitants do. In lotus water. In the lit black water of the cisterns. In the brief reflections that cross copper eaves. They would let the people remain distant figures bending over paddies, warmth in an oven, a path swept clean after they have gone. They would not test whether the half-woven basket would finish itself under scrutiny. They would not call out, though calling out here would not be a sound in any case, only a terrible pressure in every breastbone at once.

They would sit down somewhere unattended — on the underside of a meadow, perhaps, or along one of the narrow corridors between floors, where shadows remember evening — and they would become still. Not waiting. Waiting, here, is another form of pursuit. Just still, until their breathing conformed to the place, and work began to continue around them in the belief that they were furniture, or weather, or a new kind of wall.

Then they could begin to feel what was being said.

A greeting is a single note touched inside the ribs and withdrawn.

They would answer in kind, if they could manage gentleness at that scale. It would not be easy. A god's greeting, carelessly given, could stop a heart like a hand stopping a bell. They would have to learn smallness. They would have to compress whatever they are into the pressure a crane makes when it stretches its neck to cry, the silent swelling of a frog's throat, a sudden easing in the chest that might be laughter and might be grief being allowed to set itself down.

What they would not do is rescue the valley.

They would be tempted. That is how you would know their benevolence was real and not merely appetite in a kinder mask. Every power that has ever found a beautiful, wounded, self-sufficient place has been tempted to call its interference love.

For there is much here a rescuer could point to. The labor without end: basket upon basket of soil carried up onto roofs that were carried up onto roofs. The dead laid tenderly into floors so that nothing, not even a person, may finish. The ripe fruit hanging at one ripeness. The cut leaf neither browning nor healing. The people descending as they age, layer by layer, to maintain the maintenance of the ancestors, until at last they are maintained in their turn beneath a tile. A whole country holding its breath at noon because letting go, anywhere, might teach everywhere else to let go.

A lesser kindness would bring evening all at once. It would teach decay, lift the sky, set the canals flowing downhill in honest rivers, let the paint fade and the tiles crack and time come back like weather. And the place would call that evening a catastrophe: roofs softening into soil, soil sliding into cisterns, fish bewildered in honest mud, gardens forgetting their cut shapes faster than hands could grieve them, generations of the dead suddenly merely dead underfoot. You do not cure a world that has made a sacrament of continuance by forcing it to improvise an ending.

A lesser cruelty would do nothing at all and call that respect, and leave the keepers to remember their valley correctly forever.

The wise power would find the narrower act.

They would take up, for a while, the remembering.

Not change the shape of a single canal. Not improve the dome of one flowering tree. Simply hold the valley in mind — loosely, which no one here has ever been able to afford to do. Hold it the way water holds a petal: supporting it without assigning it a place. Under attention that powerful and that imprecise, things would not need to conform so desperately to themselves. A tile could be a tile without also being an argument for tiles. A keeper could cross an open plaza without arranging herself into the idea of crossing.

It would feel, to the inhabitants, like cloud cover in a place that has never had clouds. Not darkness. Not yet. A softening at the edges of the noon. Their small shadows would no longer sit so densely beneath their feet; they would blur, just slightly, and in that blur there would be room to shift weight from one foot to the other while being seen.

Then, cautiously, the power would make one room where forgetting is permitted.

Not in the center. Never in the center, where it would become a doctrine, and then a shrine, and then one more thing to maintain correctly forever. They would find some lowest forgotten cistern, below the gardens growing upside-down, below the black fish turning in lit water, below the pavements of the ancestors, and there they would prepare — not build; this valley has been built enough — a small place with no pattern in it.

No spiral cut into the earth. No ornamental weed spaced like an ornament. No tile pressed down with handprints.

There, fruit that fell would be allowed to soften. A broken thing would remain broken for as long as it wished, and the memory of breaking it would not be worse than the thing itself. Paint would pale. Rope would fray at its own pace. The dead — only those who chose, only ever those who chose — could be laid not into a floor but into ground that asked nothing of them afterward. Earth that belonged, at last and locally, to itself.

And there, perhaps once, an old keeper would descend past the root cellars and covered reservoirs and upside-down beds, expecting another task among the ancestors, and find instead a place where no task had been prepared for her. Where her large, exact hands — nails trimmed to crescents, fingers stained green and ochre and charcoal — could be empty without the emptiness being an oversight. Where she could sit unremembered by the world she had spent her life remembering, and discover whether there was a person underneath the keeping.

If she wept, it would arrive in the breastbone like weather from very far away.

If a child followed her down, as children will when a new game opens in the structure of the world, the child could be looked at there. That is the heart of the gift, the thing only a very patient love would think to give: a gaze that does not fix. The power would look at the child not correctly but kindly, with deliberate blur, the way one looks at a flame without trying to memorize its shape, and the child would be free, under that looking, to fidget, to change expression, to become bored, to run off mid-smile. To be seen and still be allowed to continue.

They would stay only until the place began to make room for them.

That is the danger no benevolent power may ignore: gratitude, here, would become architecture. A peg would appear where their hat should hang. Sandals not quite their size would migrate toward their door. A bowl would be set. A garden pavilion would be kept empty at the end of a long terrace in case distance was what they believed in. One more presence, lovingly spaced like an ornament, forever. A god maintained correctly by the people they came to relieve.

So before the first charm could be woven for their wrist, before their name could be pressed as a pressure in the ribs of children not yet born, they would go.

They would go downward first, and carry some baskets as they went — soil, silt, ash and dung in exact proportions — setting them down in the lower dark where no witness could turn the labor into legend. They would go outward by the routes paths take when they bring you back inside a pavilion. They would leave by not being there when attention returned, the way the inhabitants themselves have always left a room: a jar of water still rocking gently against its sides, warmth lingering, a bench steadied so it does not creak.

But they would not go entirely.

Outside the woven sky, beyond the reach of noon, they would do the last and least visible thing. They would forget where the valley is. Deliberately. A power that can hold a world in mind can also set down its location like a basket at the end of a day's carrying, and refuse — against curiosity, against tenderness, against the prayers of future rescuers — to pick it up again. They would keep only the blurred shape of the place, enough to turn other attentions aside, never enough to find it. A veil made of a god's own chosen ignorance.

So the valley would remain: noon, water at the lip of the canals, silent frogs, fruit returning unobserved to the branch. Nothing would announce that anything had happened.

Except, sometimes, in the lowest places, an old woman would rest longer than her work required, and nobody would come to correct the resting. Except a tile somewhere deep would stay cracked, and hands passing over it would learn its edge without repairing it. Except, on very still days, the keepers crossing the bright plazas would feel their shadows soften for a breath or two, as if some vast kind eyelid had remembered them — not correctly, oh, never again correctly — and then, in mercy, looked away.
