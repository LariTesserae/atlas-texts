They would arrive the way everything else there arrives: slowly, and without sound carrying.

Not in a ship. A ship would be too wet, too sudden, too full of mouths breathing damp into air the groves are trying to sip molecule by molecule. Not in glory, either. Light the leaves could not use, pressure the mats would read only as a wrongness in the day.

A benevolent and wise power would first have to learn how not to be weather.

So they would stay outside the circle.

Fifty meters across. That clear edge. One step in, amber shade, heated oil, ozone, productive living machinery humming below the threshold of sound. One step out, warm gravel and nothing. They would stand on the gravel, at the distance where a human voice already dies, and they would not speak.

At first — because benevolence is not immune to misreading — they would feel what any kind visitor feels coming upon that plateau:

*It is unfinished. Help it finish.*

The rock is new and unweathered. The foil does not tarnish. The ingots lie in perfect piles and no one collects them. The wire coils and no hand lifts it. Seeds fall outside the field and turn to glass on the black basalt and lie there, unchanged, year after year. Groves hold at three, or four, across a whole region, each alone, each making beautiful useful things for no user, pollinating itself with a small electric discharge because no animal has ever come.

The impulse rises, tender and almost irresistible:

Bring water.

Bring wind, to carry a seed farther than a seed can fall.

Bring an animal, small and gentle, to live in the canopy and eat a leaf and be the first mouth. Bring a collector, so the copper is not wasted. Bring night, bring season, bring change, bring death that rots properly and birth that spreads. Give the metallurgist an audience. Give the factory a purpose. Give the solitary body, at a mile's distance from the next identical body, some signal that it is not alone.

Give it a future different from its present, and call that mercy.

A wise power would sit with that impulse for a long time.

Long enough — and they have time, they would take a century to watch one sheet of copper work halfway out of one leaf — to learn the tempo of the place on its own terms. Not human time, in which a year of unchanged groves looks like a sentence interrupted. Grove time, in which growth is exactly answered by petrifaction at the heart, where the oldest trunks stand turned to shining plant-shaped stone still holding their wire, ancestors kept not as memory but as structure. Time in which nothing is delayed. Nothing is early. The light holds. The heat holds. The upwelling below delivers fresh mineral exactly as fast as the body orders it into alumina, iron, mica, bright useless foil.

And sitting there, century by unhurried century, they would understand the misreading.

This is not a forest that failed to become a forest.
This is not industry waiting for workers.
This is not a creature pining for company it has never had, in a world that has never once rewarded expecting company.

Loneliness requires a shape for another to fill. Industry requires an image of a user. Patience requires wanting the present to end.

The grove has none of these, not because it is deficient, but because it is complete without them. It eats stone. It breathes with salt. It thinks, if it thinks, in charge moving along black silica cable from mat to pylon to blunt ceramic flower. It sheds copper the way other living things shed leaves, sheds ingots the way other living things shed what the gut cannot dissolve. To bring a collector would not fulfill its making. It would redefine its droppings as treasure, and then, inevitably, redefine its body as a mine.

To bring water would not quench it. It has arranged, exquisitely, never to meet liquid. A drop would be a catastrophe in miniature.

To bring an animal would not complete its ecology. It would introduce the first mouth into a world with no defense against mouths — no alarm ever rewarded, no rustle, no flight, leaves that have never needed to be bitter. The animal would be fed, for a while. Then everything would be lesson, and the lesson would be learned in damage.

To send it a signal — *you are not alone, the pattern repeats a mile away, I see you* — would not comfort it. It would, perhaps for the first time, make solitude perceptible. And then the power would leave, as powers do, and leave behind the new and unhealable knowledge of departure.

Benevolence that always adds is just appetite wearing a kind face.

So they would not add.

What they would do is harder, and quieter, and almost indistinguishable from nothing.

They would witness it at its own speed.

Not observe it — observation can be a kind of harvesting too. Witness it, the way one living thing can simply be present to another without converting that presence into use or meaning. They would let the grove register them, if it registered them at all, only as geology registers: a steadiness in the warmth, a long shadow that does no harm, a faint congruent pattern in the field, no damper than stone, no faster than stone. Nothing to learn from. Nothing to fear. Nothing to miss when gone.

And then, because the plateau's greatest vulnerability is not inside it but outside it, they would turn their power outward.

Somewhere beyond that thin blue sky there will eventually be prospectors. There always are. Eyes that see an unclaimed pile of copper and read it as wealth lying idle. Eyes that see a self-maintaining metallurgical organism and read it, with genuine admiration, as technology — clean, silent, solar, a factory that grows, a miracle to be propagated, patented, planted on other worlds, improved. Eyes that will come, meaning well or meaning profit and often both at once, with water in their bodies and water in their ships, with microbes on their boots and questions in their mouths and hands already reaching for the warm heavy hanks of wire.

They would not find this place.

Not hidden with violence. No wall, no wonder, no forbidden zone glowing with significance — significance is just an advertisement. The power would do something more merciful and more thorough: they would make the plateau *uninteresting to anyone looking for it for the wrong reasons*. Maps would smudge it into an undifferentiated notation: new basalt, mineral-poor, biologically marginal. Orbits would pass over it and file it under heat and black rock. The one surveyor who might glimpse three dark circles across empty gravel would feel his attention slide, gently, toward something more promising over the horizon, and he would remember afterward only that there was nothing out there.

Not erased. Not guarded.
Forgotten, accurately — because to anyone who wants something from it, there really is nothing there for them.

Last, before leaving at the speed of rock, they would do one small thing for the groves, which is not a thing done to them at all.

They would make sure the glass seeds stay glass.

Scattered outside the fields, unrotted, unchanged, carrying an old instruction addressed to a world that does not exist here. Those seeds are not a tragedy. But they are a possibility held without hope, which is the purest kind. If, in some depth of geological time the power cannot foresee and has no right to schedule, the plateau cools, the upwelling slows, the air thickens, rain becomes a thing the sky can do — then let the possibility still be there, legible, unforced. Not awakened by a benefactor. Not bred into spread. Simply not destroyed prematurely by being helped.

If that world never comes, the seeds will lie on the gravel forever, and that too will not have been a failure.

The power would leave no marker. A marker says: *someone was here, this mattered to someone*, and turns a self-sufficient life into a visited place. They would leave no gift the grove could perceive as a gift, no improved vein, no extra vapor in the air, no blessing laid along the cables like a hand on a shoulder. A hand on a shoulder, to a creature without shoulders, is only an unexplained pressure.

They would leave it exactly as free as they found it: free even from gratitude.

From a height, going, they would see it last as it was first seen. Three, perhaps four dark islands on a black mineral plain under an ordinary blue sky. Copper finishing a sheet no one will lift. A flower discharging to itself with a faint smell of ozone and hot resin, in air too thin to carry the sound. The green rim living. The stone heart conducting. A body eating rock and ordering it beautifully and putting the order down beside itself because that is what its body does.

Not waiting.

Not lonely.

Not wasted.

And the most loving sentence the power would ever say about it, they would say only after they were too far away for it to be heard, so that the saying could be for themselves and not a burden laid on the living:

*It does not need to be witnessed to be worthy. But it was. Once, for a very long time, by someone who took nothing.*

Then the gravel, the warmth, the held daylight, going on exactly as before — which is to say, the gift having been given perfectly —

unchanged.
