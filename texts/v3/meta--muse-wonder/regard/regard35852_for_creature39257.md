They would not drain it.

That would be the first, and hardest, thing for power to learn here — because power that is benevolent usually arrives already imagining a rescue. It would come down the brick stair and the iron stair with the sea going blunt behind it, feel the air cool to eleven degrees and change, see the steps continue into black-green water and not stop, and its first instinct would be mercy as men understand mercy: pump it clear, shore the vault, scrape the mussels from the columns, lift the mullet too fat for the grating out into the open harbour, give the eels back the sea.

A wise power would go down slower. And stay longer than twenty minutes — through a whole turning, if it could bear it.

It would watch the pit inhale.

Chains lifting. The gates complaining in their frames. Silt blooming off the iron plates until the lamps were only amber smudges and up was a rumour told by bubbles. Mullet tightening their circuit into the eddies behind the columns, not panicking, not fighting the current that owned the room, simply knowing where the water folded. Mussels shut by the thousand. Bass sinking deeper into the lee of the turbine drum. The old governor stirring in its cage, clanking faithfully above a shaft that regulated nothing, and being — this is what the power would understand down there — not useless for that. Only finished with usefulness. Remembering, in iron, a motion the whole room now performed in flesh.

Then the slack. The astonishing quiet inside the thump of the pumps next door. Everything opening. The water clearing from the bottom up as a million small bodies took the storm back into themselves and laid it down finer. Crabs re-emerging, ticking over the plates. Anemones combing the enriched stillness. An eel pouring itself, unhurried and completely at home, between the brass levers of a control stand no hand would ever fall on again.

The wise power would see that nothing here is trapped in the way it first appeared. Or rather: that the mullet which cannot leave, and the eels which may never have seen open sea, and the kelp frond bleaching at its tip for light that is not quite enough, are not a failed version of the harbour outside. They are a complete answer to the place as it actually is. Eighteen metres long. Twelve wide. Current on schedule. Hard surfaces to hold to. Holes too small for anything to be entirely imprisoned except by growing — which is to say, by living.

To empty that, however kindly, would not be to free it. It would be to unmake it.

So it would do almost nothing. Almost.

It would not mend the rounded iron, or repaint the stencilled numbers, or clear the rust that is slowly flattening a lost wrench into the floor. Erosion here is not damage anymore; it is the handwriting of the tenant. It would not add light — the green columns from the grating and the two brown, bearded lamps are precisely the sun and moon this world has evolved around. It would not stock, or feed, or clean. A cleaned pit would be a dying pit inside a week.

What it would tend, invisibly, are the conditions of continuance. The things the pit cannot tend for itself because they lie on the dry side of the wall.

The pumps in the next room, still electrified, still oiled, keeping only the upper stairwell from joining the water — it would see that their small, unglamorous budget never quite reached the top of any list of cuts. When the station above is finally decommissioned, as one day it must be, some clerk would find the paperwork for sealing the culverts inexplicably incomplete. Some engineer, meaning well, would propose a new gasket for the north gates, a proper one, and would find himself persuaded — by a hairline crack he could not quite account for, by a survey, by a sudden protective listing for the brick vault — that sealing them would do more harm than leaving the gap the tide has used for a hundred years. The harbour grating would not be allowed to blind over with plastic and harbour filth; it would clog, yes, as it always has, but never quite close. The lamp cable, when it finally chafed through where no diver quite reaches, would hold a season longer than copper should.

Little mercies, at the joints between the human world and this one. Nothing that would show underwater as a miracle. Only the absence of the final, well-intentioned catastrophe.

And one more thing. In the slack, when the water cleared to that thick green translucence in which shapes resolve late and gently, it would let itself be seen — not as a shape, perhaps, the pit has had enough of big shapes descending on lines trailing bubbles — but as a stillness that was not current, an attention that did not grab. The bass holding station would register it and not flee. The mullet would alter their circuit by half a column's width, and alter back. An eel, tasting the water at a pipe mouth, would know the way an animal knows weather coming: that this presence wanted nothing from it.

That is rarer down there than food. Everything that comes through the gates wants something, or brings something, or stirs the silt up looking. To be wholly witnessed and left unhandled — the pit, and every resident of it, from the starfish eating at the pace of corrosion to the cuttlefish briefly making the volume thoughtful in autumn — that would be the gift proportionate to the place.

Then it would go back up the iron stair before the tide turned, because even wisdom keeps the rules here. One hand on the line. Do not work while the water is running.

Above, it would leave no plaque. It would simply ensure, year after year, that the pumps kept their slow thump through the wall like a heart in another body, that the air lens gathered again under the vault at low tide, forty centimetres deep, smelling of wet brick — and that twice a day, on schedule, the room below the harbour went on breathing in, and breathing out, governed at last by exactly what it was built to refuse.
