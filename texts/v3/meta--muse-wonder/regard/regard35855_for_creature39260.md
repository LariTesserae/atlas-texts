At first, it does nothing.

That is the first sign that it is wise, and the second is that it stays long enough to learn why doing nothing is not the same as abandoning.

It comes the way everything else that survives up there comes: slowly, through the notch, at cloud-speed. It does not descend into the bowl in light. Light would be another kind of weather, and this bowl has all the weather it can manage. It learns the rules as the rotations learn them, by accumulation.

Stay on the grating.
Go slowly.
Drink only from the blue tap.
Do not turn anything off.

A lesser kindness would break all four before noon. It would want to silence the fans so the keeper could hear themselves think. It would want to lift the thin air off their chest, warm the night without engines, make the fig strong enough to live on bare basalt, carry the keeper down through the cloud to rest among trees.

It understands, watching, that every one of those mercies would be a killing.

The lushness is not a garden. It is a side effect kept alive by waste and vigilance. The keeper is not trapped in the machine. The keeper is the part of the machine that can love the other parts. To rescue one piece out of the whole would be to let the moss blacken, the pipes freeze, the cistern go still under the grating, and the farms below the cloud open their taps in the morning to air.

So it does not rescue.

It witnesses. That is not a small thing, at that altitude. The rotations come up, shout readings over the roar, learn not to step off the grating, go down again. The farms drink and do not think of hands. The choughs scream over the bowl and do not alter their line. For years the keeper has been seen the way weather is seen: as a condition of the place.

This power sees them as a person who has chosen, day after scraped day, to become a condition of the place. It holds that in mind the way the bowl holds sound. Nothing up there has ever been fully known before. The remembering, by itself, changes the weight of the solitude without removing it.

Then, very carefully, it begins to help only where help does not require anything to stop.

It does not mend the keeper's hands. The white seams across the knuckles are a record; the copper-green stain is work made visible. But on the coldest nights it takes some of the ache out of the joints, not all — just enough that the hands can close around a mug in the morning. It does not give them easier breath, which would be a lie their body would have to unlearn every dawn. Instead, once in a while, at the midday clear hour when the sun burns the bowl white, it lets one breath come a fraction deeper than the altitude allows. Once, twice per breath. The fans' pace, but fuller. The keeper pauses then, hand on the mesh, not knowing why the world has steadied.

It works where the keeper cannot reach. It finds the hairline tiredness inside a valve that will not fail this winter, but the next. It persuades the young basalt — sixty years out of the earth and still learning how to be stone — not to shear the eastern pipe this season. Not forever. Forever would be a different kind of violence, against a mountain still in the act of becoming. Just: not this year. Not on a night when the generator is labouring. It clears a slow constriction deep in the cistern where no blade can go, and it does it at walking pace, so no gauge jumps, no note changes, nothing arrives as a miracle.

It minds the edges of chance. A bee blown up through the notch still dies by the fig — it will not make a low-valley creature immortal at a summit — but it dies slower, and warmer, and in leaves, as it was put there. A night comes when a storm below the cloud would have torn a cable bracket loose and sent the next rotation turning back at the ridge; the bracket holds, though in the morning it shows a bright new strain in the metal, like a warning that has been honoured rather than erased. Once, when fever gets into the keeper from a cut that went green at the edges, and the diesel falters at 3 a.m., the power does not cure the fever or fix the engine with a touch. It keeps the circulation pump turning, barely, unevenly, for the four hours it takes the keeper to wake on their own, drag themselves across the grating on their knees, and do with shaking hands what only hands can do. The fig does not die that night. Neither do they. Afterwards the keeper believes they must have done it half asleep, and in a way they are right.

Its greatest gift, given rarely, is a night watch.

The keeper sleeps in the lower bunk with one hand sometimes on the floor, listening through bone for a change in the iron pulse laid over the mountain's deeper one. Sleep, for them, is only a shallower form of attention.

So on a night when the cloud-sea is steady, the drip-rate even, the choughs silent in the rim-teeth, the power takes the floor. It lays its attention where that hand rests: valve-note, pump-strain, turbine-hum, water moving black and slow under the grating, fig leaves ticking one onto another. It promises nothing in words. It promises in vigilance.

And the keeper, not knowing why, takes their hand off the floor for the first time in years. They turn over in the bunk built for two and sleep alone and completely, the way people sleep in childhood before they learn what depends on them. They wake before dawn with the strange grief and restoration of it, half convinced they have been negligent. Then they hear it: everything still turning. Once, twice per breath. They put their mouth to the blue tap. Stone, and almost nothing. Unchanged. If it had tasted of blessing, they would not have trusted it.

The power asks nothing for this. It does not want worship carried up the cable in sacks. It does not leave a mark, except that things which might have failed on the worst possible night fail instead on a manageable morning, when there is light to work by and another pair of boots under the bench.

And in time — because it is wise, and knows the young mountain will outlast iron — it turns its attention to the one failure no maintenance can prevent. It does not prepare a replacement the way a company prepares a roster. It simply makes sure that, down below the cloud, among the farms drinking water that tastes of stone and almost nothing, there is now and then a child who looks up at weather pouring over a peak and feels not fear but a pull. Someone broad-chested in the making. Someone whose pace will match the fans. Someone who will arrive, years from now, walking beside the cable, and emerge from the steam by the powerhouse so gradually that the old keeper will not be able to say when they were first seen.

That night the old keeper will shout the readings too close to a new ear, and touch an elbow to turn them toward the blue tap, and not yet know they are being allowed, at last, to imagine going down.

Until then, the power stays mostly invisible, keeping to the grating though it has no feet to cut. It does not make the bowl paradise. It keeps the bowl possible. It lets the roar remain, and puts its love underneath it, like water under everything: unnoticed, continuous, moving at exactly the speed at which nothing breaks.
