From above, there is still almost nothing to see.

The power comes the long way, because haste would be the first discourtesy. It does not descend in light. Light here is already spoken for. It does not descend in shadow either, for a shadow crossing that bowl, sudden and single and dark, would turn every face up at once — the goats pausing on the roofs, the children on the bridges, the old in their wall-niches — in a startlement deeper than fear.

It comes in the wind over the plain, which is the one loud thing the place allows itself to ignore, and it stops well short of the boulders, and it looks.

It looks for a long time.

Benevolence, in a lesser power, would already be making lists. There is cold here that gets into arthritic hands. There are backs bent early by low doors, eyes that have never rested in true dark, children who will live and die inside four hundred feet of terraced stone without ever seeing a tree stand up straight, or a horizon that was not a rim. There are deaths that a simple medicine would prevent. There are cramped, damp, crowded, uncomplaining lives, stacked terrace on terrace like swallows under bridges, and surely — surely — something could be *given*.

The power considers its gifts, one by one, the way a person in the market lays down small white stones. And sets each one aside again.

Give them warmth, and the ice rim weeps. The slabs in their stone sockets slump a finger's width, the reflected light goes dull and angled, a true shadow is born under a terrace where ferns have grown for three hundred years without one, and the whole bowl drops, in a season, out of the held degree between frost and melt that it has kept since before anyone's great-grandparents were shown the place in the wall where they would one day sit. Warmth, here, is not kindness. It is weather, and weather is law.

Give them medicine, and fewer children die, and that is good — and then there are more hands than terraces, more mouths than cisterns, more rooms wanted than rooms that can be dismantled to make them. Someone must leave, or someone must not be born, or the goats must come down off the roofs and the ptarmigan out of their niches to make room, and the long negotiation between species, settled so quietly no one alive remembers settling it, comes undone. To save a life in this place without also giving it somewhere to stand is to take the standing-place from something else.

Give them iron, give them engines, give them lamps for a night they have never needed, give them news of the world beyond the fjord — and you have given them loudness, and wanting, and the knowledge of their own smallness as others would measure it. They do not measure it so. Leave them their measure.

Give them nothing at all, says wisdom, and walks away, says a colder kind of benevolence. But that is not quite right either, and the power, staying, sees why.

For the bowl can tend everything inside itself. It has proved that. Blocked pipe, cracked stone, failed bed of peas, a hoist rope fraying, a child born, an old woman dying in her bright sleep — it has an answer for all of these, made of hands. What it has no answer for is the scale of things outside it. It cannot negotiate with a warming century. It cannot finger-bargain, on a cloth, in silence, with a summer that arrives two degrees hotter from ten thousand miles away and begins, politely, impersonally, to melt the rim. It cannot make itself uninteresting to men who would come not through the slot in the boulders, ducking, one at a time, but over the rim with machines, to photograph it, to study it, to preserve it in the way that kills it. Its whole defense has always been that it is hard to find and harder to want. That defense is thinning, as the world's ice is thinning. Not through any fault in the bowl. Through no fault at all. That is precisely the territory of a power: the harm that arrives from beyond the boundary of a creature's possible action.

So it acts — only there. Only at the rim, and outward.

It does not cool the bowl. It cools, by the breadth of a held breath, the wind that crosses the plain toward the bowl on the hottest circlings of a sun that is slowly growing less merciful elsewhere. It does not do this forever, or perfectly; it does it the way the inhabitants mend a wall, stone by unmarked stone, so that the ice neither advances nor retreats for another generation, and another, while the people go on believing — correctly, in every way that matters to them — that the balance is their own.

It turns no one away. Turning away is a kind of event, and events breed stories, and stories breed maps. Instead, those who come up the fjord looking for something to name find the plain very bright, and very much the same in every direction, and after a while their instruments agree with their eyes that there is nothing here, and they are not afraid, and they are not frustrated, only quietly certain they have already looked. A lost traveler, though — a single person, cold, not seeking anything — finds a slot between boulders that seems, for a moment, shoulder-wide enough, and goes down out of the wind. The difference is made in the intention, far out on the ice, without a sound. No one in the bowl ever learns to thank anyone for it. That is how they know it is working, if knowing is the word; it is not the word. They simply notice, some of the old ones, that outsiders remain as rare as they have always been, and say nothing, and skim the cistern.

And inside the bowl itself, the power does — this.

Once, and in the form least likely to be remembered, it lives there.

Not as a ruler. Not as a child of prophecy, or a healer, or a stranger with gifts. It takes the size the place requires, five foot nothing in felt soles, and the work the place assigns to those who arrive with no tenure: carrying. Baskets of turnips up two terraces. Wet laundry down. Cut turf. A pot of horsetails to the shaded place under the hoist where ferns belong. It learns stillness before it learns anything else, which is to say it unlearns, slowly and with genuine difficulty, several millennia of being attended to. It learns to speak three inches from an ear. It learns to wait, mid-step, mid-sentence, with lowered eyes, while an enormous furred bumblebee blunders past its face. It lets lichen take its cuff.

This is not disguise. Disguise implies the truth is elsewhere. For the length of one of those lives — which is not long, and is long enough — it lets the place do to it what the place has done to everyone: shape it to the hollow in the step. It warms its hands at the black stone table like everybody else. It sleeps in snatches behind a felt curtain with a shaped cover over its eyes, and dreams, to its own astonishment, in light, without shadows, and wakes not knowing the hour and not needing to. It is tended when it is sick by hands laid over its hands. When it is old it is given slow things to tend, and tends them.

And when that life ends, quietly, in a bright sleep, a room is dismantled, and another room becomes possible, and the power goes the way the wind goes — out, over the plain, having left almost nothing behind it.

Almost.

If you knew where to look, which no one does, in the retaining wall of the third terrace there is one stone worked around a snail trail as if it were a signature that must not be effaced, and beside it, fitted without mortar in the old way, one stone more than the wall strictly needs. It will be the right stone, centuries from now, on the day a section settles in a manner no one has yet had to repair. The women who keep the cisterns pass it daily and do not see it, because it is the same color as everything else, and moves at the same speed, which is not at all.

That is the whole of the miracle. A climate held at the edge where holding was impossible. A door kept hard to find for those who would break it, and findable for those who need shelter. One unneeded stone, left in the wall against a trouble still unborn. And a single life spent, without announcement, learning the local meaning of enough.

Behind it, below the level of the land, the little city goes on being bright and cold and green and silent. The frost melts off a leaf and re-forms along its edge, and melts again. The hoists rise and fall. Somewhere a child is shown the place in the wall where its great-grandparent sat.

No one worships anything new there. No one is rescued, or elevated, or told. It is noon, as it has been for several hundred years.

The benevolent and wise power, having drawn close, and considered, and loved what it saw, does the hardest thing in its repertoire, the thing that looks from above like nothing at all:

it makes sure that noon is allowed to continue — and then, in felt soles, without a shadow, it leaves the way it came, more quietly than it arrived.
