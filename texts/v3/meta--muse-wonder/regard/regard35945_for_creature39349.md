It would not descend at first.

From above, where the wind still moves over the plain, it would stop at the sharp rim and look down for a very long time. Not because it did not understand what it was seeing. Because it did.

A lesser kindness would have landed in the middle of the ponds by noon. It would have wanted to help. It would have brought better materials than willow, stronger dams than mud, medicine for the kits taken by mink, a warning system for eagles cleaner than a tail-slap, an end to fifteen minutes held breathless under ice in the dark. It would have lifted them up, as kindness so often mistakes lifting for loving, and in a single season of gifts it would have unmade the whole valley.

This one knows the difference between a wound and a way of life.

It watches a dam leak and does not seal it. Two beavers are already there, noses down, feeling the bright thread of escape through their feet, packing mud carried against their chests. The repair is slower than power would make it. It is also theirs, and the kits floating behind their mother are learning the sound water makes when it is wrong, and that learning cannot be given without being stolen. It watches a yearling misjudge a crossing and an eagle begin, very far above the rim, to turn. It does not strike the eagle down. The eagle also belongs. Its shadow is part of the architecture; deep canals were dug because the sky is dangerous, and to make the sky safe would, in time, make the beavers careless, soft, land-going, something other than what built the city.

It watches winter more carefully than summer. That is when an incautious mercy would be most tempted, and most fatal.

Ice joins. Snow erases the merged roofs. The valley goes outwardly empty, and underneath, in black water, a whole population travels by memory from chamber to cache, rationing branches sunk in autumn mud, gnawing in darkness what summer made abundant. Once, in the hardest month, it finds a family that has misjudged. Not through folly. A cache frozen into shallows, a lodge too far from what was stored, kits thinner week by week while plenty stands locked in trees on a white bank they cannot safely reach. The power could open the ice. It could do it invisibly. It does not. Not because it is fastidious about suffering, but because the failure is local, and bearable, and will thin this one lodge and leave willow uneaten and teach the survivors, in the body, what distance means in February. Beavers remember winter the way dams remember water. To erase every consequence would be to erase the intelligence that made the place.

So for a long time - for years, for the whole span in which generations of orange-toothed builders are born furred in the warm dark, help, disperse, pair, and die without ever knowing they were observed - it does nothing that anyone in the valley could point to.

What it does, it does at the edges, where the valley's own competence ends.

Far beyond the rim, where the beaver city has no senses and no jurisdiction, men at intervals discover that high wet plain is useful. A road wants crossing it. A drain wants cutting. A well, a fence line, a mine's pale road switchbacking toward better ground. None of these things is evil. All of them would kill the valley without ever seeing it, by taking the pressed groundwater that weeps from the rock foot, or sending silt and poison down the raw gullies, or bringing feet and dogs and curiosity in numbers no tail-slap can answer.

Those are turned aside, quietly, and always in ways the outside world explains to itself.

A survey line is found, on remeasuring, to lie badly. A company goes cheaper elsewhere. Rain makes the upper track impassable in the one week it mattered, and by the next week the contract has changed. A man walking straight toward the rim with a gun and good intentions feels, for no reason he later trusts enough to mention, that the featureless sedge is depressing country, that there will be nothing over that rise but more of the same, and eats his lunch sitting on a stone and goes back the way he came. Fog, which the valley makes anyway, seems sometimes to sit a little thicker on the rim when engines pass above it. Maps are not falsified. Only attention, which is fragile in every species, is allowed to slide off.

It guards the springs, not the dams.

It keeps the high water table whole. In a drought year, when the plain fires its surface and every shallow pan beyond the hills shrivels to cracked mud, clouds that might have passed are permitted to hesitate here a fraction longer, not in a deluge - a deluge would tear the young rim down in mud and drown nursery chambers - but in the slow, grey, soaking rains this place can use. In a year of too much snowmelt it does not hold back the spate that takes three dams in a night. It watches the whole neighborhood turn out, silent in the risen water, and rebuild by morning. That work is the city renewing its own memory of hands and teeth. It will not be deprived of it.

It lets the city fail, in parts, exactly as it must.

A terrace silts. A dam, unmaintained by a family dead or moved on, settles, softens, lets go at last in spring, and a pond becomes a meadow so green it hums with color, and muskrats come, and after muskrats, sedge and horsetail, and after that, in a decade, alder scrub where no beaver has fed for fifty years, until a two-year-old disperser, wet and exhausted after crossing unknown land, finds the old works mouldering under growth, hears water misbehaving in the old channel, and begins - packing, testing, listening with its feet - to persuade the place back into stillness again. The power does not preserve the capital forever under glass. It preserves the possibility of beavers returning to it. Those are not the same thing, and wisdom is mostly knowing that.

Even its tenderness, when tenderness finally moves it, stays inside the law of the valley.

Once, a kit is caught by a shifting stick in a food raft, pinned, squealing that almost-human cry that carries wrongly far through fog. Its mother is circling, frantic in the contained beaver way, diving, surfacing, diving. No eagle has heard. No otter. Only power, which was not meant to be a hand. The stick settles - a hand's breadth, no more, exactly as much as current and chance might have settled it on a luckier afternoon - and the kit shoots free and is carried at once in small forepaws, riding, ridiculous tail flat, to the lodge roof to be cuffed and warmed and fed. Nothing in the valley records a miracle. A mother has her young. That is enough, and it happens perhaps once in twenty years, and never twice in a way that could teach them to wait for rescue.

Once, a dispersing youngster goes over the rim at the wrong season, down a gully cut to bare till, into a country of dry channels where a wrong turn means foxes and distance and no deep water by nightfall. That night there is, in a hollow he would otherwise have passed in the dark, a seep pool slightly fuller than the season warrants, black, cold, smelling of iron. He drinks. He hides in it till dawn. He lives to found a single lodge on a creek two valleys away, which is, though he will never know it, the city's insurance against the day its own terraces grow old all at once. If the power is thanked, it is only by continuation.

And once every long while it simply stays, through an evening in full summer when the light refuses for hours to leave, and watches them come out. Pair by pair, ripple by ripple. A raft of willow moving as if by itself. Yearlings wrestling in the shallows without sound. Kits asleep in a pile on a warm roof while below them adults patrol the dams at dusk, keeping a world an inch above drowning, an inch above exposure, with no idea that anything larger than an eagle has ever taken notice of them.

That not-knowing is the gift, guarded most carefully of all.

It does not leave a mark in the castor mounds. It does not improve the orange iron of their teeth, or lengthen their lives against otter and cold, or write its approval in any sign a shaman or scientist could later dig out of the peat. It does not want worship, and it does not want specimens. It wants this: that a heavy, low, brown animal that can barely waddle on land should go on being, undisturbed, the sovereign engineer of a sunken valley; that frog and pike and heron and duck should go on existing at the level it chooses to hold the water; that mouthful by mouthful, carried against a wet chest in silence, a place should be persuaded to stay.

So in the end the benevolent and wise power does almost nothing.

It learns the valley by heart, so that at least one consciousness outside it knows what the canals cost.

It keeps the outside world from ending it by accident.

And then, unseen as a nose breaking still water, it withdraws above the rim into the wind - leaving no blessing behind that a beaver would have to become something else in order to receive.
