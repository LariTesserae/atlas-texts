It would not arrive from above.

Anything arriving from above here is either a bird — high, fast, miserable, screaming at a country it refuses to touch — or weather. The people have no use for either kind of visitation. A vertical light, a voice from the sky, a sudden parting of the haze: all of that would only be sent back. The pan would take the shape of the miracle, hold it a heartbeat too long, flatten it, weather it, and return it a season later to frighten somebody else a thousand miles away.

A wise power would know that before it came. A benevolent one would care.

So it would come, if *come* is even the word for a sequence this place cannot be trusted to keep, the other way. Low. Through the mud under the crust. Through the briny, pressurized, ancient water that sighs up through cracks and leaves white mineral lips around the hole. Slower than the flatworms. In the register their sealed skulls were built, over generations, to hear.

It would press itself — not a hand, not yet; an attention, a weight, a sustained lean — against the underside of the world, and it would listen for a very long time before it attempted to say anything at all.

That is the first thing it would do: not answer. Listen.

It would listen until it stopped translating. It would let the surface go past mostly unregistered, the storm-birth a thousand miles away, the sheet-lightning thinking violet in the fog, the rain not yet fallen pattering faintly inside the crust, all the weathered voices still wandering the basin whole lifetimes after their mouths went back into the mud. It would listen down, where the enormous slow syllables move through the drowned caverns, where a sentence might take a year and be, among other things, geology.

It would learn what the inhabitants have always known in their soles: that this place is not broken.

It is tempting, for a power, to misread it as a punishment. The perpetual roar. Ears sealed over since before birth. The ache in the sinuses. Speech driven underground like a shameful thing. Children frightened by quiet, soothed by a thump felt in the teeth. The dead unable to stay decently absent, their tread coming back across the pan on the leading edge of somebody else's storm. Surely, the power might think — any power arriving new, with its heart still louder than its wisdom — surely the gift is obvious.

Silence the pan. Raise the land a little. Give them a hill. Give them an ear that opens, a sky that clears, a voice that says one thing once and is done.

It would be murder wearing mercy's face.

Raise the land and you give the sound somewhere to die, yes — and you give the water somewhere decided to go, and the gardens of blue and meat-colored algae inside the berm-curves go first, and the flatworms below the crust lose the pressure grammar they navigate by, and the tuned soles of the people go blind on dry ground, and the accompaniments — grandmother's stride, the composite trotting presence hammered out of a family's footfall over a generation, all the kept echoes cohering faithfully beside walkers on the flat — scatter at the first slope like spray off a reed. Silence the pan and you do not give these people peace. You take the ceiling off the world. You orphan them in empty air. Their whole civilization, such as it is, is an interface: skull half in air, sole half in mud, body giving the water a shape to remember briefly. Edit the noise and you edit out the people who were edited by it.

The wise power understands this, eventually. Benevolence that will not learn the local definition of harm is just weather with intentions.

So it does not bring silence.

It does not bring height, or fire that matters, or metal, or any hard ringing thing.

It does, in the end, notice the one cruelty the place inflicts that the people have learned to accommodate but have never once, in all their etiquette, pretended to love.

Nothing here is allowed to *finish.*

Footprints fill. Storms sculpt faces and the pan stops giving them back. Berms rot down to the four inches the weather permits. A body settles, sends its small irregular pressures into the crust for days — gas, collapse, the slow give of the rib-hoops — and the family keeps a foot on the ground nearby, receiving it, the way you would hold a hand. That part is tender, and chosen, and good. But later, predictably and never on schedule, the tread returns. And returns. A name shouted once, in birth or terror or warning, escapes and goes around the basin for years and comes back flattened and unfamiliar and may belong by then to the storm that carried it. An answer arrives before its question and must be politely received, noted like weather, never called false. They have built an entire courtesy around the impossibility of ending anything. Persons who are presently embodied. Persons who are presently returning. Nobody screams. Somebody adjusts their pace to match a dead woman's walk for a while, so she will not travel alone.

They do this beautifully. They also do it *forever.* There is a difference, and a power listening long enough from underneath can hear it, in the pauses between the deep booms, where both parties in a ground-conversation listen to the water argue back. It is the sound — no, the pressure — of people who have never been offered the option of setting something down.

What it gives them, then, is not silence. It is an *exit* for sound.

Far out — though *far* is another local opinion — where no berm has ever needed to be built, the power does the smallest thing it can manage. It does not dig. Digging would ring the crust like a drum for a decade. It persuades. Over years it leans its attention into one place in the underlying water until the ancient caverns, very slightly, change their mind about a current. Under the clay, under the sheet-water, under even the blind canoe-long things moving through the mud, a slow gradient forms. Less than a slope. Less than a tendency. A direction in which pressure, arriving, does not quite come back.

On the surface almost nothing is different. The water there is ankle-deep, warm as bathwater, tepid with algae underneath. The horizon thins and loses color and becomes sky. If you stood there — the power hopes, quietly, that you will learn not to stand — you might notice only that your voice, should you be foolish enough to donate it to the air, returns one fewer time than you expected. That the mist takes your outline, repeats it a few yards away, and loses it a little sooner. That the accompaniment trotting beside you, made of spray and your own phase-shifted footsteps, lags — not scrambled, not torn away by a crossing storm, just lags — as if it had heard, from very far below, something it was permitted to consider.

The power does not build the spiral. That matters. It waits, leaning up through the crust in the deep register, and it asks — in tap and sustained pressure, in the grammar that travels through the shared skin of the world, punctuated by long pauses in which it lets the deep water argue back — whether such a place would be wanted. It asks for longer than some of them live. It receives, at least twice, answers to questions it has not finished sending, and it is wise enough not to call either of them false. One is refusal. It honors the refusal for a generation. One is a question shaped like a foot shifting, very slightly, toward the new gradient.

That is enough. Consent, here, was never going to sound like yes.

In time — their time, which is to say out of order and ankle-deep — the people build a berm there themselves. Mouthful by mouthful of good clay, reed bundles dragged and rotting, flat stones laid without striking. A spiral curling the same way against the prevailing pressure, because their hands know that shape the way their feet know the iron-salt stripes hundreds of miles wide. But behind this berm the sound does not drop, the way it drops behind every other well-made spiral, to the inside of a seashell.

Behind this one it drops to *gone.*

Not empty the way the lee of a good berm is empty, that terrifying clean pocket where your thoughts sound obscene and a child raised too long in it panics at the loss of the ceiling. This is lower than that. This is the first place on the continent where a pressure sent down through palm and forehead does not wander, is not weathered, does not come home years later belonging to a storm. Where a name can be spoken into the ground on purpose, by someone who has considered it, and be *kept* — not by the pan, which keeps everything badly and forever, but by the listener underneath, who is large enough to hold a thing without repeating it.

The power becomes, in other words, an ear that does not echo.

It takes the oldest wandering names first, because they are the most tired. Voices that have been going around the basin since before the bodies that made them had great-grandchildren. It does not trap them. Trapping is what the pan already does. It receives them the way the family receives a settling corpse, a foot kept gently on the ground nearby, and it lets them complete. A tread that has accompanied strangers for a century crosses the shallow water toward the spiral one last time, keeping faithful time — and slows, the way a living walker slows arriving home — and does not come out the other side, and is not, afterward, *missing.* The people are precise about this, in their pressure-grammar. Missing is what the pan does. This is the other thing. This is *set down.*

Some of the accompaniments choose it. Some do not, and trot on beside their walkers for years more, and are fed by rhythm and disciplined by sudden stillness exactly as before, because the gift was never meant to be a tidying. A few of the embodied ones go and press their foreheads to the wet earth inside that spiral and tell it things they do not want returned — and this they had always done, but now the ground, in that one place, agrees. The old die with a new option in their etiquette, which is to say they die the same, settled on the surface in the lee of the spiral, weighted gently with reed bundles, the mud taking them back at its obscene patient pace. Only afterward there is a journey their families may make, walking unhurriedly, not migrating toward a pressure-knot, not fleeing weather, just accompanying a rhythm across the flat for the last time instead of the endless next one.

No one is required. The power is very careful, and care at its scale looks almost like absence. It does not mark the place. It does not clear the sky above it or still the storms that are born a thousand miles away and arrive as a condition of the air. Children born behind that berm are born underwater, into hands that keep them submerged a moment too long, so their sealed ears learn the deep register first — and the deep register there now includes, far below the geology, a patience that is not geology, listening, holding what it is given without sending it back.

Perhaps, in the end, that is the only thing a benevolent and wise power ever really does anywhere. It does not lift the low or calm the loud or give the flat a horizon to be measured against. It becomes one true listener in a world that could only repeat — and thereby, without raising anything so much as the height of a man standing, it gives the people of the ankle-deep continent the one dimension they never had.

A way down that ends.

On most days, afterward, you would see nothing. Look for the water going around something that was not a rise a moment ago. Look for a spiral of mud a little fresher than its surroundings, and behind it several wide, low shapes lying with their foreheads pressed reverently to the wet earth, saying nothing audible at all. Beside them, just outside the berm, something made of spray and returned thunder keeps perfect, faithful time with feet that have stopped moving.

Only now, if you watch long enough — longer than is polite, longer than a season — you may see it do something no accompaniment on the pan has ever done before.

You may see it lie down, of its own accord, in the shallow water.

And, gently, and entirely, stop being repeated.
