First, they would do nothing.

Not for a day. Not for a season. A benevolent power can afford haste; a wise one knows that haste is usually vanity dressed as mercy. So they would come to the rim in the mist, below the notice of even the parrots, and they would sit where the eastern terraces steal the morning, and they would listen.

They would listen long enough to understand what they had found.

From below it sounds like noise. From within, given time, it resolves. The swifts over the cicadas over the frogs. The howlers setting off the parrots setting off everything. The bats leaving as the nightjars arrive, the same niches emptying and filling on shifts older than any living body that keeps them. It is not many animals in a ruin. It is one being, breathing in thousands of bodies, metabolizing stone and fig and guano and rain at a pace too large to see, its heart audible, its memory kept not in any mind but in the routes — wall-top to orchard to cistern to roost — walked and flown and hopped so faithfully that the routes themselves remember.

A lesser power would want to bless it loudly. To raise one species up, give it speech, give it a name for itself, make it grateful. To restore the builders, or call their descendants back up the stairs to marvel and learn. To make the place a lesson.

They would do none of that.

Speech would be a burden here. Restoration would be an eviction. And the place is not a lesson for anyone else. It finished being human without bitterness and it is not waiting, in any part of its ten thousand throats, to be forgiven or completed or witnessed. To turn it into a message would be to take it away from the ones who live in it and give it, once again, to the ones who left.

So the wise power learns the hardest form of love, which is to leave a good thing unmarked.

What they protect, they protect at the edges.

Down in the hot low country, where the stairs begin, a surveyor's line will bend, almost imperceptibly, around a valley that does not quite stay in the mind. A road crew will find the ground unpromising and go the long way, and feel sensible about it. A man with a map and a gun and an appetite for scarlet feathers will walk all day in confident circles and come home footsore and oddly unwilling to try again. Clouds will keep their old habit of closing over the bowl by midmorning. Nothing dramatic. No wall of fire, no forgetting laid like a curse. Just the world, nudged at its margins, continuing to overlook a place that has earned its obscurity.

And subtler still, at the other margins — the ones no animal can guard.

The slow change in the lowland heat, creeping uphill year by year, will meet, at about six thousand feet, a stubbornness in the mist. The cisterns will stay half full. A sickness carried in on one bat's wing from a distant, disturbed cave will burn itself out in that one warm body, gently, in sleep, before it can learn the colony. A storm that might have taken the whole western terrace in one slide will spend itself instead in a thousand designed channels, as the builders intended, and the fig roots that are now the wall will hold. No miracles the tenants could point to, if they pointed. Only the conditions of their continuity, quietly kept — as a gardener does not make the garden grow, but keeps the gate against goats.

Inside the valley: nothing.

They will not heal the agouti taken by the owl, nor stop the monkeys raiding, nor thin the bees, nor console the frog in the drain. Predation and rot and fruit-fall are not flaws in this being. They are its circulation. To interfere there would not be kindness; it would be authorship.

Once — once only, and because benevolence, however wise, is not the same as indifference — they walk down into it.

Not in glory. Glory would empty every terrace ahead of them in a shrieking green sheet. They go small, and barefoot, and unremarkable, smelling of rain and stone, and the city does to them what it does to everything: it flows around them, annoyed, and resettles at arm's length. A capuchin watches from a wall-top with the bright entitled stare of a tenant. A frog goes on pulsing in its drain mouth. Lizards resume their push-ups on a warm stone. This is the truest welcome the place has to offer, and they receive it as the honor it is: to be treated not as a god, nor a threat, nor a returning owner, but as one more body passing through rooms sized, conveniently, for bodies.

They leave nothing behind that can be found. No shrine, no carving, no new species of orchid in the niches, no spring that wasn't there before. Perhaps — if you dug, which the animals do everywhere — you might find one afternoon's worth of work where no work was needed: a fallen branch lifted off a canal mouth and laid where the monkeys will drag it, in time, to a sleeping corner. A spout cleared of leaves. Mundane things. The kind of help you can give a neighbor without making them owe you.

And on the way out, at the head of the long stairs, they do the only lasting thing.

They do not seal the valley. Sealing is for treasure, and this is not treasure. They do not promise it eternity; eternity is not theirs to promise, and the being in the terraces, slow and settled, does not ask for it. They simply take its part, quietly, in the counsels of the world — one old, deafening, feathered and furred and rooted life, to be weighed as a life, whole and sufficient, when the fates of valleys are weighed.

Then they go down through the thinning forest into the hot low country, and the mist closes behind them, and the swifts stitch the sound shut over their going.

Behind them the cisterns sweat under their mats of fern. The parrots explode off a wall and settle on the same wall. The bats pour out at dusk in rivers, and the frogs get louder to compete, and it is night and day and fig season and mango season in the stone town that no one is saving, because no one needs to — guarded, at last, only in the one way it ever asked to be:

by being let alone to go on.
