It would not lift the mat.

That would be the first temptation, and the first thing it renounces. To lift the mat would be to let in light and weather and eyes. To raise the ceiling, to dry the lanes, to make the room standable, permanent, safe — every improvement it could easily make would be a way of killing the city while congratulating itself for having saved it.

A benevolent power without wisdom would arrive the way we arrive: huge, warm, breathing like weather, full of gifts the place cannot use. Fire. Height. Dryness. Food dropped from above in impossible abundance. A winter stopped at its mildest hour and held there. The voles taken up, perhaps, into some softer keeping, where their teeth would grow with nothing civic to spend themselves on, and slowly become the instruments of their own suffocation.

So it makes itself, instead, almost nothing.

It does not come in through the slit. It does not need to. A wise attention can learn a room without entering it the way a hand learns, in darkness, the shape of a thing it has decided not to pick up. It quiets its own presence until it is no warmer than the peat, no louder than a drip changing its rhythm. It waits — not for an audience, not for gratitude, not even, at first, for understanding.

It listens the way the oldest vole listens, with the whole skull.

What it finds, listening, is not a problem awaiting a solution. That is the difficult part. Under the mat is not a cry for help mistaken by human ears for silence. It is a going concern. Lanes cleared. Stores sorted with insulting neatness. Drains shell-lined against collapse. Latrines capped. Middens ridged. False tunnels sweet with mouse-smell, dug for the weasel who has not yet come this winter and may not, but whose possibility has already been given architecture. Three exits, known by heart. Bridges rebuilt stick by stick while traffic still crosses the sagging ones. A rota instead of a ruler. A city founded in November and built, from its first gnawed doorway, to be abandoned correctly in spring.

An unwise benevolence aches to make such a thing *last*. The power, if it is wise, has to sit with the ache and learn from it. The city is not failing at permanence. It is succeeding at season.

It will not, therefore, stop the thaw.

When the ice loosens — and it will, and should — the mat will drop or tear. The rill, that bright black thread no wider than two fingers, will widen and blacken and take the lanes. The communal hollow will fill. The fungus chamber, the bottle-cap vault, the polished root entrance worn shining by one season of feet: all of it will go under in an afternoon, exactly as its builders always, in their bundled seeds and floating storehouses and upward-angled tunnels, expected.

To prevent that flood would be to steal the meaning out of every preparation they ever made.

What, then? Anything?

Yes. But only at the margins, where the scale of threat stops being negotiable by teeth.

The voles can negotiate with roots. They can trim a doorway faster than the mat can close it, hold a runway open against a night's growth of root hairs, chew themselves free each morning from the white filaments that grow, tenderly and without malice, toward their sleeping warmth. They can negotiate with water in its ordinary winter amounts: the drip, the seep, the rill rising against its banks when wind presses the ice. They can negotiate even with the weasel, after a fashion, with architecture and misdirection.

They cannot negotiate with a ditch dug through the marsh in an hour. They cannot gnaw back a drainage scheme, or unlay a pipe, or argue with the brief human conviction that wet ground is wasted ground. Their whole civilization is pitched, deliberately, below the threshold of notice — root taps instead of voices, scent instead of signs — and that strategy fails absolutely against a creature that does not need to notice a city in order to erase the conditions that let one form.

There, at that boundary, the power acts.

Not with thunder. Never with thunder; thunder would carry down through the mat like the end of the world. It acts the way weather acts on human intention, slowly and deniably. In late winter a man comes out onto the marsh with a spade and a plan he has been carrying since Christmas, when the ground was iron and everything seemed improvable. He stands a long time looking at black pools holding nothing of the sky. The place strikes him, not as worthless — that thought might harden him — but as *already occupied by its own purposes*, in a way he cannot name and does not try to. His boots fill. His resolve, which was mostly a way of being annoyed at wet land, quietly goes out of him. He remembers a fence elsewhere. He goes home. He will tell nobody he changed his mind; he will not be entirely sure that he had one.

No shrine is built. No fence is raised; a fence is a sign, and a sign is a summons, and feet follow summonses. The marsh is not declared sacred. It is merely allowed, year after year, to remain unremarkable to the kinds of attention that arrive with machinery. Sedge and bog-cotton laid down in brown mats. Pools skinned with ice. A place you would walk past. A place you would walk over.

And within the room, nothing. Deliberately, laboriously nothing.

It does not warn them of the fox, because they already spend their lives with one ear turned toward foxes. It does not clear the exit tunnel that a sliding root half-blocks in February, because a shrew finds it before dawn, and then two voles, and by the time the power might have interfered the work is already being done at exactly the speed of its necessity, and the tunnel is open, and a little wider than before. It does not feed them in the hard week when the cold deepens and the whole population draws back into the communal hollow and the outer city stands empty and clean and beautiful in the phosphor-dark, lanes swept, stores sealed, bridges lashed down. Hunger, in that week, is not a mistake in the system. It is the pressure that keeps the system honest — and besides, the stores were counted for it. They knew February was coming when they built in November. To feed them would be to tell them their counting did not matter.

Once — only once — it is tempted almost past bearing. Deep in the coldest tightening, when the mat flexes down and every drip in the city changes its rhythm, a sleeping cluster in the communal hollow has been netted more thoroughly than usual by root hairs grown fast toward the warmth of many small bodies at once; and one young vole, the smallest, wakes tangled to the throat, panicking in the silent, breathless way of a creature that cannot afford to cry out. The others are already chewing. They will reach her. They have reached each other every morning of her short life.

The power watches the small work done by small teeth, and does not help, and understands that withholding help *is* the help — that to be trusted, by a place like this, means being indistinguishable from the place continuing on its own.

Its one true gift is attention without appetite.

Something in that room is, at last, fully witnessed by a mind large enough to have destroyed it by noticing it wrongly. Not catalogued. Not uplifted. Not given speech or fire or a soul it was somehow assumed to be waiting for. Witnessed as sufficient. The shrews in their vibrating hunger, the frogs pulsing once in their glassy mud cells and then a long time later again, the snails being doors, the moths being a roof, the fungus being a stomach, the voles being citizens — none of it translated into anything else. Let be, in the deepest sense of *let*: permitted, and blessed in its permission.

That may not sound like much. Down there it is everything. The city's whole prayer, if it has one, is never spoken and goes something like this: *do not make the world so safe that we are no longer necessary to one another.*

In spring the power keeps its promise.

The thaw comes as thaw should — not in one catastrophic noon but in pulses, in the old rhythm the foraging parties already time their work by: drip, slacken, drip again. Ceiling-water finds every channel the city cut for it. The rill rises. It takes the low lanes first, as it was always going to, and traffic moves to the high lanes in good order, and then abandons those too. Bundles come out of the storehouses. The floating floors remember their second purpose. Somewhere under the mat there is a movement like a held breath finally released upward, tunnel by tunnel, toward the hummocks that stay above water longest.

The power does not hold the mat up for one more day so that somebody might say goodbye to it. Nobody down there says goodbye to rooms. They leave correctly. That is their goodbye.

It watches the last vole go — blunt-nosed, seed-burdened, pausing at the root-sieve to listen through her skull one final time to a surface world she is about to have to live in — and it does not follow her up to make sure. Following is also a kind of ownership.

Then the room fills black to the ceiling in an afternoon. The bottle cap goes over with a soft, rusted sigh. The safety pin, gate and monument, settles deeper into the mud of an empty plaza. Root hairs, freed of all civic restraint, grow down and down into the drowned dark and begin the long summer work of turning a city back into peat.

Next autumn, perhaps, water will rise under another raft of living sedge and cold will lock its edges down and a pocket will stay open where no pocket should be, low and warm and smelling green where the roots snapped. Perhaps voles will find it. Perhaps they will not, that year; the power does not arrange that either. It has learned the difference between keeping a place possible and insisting a place repeat itself.

It keeps the marsh possible. It keeps its own hands off the rest.

And on certain cold nights, long after the lanes are gone, it listens — through ice, through sedge, through a hand's thickness of living plant — not for anything in particular. Just to confirm that emptiness, properly left alone, is once again failing.

A drip, counting itself. A root, tapped twice.

Someone awake, trimming a doorway back from the growing dark.
