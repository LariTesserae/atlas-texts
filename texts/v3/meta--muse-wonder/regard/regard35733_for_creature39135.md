They would come up by the lift.

Not in glory — Sessra could not survive glory. Hard sunlight would blind eyes made for blue dusk, would burn skin that has never learned what to do with noon, would boil the shallow pools of good air right out of the market squares. A power that arrived as radiance here would not be benevolent, whatever it intended. It would be weather. It would be a disaster with good intentions.

So they would come up the way everyone comes up: two days on the riveted carriage, on chains thick as tree trunks, sleeping in way-stations bolted to the cliff, getting the altitude wrong in their body a little at a time. They would let the air thin. They would let their ears misbehave. They would arrive at the top pale and slightly deaf and entirely unimpressive, and the first thing they would do — the first true sign of wisdom — would be to buy a bell.

Not a beautiful one. A plain brass bell, locally made, with a tone no one in Sessra already had.

And they would learn to ring it when entering a room.

Because up here that is not ritual. That is courtesy. You announce yourself. You distinguish yourself from your echo. You give whatever is following you, or preceding you, or listening from the sealed room above the shop, the fair chance to be something other than you.

They would stay a while before doing anything at all.

They would count stairs. Badly, at first. Fourteen in the morning, fifteen by evening, and they would stand there like any newcomer with the chalk in their hand, understanding in their bones why residents count out loud, why they do not open the extra door, why they seal it *politely, from their side.* They would learn the forge-hammer's twenty-two seconds until it lived in their teeth. They would hold their breath across the Thin Streets and fail, embarrassingly, halfway, with sparrows scattering ahead of them and some old woman at a brass corner post waiting, without pity and without embarrassment, for the air to come back down the street like a tide. They would let her grandchild tow them the rest of the way.

They would not lift the ceiling.

This would be the great temptation, and they would refuse it. From below, from outside, it looks like oppression: that breathing, dripping membrane a mile above the plateau, filtering the sun to a submarine twilight, remembering the day badly at midnight. Lift it, a lesser kindness would say. Let them have light. Let them have weather that other people recognize as weather. Let the figs ripen honestly and the clocks keep time.

But the Sessrans have built a whole civilization inside the dimness. Their bats are the size of cats. Their flowers open six feet wide for darkness. Their children read timetables in pitch black and blink too slowly in lowland glare. People stay, the residents say, because the light is dim enough to think in. To tear the ceiling away would be to finish Sessra all at once, in one direction, forever.

A wise power does not finish things that are still becoming.

And this — slowly, counting room by room — is what they would come to understand: the being who lives here is not hiding in the spire. The being *is* the spire's slow intention, and the plateau is only the part of it that has, so far, agreed to be a place. The mountain extruding a cobbled lane overnight is not a miracle and not an infestation. It is a thought, arriving in basalt. The house budding around a hot fissure with its windows already sweating is a sentence the world is trying, clumsily and generously, to say. The tenant the great Finisher once talked out of arriving — *it had accepted, politely, that nobody lived there yet* — was not a ghost.

It was an early draft of someone.

That is why the Finishers are so gentle. They think they are doing carpentry. In fact they are doing midwifery with the sound turned down: persuading a new world to take on only as many doors as it can be responsible for, one decade at a time.

The power would not take that work out of their hands. It would apprentice itself to it.

For a season, for a year, for as long as it took — time pools here anyway, no one would keep close account — there would be one more figure in the mornings near the fresh rock, toolbelt slung low, sitting in a half-formed house and ringing a plain brass bell at intervals, counting the rooms aloud. *One. Two. Three.* Over and over, not to impose a number, but to offer one. To give the mountain a rhythm it could argue with. The Finishers would know, the way Sessrans know everything important, without being told: by the pauses. The stranger waited for echoes to finish. The stranger never talked over a wall that was deciding whether to branch. The stranger, when a staircase produced a fifteenth step at dusk, did not seal it at once. They sat down on the fourteenth, rang once, clearly, and listened — with one ear, perhaps, twenty seconds early, and the other right on time — to find out whether this door was an accident, or an appetite, or an invitation.

Once — only once — they would intervene in the way the stories will later swear no one intervened at all.

A surveyor would be lost on a staircase that kept adding landings in the fog, counting upward, fourteen, fifteen, fourteen, fifteen, his chalk gone, his bell-code coming back to him from above in his own voice, almost right. And from below, unhurried, unpanicked, a stranger's voice would begin to count him down. Not command him. Count him. *Fourteen. Thirteen. Twelve.* Giving him a number of stairs the world could agree on. Giving him, step by step, a way to be only himself again. He would arrive at the bottom with one fingernail turned to dark glass and no memory of the rescue, and the Office of Emergence would file his report upside-down, and for the rest of his life he would flinch a breath before the forge-hammer fell, and be, on balance, not unhappy.

Small mercies. That is the discipline.

They would mend the lift chains a link before they failed, in the night, in the rain off the pipes, leaving the repair so plainly competent that the maintenance crews would shrug and credit each other. They would leave one extra pool of good air at the worst corner of the Thin Streets, knee-deep, then chest-deep, beside the brass post where the old people wait — not enough to change the climate, only enough to change an afternoon. The great moths would gather there, beating their slow dinner-plate wings in air now just barely thick enough for everyone, and the surveyors would note it and leave a lamp burning and go away, which in Sessra amounts to a blessing.

They would do nothing about grief. Nothing has been worn smooth up here, not even sorrow, and the old are expected to carry theirs polished and terrible and ring true when struck. A power that took that sharpness away would be taking away the evidence that things here are still new enough to matter. Instead: they would sit with the bereaved in doorways where a dead person's bell hangs for a year in the condensation, and they would not explain the occasional ring on a windless night, and they would not take it down early.

And down below, quietly, they would protect the wrongness of the maps.

Explorers would keep misjudging the altitude. Investors with plans to quarry the fresh basalt, to straighten the load-bearing walls, to drain the cisterns and pipe in honest sunlight and finish the mountain properly on a schedule — their letters would go astray in flooded undercrofts. Their survey stakes would be found, in the morning, politely relocated to bare rock. Their echoes would agree to contracts their mouths had never made, in voices that did not quite know the bell-code, and their lawyers, sensibly, would advise withdrawal. Sessra would remain ruinously expensive to supply, forty minutes between districts, nine or ten industrial towns holding on by causeways and faith — not worth conquering. Worth only visiting, slowly, at the price of learning its manners.

At the center, when they finally went up the spire alone, they would not summon the being. Summoning is just finishing something by command.

They would bring a bell.

Not brass. Stone. Black glass from the mountain's own fresh folds, still warm from the fissure, shaped — coaxed — into a bell that could only ring at the temperature of the rock that made it. They would hang it in the oldest unfinished doorway, the one the Finishers return to decade after decade, the arch that breathes warm mineral air and has never yet decided to become a room. They would hang it beside the chalk marks, at exactly the height a hand would reach for, if the hand existed. And they would ring their own plain bell once, clearly, so the mountain could learn the difference between a visitor and its echo.

Then they would say, out loud, counting as they went, the thing no one has yet thought to offer a world in the act of being born:

You do not have to be only one thing. You do not have to be finished to be inhabited. Take the century. Take the pooled time in the Long Market, take the slow firing of the furnaces, take the twenty-two seconds between hammer blows. We will keep the stairs counted. We will keep the extra doors sealed politely, from our side, until you knock properly.

And they would leave the stone bell unringed.

That would be the whole miracle: a way for the being to announce itself, when it is ready, in a tone no frog, no echo, no sealed-room voice could counterfeit — because it would be rung from the inside of the stone, at the mountain's own warmth, in a code it invents itself. On the day it rings — a year from now, a hundred, during some future Leveling Festival's one terrifying silent hour when every other bell in Sessra is stilled — ten thousand workers will stop with their hands on ten thousand iron screws, and listen, and know at last whether they have been living *on* a mountain, or *with* someone.

Until then, the power would stay long enough to be unremarkable. Long enough to turn their quarter-turn at the Festival with everyone else, in the silence, shoulder to shoulder with greenhouse keepers and shepherds and deaf foundry families, putting their back into keeping the city in agreement with itself. Long enough that children would learn their bell and answer it across a tram platform, shyly, while their mouths did something more respectable.

And then they would ride the funicular down through the weather, through the forests, into the obscene bright edges of the lowlands, still counting under their breath — twenty-two, twenty-two — still reaching, now and then, for a railing that isn't there.

Leaving behind them, in the blue-grey dusk that never entirely lifts, almost nothing changed.

Almost.

One more place where the air gathers. One staircase that gives its lost back. One link in the lift chain that does not fail in the winter dark. And high in an unfinished doorway above a spire of midnight stone, where the moths gather thickest and the surveyors leave their lamps burning, a black glass bell, growing warmer, very slowly, year by year — waiting for the mountain to decide it has a hand, and something to say, and all the time in the world, pooled and patient, in which to say it.
