If such a power came to the High Anvil, the first thing it would have to learn would be how not to arrive.

Anything that lands here cleanly — in one place, in one moment, coincident with itself — is either very small or very dangerous. The Smelters learned that long before they learned anything else. So it would learn, out of courtesy, to be displaced a little. To let its image lag. To let its sound come from the wrong side of the flats. To walk not by sight but by absence: by the patch of iron-flies that are suddenly *not* there, by the second shadow moving beside the shadow a boulder is supposed to have, by the pressure on the air of a body that prefers not to be looked at directly.

And it would stay a long time before deciding anything.

That would look, from any human vantage, like doing nothing. It would be the hardest work it has ever done.

Because the temptations would arrive in order, and each one would wear the face of mercy.

The first temptation would be water.

To bring the ghost-rain down the last fifty meters. To let it land, at last, on the black glass and the sulfur playas, to give the rust-crust a drink, to cool the basalt that ticks and expands at noon, to make this place — so obviously, so painfully thirsty to anyone made of water — *less thirsty.*

A benevolent power that was not wise would do it before sunset on the first day.

A wise one would feel the rain die on its own forehead, cool and gone on the tongue, and understand: nothing here is thirsty. Thirst is a memory water leaves in things. These lineages left water so long ago they no longer remember the shape of it. Their humors are hot oils and liquid salts and mercury-bright things that move through mineral veins and would be poisoned, diluted, *drowned* by kindness. The calf is born idling on furnace charge, not milk. The crust eats iron, not rain.

To bring water to the Anvil would not save it. It would be a flood in a language nothing here speaks.

So it would not.

The second temptation would be to cool it. To shade the sun, thin the shimmer, still the thermal river rising invisible off the flats. The third would be to cure it — to pull every Smelter back into phase with its own image, to make seen and real occupy the same honest coordinates, to finish, at last, the nine-hundred-year footfall in the east basin, where one animal hangs smeared into forty horizontal copies of itself, leg raised, still deciding.

It would want to finish that footfall very badly. Watching it would feel like watching a held breath.

But it would walk the plateau first — at Smelter tempo, which means it would take longer than most prayers — and it would see what the displacement *does*. How a predator lunges, furnace-mouthed and magnetic, and closes its jaws on a glow forty meters and ninety seconds from the throat it wanted. How a calf can be tended by a ring of mothers who are not visibly there. How an ecology built on enormous, slow, charged nervous systems simply has perception arriving bent, the way light bends in deep water, and anything forced straight would be forced *blind*.

And in the east basin it would not pull the leg down, and it would not lift it free. It would do something much smaller, and much more difficult: it would stand attendance on the fault. It would keep predators out of that basin, quietly, for as long as the decision takes — another nine hundred years if necessary — so that an animal caught between arriving and having left cannot be taken advantage of for being caught. It would lend the place a little extra stubbornness of distance. It would make the basin very hard to reach for anything hungry, and very easy to leave for anything that finally chooses to.

That is the kind of help the Anvil can survive: help that does not change its nature, only defends its time.

So here is what it would actually do.

It would strengthen the first lie.

From a distance, from any human vantage, the Anvil already looks empty. It would make that emptiness hold a little longer against better instruments. Not forever — forever is a kind of building, and there are no buildings here — but long enough. Survey craft passing overhead would find their readings politely uninteresting. Distances would become stubborn specifically for prospectors, for anyone arriving with drills in their intentions and ingots in their arithmetic. A fumarole an hour away would take them three days. Then four. Their echoes would start answering them a fraction too early, in voices shaped almost like syllables, until their nerve, quite sensibly, gave out.

The herd would never notice. Geologists with pure curiosity might still, occasionally, be allowed through. The Anvil has opinions about the difference.

It would tend to beginnings and endings, but only in the herd's own grammar. When a calf is born idling, chest-pistons stuttering, unable to start itself, the herd already knows what to do. It does not need a miracle. But sometimes the herd is spread thin across a crossing, sometimes the ring is slow to close. Then — once in a few centuries, no more — a low, tender booming might come from one more direction than the plateau can account for. An extra share of charge poured across the gap. A jump-start with no visible source. The calf's vents flutter orange for the first time, the hammer-mites arrive within the hour, and no one, including the calf, ever knows there was a risk it might not catch.

And when one dies, which takes years, furnace banking chamber by chamber, plates cooling to the temperature of the surrounding rock — it would not hurry that either. It would only keep the scavengers to their proper pace, and let the dead become geology in peace. In a century the corpse is a boulder formation growing rust-crust in its lee. Which is grazed. That is not tragedy here. That is pasture keeping faith with itself.

It would learn the infrasound.

Not speak it — speak is too fast a word — but add its attention to it, the way you add your voice to a choir by listening so completely that the note steadies. The herd passes route-knowledge down through pressure that rearranges your sternum: grazing-knowledge, crossing-knowledge, the memory of a destination it will reach after everyone who ever named it is a mineral deposit. The power would let one new thing pass very slowly through that song. Not a command. Not a revelation. Just the memory, carried from old to merely ancient, that *once they were witnessed by something that could have changed them and chose not to.*

That may be the only gift a complete world can accept from outside itself: the knowledge that it was seen, accurately, and left intact.

And in the end it would leave no monument, because monuments are for places that are finished. It might leave — if it left anything at all — one cooled ingot among the natural boulders that is not quite natural: slag laid down in a perfect, sheltered lee, already growing its first hand's-breadth of crust slightly faster than a century would normally allow. A small head start for a grazer not yet born. Nothing a Smelter would ever thank it for. Nothing a human would ever distinguish from the plateau.

Then it would go the way it came: a little out of phase, sound arriving from the wrong valley, image grazing peacefully on empty glass two hundred meters from where it actually passed.

The grinding would not stop. The piston-sighs would not diminish at night. Your teeth would still ache near a crossing, your shadow would still bend toward a mass you cannot see, and the metallic rain of flies would still fall all at once in the silence before a real foot comes down.

Four creatures before dark. Thousands in your molars.

Exactly as it should be.

A benevolent and wise power would not save the High Anvil from being itself. It would save it *for* being itself — which means, mostly, standing between it and everyone who arrives carrying water, and cures, and other mercies it never asked for.

It would guard the emptiness that isn't.

And it would let the herd keep walking, glowing, arriving slightly out of true, toward a destination it already remembers.
