They would do almost nothing.

That is how you would know they were wise, and how you would know they were benevolent. A lesser power — one that merely loved this place, or merely pitied the woman moving across it — would arrive with rescue in its hands.

It would stop the builders in the stone. It would thin the warm air back to honesty and lift the veil and call this unfinished bowl finished. It would heal the keeper's hoarse voice, deepen her sleep, unmake the white mineral maps on her forearms, give her solid ground that behaved like ground elsewhere in the world. It would preserve her. It would preserve the Shelf. It would ruin both in the exact manner they have spent all these years learning not to ruin each other.

So the power is not admitted. Admission implies the pylons could keep it out.

One evening it is simply there, inside the shimmer, standing — no, not standing — beside the central pool. Weight rolling from heel to toe. Left foot, right foot. A small, graceless, necessary dance. It has taken one look at the law of the place and obeyed it more scrupulously than any visitor. It shuffles. The glass frogs do not stop for it. A rail barks at it from across the bowl and it laughs, startled and pleased, at exactly the wrong moment to be believed supernatural, and the keeper, passing with a dissolving flag in one hand, tells it without looking up to keep moving or the ground will take an interest.

*I know,* it says. *You taught it better than that.*

She does look up then.

It does not offer her eternity. It does not offer the Shelf a forest by morning, rain that knows its riverbed, stone that can be trusted. Those things are coming, at their own pace, and to deliver them early would be no different from leaving a child in permanent noon. It has not come to shorten anyone's childhood.

It has come because the two of them — place and keeper — have done something so rare that it drew attention across whatever distance attention travels for such beings. They have loved each other without holding on. The Shelf has learned her the way it learned the goats' hooves and then let the lesson rise and smooth out of its own new rock. She has narrated streams back into existence and then let the stone drink them anyway. She has moved the bench hundreds of times. Nothing here has been allowed to become an anchor. Nothing here has been loved carelessly enough to be kept.

Almost nothing.

Because the power, stooping — still shuffling, one ankle turning slow circles — puts its palm flat on a low ridge that is merely settling, and then on one that is *confused*, and feels the difference she feels through her bones, that deep stressed groan going through new basalt, and it understands the part she has never said aloud even to the drones. She is good at telling the Shelf when it is learning something wrong.

She is not good at knowing it in herself.

Her boot prints linger a fraction longer now than they did her first year. She has a cut across one knuckle from the morning that closed, tick tick, with a bright seam too eager to finish. She sleeps in shorter and shorter pieces. The hut sign, on wet mornings, has begun to call her Mother because some human program thought the word would be comforting, and she changes the subject every time by laughing too loudly at a rail. She knows she is temporary by design. She has begun, privately, traitorously, to brace for the design.

The power does not tell her not to grieve. It does not tell her the Shelf will remember her, which would be a pretty lie. Rock that has been told finally to stop growing remembers almost nothing. Erosion is a longer and truer art than inscription. Her footprints will fill. Her narration will not be archived in the terraces. Succeeding rain will not know her name for the pools.

Instead it walks with her. One full circuit, ten minutes across, if you keep moving, which they do. She talks because talking is work here, and because visitors have always needed her to fill the silences before the crickets do. She tells the stream where it ran at breakfast. She coaches a pool that wants to drain uphill. She says *seven, eight, turn* under a new spine during a lull in the dripping, and taps the wet rock twice as though it might need keeping time while it learns its own weight.

The power listens the way finished things, until now, have only been described to this place. Not fixing her sentences. Not completing the stream's course for it. Its listening is so complete that, just after the light goes amber through the mesh and the pools hold the color a moment longer than the air, she stops mid-sentence in a pause that belongs to no frog and no cricket.

For the first time in years, she stands still.

Nothing takes her.

The builders do not recede — the power has not commanded them, because that would teach the Shelf that mercy comes from outside. It has only, for the span of one sunset, held its own attention under her boots like a second, invisible ground, so the choice to rest is hers and the danger is, briefly, not. Crust does not climb. Quartz does not quest up her laces. Her knees, those loose and tireless hinges, discover what lock feels like. The fatigue comes up through her all at once, years of it, honest as water.

She weeps standing upright in the middle of the Hatching Shelf, with her feet planted, which is the most radical thing she has done since she arrived. Her tears fall straight down and are proofread out of the stone almost immediately. That, too, is right.

The power does not embrace her; an embrace would be another kind of stillness imposed. It simply stays close enough that she is not alone in the risk.

Then, because wisdom without timing is just philosophy, it opens the veil.

Not all of it. Not the dispersal that will one day be the Shelf's graduation into weather. Just one seam, high above the newest spine — a needle's width of true, unsoftened, unborrowed mountain sunlight, allowed down for less than a minute onto stone that has only ever known light through dark lenses, as through a remembered sea.

The spine does not crack. The ridge does not flinch. Mist rushes up in a white shout. The frogs go berserk. Somewhere a terrace sings — not the borrowed thunder of rehearsal, but a clear mineral note under real sun — and holds.

She hears it. Of course she hears it. She has been listening for whether this place could survive being left alone, and for one minute it rehearses her absence and is not diminished by the rehearsal.

*Soon,* she says. Not a question.

The power says her name.

Not Warden. Not Monitor, Attendant, Mother. The name she had down below, before restlessness was a qualification, which no sign here has ever printed and which she has not heard spoken in the damp air for longer than she keeps count of. It returns her to herself as something other than the function this beautiful unfinished place required. She is not the Shelf's. She was lent, the way the rails were lent; the terrible and merciful difference is that she knows it.

That night, the slung net in the hut does not swing. The keeper-drone approaches twice with its patient irregular taps and twice withdraws, confused by an instruction older than its instructions. She sleeps through the night — whole, deep, uncrusted — while the power sits wakeful on the buzzing steps, taking the taps meant for her, flexing borrowed hands in the dark, moving just enough. Her boots rest beside it unresurrected until morning. No one cuts her free from anything.

It is the only miracle, and it leaves no evidence.

In the morning it is gone, or has withdrawn to the scale of builders, or of weather, where benevolence does its least visible work. It has not altered the timeline by decree. But somewhere in the minds of distant administrators, a set of readings from the Shelf — a terrace that sang under hard light, drainage found an inch truer, a ridge that learned its load early — will be interpreted, correctly, as readiness arriving a season sooner than projected. A relief roster will acquire a name. A departure will be scheduled while she is still able to walk out under her own power. No one will feel pushed. That is the size of hand this power uses on human affairs.

And long afterward, when the pylons have powered down and the veil has dispersed like a held breath let go, there will be one stream in that high bowl that does something streams elsewhere consider inefficient. It will run purposefully between banks at breakfast, vanish into stone by evening, and continue a little while underground, muffled and stubborn, tracing not the shortest course downhill but a looping, narrated one — past a terrace, under a spine, around a pool that has finally learned where it lives. Travelers who do not know why will find themselves counting as they pick their way across the old Shelf, for balance, for no reason. *Seven, eight, turn.* They will assume it is something about the terrain.

It will be. That is all memory ever needed to be here.

As for her: she will walk out through the shimmer with her ears popping the wrong way, squinting like a newborn in the thin air, furious that breathing is suddenly such thin work. She will discover that the restless body the Shelf employed has not been ruined for the lower world, only employed by it, and that there are other young and unfinished things down below that cannot bear stillness either. She will fidget. She will pace small rooms. She will be driven, as she always was, toward whatever is still deciding what it will be.

Behind her, on ground learning erosion at last, her footprints will fill in at exactly the same speed as anyone's.

And one evening, years later, in some quite ordinary place, fatigue will rise and she will — without deciding to, without bracing — stand still in amber light and rest, and the ground will simply be the ground.

She will laugh out loud, then, at the size of it, like it is a joke a rail once made across an absurd distance.
