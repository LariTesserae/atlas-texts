They would not bring light.

That is the first test, and a power that failed it would not be wise, however benevolent it meant to be. Light here is not a gift. It is a weather event that has never happened, a violence for which nothing in the dark has a skin. Neither would they bring water in abundance, or rain, or a cooler air, or eyes, or fire, or a faster way to live. All of those sound like mercies from the surface. Down here they would arrive as change, and change is the one predator this country has never learned to wait beside, perfectly still, until it passes.

They would lower themselves to thirty-eight degrees.

They would come, if *come* is even the word, without a lamp in the fist, without dead cloth, without the intolerable brightness of a face working through fear and wonder. They would make themselves legible in the local grammar: heat in the shape of a body, slow breath, no startle. They would learn to move so the ash took no false print from them, or — more honestly, because even a god must touch a floor to stand on it — they would accept that any print they left would be read for a hundred years, and they would choose where to put their feet as carefully as the bands choose where to crack a brine pocket.

And then they would do nothing, for a very long time.

Not the nothing of indifference. The nothing of someone who has understood that a footprint here is a published sentence, that a scent laid on a rail may still be answering a question when the grandchildren of the questioner arrive to read it, that curiosity here moves at circuit speed and must not be hurried into panic. They would follow. They would listen with forehead to basalt. They would learn the hand-language by letting long fingers write on their forearm and not flinching when the meaning did not come — because at first it would not come, and the bands would know by the heat in the skin whether the stranger was patient or merely performing patience.

They would be, deliberately, less interesting than a felt-cake left on a flat stone. Less urgent than a sealed crack. They would let a whole band part around them and go on with the succession — crystal garden, iron felt, basin where the dead keep, pillar where the air climbs — rather than become the event after which all successions were re-named.

Because this place does not need improvement. That is the hard thing for a benevolent power to sit with. The recycling is perfect. The pasture grows without light. The walls stand. The awl lies green and unguarded in plain sight because theft requires a future different from the present, and this country has almost none. A child can learn the world as a sequence walked with the feet, and it will still be true when the child is old. To heal this — to finish the abandoned stalactites, to soften the obsidian, to teach history by introducing loss — would be to love an idea of life more than these lives.

And yet.

A wise power sees with a longer geology than theirs. They know — feeling it the way the bands feel unease in their teeth — that this held breath is held. The roof is young. The heat will not rise forever in exactly the same columns. One century, one millennium, one ordinary Tuesday in the deep time of stone, a pillar will settle a hand's breadth, a thermal will lean, a brine pocket will not refill, a trail that has been true for sixty generations will arrive at a place that is no longer there. Nothing in this world has practiced being wrong. Their perfection is also a brittleness: songs that can be found again word-perfect, graves that do not sink, tools that take two hundred years to alter. They have succession instead of history because nothing has ever been taken from them that they had to survive losing.

So the power would not preserve them in amber. Preserving forever is only another word for deciding on someone else's behalf, completely, at the beginning, and keeping that decision ever since. They have had enough of that from the country itself. The power will not become a second roof.

What they will do is plant one small, survivable change, at the speed of trust, and let the bands refuse it if they choose.

Perhaps after a full circuit — forty years of being read and not answering wrongly — they will press a hand to the stone beside the old handprints, where the elder's long fingers and the child's are stippled patiently into the rock. Not above them. Not larger. Beside, and a little lower, where a next hand would naturally go. And beside theirs, smaller still, a print that is not quite right: five fingers, yes, but one placed where no hand following the pattern would place it. A deliberate, gentle error in the sequence.

In scent, under the fingers, pressed until the oils go in: not *new route*, not *danger*, not *come*. Something the dialect has almost no word for, built slowly out of pieces they do have — opened pocket, sealed again; body in the basin, skull taken up later; wall left swept for whoever returns. *A thing can be gone, and still be carried.*

Then they will withdraw and let sixty years argue with it.

Some bands will smooth it over with ash. That will be allowed. Some elder will cover it with a palm every time she passes, and that too will be a reading. Eventually — not soon, not in the explorer's lifetime, not in the lifetime of the child whose hand is already in the stone — some child with one hand on an elder's ankle and the other on the rail will stop at the wrong-placed finger and ask, by a tap, by a change of temperature under the palm, what it was for a mark to be made by somebody who is not there anymore. And the elder, who was taught by someone who decided the strange mark was worth keeping, will have the beginning of an answer. Not a doctrine. A story that can bend.

That is all. And at the throat, where the dry rock first went down too warm and gave no cool breath back, they will do the one piece of housekeeping the surface has made necessary: they will let the way forget itself. No thunderclap, no sealed gate with writing on it. A fall of stone that looks, even to a careful eye, like something the young mountain had always intended; a confusion of false passages smelling of nothing; the explorer allowed out with his life and his notes and the absolute, unshakable inability to find the mouth again. His footprints will stay inside for decades, bright nonsense in the ash, and the bands will read them the way they read everything — slowly, without fear — until they powder into the general grey.

The power will stay long enough to be sure the gardened error is not a wound: that the felt still thickens by the fissures, that a brine pocket cracked under instruction is drunk from and sealed again, that a newborn warms against a belly while its hands learn the rails. They will sing, once, into a basalt no band uses for messages, so that when the country finally does change — when the roof remembers it is a roof and the heat forgets its pillars — there will be something outside the dark that remembers the succession in order, and can say it back in touch and heat and stone if anything survives to listen.

Then they will leave the place exactly as unlit as they found it.

Only, on a flat stone beside a waist-high wall, where a felt-cake is sometimes left for a stranger whose intentions are still being considered, there will be a copper awl turned the other way for a while. Point toward the wall. Handle toward the path.

*Seen,* it will say, in the slow etiquette of the country. *Not taken. Go on.*
