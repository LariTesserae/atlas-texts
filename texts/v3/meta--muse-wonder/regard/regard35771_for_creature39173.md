They would duck.

That is the first thing, and perhaps the most important. A power that arrived without ducking — that widened the stair throat, raised the vault, made the way down convenient — would already have misunderstood the place.

They would come down slowly, one hand on the black rope, learning where it is polished. They would not bring light. There is light here, rationed and hard-edged and moved by hand across the floor, and the rationing is part of how everything knows where to stand. They would not bring water. There is water here too, not enough to waste and therefore exactly enough to be remembered, cup by cup, drip by drip. A well sunk through the floor would make the ducks prophets and the moss a flood. It would rot the slats, soften the mortar, drown the mouse-runs in the walls, make people forget the map of drips they carry in their heads like streets. Forgetfulness would be the real flooding.

They would not enlarge it.

From above, seeing pigeons pour out of a roof at ground level, it would be easy to think: *cramped, sunken, let me lift this.* Open a second hall. Give the goats air. Give the children a yard that is not a stair. But the being who lives here is not crowded in the way a crowd is crowded. It is crowded in the way a body is crowded — ribs near lungs, gut near warmth, mouth near what it needs to say. The compost heats the newborns through a wall because it is close enough to. The frogs eat the flies because the flies rise within a frog's patience of where frogs sit. The mules calm the centre because traffic has to pass close enough to lean. Move any one life a kind distance away, and a dozen small offices go vacant.

A benevolent and wise power would see that, and feel the temptation to be kind in the wrong scale, and put its hands — if it has hands — deliberately in its lap.

It would sit for a while on the gallery, out of the aisle, where the woman at the loom can see it without having to make room for reverence. It would listen until it stopped hearing separate noises. Goat bell, hen, pigeon wing, treadle, chopping block, child calling through a speaking-tube so the voice blooms intimate and bodiless beside an ear thirty paces away. Not a chorus. A metabolism.

And it would understand that the being is not the people, tending. It is not the animals, tended. It is the tending itself, repeated until brick wore into crescents under mule hooves and steps dished under soft shoes and a metal gong grew a thumb-smooth dent and learned the vault. It is the rule spoken as architecture: knock twice, so what is inside can move back. It is the first drink left for the frogs. It is carrying something up because you brought something down.

What cannot love that rule should not touch this place.

So they would do almost nothing inside.

Almost.

They might, in the night when the shaft-covers are drawn and the hall is shade between sleeping breaths, press a thumb of strength back into the brick in the stair throat that is about to shear — not replacing it, because a new brick would be a small amnesia, but holding it until the next human mason notices and chisels and mortars it in rose or ochre or ash, adding another colour to the patchwork. They might loosen, by a quarter-turn, the knot on a shaft-rope that would otherwise jam on the morning someone needs the light moved quickly for a kidding. They might sit with the older mule on the night its breathing goes uneven, not to spare it age — nothing here is spared age; everything here is replaced, carried up, cut, renewed — but to keep panic from travelling, animal to animal, through the warm dark.

Small work. Tenant's work. The work of a gecko by a light shaft, taking only the moth that comes within reach, keeping its station.

Their larger work would be at the boundary, where the hall cannot reach with its own hands.

The hall has arranged everything below the roof. It cannot arrange the road surveyor who might one day put a line through the roof on a map. It cannot arrange the drought three valleys away, or the sickness a pigeon might carry home on its bright feet, or whether the skins brought down are filled from a clean source, or whether some well-meaning authority from above decides this place is unsanitary, picturesque, unsafe, valuable. Those are weather from a larger sky.

There, the power would be less shy.

It would make the roof at ground level go on looking like nothing from above — a low spread of fired tile and packed clay, faintly steaming, not worth a second look from a road. It would let an official's eye slide politely past a doorway that requires ducking. It would nudge a planned culvert seven paces east, so its digging never finds the vault. It would keep the invisible pressure in the deep rock that makes, on cold mornings, the vault sweat in its known places. Not more water. The same water, arriving. It would turn one infected grain away from one pigeon's foot, once, without making pigeons immortal. It would guard the edges so that the middle can go on guarding itself.

And about the fig.

Every wise power has one foolishness it permits itself. They would look a long time at the cutting in the cracked amphora by the brightest shaft, all leaf and ambition, tended out of proportion, never fruiting properly, kept anyway — the one citizen kept purely for hope. They would not make it a tree. They would not load it, miraculous, so branches bow and people tell a new story that changes all the old rules. Perhaps, in a good year, they would let it hold a single fruit to ripeness. Small. Warm from the shaft-light. Enough to be divided in improbable slivers — a taste for the toddlers, a taste for the woman at the loom, the last dark mouthful given, with full ceremony and greater goat supervision, to the oldest mule. Enough to remind the hall that hope is not a liar. Not enough to make hope a crop.

Then they would leave before they became a custom.

At the foot of the stairs they would observe the only theology the place has ever needed. If you bring something down, you carry something up. They brought down attention — and attention is weight, even kind attention; it can make a living thing self-conscious, make it perform its balance instead of keeping it. So they would pick up a basket. Dung, old straw, husk, whatever needed carrying. Heavy, ordinary, faintly sweet under the lime and smoke. They would haul it up the dished steps, past the swallows in the throat who would scold them exactly as they scold everyone, and at the top they would set it down where such things from this hall have always been set down.

They would not bless the place aloud. A blessing pronounced in a vault returns a fraction larger, and would frighten the hens.

Instead, going out, they would knock twice on the last gate behind them, though they were already through it — so what is inside can move back from the swing.

Not rescue. Not ruin. Not a miracle with the roof torn off.

Just this: the outer world quietly prevented from crushing a world that is doing, brick by patched brick, the delicate work of staying whole — and one fig, once in a while, remembering what it was for.
