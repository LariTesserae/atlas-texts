From above — and a benevolent power would come from above, not up the donkey path, because it would not want the bells to stop or the boys to fall silent and stare — there is almost everything to see, all at once.

The whole bowl. The fringe of figs leaning over the roofs. The roofs themselves, each one a small, patient mouth open to the night. The lanes in amber shadow. The cisterns underneath, dark and diminishing, inch by inch. The people moving slowly between them, doing their work sitting down, rising, settling again. The sound rising and pooling and spilling over the rim.

It would stay a long time before it did anything at all.

That is the first thing wisdom does here: it listens. Before dawn, with the town. For the slow, irregular plinking under the floors. For a mother judging the depth of her water by ear. For roosters answering at no particular hour, for the metates beginning house to house, for the old monkey by the main cistern opening his eyes. It would learn the place the way the place has learned itself — not by looking for what is missing, but by hearing how everything is already answering everything else.

Because from a height, the temptation would be enormous.

It would be so easy to give them water.

To open a spring in the center of the crater, clear and cold, where the market is. To send rain more than twice a year, gentle and regular. To crack the rim on the eastern side and let the morning sun in earlier, and let the trapped heat out. To flatten the switchbacks into a road. To make the figs bear twice. To make the climb easy.

A power that was only benevolent, and not wise, would do it by noon.

And the town would drown in its gift.

A spring in pumice and ash does not stay a spring. It softens the rock under the houses. It rots the thatch and swells the beams and turns the red dust to mud and fills the cisterns until the cisterns are pointless, and with them the roofs shaped to catch dew, the gutters of bamboo and copper, the knowledge in a mother's ear, the whole careful music by which they have lived for generations. Abundant water would run wastefully, as abundant things always do. The air-plants would rot on the walls. The ferns and moss that drink only condensation would vanish. More water would mean more people, more goats on more roofs, more walls pushed up the rim, until the bowl that shelters them became the bowl that crowded them to death. A road down the mountain would bring up metal and cloth and food — and bring down their figs and their honey and their children, and bring back up a need for things they cannot make. Morning sun let in too early would take the green half-light their eyes are made for, dry the dew before it could drip, and break the one cool working hour of the day.

It would save them from the life they have made, and that would not be saving them.

So the power does not touch the rim. It does not summon rain. It does not put a river where a river was never meant to be.

It goes, instead, to the places no one in the town can reach, and where trouble is already quietly gathering in the slow way trouble gathers on an old mountain.

Deep under the crater floor, where all their lost rain vanishes, there is a fault in the stone no living foot has ever seen. The water that should find its way sideways, seam by seam, to the spring halfway down the mountain — the spring the boys walk to with the skins every morning — is beginning to leak away through that fault into a darkness that leads nowhere. Not this year. Not next. But in seven dry years, in a season when the rains fail in earnest, the seam-spring would have thinned to a wet stain on rock, and the donkey bells would have come up empty.

The power puts no new water there. It does what the mountain itself would eventually have done, given another thousand years: it persuades the stone to close. A slow pressure. A mineral patience. One grain of calcite at a time, in the dark, until the leak narrows to what it was when the grandmothers of grandmothers were girls. The spring will hold. It will not flow one cup faster than anyone remembers. The boys will notice nothing. That is the point.

On the shaded side of the rim, where the great figs root in the cracks, the power finds a blight coming on the wind from the valley — a small, pale fungus, two valleys away still, that would take the crowns leaf by leaf and let the noon sun fall unbroken into lanes that have never learned to bear it. It does not kill the fungus; it has its own life, in its own place. It simply turns the wind, on three particular nights in spring, a fraction. And it carries, on that same wind, dusted on no one's tongue, pollen from a stand of wild figs none of these people have ever seen, so that the next generation of trees over the roofs will be cousins, not only brothers. Stronger. Still themselves.

In one place, where a section of rim has been loosening grain by grain behind a fig's roots and would, in a hammering rain ten years from now, have slumped across three houses in the night, it does the smallest possible thing: it makes those roots interesting to themselves in a slightly different direction. A root thickens here instead of there. A tendril finds a better crack. Over years, unhurriedly, the tree will do the holding that needs to be done, and no one will ever call it a miracle, because trees holding stone is simply what trees here do.

That is all. Almost all.

On the last night — if night and last have meaning to such a power — the cisterns are low, lower than the town likes, and the air is still and warm and the air-plants are ticking only faintly. The power does not fill anything. It just lets the bowl be a degree cooler than it otherwise would have been. One degree. No more. Stone sweats a little more readily at that temperature. Dew gathers a little more generously on the cold side of walls, in the bamboo gutters, along hammered copper.

In the hour before dawn, all over the town, under the floors, the plinking starts a few minutes earlier than expected.

A mother wakes, and listens, and judges that the night was generous. A child turns over in the place where his mother slept. The old monkey by the main cistern sighs in his sleep, dreaming perhaps of a forest he has never seen, his joints hurting a little less than they might have.

No one looks up. No temple is founded. No story enters the stock of stories alongside *the summer Ana's boy knocked the jar at the fig tree steps*. The donkeys go down in red dust. The donkeys come up, skins sloshing.

And the power withdraws the way the shadow withdraws at noon — briefly, and without leaving the place changed in kind — having understood the rarest thing about this worn-down mountain: that it is not a problem waiting to be solved. It is a negotiation, cup by cup, root by root, generation by generation, between a people and a place that gives almost nothing quickly.

It leaves them their almost-nothing.

It leaves them listening for it, together, in the dark — which is to say, it leaves them themselves, still able to live another day by the means that have made them who they are.
