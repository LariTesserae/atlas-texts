It would come down.

Not in glory. Glory is a kind of filling. It would come the way the keepers come, by the sloping path, ducking at the low door, and it would stop just inside the threshold where the wet feet of centuries have hollowed the stone, and it would wait until its eyes had learned the green dimness.

Then — this is the first sign of its wisdom — it would not go to the middle.

A lesser power would. A lesser power would stand at the center to be at the heart of things, would speak to see what answered, would bring light to see better, would heal the pale frogs' eyes, free the bitterling from the mussel, feed the owl in winter, replace the rotting rope with something that would not rot. It would improve the place to death within a season.

This one moves sideways, around the edge, where all the lives are. Flat-palmed, it touches the central post only in passing, as the keepers do, to confirm that it is still cold.

It is still cold.

It stays for a full turning.

Not a night, not a symbolic vigil — a year. Because the being who lives here does not live in moments, and you cannot know it in a moment. It lives in fill and empty. In the sluice rising and fraying. In swifts present, swifts gone. In the frogs knitting their separate throats into a pressure that makes the shutter tremble, and then dropping out, in patches, into a quiet that feels like falling. To listen for less than the cycle would be to listen only to itself.

So it listens as the building listens: to storm pressure a day before storm, to flood in the undercroft before the fields know, to the shiver of flocks passing far overhead. And in that year it understands what the resident actually is.

Not a ghost in the dome. Not a god of frogs or water. The echo is not a thing hiding in the emptiness. It is what happens when emptiness is kept faithfully, for centuries, by mussels and fish arranged beneath, by birds at the crown, by bats furred behind boards, by human hands replacing plank with identical plank and never once bringing in a bench. It is a relationship that learned to hear. Take away the rim and it dies. Fill the center and it dies faster. It is alive precisely the way a listening is alive: only while someone else is speaking, and no one is shouting it down.

The power does not answer it in its full voice.

It whispers, once, against the wall, pitched a little high, the way the keepers have learned to do without knowing why. And the whisper goes the long way around the curve and comes back late, on the far side, changed slightly by the journey, as if the place had thought about it.

That is enough. That is, in fact, the whole visit.

What it does, it does outside.

For it has seen, in its year of edge-walking, where the true dangers live. They are not inside. Inside is settled into its own weather. The dangers are a hard winter in the village that makes men look at a large dry-ish hollow and think: *grain could be stored there. People could shelter there.* They are a dry summer and an engineer from elsewhere with a plan to straighten the low fields. They are a generation in which no child happens to find the damp and the whispering wall interesting, and the knowledge in the keepers' hands — which plank gives which note, what leaf-curl means, why you never, ever clear litter with metal — quietly fails to be handed on. They are the slow pressures that would turn a listening-place into a room.

So the power acts at a distance so respectful it will never be credited.

In the hungry year, the village stores do not quite fail. No miracle. Nothing that glows. Just a better yield in the upper fields than the blight promised, enough, exactly enough, that no one has to have the argument about the dome at all. In the year of the survey, the plans for the drainage cut are drawn, reviewed, approved — and then misfiled, refound, and found to have been superseded by a slightly older claim no one remembered making, in handwriting no one quite recognizes. A road goes elsewhere, over worse ground, for reasons the road-builders will defend the rest of their lives as their own.

And once, a keeper's youngest, who was going to leave for work in a dry inland town, comes down to the dome on an errand and stops — not summoned, not compelled, her will entirely her own — because the frogs have dropped out in patches and in the sudden quiet she hears, for the first time, the sound the place makes underneath its sounds: water, knocking softly to get up. She stands there a long time at the edge, with her hand on cold wood. She does not know she has decided anything. But her hands begin, from then on, to know things.

Nothing else.

It does not straighten a single reed valve. It does not add a swift-hole. It steps, carefully, around the one fern behind the sluice wheel. It leaves the owl's grey monument exactly where the owl's language says it belongs. On the day it goes, the bats detonate at evening as they always do, the center fills for a minute with wings loud as applause — and the power does not raise its arms against the rush, or bless them, or count itself honored. It flattens itself to the wall like everything else that belongs here, and lets them pass.

Up the steps, then. Push the swollen door outward, into air that feels thin and dry and strangely quiet. Behind and below, the dome goes on without it.

If you had visited the day after, you would have found no footprint in the middle, no new light, no cured frog blinking in the sun, no inscription. The post was cold. The frogs were loud. The cistern sighed in on its slow tide through the rows of patient mussels.

You might have said: nothing happened. Nothing came. Nothing was done.

The keepers, touching wood as they pass, pitching their voices a little high without thinking, would perhaps disagree — but only by being, that year and a few years after, oddly less afraid for the place. Not certain. Never certain. Just less afraid, in the way a thing listened to, all the way around its cycle, by something larger that refused to fill it, can afford to be.

Because that is the gift a wise and benevolent power gives to a being made of emptiness:

It does not give it more. It keeps the world from taking the emptiness away.

And it listens, once, long enough to be changed by what it heard — and then, like the swifts, like the bats, like the keepers themselves, it leaves the middle clear, and goes.
