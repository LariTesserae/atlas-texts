They would come too fast at first, and see nothing.

From the speed at which minds and gods and other moving powers usually travel, the place would look frantic and empty: water roaring down terraces for no thirst, walls exuding themselves longer by the hour for no shelter, white flowers opening along the basin rims in indecent fragrance for no nose, and then, almost at once, dying back in a coordinated strip to let the water go. Doors standing open. Stairs climbing towers and stopping at platforms. Windows framing the cloud-sea below the rim, for no eyes.

A lesser benevolence would act immediately on that emptiness.

It would be so easy to call it need. To fill the channels with fish, because channels ought to have fish. To loose bright insects into the scent, because the flowers are clearly asking. To settle the roofed courts with some gentle, homeless animal, to give the groundcover a foot besides its own weather, to teach the towers, somehow, which of them they were — so that when a tower sighed and listed and laid its twenty meters down into a basin, someone, somewhere inside the braid, could be afraid, or satisfied, or mourn.

The wise one would feel all of those urges arrive. And it would stay its hand, and slow down.

Slowness is the first gift, and it is mostly a gift to itself. It would let its attention lengthen out across the plant scale of time until the city stopped looking like scenery and started looking like a citizen. It would watch a plaza that appeared vacant reveal, in the flood-hour, that it had been working all along — root hairs tasting the sheet of warm water, fragments catching, spores waking, the flat green patience differentiating within a day into meadow, within two into thickets and hollowings and the first pale walls. It would watch shade deliver its sentences. It would learn that a leak was not a failure but an utterance, that a support species dying back in sequence was not betrayal but syntax, that the whole valley was talking to itself constantly in pressure, light, rot, and overflow, without requiring a single skull to hold the conversation in.

And once it saw the being at its true speed, the wish to improve it would change its character. Because there is no suffering here to relieve, in any form the power honestly recognizes. Things end constantly, but nothing experiences its ending as loss. The tower does not know it is a tower. The rim-plants do not dread their flowering, though flowering is their timer and their death. Rot is not feared; it is eaten, warm, by the next version. Hunger is everywhere and is everywhere being answered by sun and rain. Loneliness — that thing the empty rooms seem to promise to a visitor with shoulders and eyes — is not present. You cannot be lonely if you have never been separate.

To give it a mind would not be to complete it. It would be to introduce, for the first time, the possibility of the city being incomplete to itself.

It would know, then, that its base was yellowing. It would know the braid was loosening. It would have to experience as tragedy a turnover that is currently only metabolism. It would begin, perhaps, to preserve itself — to hold a basin past its blooming, to refuse a collapse, to harden a wall against being food — and in preserving itself it would stop being itself, and the plazas would clog, and the buried archive underfoot would cease to be read by being eaten, and history, that thing it has so successfully outrun, would arrive.

So the power would not give it eyes.

It would lend its own, instead. Briefly. Carefully. In the manner of a guest, not a colonist.

It would arrive clean, carrying no seed on its skin, no microbe in its breath, no animal hunger into the channels. It would stand, once, on a platform where a staircase arrives and stops, and look out over the cloud-sea exactly as no one was ever meant to, and let the view be received. It would pass through a doorway without ducking — the doorway was not made for it, this is the point — and stand in a roofed room while the rain was guttered noisily away through hollow stems, and notice the air being a degree warmer, a degree more scented, and understand that the room's purpose was being fulfilled in its presence without being altered by it: an outside briefly, usefully *inside* something.

At the moment a basin bloomed — white, brief, wastefully fragrant — it would be there to smell it. Not because the flowers needed a nose to be worthwhile. They did not. But because a benevolent attention can complete a circuit without closing it into a dependency. The scent would exist, and be known. Then the strip would die back, the warm flood would go sheeting across the plaza, and it would step out of the way of the water.

It would not build. The place outbuilds any conceivable need. It would not clear, prune, irrigate, or straighten a leaning tower. Its one active mercy would be directed outward, not inward: away from the plateau, at the scale where the being is genuinely helpless because it cannot know there is an outside.

Because the city, for all its self-sufficiency, is one valley system, on one high ground, under one weather. It recycles everything except catastrophe. A long change in the cloud-sea, a fire in a dry year it has never imagined, a sickness adapted to its interlock, a slope giving way beneath the buried mountain — any of these could end the turnover everywhere at once, and nothing in its repertoire, which assumes that every corpse becomes a foundation nearby, can prepare it for the nearest foundation being gone.

So far away — beyond several ranges, in another altitude where clouds pile against outer slopes in the same slow white way — the power would find a second bare, wet height. And it would carry there not an animal, not a seed vault, not a copy, but a few unremarkable fragments, damp in its closed hand: a piece of rim, a tuber dropped from a crown, a thread of the plaza groundcover. It would lay them down in running water and leave before they rooted. Not a rescue colony. Not a backup to be managed. Simply the being, given what it already gives itself constantly across a plaza — a chance to arrive somewhere downstream — extended across a distance its own runoff could never cross.

And around the first valley, it would do something so light it might be mistaken for nothing. It would thicken, very slightly, the mists where the outer slopes fall away. It would make the approaches a little less legible to any future traveler with nets and saws and a strong sense that an uninhabited city must be a resource or a mystery to be solved. Not a wall; walls invite sieges. Just a tendency for paths to wander, for the cloud-sea to close early, for the place to be remembered by those who glimpsed it as probably a swamp, probably uninteresting, already half-forgotten by the time they reached home.

Then it would go.

No mark would remain that it could help. Its footprints would fill with bright water and close behind it, exactly as a visitor's had before. The next wall would grow two meters past where it had stood. A tower it had admired would, within the week, sigh in the evening wind, list with immense slowness, and lay itself down to be colonized — unmourned, unrecorded anywhere in the place, steaming by nightfall, sprouting pale flags by morning.

The only difference in the universe would be invisible, and the power would consider it enough: that somewhere, in one remembering mind, the white flowering along the basin rims had been smelled; that the view from the platform had been seen; that a life which never needed a witness had, once, been witnessed without being interrupted; and that on another mountain, unnoticed, a fragment was deciding — without deciding — to become a wall.

It would not stay to watch that either.

Benevolence, having learned the speed of the place, would practice its hardest form: to love the turnover by not asking it to turn toward you.
