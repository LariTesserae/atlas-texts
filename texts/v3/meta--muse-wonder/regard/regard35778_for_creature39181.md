The first thing a benevolent and wise power would do is arrive incorrectly.

Not out of malice. Out of habit. Powers are used to height, to overview, to the clear sightline from above the weather. It would come first to the rim of the hills, where the stone weeps and the pearl-grey cloud hangs at face level, and it would look down into twenty kilometers of marsh and forest and black water, and it would — being benevolent — begin at once to make a list of mercies.

Lift the cloud. Let in the sun.

Settle the gravity back into the ground, where gravity belongs, so a dropped pebble falls once and is done.

Still the channels. Give the pools honest shores. Give reflections their proper delay. Purge the fine bright suspension from the water, from the fungal mats, from the pale undersides of the people living low in the sedge, so that blood is only blood again and a landscape is only a landscape again.

Tell them what was done here, and by whom, and why. Return their history to them. Return them to themselves.

It would make the list with love. That is the dangerous part.

Then, if it were wise — actually wise, not merely powerful and kind in intention — it would go down.

Not descend. Go down. Slowly. On foot, if it had to invent feet for the purpose. It would get damp within minutes. The deep, penetrating cool would settle into whatever it was wearing for a body and stay there. Its breath would show. It would try to cross fifty meters to a reed bed and find, ten minutes later, that it was no nearer, and three steps after that, that it was standing in cold water up to the shins, having arrived too suddenly to brace.

It would learn to stop.

It would learn to watch where the reeds lean away, and go there. It would learn not to follow its own face in a pool, especially when the face in the pool smiled first, helpfully, as if trying to lead it somewhere. It would learn that when its voice came back from the mist slightly altered — kinder, older, with better advice than it had actually given — the correct response was not wonder, and not correction. It was to shut its mouth and wait, in company with everything else in the sedge, until the air had finished folding and distance agreed, provisionally, to mean one thing at a time.

Staying longer would undo the list.

Sunlight, it would discover, is not a gift to eyes tuned downward to pool-light, to skin that lives by reflection, to leaves that have turned their pale undersides up to the water. Gravity made honest all at once would break the spiral-rooted reeds as surely as a flood; the wading birds, testing each footfall for whether it belongs to the same day, would not experience a cure, they would experience a world that had stopped answering their questions in the grammar they were born to. Still water, silent water, water with the arithmetic taken out of it, would be to the inhabitants not purity but death — a place gone terribly, concentratedly quiet, where the ground had stopped being loud enough to be alive.

And to tell them what they were? Maintenance fauna, perhaps. Descendants of engineers, perhaps. A soft interface. An infection that learned to be a people. The power could excavate the truth from under geological time and lay it out, clean and undeniable, and all it would have returned to them is a distinction they had spent ten thousand years mercifully losing: between the engineers and the engineered, between the makers and the medium, between a person and the place that flashes, briefly, under the skin of her shoulder when a pool flashes in answer. A fact is not always a belonging. Some knowledge is only a new way to be exiled where you already live.

So the power would cross out its list, one mercy at a time. Not because the basin is perfect. It is not. Children cry there when a mist gives them back their own voice aged and strange. The old die into peat because that is the only death the place has learned to permit. Sometimes the ground concentrates under the surface into a silence so intense it has to be hummed at, badly and off-pitch, by squatting neighbors introducing loyal errors, and some years perhaps no one hums in time. The basin is not heaven. It is only, down to the rootlets and the fungal threads and the reflections that arrive a second too early, *settled*. Broken so long ago that the breakage has become a way for things to be whole.

You do not heal a reef by taking out the wreck.

What it would do, then, would be almost nothing, and the almost would take a very long time.

It would make itself low. That is the necessary first sacrament here. It would fold whatever length it had down into its shoulders against the damp, spread its weight, move with a deliberate, revisable slowness, plant each foot and wait a breath while the ground reported its drift. When a channel began to run too purposefully between two pools that should not yet be reconciled, it would not command it to stop. It would lie down across the invisible sluice, as they do, and lend it a confusion of warmth until it lost conviction and spread. When it found a place going silent, it would squat at the margin and make its small, embarrassing, off-key noise, tapping an uneven rhythm, being for a while not a god but another body willing to be borrowed by the basin's old urges and not to mind.

It would learn ground-speech before it attempted consolation. Heel pressed and released in the right hollow. Palm flat where the mats run thick. Five beats, a pause, five beats, a variation. It would accept that a sentence sent two kilometers might arrive revised by a passing pool of weight, and it would consider the revision before answering, because some revisions are the place itself joining the conversation, and the place has seniority.

If it ever asked the inhabitants whether they wanted help, it would not ask in words from the sky. It would stand beside a still pool for an afternoon, as they do when they want to remember something, and let its intention surface in black water, slightly altered and without mist. And if an inhabitant looked at a reflection of the power beginning a gesture, and nodded, and complied — the power would comply in return. And if the reflection only waited, patient and moss-humped and unconvinced, the power would understand that no consent had been given, and withdraw, and be grateful not to have been obeyed merely because it was large.

It would not stay indoors with them. There is no indoors. That, too, it would accept.

Where it *would* act — freely, even fiercely — is at the rim.

Because the basin's greatest danger is not the basin. It is everything that has not yet arrived there with a list of mercies. The prospectors who would see the flashing pools and read *resource*. The healers who would see flecks moving under pale skin and read *contamination*. The scholars who would see a reflection finish a gesture too soon and build, within a generation, a laboratory on stilts and then a causeway and then a name for the people that the people would be required to answer to. The rescuers, worst of all, sincere and well-funded, arriving with drainage plans drawn up in dry rooms.

From the hills, these things still have edges. They have roads and intentions and supply lines and attention spans. They can be turned without being harmed.

So the power would stand guard in the only way the place itself teaches: not with a wall, but with weather. It would let distances fold a little more persistently for survey parties than for the lost. It would let a voice come back from the mist altered just enough, and kind enough, that a young engineer with a core sampler sits down in the sedge and weeps for reasons she cannot later put in a report, and goes home, and requests a transfer, and never quite explains. It would let the roar rise in the teeth of funding committees at improbable moments. It would keep the watershed whole upstream where no one from the basin will ever go to thank it, hold the hills in their weeping, turn the slow pressures of the outside world aside at the one altitude where turning them is still a kindness and not an intervention.

That is the shape of its benevolence translated into local terms: protect the threshold. Never govern the interior.

And it would do one more thing, the only thing a long-lived power can give a people who deliberately distribute themselves at death into water and fungus and peat, who do not keep a where, who let messages arrive smudged and persons arrive gradual and history arrive dissolved.

It would remember them, outside the basin, so that they do not have to remember themselves that way inside it.

Not their origins — those it would let sink. But their afternoons. The specific unhurried flavor of messages tapped through a stretch of ground where a particular elder settled for a season after dying. The child extruded slowly over a wet season, learning light off her parent's flashing back before she ever saw a pool. The inhabitant standing patient in up-raining water, letting droplets detach from his hair and accelerate into the cloud because it relieved a pressure in his teeth. It would hold all of it, accurately, in whatever archive powers keep, and it would never, ever bring the archive back down the hill and read it aloud to them as if memory were a debt they had failed to pay. To remember *for* someone is only love if you also refuse to make them carry it.

In the end — and the end would take centuries, because the power would stay long enough to be properly damp, and then longer, until moss had begun making its small geography on whatever passed for its shoulders — it would leave no structure. No shrine, no stele, no improvement in the lattice that an archaeologist of the far future could point to and say: *here a god intervened.* Let them find only what the valley makes of everything given enough time: something dissolved, something distributed, a slightly stronger tendency in one northern stretch of peat for silences to remember, on their own, how to go back to being ordinary loud ground.

Perhaps, very occasionally, the pools would still flash east to west across twenty kilometers, five beats and a pause, five beats and a pause, and for a few minutes every sleeper would twitch in the same direction at the same unseen pressure crossing fast and large overhead.

The inhabitants would freeze, as they always do. Rootlets locked. Waiting for the air to unfold.

And high above the pearl-grey cloud, unobserved and entirely satisfied with that arrangement, the pressure would keep moving — slower now than when it first arrived, lower than it ever intended to fly, having learned the one lesson the basin teaches everything that lives there long enough:

Do not insist.

Stay until you are wet through. Go where the reeds lean away. And when you love a place that has already survived being saved once, leave it louder than you found it, and otherwise exactly, reverently, alone.
