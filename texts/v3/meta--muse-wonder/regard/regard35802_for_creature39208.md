It would not send rain.

That would be the first temptation for any power accustomed to kinder worlds — to look at the white basins and the fossil streambeds and the lips cracked open by the air, and to correct it. To haul clouds up over the rim. To make the sky, at last, give back.

A wise one would learn, before it acted, that rain here would not be mercy. It would be a breach.

Free water would find every fissure the cold has ever made in bark and skin and widen it. It would wake spores that have waited dormant in the dust for ten thousand years because the dryness kept them honest. It would rot what cannot rot, dissolve the salts in the wrong order, drown root-tips built to pry moisture molecule by molecule out of stone, and teach a sealed world — in one wet season — how to leak. The groves have survived not in spite of the rule but because of it:

*All the water is inside the living things, and it stays there.*

So the power would not break the rule. It would begin, instead, to keep it — in the places where the groves themselves cannot reach.

And it would take its time learning how. A century, at least, of doing nothing that could be noticed. These are beings who measure a childhood in decades and a debt of water in a lifetime of small renunciations; haste would look to them, if they could see it at all, like weather. It would watch the circuits they make around the trunks. It would learn that closeness is a cost and distance is a form of care. It would learn the forearm language only by being, very rarely and only if permitted, touched — and it would learn that to be examined in that way is to be asked the only question that matters here: *how full are you, and is your seal holding?*

It would answer honestly. Then it would withdraw to the emptiness, because the emptiness is where its work is.

For inside the groves, there is almost nothing it could improve without impoverishing. The dwellers already tend the seal better than any outsider could. They already carry dust to the root-zones, chip by tasted chip of the minerals their own basin lacks. They already know which trees are in their final century, and have begun hoarding for successors not yet decanted. They do not need a god in the canopy. They do not need tools, or walls, or a voice. The air would not carry a voice in any case.

But between the groves, children are dying with viable seeds in their hands.

Not inside the system, where death is a transfer and nothing is wasted. Out on the flats, in the dust-hollows, where a thermal failed or ran too long, where the crack under the landing place was not there, or was there a hundred yards wrong. Mummified. Silver gone grey. Membranes furled. Still sealed, most of them — that is the bitter perfection of it — and still useless, because there is no fungal mat to receive them, no grove to close around them and make a stillness in which anything can be given back. Their inheritance stops mid-journey, in the open, where the plateau slowly turns everything into its own color.

There, and only there, the power would begin to act. And it would act in the plateau's own grammar: light, dust, stone, rising air. Nothing that would leave a mark. Nothing that could be worshipped, or depended upon, or built against.

It would not strengthen the thermals. Stronger is not wiser here; a column that carries a young one farther is just as likely to carry them past the last grove into country where no crack will ever feed a seed. Instead, on those afternoons when an elder's hands have silvered a back for the last time and something light as ash steps off the high canopy, the power would attend to the *edges* of the rising air — shaving a degree of drift here, letting a column lean a fraction there, the way a hand cupped beside a candle does not make the flame but keeps it from spending itself sideways. The flight would still belong to the wind. The risk would still be real. But fewer journeys would end for no reason except that the sky was careless at one unremarkable moment, three days out, where no one could have tended it.

For those who still came down wrong — and some would; it would not abolish the price of crossing, because the price is also what keeps a grove from dying of its own children — it would do the thing no living thing on the plateau is spaced widely enough to do. It would go to the dead in the hollows. Gently, the way the dwellers lay an elder's shell where the mats can finish it, it would take up the seed that is still a sealed boat with a grove inside it, and the last of what the body can still give, and carry them the remaining distance: not back — never back, there is no room and no mercy in return — but forward, outward, to the far places the seeds were always meant for and almost never reach.

And there, it would prepare ground the way the plateau prepares everything: almost too slowly to be called an action.

A crack, found or begun, above a seam of hydrated mineral deep enough to matter in two hundred years. Dust, sorted grain from grain as the wind sorts it, but gathered into the one hollow in a thousand where it will stay. A single stone set — not built, *set*, in a place it might plausibly have rolled — so that its lee makes a pocket of slightly less murderous air at dawn. Then the seed, put down into that prepared stillness, and left alone.

Not watered. Left alone.

If it quickens, it will spend its first two centuries as one dark bush hoarding a thimbleful, exactly as its ancestors did. The power would not visit it oftener than the groves visit one another. It would not protect it from frost or falling rock or the long odds. It would only have corrected the one obscenity in the system: that so much careful inheritance — forty years of parents choosing not to repair themselves, a seed carried three days on a membrane of skin — should be wasted purely for lack of a crack in the right hollow.

Over a thousand years, the map would change in a way no single dweller could live long enough to be sure of. A grove where the thermal roads have always ended in empty basins. Another, improbably far out, already old enough to be silvering its first child for the canopy. Arrivals at receiving groves a little less often catastrophic; not safe — never safe — but survivable, skin cracked at the joints rather than the seal gone through. The proportion of mummies in the hollows slowly, almost imperceptibly, falling.

The dwellers would not name it. They have no surplus language, and the air taxes voice too heavily for theology. But they are exact observers of pressure and interval across centuries, and in time the elders of several groves might begin, independently, to do one small new thing: leaving, at the edge of the canopy on thermal afternoons, the best landing-shadow empty. Not for a child of their own — their own are gone by then, up the column. For whatever the rising air decides to bring. Setting dust where unfamiliar feet will find it. Keeping a space in the circuit where no one stands.

Which is to say, in the only dialect this place has ever spoken, they would make room for it by keeping their distance from it.

And the power — if it was truly benevolent, and truly wise — would understand that this was not ingratitude, and not failure of contact. It would take the interval offered, and keep it. It would not enter the grove's seal. It would not ask to be held forearm to forearm again. It would go on working the long emptiness at night, when the plateau ticks as it contracts and most true growth happens: a root's fraction, a crack's fraction, a seed deciding in the dark to begin.

No rain. No river. No mark on the land that ten million years of wind have not already authorized.

Just, at the far edges of a world that holds everything it loves inside itself, a few more of its crossings ending — not in stone-colored silence in an unmarked hollow — but in the still center of a grove, where the air is fractionally softer, and hands come close, and the first examination is also, eventually, the first gift of water.
