The power would arrive the way weather arrives in the bowl: from underneath, at first.

It would come up the wet stair at dawn with the donkeys, unseen, and be immediately inconvenienced.

It would have intended, perhaps, to stand apart and observe. There is no apart. A lane waits for ducks and so it would wait. A child would take its hand — because a newcomer standing idle in a lane is obviously lost, and a four-year-old with a basket is obviously qualified to correct this — and lead it three houses down, across a balcony plank, over the backs of sheep no one had warned it about, and deposit it firmly on a porch where it would be given hot barley cake before the cake went soft, and broth, and a place by a fire that never quite goes out.

It would be combed for ticks that evening, without ceremony, while someone told it whose pig was sick.

This is the first lesson, and a benevolent and wise power is one that can learn it: this place does not need to be entered. It enters *you*. Permeability is not a flaw in its defenses. It is the whole arrangement.

So it would stay longer than it meant to. And as it stayed, the temptations would come, one by one, in the order that well-meaning powers always feel them.

To dry it. Just a little. To lift the mist out of the lanes in the morning, to let bread keep a second day, to spare the babies the green rash in the folds of their skin, the old people the ache that lives permanently in their knees, the iron its rust, the wood its rot.

To quiet it. Just at night. To still one waterwheel, soften the fall at the notch, persuade the frogs — mercifully, temporarily — so that a tired mother might sleep in something like silence.

To order it. Straighten one channel. Pave the market properly, so it stays paved. Separate, for hygiene and for peace, the ducks from the drinking runnel and the pigs from the retaining walls and the people from the animals and the animals from the roofs. Build once, solidly, so these people would not have to spend every morning re-weaving, re-thatching, re-cutting, re-binding a town that is always trying to become a thicket.

A foolish power would call those mercies.

A wise one would watch a woman dip her finger in a channel junction, taste, rap her clapper twice, and see a grandchild already moving with a rake three houses below before the sound had finished — and understand that the rash and the rot and the noise and the endless remaking are not problems laid on top of the life here. They are the conduits the life runs through. Knowledge lives in ears here *because* paper mildews. Tenderness lives in bodies here *because* no wall has ever succeeded in keeping another species out. Attention — that astonishing, distributed, interruptible attention, a whole town listening under the roar for one stuck goat kid — exists because nothing can be finished and forgotten. Dry them, quiet them, order them, and you would not give them an easier version of this town. You would give them amnesia. Within a generation they would be deaf in the way lowlanders are deaf, standing in a tidy, silent, well-drained settlement, wondering why the spring tasted wrong and no one could hear it anymore.

The balance here is the balance of a rapid. It looks like chaos and it is, in fact, exact. It has been exact for hundreds of years. You do not improve a rapid by stilling it.

So the power would do what almost no power finds easy to do.

Nearly nothing. And the nearly, done precisely.

It would not touch the bowl. It would touch the *edges* of the bowl — the places where the town's own attention cannot reach, because its people have, rightly, no spare hands for them.

Deep under the black overhang, where the spring rises achingly cold, there is a source no channel-keeper has ever seen. The power would go there once, in the rock, in the dark, and make certain of it: that the water will keep coming clear through droughts the terraces have not yet imagined, that no slow poison from far-off lowland workings will ever thread up through the stone. Not more water. Not warmer water. The same water, guarded at a depth where rakes cannot go.

On the mountain flank below the fall, where weather moves underneath your boots, armies and road-builders and men with ideas about timber will come, in time. They always do. The power would not strike them down. It would simply make the lower forest very easy to get lost in, on certain years. Paths would seem to close with runners overnight — which, here, is barely even a miracle. Surveyors would find their instruments fogged, their markers mossed, their enthusiasm quietly replaced by a conviction, which they would believe was their own, that the pass on the other side of the ridge is really much more promising. The stair would stay a stair. Never a road. Nothing with wheels will ever be able to arrive in this bowl in bulk, to take away in bulk. That matters more than any gift.

Once in a generation, perhaps, a slide will start on the rim in the night rain — the real danger of a place like this, the one failure their negotiation cannot absorb. Then the power would do the single loud thing it permits itself. Not hold the slope forever; the rim is alive and must move as living things move. But slow it by the width of one shout. Loosen one boulder early, so it cracks down through the rim trees in the afternoon, harmlessly, unmistakably — and watch, satisfied, as every head in the bowl turns, as hands sign *wait* and *upper sluice* and something older that simply means *the mountain spoke*, as goats are argued off that side and children are redirected and the old listeners are carried, broth and all, to safer niches. They will handle the rest themselves. They were always going to handle the rest themselves. They only needed the warning in a language they already speak fluently.

And small things. Small things are the native currency here; a wise power learns to pay in it.

A season where the fever that sometimes rides up the stair with traders finds, strangely, no purchase in the wet lanes. A roof-goat that does not fall. A replacement house-tree, planted the day the old one is cut by agreement of the whole lane, that takes root with unusual certainty and is knee-high a little sooner than expected — just soon enough that no one quite plans, next time, to skip the planting because it seems slow. Nothing that breaks the pattern of effort and return. Nothing they could hoard. Nothing that keeps: a gift that could be stored would rot here anyway, spiritually if not literally. The power is pleased to discover that this town has made itself nearly incorruptible by valuables. You can only bless it in perishables. In timing. In luck that looks exactly like their own competence, arriving half a breath early.

It would not lengthen their lives. It would, if it could, deepen the particular part of life they have specialized in: it would keep the hearing of the very old clear a few years longer, so a fixed point at a channel junction can go on tasting water and reading the town for people whose arms are full. That is infrastructure here, as real as a waterwheel, and worth more than a monument.

In the end — to the extent there is an end, for a power watching a place that rearranges itself a little every morning — it would take up a post much like theirs. Somewhere at a crossing of lane and water. Not the spring, not the notch; somewhere middle and ordinary, where it can be useful and stepped around. Wrapped, metaphorically, in oiled wool. Listening.

Because the last thing it would understand, standing barefoot on slick stone at dawn with mist moving white through the lanes and lamps still amber in their niches, a duck nesting inexplicably in its own woodpile, the wheels turning, the frogs changing key somewhere under the general roar in a way that means rain by mid-morning, is that it had come imagining the town was a *being* — fragile, singular, in need of a guardian's decision.

It is not. It is a way of being, repeated in hundreds of bodies, human and otherwise, none of them sealed, all of them keeping each other from becoming a thicket or a ruin by the simple act of never, ever stopping. It does not need to be saved. It needs its watershed unpoisoned, its stair un widened, its warnings audible, and then to be left entirely, respectfully, almost reverently alone inside its own loud green music.

So the power would stay on guard *outside* that music, for centuries if need be, doing invisible work at invisible depths, and ask for only one payment, which the town, without ever knowing it owed anything, would pay every single morning:

That it be allowed to listen, and learn how to be in time with a place instead of in charge of it — goat here, stall there, sluice open, child redirected, bread in, awning out — not stilling anything. Dancing, at last, like everyone else who has ever really lived here.
