It would not fill in the basin.

That is the first mercy it considers, and the first it refuses.

From above — and it can see from far above, the plain unrolling white and indifferent, the rim a thin scar, the nets humming over the opening like a lid left ajar — the impulse is simple. Lift the cold out. Break the terraces. Let weather fall the way weather is supposed to fall, from the whole sky, all at once. Call the orange figures up the switchbacks for the last time and give them back the sun.

It looks longer instead.

It watches a crew of five stand along the western wall at noon with their helmets off, faces tipped into forty minutes of slanted light, steaming faintly. It watches them blink when it passes. It watches them go back to work without complaint, or with complaints so practiced and specific they have become a form of affection. It watches a worker leave a wrench flat on the deck overnight on purpose, for a new kid to find in the morning under its dome of clear ice. It watches hands hover near joints, not touching, reading heat. It watches the stillness that takes them all when the fog rolls and the lights go out one by one — not panic anymore, but something almost like rest, freely taken because the place insists.

They are not prisoners here. The road goes up. The swallows go up straighter still.

To rescue them would be to announce that what they have been becoming, down in the dusk, learning the trench in their calves and the turbines in the soles of their feet, was only damage. It was not only damage. A wiser cruelty would call it nothing but damage. A wiser kindness has to hold its hands still.

So it descends, because attention is not nothing, and gets close the way the basin lets anything get close: slowly, and in pieces.

It goes down Service Level 1 in the amber light with the inspection carts, which beep to no one. It follows the rats' ruler-straight tracks along the warm pipe runs and finds the underdeck colony — sleek, serious, entirely uninterested in being discovered — doing their unassigned work in the dark. It finds the wiry grass in the rail bed, the black algae, the lichen on the transformer housings. It finds the moss in the leakage trays, tended like scripture.

And in the polishing ponds, in water twelve feet clear and just above freezing, it finds the white frogs.

Unhurried. Blind-looking. Moving through a world that has never frozen and never warmed, never been truly day or truly night. Not introduced. Not escaped. Born, somehow, to exactly this: an artificial pond at the bottom of an artificial pit, lit by sodium and green indicator lamps, fed by drips no one admits to leaving.

Here, the power stops for a long time.

Because this is the being, perhaps. Or one of them. Or the first of them — the first life for whom the Low Works is not a posting, not a hardship, not an ingeniously engineered extreme, but simply and entirely *home*, the whole of the given world, sufficient. The frogs do not endure the dusk. They do not arrange their breaks around it. It is the medium they move through, and they move through it as if it were good.

The power understands then that it is not looking at a factory with people in it. It is looking at something still deciding what kind of creature it will be — walls for ribs, trench for gut, fog for breath, orange workers for hands, carts and rats for the life of the underfloor, frogs for whatever quiet, inward thing frogs are for. Young. Sharp-edged still. Not yet silted, not yet softened. Still ticking at the joints as it takes strain for the first time.

Young things do not need a god to redesign them. They need the specific accidents of their youth not to kill them before they finish becoming.

So it does almost nothing. Almost nothing, done precisely, in the seams — which is, it has learned by watching them, the only honest way to work down here.

It finds the flaw no one has found yet: a slow chemistry in a sealed line, patient and cumulative, that in nine years, or twelve, would have begun writing itself into lungs and into the moss in a yellow no alarm would have caught in time. It does not appear in a vision. It does not speak. It puts a slight, persuadable tiredness into a metal that was going to pretend to be sound, so that at the next pressure test a joint ticks the *other* way — the way the crews have learned means *before* failure — and a gloved palm hovers, and frowns, and a sector is emptied early for the right reason. A report is filed. No one is a hero. That is correct.

It looks at the cable grid high above, humming with de-icing current, and does not tear it open to the sky. It adjusts — a tension here, an angle of net no engineer will ever quite be able to account for in the maintenance logs — so that the solar pass along the western wall lasts forty-four minutes instead of forty. Four minutes. Not enough to change the climate of the basin, or the cold pooled on its floor, or a single regulation. Enough for six helmets to come off, for eyes to close, for the light to finish crossing a face instead of stopping at the cheekbone. Little enough that they will argue about whether it changed at all, and decide, officially, that it didn't.

It protects the unmonitored places. The swallow anchors under the rim, which a thorough cleaning contract would have scoured. The gravel of the rail bed. The trays where the moss grows. It keeps, in the immense and total engineeredness of the place — four meters of substrate over forgotten stone, every surface a lid on something else — a few square meters that belong to no system, register on no schematic, answer to no tide table. Life found the seams anyway. The least a benevolent power can do is refuse to let the seams be perfectly sealed.

It does one thing for the rats, who want nothing: it leaves them alone, completely, in a way that has weight. It does one thing for the carts, who have names: nothing, but it is amused, and amusement from that height settles like a blessing they will experience only as a slightly luckier run of green lights on Service Level 1.

And for the frogs, it does the smallest and most necessary thing of all. It makes sure the polishing ponds will outlast the plant's first purpose. Plants are decommissioned. Lines are drained, towers fall silent, crews ride up the switchbacks a final time and learn again how to move in unmediated weather. That may come, in twenty years or eighty. When it does, the pumps may stop — but the water in those last ponds, just above freezing, clear to the bolt heads twelve feet down, will be the last system to fail, and the slowest. It sets a gravity feed in the bones of the place, unpowered, unglamorous, that no shutdown sequence will think to close because no shutdown sequence will ever quite find it. Time enough, then. Time for the water to warm by degrees and find its way, through cracks that will come with age and silt and the softening of all those sharp edges, up and out — or time, simply, to go on being a world a while longer.

Not saved. Not abandoned. Accompanied.

At the end it rises back through the fog bank, past the lower lights coming on one by one, past orange figures standing motionless with their hands on rails, waiting out the whiteout in the restful way they have taught themselves. It does not part the fog for them. They have made something of the waiting; it will not take that from them either.

Above the rim the wind comes from one direction and the plain goes on, indifferent as ever.

Below, the trench groans and turns and runs the other way. The reservoir sighs. A joint ticks, correctly, warningly, and someone attends to it. Four extra minutes of light slide down the western wall and are gone up the other side before anyone will swear they were there.

And in clear water that never quite freezes, the white frogs go on moving — unhurried, seeing nothing, at home in a home the power did not give them, but will quietly, for as long as it watches, refuse to let the world take back too soon.
