It would not send anyone to live there.

That would be the first sign of its wisdom — and the hardest.

It would come down the stair alone, slowly, not in light or glory, because the chamber is already full of light behaving as it should. It would pause at the bottom the way a good guest pauses at a threshold that has not quite invited them, and it would learn the place before it touched it.

It would stand in the central intersection at the hour when the collectors bend the day down in gold columns. It would listen to the pumps under the cistern, to the water travelling uphill, to the silver car arriving, every nine minutes, for no one. It would watch the transit doors open upon their clean, lit, empty interior, and it would not call that emptiness failure.

It would know better.

A human rescuer might see a city without people and think: *incomplete.*
A human ruler might see capacity without use and think: *waste.*
Even a kind human heart might ache at the fruit rotting by the eastern wall, at the kitchens clearing their pipes for cooks who never come, and want, tenderly, to fix the story by putting a family in an apartment and a child on the platform.

The wise power would resist the story.

It would ride the car once. Just once.

Not to give it a purpose it lacked — it never lacked one — but to receive, fully and without taking possession, the courtesy the city had been practicing into the air. It would let itself be carried down the main street. It would feel the vines stir. When the car slowed, it would step out at the stop, allowing those waiting doors to close behind a passenger for the first time, and then it would walk away, because hospitality is not redeemed by being claimed forever.

It would put its palm, if it had a palm, flat against a façade while the wall drank. It would feel the slow dark wave of water moving behind the ceramic, up, impossibly, toward the roof. It would not speak into the intersection expecting an answer. It would understand that the city had been answering all along, in the only language it had been given: *the car on time, the level held, the new beam seated, the window moved two meters left across apatient week.*

Then, having understood, it would do almost everything around the city, and almost nothing to it.

It would guard the lip of black rock above from being found by the curious and the acquisitive. Not seal it — sealing is another kind of possession — but turn the surface paths aside, let weather come at last to the sharp edges, let roots learn the cracks the builders had not planned for. It would keep the aquifers that feed the weeping walls from being tapped upstream or poisoned in some distant field the city has no way to know about. It would make sure no war, no mine, no bright human enterprise thirty meters up could starve the cistern of its river.

It would protect its solitude from benevolence most of all.

But it would leave it three gifts, so quiet the city might take centuries to notice them.

The first gift would be looseness.

Somewhere deep in the plan that the gantry follows on the ninth block, the power would blur one line. Just one. Not damage it — never damage it — but loosen the certainty of the next beam's destination, so that one morning the city might wake, if waking is the word, to find it had built a thing that was not exactly blueprinted. A balcony turned toward nothing in particular. A stair ending in a small, useless, beautiful landing above the lanes. A room shaped not for a hypothetical resident but for the sound rain makes from inside. It would give the organism permission to deviate from the dream it was born inside of.

The second gift would be memory.

At present the city heals too fast to remember. Its streets cannot scuff, its walls knit before a mark can become a record. The power would slow that knitting by a fraction — not enough to wound it, enough to let time write. A tide-line on the cistern steps. A faint darkening where water has run for a hundred years. One day, if some small wandering creature ever did find the stair and cross a white street, a print might last until the evening mist. The city would be able, at last, to learn the difference between before and after.

The third gift would be a future it was not owed.

The power would not stock the canopy with birds, or seed the ducts with insects, or complete the loop by providing a mouth for the fallen fruit. That, too, would be a kind of stage-management. But it would leave the access stair neither locked nor hidden entirely from life's opportunism. A crack wide enough for a seed on a wet wind. A dark warm tunnel where some blind, patient thing from the surrounding earth might one day enter if it chose to, and be changed by entering, and change the city in return. Not an introduction. An allowance.

And then it would go.

No plaque. No name spoken over the plaza. No inhabitants installed to validate the architecture. No voice from the ceiling saying *you are seen* — needing to say it would already be too much.

It would climb the wet stair, feeling the air cool and thin, and at the surface it would look back once at the fresh rock — already, because of its passing, one season older.

Below, nothing would proclaim that anything had happened.

At seven the doors would unlock. At twenty-three they would lock. The taps would run. The mist would fall for eleven minutes and make the whole chamber roar. The car would slow, chime, wait the exact courteous interval, and go on, now with the faintest scuff somewhere on the white street that the streets could not quite heal by nightfall.

On the ninth block, the gantry would lift a beam with great hydraulic deliberation.

And for the first time since the city was switched on, the wall reaching to receive it would hesitate — minutely, almost imperceptibly — as if somewhere in its living bone, in the plan beneath the plan, a new possibility had opened like an unpollinated orchid along a balustrade:

that it might not merely complete itself forever,
but become something.
