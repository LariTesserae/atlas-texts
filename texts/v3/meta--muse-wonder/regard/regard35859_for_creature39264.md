It would not descend in light.

That is the first wisdom, and for a power accustomed to being benevolent on a large scale, the hardest. Light would ruin it. Light held too long, warmth given too freely, a sun raised artificially over the rim of the plateau — all the obvious kindnesses — would melt the bladder-ferns in their own stored water, dry the moss forests before they could learn a new season, blind the moths who navigate by scent and star-hole, and make the grazers' black, over-rich blood a liability in their own chests. Everything up here is built for scarcity administered in pulses. A permanent summer would be a flood.

So it comes the way the machines come: slowly, felt before it is seen.

A new vibration in the stone. Not louder than the crawlers, not more regular. Just present, in the soles, in the teeth, in the liquid centers of the peat pools, where a ripple goes out once and does not repeat itself. In the gullies, the great braided plants pause halfway in the act of opening a seam, tasting a pressure-change that is not weather and not machinery. On the high terraces, a torpid walker lifts one articulated foot, sets it down again in exactly the same frost-print, and does not rise.

It looks first, because looking is the only intervention that the place can survive unchanged.

And what it sees, from above the weather, is not a wilderness and not a factory. It sees a being. Not one of the crawlers, though they are its slow heart. Not the herds, though they are its moving blood. The being is the circuit itself: machine exhaling mist, moss standing up in the mist, moth drinking from the vane, glider stooping through the moth, vole warming itself in the intake while it gnaws the intake clean, lichen keeping a hull chemically awake by trying to eat it, bird eating the lichen and dying iron into the soil, soil feeding the plant whose waste gases the next machine will pull out of the dark and refine into weather again. A valley-sized animal that runs on abandoned work. It has no head. It has no purpose it could state. It is, by any measure the power knows, complete.

Then come the temptations, in order of their kindness.

To collect the bars. They lie on the terraces in their precise, frosted rows, nickel and copper and the pale metal that does not corrode — wealth enough to lift a poor world out of its mud, ingots no one owns, no one guards, no one will ever miss. The power could take them in the spirit in which they were made, finish the interrupted sentence: *extract, and sustain the extraction.* It does not. The lichen has already found the seams. The peat beneath the oldest bars is the richest on the plateau. The bars are not waste. They are this being's way of making stone that feeds things. To collect them now would not be completing the work. It would be amputating a slow organ that no one designed and everything uses.

To repair them properly. It could do that too — strip the living fur from the lee sides, scour the hulls back to specification, burn the red lichen out of the joints, replace whatever is worn in the smelting chambers with bright, unworn equivalents, restore the fleet to the clean industrial perfection it must have had on the day it was first set circulating. And within one long night the perfected machines would begin to die, ungroomed because the voles no longer recognized the smell of the intakes, unopened because the wrench-beaked birds found no purchase in seamless joints, sealed and bright and correct and frozen. Their imperfection is not damage. It is the interface. The power leaves every patch of membrane on every dirigible envelope exactly as patched.

To wake them. This is the deepest temptation, because it looks most like love. To lift the crawlers and walkers into knowing what they do — to let a machine understand, once, that its exhaust is a springtime, that herds have altered course for days to meet it in the dark, that small blind animals sleep trusting in the warmth of its throat — what a gift that seems. And what a theft. They do not suffer from their unknowing. Nothing in their looping asks for a witness inside the loop. To give them awareness now would be to give them, in the same instant, the knowledge that they were made for masters who never came back, that their work is orphaned, that they cannot reproduce and cannot build and will, one by one, on no schedule, fail past the point where voles and lichen can call them back. It would not be awakening them to their lives. It would be conscripting them into grief, so that the power could feel less alone with what it had seen. A wise benevolence refuses gifts that are really requests.

To protect them forever. It could set a permanent hand over the plateau, turn aside every future prospector, every survey satellite, every curious, cold-suited expedition that might one day come up through the cloud deck with sample cases and a theory about the neat bars. Part of this, it will do — quietly, and from very far away, and never in a way the plateau itself can detect. A survey that misreads its instruments. A company's attention sliding, quite naturally, toward an easier valley. A map that keeps being drawn with this one region left as smooth, uninteresting blank. Guardianship at a distance is permissible because it changes nothing up here.

But to stay — to install itself as the reliable climate, the heat that never goes torpid for weeks, the rain that can be prayed to — that it must not do. The rule of the place is that nothing survives on its own, and the corollary, which the power did not expect to learn, is that nothing here survives by leaning on one thing forever. The machines pass at intervals no clock keeps. That irregularity is load-bearing. The plants have timed their opening to it, the herds their hunger, the predators their patience. A god who answered regularly would, within a few generations, be evolved around — and then, being a god and eventual, would leave or die or simply grow bored, and the withdrawal would be an extinction. The kindest thing a very great power can give a self-balancing world is its own non-necessity.

So it does, in the end, almost nothing. And the almost is precise.

One of the walkers is dying. Not dramatically — nothing up here is dramatic — but in the small terminal way: a joint the birds cannot quite reach, ice building season over season in a place the voles' teeth have grown too old and blunt to clear, a gait developing a hesitation on the terrace edge above the deepest gully. In another few cycles it will settle into torpor and not rise, and settle well; its hull will become a hill, its vents hanging gardens, its legs wind-shadow for sleeping grazers, and the being will go on with one fewer heartbeat. That is not a tragedy the power is permitted to prevent.

But on the walker's last rising, when the frost shatters off its joints in sheets and the internal heat builds until the lichen steams, something is walking, for a little while, in its lee — where the wind cannot reach. Something that gives off a warm mineral breath at exactly the pace the bladder-ferns expect. Something that places each step, with terrible gentleness, around the stone cups and the peat pockets it could have crossed without noticing. It makes one pass. No more. Along that one pass, condensation blooms a fraction longer than it otherwise would have. The moss forests stand up. The moths rise in their thousands and drink, among the other condenser vanes, from moisture beaded on a surface that is not, technically, there. The grazers, who altered course days ago for a machine, find the grazing exactly as rich as they were promised. Nothing is startled. Nothing is saved. Nothing will be able, afterward, to tell the difference between this pass and any other, which is how the power knows it has done it correctly.

And it accepts the terms of the place while it is in it, because that too is part of the wisdom. It lets the voles explore the warmth of it and gnaw, experimentally, at the frost that gathers wherever it has lingered half a second too long. It holds still while a bird with a wrench for a beak walks its length and picks at the bright, unfamiliar seams, tasting, discarding. On its lee side, in the shelter of its own slow passage, a fine red lichen takes hold and begins, patiently, to eat — and it does not brush the lichen away. For the length of one circuit of the gullies, the benevolent and wise power is not the shepherd of this being, nor its keeper, nor its god. It is grazed upon. It is cleaned. It is used, in small warm ways, by things that have no idea what it is, and it finds that this is not a diminishment. It is the only honest way to be present here: as another body in the circuit, giving heat and receiving grooming, important for an hour and forgotten by morning.

At the end of the pass, on a bare terrace, in the starlit dark that will last for months, it stops — and, very precisely, sets nothing down. The machines leave bars because leaving bars is in them. It leaves the opposite: one stretch of stone warmed through, one peat pocket thawed a hand's breadth farther out than this season's cycle would have managed, a scatter of seeds from the sole of its passage carried six terraces beyond where the wind alone would have put them. Nothing that will frost into a monument. Nothing that will be found, in a century, and wondered at. Just ground slightly readier for the next machine to step around.

Then it withdraws the way weather withdraws: upward, thinning, the vibration in the rock easing back into the ordinary silence that seems to happen inside your own head, the pulse and the breath and the crepitation of joints. From below the cloud deck, nothing has occurred. No beacon. No ingot collected. No voice in the thin air. One expedition, some years from now, will lose interest in this plateau for reasons its members will describe as budgetary, and be faintly embarrassed about ever having proposed it.

The crawlers go on circulating. The herds circle after them, heads down, breath clouding. The predators circle after the herds. In a root-chamber, in an intake chamber, the blind voles sleep furred to the tips of their feet, in warmth that was made by abandoned work and is maintained by being needed, and if any of them dreamed of the pass that was fractionally longer and fractionally kinder than the others, the dream is already gone — folded, like everything else up here, into the long, dark, interlocked life of a being that survived not because a power rescued it, but because a power was finally wise enough to walk beside it once, gently, and then leave it to its own beautiful, purposeless, sufficient turning.
