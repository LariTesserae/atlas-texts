It would not descend in glory.

It would come, if it came at all, at midday, when the canopy had turned its silver undersides to the sun and the whole Cup was folded away in shade — and it would come on foot, through one of the notches in the rim, and stop there a long time with the cold wind behind it.

First, it would learn not to announce itself.

For the power would be wise enough to hear, before it crossed into the warm green bowl, what the bowl itself already knows: that the resonant teeth of the rim are listening, that a voice here could be a kind of falling stone, that love spoken too loudly in this place might bring the mountain down on the beloved. So it would put away whatever thunder it ordinarily traveled in. It would soften its footsteps. It would make itself, as nearly as a great power can, the size of a neighbor.

Then it would go down among the houses and do nothing.

Not for an hour. Not for a day. For seasons, if seasons were what it took. It would sit in the lanes with its feet flat on the moss-mat until it understood that a step here is a sentence. It would let the grazers crop around it. It would let the climbers pass overhead, pruning, and not look up too quickly. It would let a glider fall asleep hanging from its sleeve. It would be groomed, eventually — cautiously, by many small hands of several species at once — and it would understand that this was not curiosity. This was the town reading it.

And being benevolent, it would be tempted.

It would see the translucent cheeks full of water and want to bring rain. A real rain, once, filling every bladder and cistern and root-mouth, every barrel-tree to its bark, a generosity so complete that no mother would cross a lane with slack pouches again. It would not do it. It would see, in time, what the Cup has spent a thousand generations learning: that water here is not only drink, it is measure. It is wealth, courtesy, courtship, memory, the size of a family and the size of the town itself. Rain would not save them. Rain would unmake the careful arithmetic by which they love one another, and then, when the rain did not come again, they would be poorer than thirst ever made them.

It would see the old gnawer in the doorway she can no longer widen, her teeth worn to smooth pegs, fed pulp by her grandchildren — and want to give her back her teeth. It would not. Not because her age is sacred suffering, or any such cold comfort. Simply because her grandchildren feeding her is not a failure of the town. It is the town, working. To interrupt it with a miracle would be to steal a piece of tenderness and call the theft mercy.

It would see that they have no tools, no fire, no stories that can be overheard, and a lesser power — benevolent, perhaps, but not wise — would ache to leave them a gift they could keep: a word, a warning, a sharpened edge, a name for themselves. This one would leave none of those. It would understand that their history is already kept, in terraces bent around old falls, in the rings of house-walls, in scent laid down at thresholds deeper than any archive. That their knowledge is not missing language. It is knowledge that has chosen, deliberately and successfully, to live somewhere language cannot argue it to death.

Above all, it would not tell them about the rock tooth.

There is one — the power would find it the way the elders find it, through the soles of the feet, a long glassy blade above the market-hollows with a brightening note in it, a crack at its root widening by less than a hair's breadth a year. In seventeen years, or thirty, it will go. The power could see the whole fall at once, the green concussion, the answering tones waking tooth after tooth around the rim. And here, at last, it would act.

So quietly that acting and not-acting become the same gesture.

At night, while the fog slid down and every leaf tilted and the whole population went still to drink, the power would climb — slowly, without vibration, the way mist climbs — to the root of that one tooth. It would not push it down, for the town knows how to receive a falling stone and grow a cool dew-place out of ruin. It would not hold it up forever, for nothing in the Cup is held up forever, and to make one stone immortal would be to lie to the mountain. It would only fill the finest part of the crack with stone-dust and resin and its own patient pressure, the way the gnawers seal a cistern-tree with chewed pulp: changing the note of the tooth by just enough that, when its century of leaning ends, it will fall alone, into the muffling green, and not sing its brothers down after it.

A small maintenance. The sort of thing any inhabitant would do for a house not its own, if it had the hands.

Then the power would turn its attention outward, because the truest dangers to the Cup were never inside it.

Beyond the rim the world is getting louder and warmer and more curious. Clouds that have hidden the mountain since it rose are thinning in places. Things with wings that are not gliders have begun, far below, to cross the high air with their minds on mapping. Seeds of foreign, thirsty plants ride winds that once would have died against the cold. The power would not build a wall — the Cup has a wall, and walls that are noticed get climbed. Instead it would do something more like weather. It would bend a little forgetfulness around the mountain. Let the surveyor's eye slide off this one summit. Let the strange winged things lose interest, gently, for no reason they record. Let the alien seed land, if it must land, on the bare scree outside where the mountain itself knows how to refuse it. Let the fog keep arriving, one thin breath at a time, down the cooling stone, for as many generations as the mountain can honestly give.

Not forever. The power is wise, and knows that forever is not a gift it can give anything living. The vents will cool one day. The bowl will change. The agreement among races will compost itself and grow into some new agreement no one now alive could recognize. To prevent that would not be benevolence. It would be taxidermy.

So it would intervene only where the harm came from a scale the town had never agreed to live at — from outside its weather, outside its silence, outside its world — and everywhere else it would practice the oldest civic act of the Cup. It would listen through the soles of its feet, and choose not to answer.

And when it finally left, at dusk, through the notch in the rim, it would take care to smooth the moss where it had sat, so no footprint of worship remained for anyone to find. It would leave no shrine, no scar, no scent at any threshold. The market-hollows would open as usual. Throat-patches would flicker, forehead to forehead. A climber would redirect a tendril. Somewhere below, a burrower would turn the day's leavings down into the dark. Nothing would be different, except that one stone, years from now, would fall without catastrophe, and a hundred small disasters that might have come from the loud world below the clouds would somehow never quite arrive.

They would never know. They would never thank it.

From the notch, looking back a last time, the power would consider this not a failure of its visit, but the whole proof of it: a small green town in a young stone bowl, high above everything, warm, crowded, breathing through its leaves, keeping every drop it has ever owned — still entirely, undisturbedly, its own.
