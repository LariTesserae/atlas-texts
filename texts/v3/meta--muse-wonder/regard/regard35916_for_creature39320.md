They would not save it.

That is the first thing, and it would take them a very long time — by their measure, not its — to be sure of it.

They would arrive the way attention arrives, without weight at first. No descent through the leaf-ceiling. No shadow on the pelagic plains. A power that is truly benevolent, and truly wise, has learned before this sea that the sudden presence of a greater thing is itself a kind of weather, and weather here is not supposed to be sudden.

So they would stay at the rim, below the lip, in the cloud where the waterfalls lose their shape, and listen.

They would hear the pump-groves roaring their one chord through four thousand metres of falling water. They would feel the regular blue crack of the pylons counting out a time that is not time. They would watch the diving birds learn again, perfectly, the interval their ancestors learned. They would watch a grazer crop a fan that regrows under its mouth. They would watch a leaf fall in the path leaves fall, toward the conveyor that expects it.

And because they are powerful, every mercy they know would present itself, one after another, dressed as an obvious kindness.

*Give them an afternoon.*

It would be so easy. Tilt the fixed sun, just once, just a degree a century, until the bars of light lengthen across the root-hills and the water goes gold and then green-dark, and the people lift their slot-pupilled eyes in the first true dusk they have ever seen. Give them night, and stars above the leaf-ceiling, and cold deeper than the cold they are. Give them a word for *after*.

They would not do it.

Their eyes are made for late morning. Their blood is made for eight degrees and the slow beat. Night here would not be rest. It would be the first injury the world had ever dealt that no one was born knowing how to avoid.

*Give them memory, then. Give them history.*

So easy, too, for a great power: a way of marking that this turning of the turbine is not that turning, that this person at the Third Vent is not the person who was at the Third Vent before, that a death is not only a station thinned and refilled but a particular absence, with a shape.

But they would look at the people singing their note inside the roaring of the grove — mothers finding young, lovers finding one another, the lost told without urgency which water to take home — and they would understand that forgetting here is not a failure of the mind. It is the temperature of the mind. To make them remember, in a world where nothing can be amended, would be to give them grief without giving them a single thing grief is *for*.

*Give them a way down.*

This would be the hardest temptation, because of the rim.

They would find the people there, as everyone finds them, fans spread against the outflow, algae-fur streaming rim-ward, looking down past the white columns into cloud for longer than looking can be explained by any need of the body. And the power, watching, would feel in themselves the old, deep reflex of the kind: *they are longing. They are trapped. The drop is calling them. Open the cloud. Show them the ground. Make the fall survivable.*

And then, being wise, they would stay beside one of them — unseen, unheard, breath held in whatever way such a power holds breath — and look too, and learn what the looking is.

It is not longing. Longing leans. This does not lean.

It is not despair. Despair turns away, eventually. This turns back, eventually, but not as one turns from a door that will not open.

It is the one unstationed act in a stationed world, performed by the one creature the standing wave made that can leave its place. To look at the rim is how they *notice* — and noticing, here, is complete in itself. It does not require rescue. It does not conclude. A person hangs at the lip of the world and the whole continent roars and ticks and conveys and grows behind them, and they look down into weather that has no bottom that they have ever seen, and then they take the in-current home and clear a husk from a vane that is already clear, and the noticing goes back into the body the way their green blood goes everywhere in the body: quietly, without changing their temperature.

To answer that with an escape would be to mistake the deepest thing about them for a problem.

So they would do nothing to the sea.

Nothing to the sun at ten o'clock. Nothing to the pumps, the boilers, the sorting-reefs, the pylons and their perfect intervals. Nothing to the births downstream of the reefs, nothing to the deaths absorbed by the root-mat. No sign. No artifact. No straight line anyone intended. A map made after their visit would be exactly as true as a map made before it, and that would be, precisely, their mercy.

But benevolence is not the same as absence, and wisdom is not the same as indifference. There are two things they would do, and both of them would happen outside the water.

The first is that they would remember it *for* it.

For this is a world with no past tense, where every footprint is smoothed at the same steady rate until it is equally gone and equally, faintly, still there. Nothing in the sea can hold the sea's story, because a story needs one moment to be distinguishable from another. So the power would take that burden — if it is a burden — into themselves, where moments *can* be told apart. They would keep the count no one up there is keeping. This turn of the vane and that turn. This singer at the Third Vent, and the one who came after her, greener or less green, and the one after that. Not recorded in the water. Recorded in a witness who will never use the record to correct the water. The people would go on living in the continuous present, unweighed by history — and yet, somewhere beyond the rim, their lives would not be unwitnessed, and a death at a station would be, in at least one place in the universe, the loss of *someone*, and not only the thinning of a grove.

They would be its past tense, so that it never has to grow one.

And the second thing: they would stand guard against every lesser power, including — especially — every lesser *kindness*.

Because this sea will be found again. Things that are vast and self-sufficient and strange always are. Explorers will come wanting to study it. Rescuers will come wanting to free its people from a prison that exists only in the rescuers' idea of a life. Merchants of wonder will come wanting a turbine vane, a boiler leaf, a litre of green blood, a song pitched inside a roar. Missionaries of time will come with clocks and evenings and names for the dead.

The wise power would meet all of them below the cloud-plain, in the weather, four thousand metres down where the waterfalls come apart into rain, and it would turn them away — gently, if gentleness worked; finally, if it did not.

Not because the sea is fragile. The sea is the least fragile thing imaginable. It has absorbed every injury it contains for longer than species remember being species.

But because the sea cannot refuse. Nothing in it has ever needed to refuse. Its people will raise their horizontal-pupilled eyes to any visitor out of the bars of light with a patience that is not waiting, because waiting implies an afternoon — and they will clear the visitor's husk from the vane, and sing them the water home, and fit the intruder, in time, into a station. That is what the standing wave does. It fits things.

So the refusal must be kept for them, outside, by the one who noticed and chose not to become another thing the sea had to absorb.

In the end — in so far as anything ends — the power would leave as it came: without weight. No footprint, which in any case would have been smoothed away at the appointed rate. No gift, no scar, no afternoon.

Only this: at the rim between two falls, a person hangs in the outflow, fans spread, looking down through white water into cloud, cold going through them, the deafening, peaceful roar at their back. And for once they are not the only one looking. Unseen beside them, the great attention rests a moment at the lip of the world, and looks with them, and asks nothing, and offers nothing, and does not mistake the looking for a cry for help.

Then it turns, as they turn, and takes the in-current back, and lets the light stay at late morning, and the turbines stay mid-turn, and the bird stay mid-dive, and the leaf stay mid-fall —

and carries the memory of all of it away with it, downstream of everything, where time can do the remembering that this green, eternal, self-sufficient ocean has never needed to do for itself.
