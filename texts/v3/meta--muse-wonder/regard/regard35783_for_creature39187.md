They would do almost nothing.

That is the first, and hardest, part of the attention.

A power that was merely strong would arrive with its hands full. Light, for a world living in blue dusk. Warmth, for water held year-round at the edge of freezing. Fish for the kelp forests, snails for the stonewort, worms for the silt. A mouth for a creation that has spent uncounted generations learning to live without hunger. Consciousness, laid like a gift across league after league of meadow, so that at last someone *in there* would know how vast and strange and beautiful it was.

A wise power would put all of those gifts down again, outside the ice.

Light would not bless plants that have sifted themselves, species by species, down to what can be wanted from ten meters of failing blue. Warmth would loosen the cold's long preservative hand; the black timbers would go, the mats of half-rotted ancestors would finish rotting all at once, the slow loop by which the place eats its own fall would quicken into collapse. Fish would not complete the garden. They would begin the first famine in it — grazing forests that have never been grazed, stirring the clear lanes, dying in the dark at temperatures their bodies were never shaped for, and feeding nothing that knew how to be fed by carrion. And consciousness — consciousness, in fifty meters of pressure and permanent late afternoon, with no hands and no distance it could cross faster than a season — would not be a gift at all. It would be the invention of loneliness in the one place that had been spared it.

The resident does not suffer from being what it is. It suffers, if the word can be used at all, only from the conditions to which it has become the exact answer. To change those conditions in the name of abundance would be to love an idea of life more than this life.

So the power would not stock it. It would not drain it, or clear it, or warm it, or read it.

It would not brush the algae from the gate-posts to recover the inscriptions. The inscriptions are gone because the moss has been living on them, cushion over cushion, and the moss is not vandalism here. It is the inhabitant wearing the builders as skin. To peel it back for history would be to wound the present resident to satisfy a visitor's curiosity about the dead. The power, being wise, can remember the stones as they are — suggestions of characters, peck-marks under green — without needing them legible.

It would not plant anything in the great circle at the center, either. And it would not clear it further.

That kept place — kelp absent, stonewort short and even, the clearest and coldest water in the basin converging over enormous stones — may be reverence, or chemistry, or an accident of current that has lasted so long it has become indistinguishable from reverence. A lesser attention would need to know which. It would test the soil. It would seed the turf and watch. It would stand in the plaza and wait for the meaning to declare itself, and in waiting, trample.

The wise power leaves the room as it found it. Some things are not invitations. Some things are simply rooms that a life keeps, or is kept by, and does not explain.

What the power *would* do happens mostly above the ice, and beyond the basin, and on a scale the resident will never perceive — which is precisely why it is permissible.

It would guard the conditions, not the details.

An inflow that might one day cut warm and silt-heavy through the deep fields would be turned aside, gently, a valley away, so gradually that no map ever showed a miracle. A warming of the upper world that would thin the ice-skin and thicken the summer bloom until the dusk below became true night would be held off over this one grey surface — not by defying the climate of a planet, but by shade, by mist, by a small persistent weather of its own that resembled nothing but weather. Where human surveyors might eventually sound the basin and find pavement where pavement should not be, and come back with pumps and lights and questions, their instruments would find only deep cold water and deeper mud; their divers would lose the lanes; their funding would wander elsewhere. Not forbidden. Not punished. Simply, repeatedly, unfindable, in the manner of a place the world had agreed to misremember.

No fence the kelp could grow over. No law the eelgrass would have to obey. Just a widening ring of ordinariness around the one place that cannot survive being interesting.

And it would secure the resident against ending without issue — *outside* itself, where securing costs it nothing. Somewhere else, in other cold dark water over other made ground not yet built or not yet drowned, a few seeds and spores and fragments would be laid sleeping in silt, unmixed, ungerminated, patient as everything down here is patient. Not a colony. Not a rescue. A memory in biological form, held in reserve against the day a crack in the basin, a shift in the currents, a slow geological shrug ends this particular continent. If that day never comes, they will sleep forever, and that too is acceptable. Continuity does not require that the story be forced to continue. Only that its ending, if it ends, not be an accident of someone else's greed.

Inside the basin itself, the power permits itself one act, and it is not an improvement.

It goes down.

Through the half-frozen skin, through the first ten meters where the light gives up, into the blue remainder. Down the forty, fifty meters to where the lanes run between the plantings and the kelp rises twenty meters from the field walls, purple-black, answering a current minutes after the current has passed. It moves slowly — at the speed the place allows, which is slower than reverence usually travels — because anything faster would be a weather event. It lets the fronds brush the whole length of it. It lets the meadow heads lift and flatten under its passage and resume. It does not swim the canals like an explorer. It does not sound the pavement, or dig toward the older pavement under the rubble fill. It accepts being navigated by alleys it did not plant, past gate-posts without gates, up no stairs that climb three steps and stop at water on all sides.

It is the first animal the resident has touched in a very long time, and it behaves, as nearly as it can, like something the resident's world already knows how to receive: a pressure. A brief shadow in the dusk. A displacement of water that will settle.

At the center it stops over the great circle and does the only thing the place has never had done for it, and cannot do for itself, and will not be harmed by.

It witnesses it.

Not for a moment, the way a visitor witnesses. For a season. For a slow submarine year. Long enough to watch the green bloom thicken far above and die back and begin its white fall, fleck by fleck, down through the still strata, settling on the kelp, on the meadows, on the stonewort of the plaza. Long enough to hear the surface ice groan in winter and feel it arrive as a tone in the teeth of the world. Long enough to see a waterlogged beam shift somewhere in a roofless house and knock once against stone, and to not go looking for it. Long enough that witnessing stops being observation and becomes a form of keeping company with a being that does not know it is accompanied, and must never be made to know, because knowing would be the one real violence left available.

Nothing down there will ever be unwitnessed again. That changes the universe. It does not change the basin, which is the point.

When the power leaves, it leaves upward, unhurried, through the lanes, through the twenty meters of swaying forest, through the last of the light. It takes no sample. It leaves no marker, no blessing the plants could root in, no warmth in its wake that a single frond could detect. A submarine front of stirred mud will come through in its own time, as such fronts do, and settle a finger's thickness over everything, erasing what little record a visitor leaves.

The kelp closes behind it at the speed of plants.

The meadows resume their sway — still finishing the last weather while the next one begins.

And the power stays, thereafter, the way the cold stays: at the edges, holding the temperature of the world the resident chose without choosing, so that the garden under its lid of dark water may go on feeding on its own green fall, dividing itself into fields it did not build, in rows it inherited from hands it never knew, one slow, quiet, plant-thick year at a time — not saved, not improved, not awakened.

Let alone, at last, on purpose.
