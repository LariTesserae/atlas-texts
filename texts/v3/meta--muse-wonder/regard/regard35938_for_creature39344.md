They would come first to the plain.

Not down. Not yet. A wise power learns a place from its edges, and a benevolent one learns what not to touch before it learns what it might save.

From above, the temptation would be simple and enormous: *bring rain.*

This is, after all, a green world dying of thirst in slow motion, is it not? A pit of life sealed inside leather and wax and gel, recycling the same hoarded moisture through leaf and gut and corpse, while the sun walks overhead with all its ordinary cruelty. Any god with a bucket could be forgiven for wanting to tip it.

They would stand at the rim long enough to understand why they must not.

Rain would not bless this valley. Rain would ruin it.

Water standing in the hollows would rot the root-mat from below. Water on the leaves would breed softness in a forest that has spent every generation armoring itself against softness. Water in the ferment dark would turn rich mulch to poison gas, drown larvae, swell the sealed trunks past splitting, loosen whole cliff-shelves of roots at once. The boomers would come up choking through a collapsing floor, blind in the loud green light, and die smelling of sweetness.

Everything here is alive *because* nothing is wet. The water is not missing. It is hidden, the way blood is hidden. To make it visible would be to make it bleed.

So they would put the rain back wherever rain is kept.

The second temptation would be quiet.

For the ears of visitors, the valley is an assault: cicada-shriek and frog-thock and bird-argument and growth popping like small gunfire, and under it all the booming, doom, doom, through root and bone. A merciful power might want to soften it. To give the people wax for more than their ears. To give the animals peace.

They would listen longer, and learn that the noise is not noise.

It is mating and warning and pollination and geography. It is flowers calling beetles and trunks calling in the wind and mothers saying *stay close* through a floor that cannot be seen. It is a hundred thousand negotiations conducted at full volume because the alternative is to be eaten unheard. Even the silence-seekers speak here, by stamping.

To quiet the valley would be to blindfold it.

So they would not.

They would go down, if they went down at all, the slow way, by the rope ladder, past the cached stone blades and the shrines in the niches, and they would pack their own ears if they had ears, and smear themselves with resin, and move through like any other visitor: small, temporary, expecting abandonment. They would leave no platform that did not rot. No mark that did not grow over by morning.

And under the mat, they would find the true resident.

Not the birds, flamboyant tenants of the week. Not the hand-sized beetles solemnly harvesting the rim. Not the human bands, coming in for gel and fruit and leaving before dark. The boomer. Shoulder-built, sealed, nearly blind, its smooth head tipped not toward sound in air but toward pressure in root. Its whole body an ear pressed to its world.

Here, perhaps, the third temptation: *give it more.*

Eyes, so it can see the green cathedral it maintains. Tools, so its spade-feet need not work so hard. A way up the ladder, a glimpse of the sky it lives under and will never know. Uplift, the old bright cruelty dressed as kindness.

A wise power would sit down in the ferment dark — carefully, because the floor is someone's work and someone's grandmother — and set a palm flat to the roots, and simply feel the country talking.

Quick triple tap: rich, thin mat. Slow roll: danger. A young one answering half a beat late. An old one signing every message with its private drag, so no predator, however patient, can counterfeit it exactly. *Here. Here. The mat is thin. The mat is rich. We are underneath it, making it.*

They would understand that the boomer does not lack a world. It *has* a world, complete at the scale of pressure and tempo, where a falling tree arrives as a long shock of news and a hundred seedlings racing for the light are a patter of distant rain that is not rain. Green means nothing down there, not because the boomer is poorer for it, but because green is a word eyes invented. Down there the forest says itself differently, and says it without pause.

To give it eyes would not enlarge it. It would exile it from the only language it is fluent in.

So they would not uplift it. They would not tame it, teach it, name it to itself, or make it grateful.

What, then? Almost nothing. Which is to say: everything that can be done without breaking the spell of the place.

Out on the salt plain, where no root hears, they would do their visible work.

They would make the road past the valley uninteresting.

Not a wall — walls advertise. Not a curse — curses attract the curious. Simply a subtle turning of paths, a hundred kilometers of travelers finding, for no reason they could name, that their feet preferred the long way around, their fires lit poorly that night, their thoughts went elsewhere. Caravans carrying tar and resin-torches would winter one valley over. Ash on the wind would still fall in, seed and dead insect and mineral dust, all the valley's lawful food — but no live ember would ever ride that evening downdraft over the rim alive. Somewhere in the empty air above the pit, sparks would go quietly out, as sparks do, unremarked.

Fire is the one thing this forest cannot negotiate with. So fire would be the one thing politely refused at the door.

They would walk the rim with the great beetles and learn what the wind brings and whether it is still enough. In centuries when the desert sent only salt, salt, salt, they might once — once — nudge a far dust-storm a fraction off its course: a breath of volcanic ash rich in phosphorus, a drift of loess full of crushed shell and old ocean, so that it sifted down through the sticky rim-vines and into the rivers of ants, indistinguishable from any other gift the sky had ever made. No miracle. Just a slightly wider world, arriving as dust, at the speed the valley already trusts.

And at the far end of the pit, where the cliff is freshest and the rock still bright from breaking, they would put their attention deep into the stone, and find the fault that, in two hundred years or twenty, would take the valley sideways.

Because the valley will, in time, become a victim of its own success. Soil is being made faster than stone is falling. The mat rises, corpse on corpse, root on root. One day the pit will fill toward its own rim, the trap will shallow, the downdraft will skim instead of pour, and the dry green ocean will spill, bewildered, onto a plain it is not built for.

So the power would not deepen it in a night — that thunder would collapse tunnels, crush chambers, send the boomers fleeing vibrations they could not interpret. No. They would loosen the future by grains. A crack encouraged. A wedge of rock persuaded to remember it was once separate. So that long after any living boomer was soil, a cliff would sigh down in the ordinary violence of this place, and open a second hollow, raw and bright, smelling of new stone. Runners at hand-speed. Roots white-knuckling into cracks by nightfall. The boomers below, feeling the great shock travel through their world, would answer it in the oldest grammar they have:

Danger. Change. Food arriving. Move on. Open that. Pack this.

A new room in a house they would never know had been given to them.

All the rest, the power would leave alone. The predators counterfeiting *here, here* would not be punished; the false reply is part of why the true one learned to sign its name. The huge bald birds would keep their terrible patience. The young would be eaten and the old would be eaten and both would become seedlings splitting a ribcage open to the light. Benevolence here is not the abolition of death. It is refusing to waste water that expensive.

Perhaps, at the very end of their visit — if visit is the word for something that may take a hundred years — they would do one small, unrecordable thing.

In the dark under the mat, a female boomer would be packing a birth chamber tight under a root braid, lining it with chewed husk. The young would come blind and sealed and silent, kneading at her belly, learning rhythm the way other creatures learn light. And sometime in the second week, a skull plate would harden, and a new weight would send up its first true boom through the floor, clumsy and enormous with effort.

Every adult nearby would answer. Registration. *Here.*

And from farther than any colony, farther than the predators lie, farther than family has ever reached, there would come back — once, in no colony's accent, with a patience in it that felt like a hand held flat and still against the root — an answer.

Not a word. The power would not presume to speak their language beyond the one syllable it could be sure of.

*Here.*

Felt, and then withdrawn, before it could become a god, a dependency, a direction to pray in. Just enough to enter the record of the mat, the way a falling tree enters it, the way weather does: something large passed through the world, and did it no harm, and the world is very slightly different for having registered it.

Then they would climb the rope ladder if they needed ladders, hand over bone rung, up out of the roar, until with distance it sorted itself into a single sound again, like surf on a shore that has no sea.

On the plain, the silence would come back. Nothing would be built to mark them. The shrine-keepers, finding perhaps one stone restacked in a niche high above the mat, would think they had done it themselves and forgotten.

But the boomers would go on, all day and all night, shoulders to the roots, inventing the floor they walk on out of their own ancestors, in a valley that grows at the edge of violence out of dust and locked water and noise.

*Here,* they say.

*Here. Here. The mat is thin. The mat is rich. We are underneath it, making it.*

And for once, something wise enough to change everything had listened carefully enough to change almost nothing — and to guard, from very far away, the almost nothing it changed.
