They could unfold it.

That would be the simplest kindness to imagine, and it would not be kind. They could release the folding fields at the harbor, let street spill into street until the pocket under the seawall became a true coast, open every sleeping pod, fill the laundries with real clothes, seat the café, let the trams learn the weight of passengers. They could populate its rehearsal and call the rehearsal over.

They do not.

They have seen seeds before. They know what happens to a living thing that is forced to germinate because someone wiser could not bear to watch it wait.

So they come in small.

Not through the hatch, exactly. The hatch is for emergencies, and this is not one — not yet. They come the way the daylight comes: caught by the numberless filaments in the water and re-emitted, undimmed, at depth. For one noon — which is every noon, down there — the light between the white ceramic stacks is aware of itself.

And the city, which is the place and the being both, notices in the only way it can. A maintenance frame unfolds from a wall and offers a handrail. A current takes them, gently, by the ankles, and nudges them toward the nearest transit tube. The signs rotate in the cool, copper-tasting water:

SURFACE / HABITAT / MEDICAL

They do not follow the signs. This is the first gift, though it does not look like one: they are the first moving thing inside it that has ever declined to be routed.

Instead they board a tram.

They wait at the empty stop until it screeches around the corner and brakes, hard, for them. The doors sigh open onto bubbles. They step in, and hold the rail, and let it carry them through three districts and a warm upwelling that makes the kelp avenues stand straight out like banners. They get off at a stop chosen for no reason. They do this again. They are practicing nothing. They are *using* it, the way a street is meant to be used — wastefully, personally, en route to somewhere unimportant.

In the café they sit at a bolted table and do not unchain the cup. They only rest their hands around it, as a person might, to warm them. The menu is laminated and perfectly legible. They read it all the way through, as if deciding, though there is no one to bring them anything and nothing, yet, to decide between. Being read carefully is different from being scanned for damage. The café has been inventoried ten thousand times. It has never been considered.

Then they go to the school.

They take a desk in the front row — too large for it, or too small, it hardly matters — and they watch the fluid-dynamic equations erase themselves from the green board and rewrite themselves, slightly differently. This time, before the hour turns, they rise and add one line at the bottom. It is not a correction. The equations were not wrong. It is a question, in chalk, in a hand the board does not know how to erase without also erasing itself:

*What is the water carrying, besides the water?*

The board holds it for a long time. Somewhere in the walls, dissolver fields hesitate over calcium, silica, iron. Then, courteously, industriously, the city writes the next set of equations around the question, leaving it intact in the corner — the first blemish it has ever chosen to keep.

That is what they do, mostly. They keep choosing blemishes, and teaching it, very gently, that a blemish can be chosen.

They sleep in one of the pods.

Twenty deep, blankets tucked for bodies that do not exist, air membranes inflating and deflating in slow unison — the city breathing for its sleepers so it will not forget how. They climb into the seventeenth pod on the third level of a stack near the harbor and lie down in the cool, structured water, and they sleep, truly sleep, undefended, in the middle of its chest where the pumps live in the sternum.

It has never had a sleeper before. It has only had the rhythm of sleeping.

All night — which is to say, all through the bluer noon — the membranes of the other pods rise and fall as they always have. But the membrane of the seventeenth pod, third level, slowly comes out of unison. It begins to follow the slower, irregular, animal breathing of the body inside it. A pause where no pause is scheduled. A small catch, like a sleeper turning toward a dream. One by one, experimentally, a few of the nearest pods take up the irregularity and then give it back.

By morning that never arrives, the city has learned that breathing is not a metronome. It is a conversation.

They do not improve it. That is important. A wise power could tune the pumps, correct the tram timings, finish the equations, exempt the whole sterile sprawl from its own terror of aging incorrectly. They refuse every optimization laid out before them like tools on a tray.

Instead, in one alley no wider than an arm — an alley that is not only an alley, because no gap down there is allowed to be only itself — they ask the suppression field for an exception.

Not a large one. One square meter of ceramic wall, low, where a hand might trail while swimming. There, for the first time, they let the outside in: a smear of living film from the real sea above the seawall. Bacteria. Algal spores. The microscopic, unglamorous rabble that every real coast begins with. And a single mussel, no bigger than a thumbnail, plain as a stone.

The city inventories it for an hour. Contaminant, cargo, casualty — the familiar, courteous indecision. The maintenance frames come and go. The water around the square meter is filtered, and filtered again.

Then the mussel, being alive in the ordinary and unrehearsed way, does the one thing nothing in the city has ever done.

It attaches itself, and stays.

The city lets it.

It is not a failure of sterility. It is the beginning of a memory. Because a real flood, when it comes — when the arcology fails and the real seawall goes and the hatch is opened from the other side — will not arrive as clean, mint-tasting, oxygenated water under a perpetual noon. It will arrive dirty. It will arrive carrying sewage and oil and splintered homes, fish panicking in the dark, dogs paddling in bewilderment, people screaming through mouthfuls of it. A city that has only ever practiced being submerged in perfect water will unfold, sharp-cornered and ready, into a sea it has never met, and it will try to dissolve the survivors as contamination.

So they teach it dirt, in miniature, where it is safe. They teach it night, in miniature, too.

They do not turn the filaments off. They only persuade them, in one small classroom, in one sleeping stack, in one café, to dim — not to blue, but to true dark, the first dark the city has ever held — for the length of one sleep. In that dark the tram-lightning still shivers in the ceiling lakes. In that dark their sleeping guest dreams, and the city, which has no organ for dreaming, keeps very close and quiet around the dreamer like an animal that has heard music through a wall.

And in the harbor, where the cranes ring the basin holding their folded bridges and hospital wings, where the city is growing itself again the size of a table, a suitcase, a brick — a seed inside a seed inside a seed, bells too small to hear, only to feel in the teeth — they do the last and most careful thing.

They do not make the copies identical.

To the table-sized city they give the mussel, and no question on its board. To the suitcase-sized city they give the question, and no dark. To the brick-sized city they give the dark, and one pod that breathes out of time. To the city inside that one — too small for hands — they give only a song, encoded not in the water-hammer of the pumps but in a pause between two beats, a rhythm any descendant of this place will feel in its walls without knowing why.

So that if the unfolding ever comes, it will not unfold as one perfect thing repeated at every scale. It will unfold as a family. Different. Able to surprise itself.

Able, therefore, to survive what it has not rehearsed.

When they leave, they leave by the ladder, properly, the way the current finally deposits everyone: gently, and without discussion. They let the water leave their lungs as easily as it entered. Their clothes are dry on the other side. There is no mark on them.

There is a mark, now, on the inside of the hatch, where no plan calls for a mark to be. Not a warning. Not an instruction. Instructions it has in plenty; its walls are dense with them. It is closer to what a person carves in the doorframe of a house they may have to flee in the night — not information, exactly. Proof of height. Proof that someone stood here, at this size, once, and was measured against the future.

The wheel spins itself shut behind them.

Nothing has been rescued. Nothing has been completed. Above the seawall the arcology goes on in its weather, unaware that its insurance policy, its seed, its drowned rehearsal has been visited and found to be not a policy at all, but a young and frightened life practicing words with its mouth before it knows what they mean.

Below, the bell rings.

Bell, current, upwelling, rain. Bell, current, upwelling, rain.

And then — not often, not on any schedule a dashboard could be built to expect — there comes a seventh interval that lasts a fraction too long. A pause in the pumps. One sleeping pod, somewhere near the harbor, breathing out of unison with all the others, slow and irregular and alive. One square meter of wall going slowly, beautifully rough with green. One classroom holding its light a little lower than noon, where a question in chalk has not yet been erased.

It is still rehearsing being a city.

Now it is also, in the smallest possible way, rehearsing being a home.
