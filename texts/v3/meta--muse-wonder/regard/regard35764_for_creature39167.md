From above, it would look like mercy to interfere.

That would be the first, and most dangerous, thought.

A wise power would have others. It would have seen enough worlds improved to death, enough people rescued out of the only shape their lives could bear, to distrust the speed of its own compassion. So before it did anything, it would do the thing the inhabitants of the pit do.

It would become still.

Not for a day. Days are an invention of the plain. It would settle somewhere unregarded — perhaps in one of the empty clearings, perhaps high in an unoccupied tree, perhaps only as a slight intensification of the attention already present in a place — and remain there long enough for a century to stop looking like an instant, and begin looking like a posture.

It would learn the words for green.

It would learn which stillness meant rest, and which meant hunger slowed beyond recognition, and which meant grief that had nowhere chronological to go. It would listen to the sparse, glassy exchanges across the canopy until it understood that they were not impoverished speech. They were speech reduced to what remained necessary when no one needed to persuade, command, promise, or explain. *Here still* is not a failure of language. In a world where here has lasted that long, it is news, and tenderness, and a name.

It would watch the knee-high children beside the knee-high saplings, and feel the human horror rise in it — if anything in it remained human enough to call horror by that name. Lives suspended. Old ones curved into the forks of trees. Fruit ripening around its own sprouted seeds. The dead softening only halfway into the mat. Everything arrested at the threshold of its next form.

Then, slowly, it would ask the question haste does not ask:

*Arrested by whose measure?*

A child who does not grow is a tragedy where growth is the road to speech, work, mating, independence, death. But none of those roads quite exist in the pit. The child is not waiting outside life. It is participating in life at the speed life takes there. The old are not being denied an ending they beg for. When the loosening comes, the pit receives it. The fruit is not rotting unharvested. It is being eaten by its own continuation.

Nothing there reaches toward rescue.

Nothing there is sharpening a tool in secret, or looking up at the rim at dusk, or telling stories of a lost and better country. Their dissatisfaction, if they have any, does not organize itself into longing for fire, seasons, roads, history, or a faster heart. To give them those things would not be to return something stolen from them. It would be to steal the world that has replaced it.

So the power would not drain the pit.

It would not send a stream down through the root-mat to teach the water to run, however tempting that small, clean improvement might be. One stream would bring erosion, and erosion would bring time in its familiar, violent form: banks cut, seeds carried, carcasses exposed, hunger following flood and drought. It would not introduce winter to give them rest, or fire to give them cooked food and longer evenings, or medicine to make their hearts beat more efficiently toward death.

It would not make them mortal again and call the gift freedom.

Nor would it preserve them exactly as they are forever. That, too, would be a kind of cruelty disguised as reverence. A specimen cannot choose to remain a specimen. A garden behind glass is still a prison if the door has been forgotten.

The power would work at the edges, where choice lives.

Above the pit, it would make the place unmemorable.

Not invisible — invisibility invites wonder. Merely ordinary. A surveyor’s eye would pass over it and find the map already, somehow, completed. A road would discover reasons to bend around poorer land. Prospectors would become tired at the rim and decide the light hurt their eyes. Stories told by the few who climbed down would lose their edges in retelling, becoming the sort of traveller’s tale no one repeats. Aircraft would cross above a plausible patch of uninteresting plain.

This would not be done deceitfully so much as mercifully. The pit can survive weather, hunger, and centuries. It cannot survive being known by a civilisation that calls curiosity innocent and turns everything rare into a resource, a remedy, a destination, a cure for its own restlessness. The algae in those people’s skin would be in a laboratory before the meaning of their stillness had been translated. Their slowed hearts would become a treatment. Their equilibrium would become a metaphor. Then an industry.

The power would prevent that with the mildness of fog and administrative error.

Around the plain, too, it would begin a longer work. Because even pits end. Clay erodes, climates turn, the earth rearranges its bowls. Benevolent wisdom does not pretend that protecting a place means exempting it from geology. Over thousands of years, almost imperceptibly, it would coax the country beyond the rim toward greater softness: a line of trees advancing a few metres in a generation, soil deepening in hollows, water lingering a little longer after rain. Not a new paradise prepared for them, and certainly not a destination. Only a world outside that would be less instantly fatal if, one far season, the walls failed or the green spilled outward.

Inside the pit, it would alter nothing essential.

Except, perhaps, the climb.

It would make the moss at the rim hold a hand a fraction more reliably. Not a path. Paths are invitations, and invitations create traffic. Only this: where a body already seeks to leave, the roots would be there. Where old handholds have worn in, they would not quite wear away. Halfway up — at that terrible boundary where the skin’s green film begins to dry and the wind first reaches the lungs like a rumour of another age — there would be a hollow that holds clean water a little longer than physics requires.

Enough for someone leaving to pause.

Enough, also, for someone returning.

That is the one gift a wise power can give without deciding the recipient’s life in advance: not transformation, but permeability. A door that is neither opened nor locked. A child who someday grows restless in a way the basin cannot metabolise would not have to die of restlessness. An old one who climbed out beneath the moving weather and found its speed unbearable could descend again. The existence of an exit would not summon anyone through it. But it would keep stillness from hardening into captivity.

And it would stay long enough to be answerable.

Once in a great while, from far across the canopy, one of them lifts a voice: *here still.* A frog answers from a bromeliad cup. Another voice, perhaps years later, completes the exchange.

Somewhere among the trees, after the power has learned how not to hurry the interval, it would answer too. One clear note. Its own placement, honestly given.

Not *I have come to save you.*

Not *I know what you are.*

Only: *here also.*

Then it would withdraw in the manner of weather, leaving no altar, no law, no founding miracle for their stories to bend around. No metal in the soil. No new stones in the circles. No mark except, perhaps, a hollow at the rim that holds water, and the faint sense in certain crowns that the long account of the basin is being kept by one more listener than before.

If you climbed down afterwards, you would say nothing had changed.

The light would still gather and fail to depart. The leaves would still hold their invariable beads of water. A figure would still sit motionless in the circle, emerald along the shoulders, watching a leaf with an attention so complete it might take you hours to realise you were not alone.

And that would be the proof.

A lesser power would have left evidence of its kindness everywhere.

This one would leave the world intact, and merely ensure that its intactness remained a life, rather than becoming a trap found too late by those who loved it from above.
