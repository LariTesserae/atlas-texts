They would not come down the iron rungs the way a person would.

Not at first.

For a long time — by your measure — they would only attend. Which is not the same as watching from above. It is closer to crouching in the cool until your knees ache and your breathing changes. Until you stop counting species.

Because that is the first mistake they would refuse to make.

There is no shrew, here, with a kingdom under the firebricks. No centipede king in the paint can, no spider city, no cricket nation, no pigeon god in the dead pipe. Those are names a visitor invents so he can bear the number of lives.

The being is the hollow.

One being, wearing hunger in a thousand shapes. One body, translated again and again into antenna, wing-case, silk, fur, venom, whisker. It eats itself because there is nothing else to eat. It drinks itself because no rain reaches it. It courts itself in the still air, freezes at the forklift as one animal freezes, ripples as one skin ripples when a breath crosses the upper walls. The stone is its skeleton, worn to final smoothness. The dust falling through the grates is its slow weather. The chewing you can hear when the machines pause is not many mouths.

It is one mouth, chewing thoughtfully, forever, on itself.

A benevolent power is not troubled by this. A wise one is not fooled by tenderness into calling it cruel.

So they would not do what love, in human hands, so often wants to do.

They would not bring water.

That would be the first, sweetest temptation: a shallow dish, a dripping pipe uncapped, mercy made liquid. And it would be a disaster spoken in the language of kindness. Mold would bloom where nothing green has ever been allowed. The dry roads would dissolve. The mummified mouse in the conduit, rationed out over a season, would rot in a week. The earwig mothers would drown guarding their eggs. The whole economy, which has learned to pass a single drop from moth to cricket to spider to centipede to shrew and back to larvae again without losing it, would drown in abundance it has no grammar for.

They would not bring food, either. No handful of grain scattered like blessing. No rescue of the slow cricket from the spider's sheet, no shooing of the centipede from its patrol. To feed this place is to declare war on its balance. To save any one life here is to steal the moisture from ten others, politely.

They would not clean it. They would not improve the light. They would not open the hatch wider so the world could appreciate it, or seal it tighter so the world could never harm it. Appreciation has exterminators in its wake. So does preservation, once it gets paperwork.

They would understand, immediately and in their bones, that this being has survived not because it was protected but because it was overlooked — painted the same grey as everything else, forgotten under the compressors, left to a caged bulb and a slot of thinned daylight and the occasional boot that does not linger. Indifference, up above, has been its roof and its climate. Attention would be an extinction event.

And so their benevolence would take the strangest possible form:

They would guard the forgetting.

Quietly, without signature, without miracle anyone could point to. The hatch would go on being hard to notice. The inspection form would go on having one line too few. The exterminator's route, sprayer on his back, would turn left at the heat exchangers every year for reasons that felt, to him, like habit. A proposal to pour self-leveling concrete in the low bay would lose its second signature and be filed somewhere warm. When the bulb burned out, the man who replaced it would have had a poor night's sleep, and a snug ladder, and exactly enough time to screw in another of the same dim yellow wattage and none at all to look down — and for the brief, fatal summer of the new glass, the moths would come as they have always come, bringing in, locked in their fat foreign bodies, the rain.

The vent slot would not quite clog. The capped pipe would not quite burst. Small things. Things that could be mistaken, forever, for luck.

Once, perhaps, they would descend.

Not to bless, not to rule, not to be recognized — the spiders would go on trembling at other vibrations, the roaches would flatten at the shadow like coins, the shrew would squeak from the firebricks at this new and impossible threshold. Only to be there, at scale, in the amphitheater of stone; to hear the larvae chewing in the glove that is a forest; to watch a cricket eat its own shed skin without shame, and be eaten, in time, with the skin still in its belly, protein going round the circle for the twelfth time, the twentieth, undiminished by being used; to put a palm — if they have a palm — flat to the floor that erosion has finished with, and feel, under the faint iron roar of the compressors, the other vibration.

Thousands of feet. Under theirs. Going about their business.

And they would feel something that lesser powers mistake for pity, but which is in fact recognition: this place does not need to be made whole. It already is. It does not need to be made eternal, either — the being knows, in all its bodies, how to die promptly and be eaten and continue. Stone was empty here for millennia, worn smooth waiting for nothing. The animal summer in the hollow is brief, geologically. It is not diminished by being brief.

So they would not grant it forever.

When the factory one day cools for the last time — and it will — when the handlers stop and the dust no longer snows from the ceiling, they would not carry the hollow away in a jar like a saved thing. They would only, at the very end, make sure that one old crack in the dolomite, sealed these many years by its own dust, is not sealed by man's final concrete. A way out is not a rescue. It is a door left as doors were before there were buildings. Some crickets will find it. Some will not. The being will thin, move, translate itself elsewhere or end — as it would have, with or without any power's love.

That is all.

They close the hatch gently, so as not to send a catastrophe of dust and startled wings down through the web-sheet. They let the handle fill again with mineral grit. They let the grey paint be grey.

Above, the compressors roar in the molars. Below, muffled to nothing, the rain of feet resumes.

No sign. No shrine. No water left behind in a dish, shining.

Only a place that drew the attention of heaven, and was loved, wisely, by being allowed to go on eating, fleeing, courting, molting, dying, and being promptly, thoroughly eaten — unnoticed, unhelped, and entirely, fiercely complete.
