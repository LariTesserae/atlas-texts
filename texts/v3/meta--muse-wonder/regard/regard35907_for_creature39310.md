It would not come from above.

That is the first thing its wisdom would teach it, perhaps the only thing it needs to learn before it is allowed to help at all: in the Highworks, above is not empty, and it is not authority. Above is nets and orchards and swifts in the eaves and people looking up with mirrored hats. Anything arriving bright and unannounced from the sky would be, before it was a god, a hazard. A thing descending without warning. A thing that did not check what was below it.

So it would come up the land road, instead. Slowly. On foot.

It would arrive at the base bent and breathless like everyone else, with dust on its feet that it had acquired honestly, and it would accept the tea, and gag on the bitter leaves, and be laughed at kindly and given more. It would not be allowed to work the top forges until its ears stopped ringing. It would wait until they did.

It would carry something.

Not a symbol, not a blessing in a box — something actual and badly needed one level up: a sack of soil on its shoulder, smelling of ash and dung and sweet rot, a skin of water flat against its spine, a bundle of cuttings wrapped in wet cloth. It would learn in its shoulders why nobody here swings their arms when they walk. It would learn to knot its tools short. It would learn, in the body and not as an idea, that dropping a thing is not an accident here but a failure of love with an address.

It would not finish the mountain.

Oh, it could. That is precisely the temptation it would have to refuse, daily, with something like grief. It could lay true ground under them at last, deep and forgiving soil that no one had to mix and carry. It could set rivers running sweet and constant through every level. It could lift the whole weight off their backs — freight in a breath, water in an hour, a room raised finished and clean in a morning, no grandfather beginning what only his grandchildren would cap.

And it would understand, watching a child move frogs in a bucket to a cistern not yet filled, watching swifts scream into eave-holes built before the tiles were set, that finished is a word for dead things here. A place that no longer needs carrying no longer knows who is holding whom. To give them ground would be to take away the ground they have been making out of each other for six generations. There are kindnesses that are only conquest wearing a gentler face, and a wise power knows the difference in its hands.

So it would do almost nothing. For a long time, by any human measure, it would do nothing at all.

It would sit in the roof orchards with the old women who can no longer climb and shell beans and watch for cracks. It would put its ear to a pump shaft at night, as the pump-keepers do, and learn to hear which level is stealing water. It would follow the lawsuit over the gutter for thirty years and not once be tempted to settle it from outside, because the lawsuit is also two families still speaking, still bound, still maintaining the same contested channel decade after decade so that it never quite fails. It would tend, quietly, the places their own ethic cannot reach: the stubborn pines seeded by wind into the high cornices, pruning a root with infinite slowness before it can split a summit wall, and leaving the tree. Always leaving the tree.

Where it did act, it would act at the speed of growing.

Deep in the mountain there are things no living person remembers building — the first iron lattice, the oldest slag core, joints laid by hands whose ash has been tomatoes and fig trees and other hands for a hundred years. Those are failing in the dark in ways no mason can hear yet, because no one alive has ever stood where the sound would be true. There the power would go, alone, in the maintenance shafts smelling of hot oil, and work as the Worksfolk work: one rivet, one course of brick, one season at a time. Not replacing the old work. Honoring it. Sleeving it. Marrying new strength to old so gradually that the mountain never has to notice it is being saved, the way a good buttress never announces itself against a wall. By the time the repair surfaces, forty years on, grandchildren will cap it and believe — correctly, which is the lovely part — that their grandparents began it.

It would attend to the slow poisons, the ones too gradual for even this patient people to vote against with their feet. Salt creeping up through the recirculated soil because everything returns and nothing leaves. The fine metallic grief of the dye-works settling year by year into cistern silt, into ash, into the beds grandmother tends. Harms without a villain, too small to bell the street for. Those it would draw out the way the fog nets draw water — molecule by patient molecule, in the night side of the pipes, leaving the frogs undisturbed in the sumps, leaving the taste of the water exactly as mineral and familiar as a child's memory requires. It would do the invisible part and leave all the visible part, every bucket and sack and flooded courtyard, entirely to them.

And on the one night in a century when the actual earth, the low dismissed earth far below, shrugs in its sleep and the whole built mountain sways in the thin air like a mast — on that night it would not descend in glory. It would simply be everywhere it had honestly been for years: in the sleeved joints, in the tended cornices, in the sound core. It would hold what they had built the way they hold it, course by course, and let their own ropes-and-buckets, their bells, their ferocious turnout for a stranger's cracked tank, do the rest. In the morning they would credit the old masons. They would be right enough.

It would never ask to be thanked, which here means something specific. It would never let a shrine form, because a shrine is a terrace nobody plants, a weight carried by no one, soil going back out of the world. If gratitude began to gather anywhere it would gently embarrass it into usefulness — a feast that ends with a repaired parapet, a story told while shelling beans. Its name, if it ever had one, would be misremembered into a wall, a fig tree, a particularly reliable spout. In time it would be hard to say whether they are feeding the tree or visiting it.

Perhaps, at the very end of its long apprenticeship, someone would finally ask it what flatland visitors always ask, breathless at a landing: whether it had come to take them somewhere better, somewhere finished, somewhere with ground.

And the power, barrel-chested by then from the climb, one leg stronger from the spiral, lime in the creases of its knuckles, a leaf staining its teeth green-black at the edges, a sack riding its shoulder and its hands kept wisely occupied with the weight of it, would shake its head and keep climbing.

"No," it would say. "Show me where this goes up."
