They would come barefoot.

Not out of reverence — at first, anyway. They would come the way the bowl requires everything to come: slowly enough to be let through. Whatever they were outside the rim — and outside the rim they might be considerable, they might move rivers by deciding to, they might have the sort of attention that suns notice — inside, their first lesson would be a foot thickening against the water, ridiculous, held. The second lesson would be the other foot, patient, parting. The third would be their voice arriving at ten paces and then politely ceasing to be a voice, becoming first a texture, then a warmth, then nothing at all.

A benevolent power shouts its blessings. A wise one learns the conversational radius, and lowers itself to fit inside it.

So they would not enter loudly. They would not enter *much*.

They would stand a long time on the white marl first, getting the whole place at once the way you are supposed to: one room of landscape, shallow, bright, finished. They would feel the light come up off the water into their face and notice their shadow displaced a fraction, pale, falling as if through a lens — notice it without correcting it. That would be the first test, and they would know it was a test, and smile, because everything in the bowl is watching everything else and the examination is mutual.

The temptations would arrive in order. They always do, for the powerful.

The first temptation is sound. To give it back. It would be so easy, and it would look like mercy: lift whatever the plinths lay down, let the ibis be loud again, let the frog chorus come up at dusk the way frog choruses are meant to, let the keeper shout a warning across the water and be heard. The power would stand among the emerald and rust-red and candied-yellow frogs with their throat-sacs inflating in bright silent waves, handwriting running around the pool, and understand — slowly, the way the mud accepts weight — that this is not muteness. This is not a place that has been robbed of a language. This is a place that has spent ten thousand years fluently speaking another one, and restoring the first would be vandalism dressed as healing. You do not cure a library by teaching the books to yell.

So they would say nothing across the water. They would wait until they were close enough, and then they would speak softly, or not at all.

The second temptation is the river. Somewhere under the lattice's fine persuasion there is an old exit, a way the depression once drained when it was younger and still going somewhere. A benevolent power thinks in journeys. It would be tempted to open the way out — to call this a prison because the doors are shut, to free the softshells' descendants back into the moving world they came from. And then it would watch a Finished One press its broad shell against one of the invisible partitions, an inch, two inches, the color blooming copper under its throat and blue at the shell's edge and sudden coin-silver down the veining, and pass through like a thought through a mood, and it would understand that shut is not the same as *held*. The walls know them. They have had longer than empires to learn the exact slowness the walls require, and they use the knowledge daily, at their stations, in their drifts, unhurried as weather. Nothing here is straining at the rim. The only things that strain at the rim are visitors, briefly, before the water teaches them.

The third temptation, and the worst, is forever. To make the holding hold. To take the lattice — that vast faint ticking under the soles of the feet, that machinery holding a whole ecosystem at the exact final point of its erosion, rain not allowed to cut, flood not allowed to deepen — and shore it up with a stronger hand, seal it, guarantee it, put the bowl beyond time the way the bowl has put itself beyond noise. That one would take the longest to refuse, because it wears the face of love most convincingly. But a wise power knows what the keeper knows, standing in her hut of woven reed and permitted salvage, and what she will tell you if you ask: the bowl is not kept *from* ending. It is kept *at* its ending, alive inside it, endlessly — the lotus indecently lush in water too shallow for lotus, the fish attending, the crabs raising their white semaphore claws, a hundred species nesting in a density that should be war and proceeds like a well-run market. Stasis that is merely enforced becomes a museum in a generation. This has not. Something in it is still being chosen, daily, at noon, on the disc.

And that is where the power would finally understand what it had actually been summoned by, if summoned is even the word.

At noon the oldest ones are there, or arriving. Thirty paces of seamless grey under an inch of green water. The frogs ringed in their hundreds. The dragonfly stratum held at knee height, green and amber and violet, the swallows coming down through it. The Finished Ones settling into their loose exact constellation, never quite the same, never quite different, pressing their enormous webbed feet flat — feet tipped with unbreakable ceramic nubs, a century of lost knives taken up grain by grain out of the mud — and going still. And then the lattice answers something in the sky: one patient adjustment, felt in the teeth, every partition in the bowl rippling once in sequence, the color going through the assembled bellies in a single traveling wave, copper to blue to a silver so sudden it whites out the veining, and it is done, and the afternoon begins.

Whether they are commanding that, or translating it, or lending their bodies as the needle lends itself to the compass — the keeper will not say, and the power, being wise, would recognize the not-saying as the most accurate available report.

But they would notice the thing the casual eye slides off of. A signal that is sent every day at the same hour, in the same place, by the same beings, for longer than any living memory — that is not a reflex. Reflexes do not require a constellation. It is closer to a question asked at the speed of mud accepting weight, or a consent renewed, or a report filed by instruments built out of a hundred years each: *we are still here, we are still finished, we are still, inside the finished shape, alive.* And something underneath has been answering it, patiently, enormously, one adjustment at a time.

You do not interrupt a conversation that old by answering it louder. You do not take over the answering, either, and call the relief a gift; you have no way of knowing whether the Finished Ones experience their part in it as labor, or as prayer, or simply as what their blood does at that hour — and the keeper's whole theology, insofar as she has one, is to step around them in the dark by memory of their maps and leave the distinction to the bowl.

So the power would do, at the center, precisely this: stand outside the ring of frogs, in water up to the ankle, barefoot, and let the adjustment pass through the soles of its own feet without answering it. Witness, not relief. To be one more thing that knows they are there, and says so by staying.

And then, having refused every large thing, they would do the small things — which in the bowl is not a lesser category. In the bowl the small things are the whole of ethics, because nothing large can move there without thickening.

They would learn a few of them by their veining, the way the keeper's grandmother did, and her grandmother before her, the maps branching across the scutes and then ignoring them. They would bring lotus stems in the dry weeks. They would help patch the hut, using only materials the bowl has already decided to permit, and they would accept without argument when a salvaged panel was quietly unmade into bright grains instead — no sulking, no override; they would note the refusal the way you note a boundary in a friend's house. They would be rolled over, once, by otter cubs in the deepest channel, waist-deep, and used briefly as geography, and count it an honor accurately. They would watch a hatchling come down the marl in the wet season — a coin of translucent amber, all signal and no shell, flashing in fast uncoordinated pulses, visible a hundred paces in the luminous shade — and they would watch some of them be eaten, because everything watches everything and that, too, is custom, and they would not reach out a hand that could have covered the whole slope like weather. The ones that reach the water go mercifully grey within the year. The process is not improved by rescue. It is, in fact, the process.

All of their actual *work*, such as it was, they would do far away, where work can be large without being loud.

Beyond the rim, where the fields stop working and roads are thinkable again: that is where a wise power spends itself. A surveyor's map, quietly wrong in one unimportant-looking watershed, wrong the same way in every archive, generation after generation, so that no road ever quite gets proposed through the drainage. A drier country getting, decade by decade, almost imperceptibly kinder — a well that does not fail, a tree line advancing at the pace of grass — so that whoever might otherwise come walking in out of it, hungry, arrives at the bowl fed, or does not arrive. A law about weapons, forgotten by kings, remembered by border guards, whose origin no clerk can find. Weather, nudged at the distance weather can be nudged without lying about it, so that the rains which fall on the bowl keep falling the way the bowl prefers: no flood permitted to deepen, no drought permitted to meadow it.

None of it signed. None of it visible from the disc. If the keeper noticed anything, in the whole of her life, it would be small and deniable: a season when fewer strangers reached the rim lost than usual. A granddaughter who wanted, unaccountably, to stay and learn the maps. A broom mended with allowed material, mended well.

And at the end of their visiting — which might be a day, or might be the length of the keeper's life spent at conversational radius, time being one of the things that moves at different speeds in there — they would go up onto the marl at evening, when the oldest females climb to dig their single shallow nests in the polished stone-soft ground, one egg apiece, sometimes none, warm far longer than an egg should stay warm. They would not bless the eggs. The lattice has already grown a thread of itself up into the shell before it is laid; the eggs are spoken for. They would just keep the herons' customary distance, and the otters', and the bowl's, and watch the water sheet off the great low shells and rejoin and travel with them, beading, bright — condensation loving them, as it loves everything finished here, wearing them in a second outline of dew.

Then they would leave the way they came: slowly enough to be let through, at the speed of mud accepting weight. It looks like stillness until you measure it against a reed stem. Then it is a tide.

Behind them, nothing changed. That is the report the frogs would file, stem to stem, in color, unheard: *nothing changed.* The partitions would stand up again in their terraces. The mist would stand waist-high at dawn and be cut off at the boundary as if by a wall. The twenty-minute rain would cross east to west with the sun shining on both sides of it. At noon the constellation would revise itself on the disc, and the color would travel throat to tail, and the lattice would answer the sky once, patiently, enormously —

— except that perhaps now it would not be answering only the sky.

Perhaps, very far below the ticking, in the register where bone conducts and ears never quite arrive, there would be one more presence keeping the time alongside it. Not relieving it. Not sealing it. Just attending, the way the fish attend, the way the crabs raise their claws toward the signal — one more witness in a place that runs, entirely, on attention.

A benevolent and wise power does not save a place like this.

It believes it.

And then it goes and stands, forever, at the distance where belief can be useful — outside the rim, in the loud world, making sure the loud world never learns the address — while inside, at the exact moment before stillness, the Finished Ones go on finishing, flashing rose and amber and pale green through the clear water, speaking at the tops of their voices, in a language that makes no sound at all.
