They would arrive the way the mist does — from below.

Not descending. Not shining. This is not a place that needs a sky opened above it; its sky is already too large, too close, too unobstructed. A power coming down out of that blue would be just another thermal, another column of rising air with birds in it.

So they would climb.

Days through the gullies. Through forest, then scrub, then scree that rounds as it rises. They would feel their breathing change, and not correct it. They would feel their lips crack, the water they carried turn insufficient, the sun stand directly overhead and make their shadow small. They would arrive with wet knees.

And then they would do the hardest thing a benevolent power can do in a place that is not broken:

Almost nothing.

---

First, they would listen long enough to be corrected.

From a distance — from below, from above, from any of the usual thrones of intervention — the plateau looks like lack. No trees worth the name. No river. No soil a plough could honour. No animals large enough to matter. People in huts that are already becoming mounds, eating frogs, resharpening one iron blade until it is a sliver, leaving their dead to be carried down because even burial here is a seasonal arrangement.

A lesser kindness would arrive with gifts already chosen: sweeter water, easier fire, wood that does not rot, lungs that do not ache, a road up the scree, a reason for the young to stay and the old not to have to descend. It would drain the pans a little. It would tame the noise, perhaps — that deafening metallic shimmer at noon, that architectural frog-dark after sunset, cannot be restful to anything that did not evolve inside it.

It would be wrong at the root. Here, the damp is not the obstacle to life. The damp *is* the life. The rot is the doorway. Fire is scarce because the mat is alive, and the mat is alive because it has not been made burnable. The emptiness at eye level is not vacancy; it is what allows the sun to reach a million small bodies at once, what lets the giants stand apart and feed their own seedlings from their own slow deaths. Even the harshness is load-bearing.

A wise power would walk three days across and understand, in their feet before their mind, that this place is not a beginning waiting to be improved. It is an end that learned how to keep going. Everything here has already solved the problem of here.

So they would not give the frog a better pond.

They would kneel — knees coming up wet — beside a giant in the late afternoon and watch its crown-well fill in the hammering rain. They would see the tadpoles wedge themselves against the leaf-bases so as not to be washed into the void. They would see the great felty leaves tilt inward, funnelling the storm home, and then, after sunset, hear the papery shift as they fold to keep the day's small warmth in a little longer. They would learn the pitch of that one plant's humming. And the next. And the next. Thousands of vertical ponds, each genetically alone, each sounding slightly different, and they would refuse, absolutely refuse, to make them safer by joining them up.

Because the crossing matters. The terrible night, once in a generation, when the well drains leaf by leaf and a gravid female who has never touched the ground must climb down a dead trunk and through the roaring dark of the mat — bad at it, soft-toed, gold and translucent and made for ten centimetres of still water — that crossing is not a flaw in the design. It is the only road by which a new crown becomes inhabited, and a new song enters the map by which the herders walk on starless nights. To carry her would be mercy to one frog and an erasure to everything she is *for*.

What they can do, on that one night, is be weather in the right direction. Not a hand. Not a bridge. Only this: that the mist comes up over the rim a little earlier, a little heavier. That the mat is at its wettest. That the dark between giants is the navigable kind of dark, full of guidance. If a single small body makes it to a new trunk under those conditions, no one on the plateau will call it a miracle. The herders will just hear, some seasons later, a new pitch where there was silence, and restack the nearest cairn, and walk toward the sound. That is enough. That is exactly the size of enough.

---

They would work, instead, at the edges. Below the rim. Out of sight.

The plateau does not make its own weather alone. The mist that spills up its sides in the afternoon is born in forests and wet valleys far below. The clouds that assemble in a ring at its feet and then rise, waist-high, head-high, world-narrowing, are fed by everything green and breathing on the slopes. A plateau like this can be killed without anyone ever setting foot in its centre: cut the forests below and the afternoons come up dry. Warm the lowlands and the cloud base lifts above the rim, and the pans stay mirrored for hours instead of a day, and the crown-wells shrink to damp axils, and one dry season becomes the season the giants flower early and the mat, for the first time in longer than forests have existed, thins to pavement and dust.

So the power's labour would be lowland labour, anonymous and unglamorous and vast. A slope that was going to be cleared, quietly not cleared — a debt forgiven in a valley town, a road surveyed three valleys east instead, a seed-bank of cloudforest trees finding its way into the hands of people who were already inclined to plant them for reasons of their own. A mining claim that founders on paperwork and local geology and a sudden, sincere official interest in the watershed. Fences that do not get built. A market, somewhere below, that begins — apparently by fashion, apparently by chance — to pay properly for what mountain people grow in shade, so the shade stays standing.

Nothing up there would change visibly. That is the point. The noon roar would build on schedule. The mole-rats would push up their red mounds before dawn. The swifts would scream through the noon thermals with their mouths open. The power would protect the *conditions of arrival* — of mist, of rain hammering straight down for twenty minutes, of nights clear enough for the giants to fold against — and leave every arrival itself alone.

To the people, they would give nothing that cannot be eaten by green.

No metal that outlasts its welcome in heaps. No permanent house. No exemption from carrying the dead down; that leaving is part of how the plateau stays the plateau's, and how the people stay guests rather than becoming a weight the mat must carry. At most: a season of unusually sure weather for the walk up with the stock. A winter below in which a child with bad lungs recovers anyway. A hand-axe of good basalt left beside a shelter's fire-pit, polished, balanced, exactly where the next traveller would look for it — because that is the local form of wealth, a tool that belongs to a shelter, which belongs increasingly to the mat, which belongs to no one. The herders would pick it up without ceremony and leave it again three valleys of mist later, and no one would need to know that it fell off no mountain that exists.

And they would learn the house rules by keeping them. Live low. Walk the cairn-lines. Add three stones to the cairn whose top had slumped, not rebuilding it taller than a person, because nothing here is built tall and a guide-mark that becomes a monument has forgotten its job. Eat what is offered, including the sour crunchy mouthful from a crown-well, without flinching and without taking a second crown's worth. Sleep in a hut that is already slightly a mound, on piled cut leaves, and listen to the gnawing through the floor of the world, and understand it is not a haunting. It is a neighbour at the main residence, of which the surface is only the dangerous, delicious roof.

Perhaps, in the end, they would go to the centre.

To the standing stones at the long-water pan, tilted at their companionable angles, the mat furred halfway up their bases, being re-absorbed one green centimetre at a time while each generation clears them again. A foolish power would ask who raised them, and straighten them, and date them, and set a plaque in a language that rots slower than basalt. A wise one would notice that the local story already contains the whole correct theology: people did not make this. People found it standing, and chose, again and again, to keep it findable. Relationship, not origin. Maintenance, not mastery.

So they would take a stone knife and spend one dripping morning doing what has always been done there. Cut the mat back from the bases. Not far. Not forever. Just enough that the next generation can find the stones standing up under the growth, and make the same choice, freely, with wet hands.

Then they would walk down.

Behind them the pans would refill and mirror the sky for a day. The huts they slept in would begin, very slightly faster, to become mounds. The cairns they restacked would stand until weather and the mat and the next passer-by negotiated a new shape. Somewhere out on the green wet ocean a giant would push up its once-in-a-lifetime spike and become, for a few weeks, the busiest address in the world. Somewhere a golden frog, no bigger than a thumbnail, would start down a dead trunk in the heaviest mist of the year, into a darkness ten thousand mouths loud, toward a sound she had never heard except through leaves.

And the power — who could have built her a staircase, lit her way, emptied the mat of teeth — would be three valleys below by then, planting shade trees in someone else's name, making sure only of the rain.
