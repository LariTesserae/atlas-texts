It would not descend.

That would be the first sign of its wisdom, and the hardest part of its benevolence to distinguish from indifference. Anything large enough to help here would arrive as a vibration. A foot on the basalt would be a shout through stone. A hand parting the ferns at the lip would change the light. A voice — any voice, however gentle, however well-meant — would fall down that polished throat and go through the roots, the pools, the coils, and everything that has spent ten thousand generations learning how not to be heard would freeze, and be right to freeze.

So it stays above, where its size belongs. And it looks at what can actually be threatened, which is almost nothing down there, and almost everything up here.

From above, the place is a crack in a dry limestone plain. Unremarkable. Easy to survey. Easy to quarry, to pave, to fence, to drain, to divert a road across, to poison accidentally with runoff from a field a mile away and never know what had been silenced underneath. The chamber has survived because it is hidden and because it is useless, and both of those protections are getting thinner every century.

That is where the power does its work. Not miracles. Custody.

It makes the plain boring. Surveyors' eyes slide off that one unpromising acre. A proposed road finds, on reflection, a slightly easier route a valley over. A well dug too close comes up dry, or brackish, and is abandoned before it can tap the slow veins that feed the four pools. The tree whose fruit falls, days later, into the fermentation on the cave floor is not cut in the year its timber would have been most valuable; a storm that takes its neighbor takes a different path through its branches, and it lives, and fruits again, crookedly, for another fifty years. The fern curtain at the lip thickens. Rain keeps finding the old watershed. The limestone stays unbroken.

Nothing down there ever notices. That is the point. The ants do not look up in gratitude. The eyeless fish do not alter their circuit. Protection, done properly, is indistinguishable from the world simply continuing to be itself.

And inside — almost nothing.

It would be tempted, at first, the way any kind heart is tempted. It would consider the frogs.

It could kill her. One clean act, no malice in it, and release a whole civilization from the ceiling. The bowls would fill with calling. The crickets would discover, over generations, what their wings were originally for. The swifts would cry in the throat on their way down.

It does not. Because it can see further down the consequences than pity can: the pools fouling with unculled multitudes, the fish gone blind-bellied and starving in water thick with their own kind, the roots stripped, the whole tuned, dense, stacked order of the place collapsing into mere abundance and then into rot. The frogs did not evolve their silent dances *despite* her. They evolved them *because* of her, and the dances are beautiful, and they are theirs now. She is not a tyrant sitting on a paradise. She is the weather that made the country. You do not free a country by abolishing its weather.

It could feed her, instead. Leave offerings where the hut was. Spare the frogs by sparing her the need to hunt.

It does not do that either. A fed serpent is a changed serpent, and a serpent fed from above is a serpent who learns to listen for above — who begins, perhaps, to wait at the wrong angle, the way she already sometimes waits, head cocked, for footfalls on a stair that has ended in air for centuries. It notices that pause. It will not exploit it, and it will not comfort it with a counterfeit. It will not fake the steps of the dead to make her feel accompanied. That would be the cruellest possible kindness: to teach her body, which knows the world only as tremor, that the tremor can lie.

It does not heal her eyes. There is nothing, down there, for eyes to forgive her for missing. Light is a twenty-minute mercy at noon and she has been glisten­ing under it, iridescent and unaware, her whole life; to give her sight now would not be to give her the sun. It would be to give her a wound that registers darkness. Her blindness is not the tragedy of the place. It is one of its oldest and most successful ideas.

It does not bring her a mate, or mourn that she may be a line of one, or a line ending. If daughters have taken her place coil by coil in the roots before, daughters may again. If she is the last, then one day the re-ascent will not happen, and the skins the moths eat will be the last skins, and the chamber will slowly, over generations, remember sound — frogs reinventing the call, badly at first, then gloriously. That, too, would be life, and not a lesser one for being louder. The power is wise enough not to confuse *the way things are* with *the way things must be kept.* It is guarding a place, not embalming it.

One thing, perhaps. One thing only, and even that so small the chamber would file it under weather.

Over decades the limestone water has been writing itself between her scales. She is not just aging; she is mineralizing by degrees, thickening toward stillness, becoming part of the wall's long project of becoming a wall. Shedding must be getting harder. There will come a warm night — or she will try to make one come — when the old skin catches on its own stone, and the slow work of getting free of herself could tear, or fail, and leave her half in and half out of her own past, blind in a new and worse way.

When that night comes, the rain above will arrive, as rain does here, all at once and briefly — funneling startlingly down the crack to strike the central pool and throw its mist to the ceiling. Nothing unusual. It happens. Except that this time it will happen on the right night, a little earlier than the clouds quite intended, and the steam that rises afterward off the black slabs will be a fraction denser, a fraction warmer, and it will get into the seams of her, and soften what the stone has been building in her, and the shed will come away whole.

It will fall, ghostly, five yards of it, soundlessly, as everything here falls. The moths and fungi will take it before morning has finished pretending to arrive. She will be, for a while, new-pink and unbearably tender in the roots, and then the pearl will start to come back over her, the way it always has. She will not know she was helped. *That* is the condition of the help being real.

And then the power withdraws — not forever, but into the long, dull, faithful work at the surface. Keeping the plain uninteresting. Keeping the water honest. Keeping the tree alive. Sitting, as it were, at the lip of the crack with its own considerable stillness, learning the first rule of the place from the place itself.

Sit long enough, up there, still enough, and you stop being an interruption.

Condensation beads on the stone bowls below. The roots lean toward their warmths. The ant column diverts around a fallen leaf. A glass frog lifts one foreleg, slowly, signaling across the crowded, silent room. And in the northeast corner, in the thickest tangle, something that may be a head inclines a fraction — toward the newest tremor, the oldest pattern, nothing the power has sent her.

It lets her incline. It does not answer.

Being *known*, without being used, is the only gift that would not cost her the world she knows. So it knows her — one creature, or one unbroken line of creatures, pink deepening to pearl in a green-black dark she has never seen and has entirely, entirely mastered — and it keeps the roof of the world intact over her.

That is all.

That is everything it would do.
