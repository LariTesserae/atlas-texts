At first, it does nothing either.

It has to learn the timescale. Any power that arrives in that chamber moving at the speed of intention — even a benevolent one — arrives too fast. A footstep is an event there. A held breath is an event there. A leaf takes a week to open, a fern unfurls by remembering yesterday, rain is two minutes of weather every three quarters of an hour, and the whole orchard lives by holding still until holding is full.

So it keeps to the catwalk.

That is the first thing it does, and it is not nothing: it obeys the rules the place has already made for bodies in it. It does not step on the bright crusts, thin as they are over scalding pockets. It does not stand in the vent paths when the floor sighs. It does not touch the held pools in the fronds to see whether anything is living in them. Nothing is. It understands, slowly, that the emptiness is not a failure waiting to be corrected.

It is very tempted, at first, by the usual mercies.

Bring light. There is a slit above, choked with grating and surface vegetation, admitting only a diluted grey smear that never reaches the floor. A wise hand could clear it. A kind hand could widen it. Sun could fall, for the first time, in a true column, and strike the black-green leaves and the wet stone, and everything would look, to eyes made on the surface, redeemed.

It does not.

It has watched the young shoot at the north end, pale and blind, curling not up but down and in, toward the seam that breathes heat from the fresh fracture. That curl is not a mistake. It is the first honest sentence spoken in this place without a human accent. Light here was never the promise the chamber kept. Light was the accident — thin, intermittent, late. Heat kept its promise. Mineral water kept its promise. The pump kept its promise, forty-five minutes at a time. The new generation is orienting by what is reliable, and to flood them now with sun would not be rescue. It would be translation back into a language their parents were already bred out of.

Nor does it bring animals.

No frogs for the warm cupped pools, though frogs would love them. No moths, no beetles, no ingenious little pollinators released from a merciful sleeve to do the work of the hand-brushes laid out in size order in the shed. That would seem like completion to an old ecology: flower, wing, egg, larva, bite. But this forest has never had a mouth in it. Its leaves are perfect because nothing has ever needed to eat them. To introduce hunger now — however small, however natural by surface standards — would not be to free the orchard. It would be to sentence it to a war it has built no defenses for, on a timescale it cannot answer. A week to open a leaf is no reply to an appetite.

It does not heal them, either. It does not unlace the roots from the copper returns or teach them honest soil. There is no honest soil down here. The roots have flattened against warm pipe, whitened where the mineral crusts them, gone halfway to stone and salt at the points of contact, and that halfway state is the truth of this being: not plant installed in a building, not building overgrown by plant, but a single slow circulation learning to be one thing. The elders catch steam in their arms, tip, pour it down their own trunks to their own roots, and give the cooled surfaces back to the air so steam can gather again. To separate organism from installation now would kill both halves politely.

What lives here is not seven or eight plants. What lives here is the loop.

So the power guards the loop, and does it almost entirely from outside the chamber, where its faster kind of causality can do less damage.

It guards time first. Above, in the world of clipboards and budgets, No. 3 — Low Condenser is an asset with a service life, a line item, a decommissioning date penciled in by someone who has never stood long enough in the hot dark for their eyes to finish adjusting. Sterilization protocols exist for places like this. Grouting crews exist for fresh fractures with glassy faces. There are men who would see the unplanted fern at the north wall, no bigger than a hand, and log it correctly as biological fouling.

The power misfiles things.

Not dramatically. No lightning, no edict. A form settles to the bottom of a tray and stays there, damp. An inspection route is redrawn for excellent reasons and somehow crosses the switchback stair off its loop. A pump that should have been replaced by a more efficient unit — one with a faster, harsher cycle that would scour the leaves and crack the rhythm to pieces — fails to arrive, and the old pump, patient, long-breathed, is serviced instead and left to breathe on. The stencil on the wall, NO OPEN DISCHARGE, acquires the bureaucratic force of a commandment nobody quite remembers issuing and nobody wants to be first to disobey.

And the fracture is not sealed. That is its one close intervention, if it can even be called one. Twice a year the building tries, in its small unconscious ways, to close its own wound — scale thickening in the seam, sprayed concrete above it swelling with absorbed heat, a maintenance instinct in metal and mineral alike. Twice a year something keeps the seam open by the width of a root hair. Not wider. The young plants are not ready for a flood of new heat, any more than they were ready for sun. Just open. Just breathing. A promise continuing to be kept at the rate the pale shoot can curl toward it.

Inside, it allows imperfection to begin, because perfection was the signature of the cultivated generation and sterility was its price.

A leaf, tipping late, spills half its pool onto bare stone instead of its roots, and the water finds a crack and stays there long enough for the sponge-moss to thicken at its edge. The power does not correct the leaf. A fern at the base of the apart-standing elder, the one whose crown never fully opened, seeds a fraction farther than the drip-zone should permit — into the runoff, into a place where the cycle only reaches it every third or fourth rain. It will be smaller for that. It may be hardier for that. The power does not carry it water. It only ensures the manufactured rain still drifts plant to plant in its slow band, and that when the far young ones are reached, the band lingers — not longer, exactly, but a breath less hurried, the way you slow, without deciding to, passing a sleeping child.

And once, only once, it moves the hand-brushes.

Not away. Not out of reach, not into the dark where the descending human will panic and bring down brighter lights and more hands to search. It moves them two inches out of their size order. The largest where the smallest should be. Enough that the visitor who comes down the switchback, does what hands are needed for, and climbs again, will pause with a brush lifted, and in that pause — heat pressing on the skin, iron in the mouth, pilot lamps ticking amber — will actually look up from the task.

What he sees, on that particular cycle, is the rain crossing. Leaf to leaf in the drifting band. The great fronds darkening, beading, tipping one by one, never together, each on its own time, pouring their held weather down to their roots in muted sheets. And past the last spaced elder, in the corner no brush has ever tended, a second pale shoot, smaller than the first, uncurling downward toward the heat in the broken wall.

He does not log biological fouling.

He stands on the catwalk a full minute longer than his schedule allows, in the rich smell of hot stone and green sap, almost sweet, and he logs: *Condenser orchard functioning. No intervention required.*

Then he puts the brushes back exactly as he found them, out of order and all, because some small and unexamined reverence has instructed his hands. He will not remember deciding that.

That is the most the power ever asks of a human here: a minute of attention, and the paperwork of mercy.

After that it withdraws the way weather withdraws — not gone, but no longer local. It leaves the single plastic chair outside the control shed to go on warping slowly in the residents' climate. It leaves the kettle, the clipboard filming with water, the black window. It leaves the elders to their decades of standing and condensing, lush, unwounded, laced into the plumbing. It leaves the ferns to take their days unfurling, the moss to drink the drips before they can ring on stone, the young at the north end to be smaller, paler, less perfect, and native.

Years from now the machinery above may stop for the last time. Turbines still, boilers cool, the world at the top of the stair moves on to other kinds of power. If that day comes while the seam in the rock is still breathing, the orchard will discover — at its own pace, leaf by leaf, root by white root — that the pump was only ever an apprentice season, teaching it a rhythm the deep earth already knew more slowly.

By then it will not need the lesson.

Until then: no sun forced, no mouths delivered, no wound sealed, no loop broken. The hot dark kept. The rain kept, two minutes at a time.

The power does the rarest thing a power can do for a young world that has just invented its own north.

It lets down-and-toward-the-heat be north, and it stands aside long enough for the turning to finish.
