First, it would not warm the place.

That would be the kindest-looking catastrophe imaginable, and a wise power would see it in an instant. It would arrive, as everything must, dazzled and freezing — feeling that flat white light pour onto it like an accusation — and its first impulse, if it is truly benevolent, would be pity. *Raise the temperature ten degrees. Thicken the air. Bring rain that lasts. Melt the ice out of the gorges and let the rivers begin properly. Let them rest.*

And then it would look down at ankle-height, into the knee-high fog, and understand that it had nearly committed a murder disguised as mercy.

The cold is not an enemy this life is losing to. The cold is its author. Warm the plateau and the bladder-pines burst, their trapped sun suddenly redundant, their interior rain flooding roots evolved for teaspoons. The furnace mosses, which spend half a year saving sugar to burn for a winter, overheat and rot in their own steam. The smelters' bacterial cultures — those dull red civilizations they carry in their guts, fed mouth to mouth across generations, born beside ingots — cook past red into white and die, and the great beasts die with their furnaces lit too well, which is only a faster way of going out. The gape-mouths stall in air that no longer pours downhill with the same reliable violence. The whole vast machine does not relax. It seizes.

Heat here is not the absence of suffering. Heat is what they *do*, the way birds do flight. To give it to them for free would not liberate them. It would make them unnecessary to themselves.

So it would not warm the place.

It would not rescue her, either.

That would be the second temptation, and subtler, because she is — once you have come down off the summit and into the warm mat and matched her pace for a while — so clearly a *someone*. The piston that misses a beat she compensates for. The sister whose furnace pitches lower. The white score down her third leg. The moss garden on her back, groomed by blind tenants who are themselves eaten by moths in her updraft. You want to single her out. You want to say: *this one, at least, should not have to keep chewing. This one should sleep properly, once, without idling her heart against the dark. This one should know she was seen.*

A wise power would resist that with both hands.

Because singling her out is just a warmer word for separating her, and separation here is the one true death. Make her a queen and the herd parts around her and the wind gets in. Make her a prophet and she must lift her head out of the shared climate to speak. Make her chosen — beloved, exempt, specially warmed by heaven — and you have placed her, perfectly preserved and spiritually comforted, alone in the cold.

She does not perceive the world in a way that has a slot for that kind of love. She knows individuals by rhythm, by vent and thud and the note of a neighboring furnace pressed against her flank for days until their hearts synchronize. Love, in the only dialect she speaks, is not *I see you above the others*. It is *I will stand on your windward side and my outer plates will rime so yours don't.*

So the power, if it wants to love her, has to learn to do it in that dialect.

Here is what I think it would do.

It would come down into the valley and become unremarkable.

Not disguised as a smelter exactly — disguise implies it could take the shape off again, and that kind of tourism is its own cruelty — but genuinely entered into the arrangement: a body among bodies, flank to flank in the slow river of them, subject to the same contract. *Do not stop making heat. Do not ever be alone in the cold.* It would eat feather-lichen and iron-rich scree it does not need. It would let the four-hour grind set the tempo of its thoughts. It would gear its own vastness down through a piston-heart and feel, perhaps for the first time in an existence spent being exempt from consequence, the long building pause between beats — that terrible, patient interval where pressure either gathers or fails, where you find out whether the furnace caught.

It would not be the strongest in the herd. That would be another way of standing apart. It would be, deliberately, somewhere in the middle of the distribution: strong enough to be a good wall for somebody's young, weak enough that in the long night it would need to press close and be kept. It would let furnace-moss colonize its seams. It would let small blind things live in the moss and groom it and be eaten over its back. It would void an ingot now and then, ringing into the mat like anyone else's, and feel the disproportionate, unsolemn joy of plants turning toward something it had made — not a miracle, just copper, just warmth that would last a few days and cool, and be enough for a cluster of newborns to press against while their starter cultures caught.

And it would walk. At their pace. Which is to say it would surrender the single greatest privilege of power, which is not strength but *speed* — the ability to skip the interval between wanting and arriving. It would take a season to cross a valley it could see across. It would turn toward a new crack in the rising plateau because the herd turned, because someone felt a hot mineral breath through their feet three ridges away, and it would go, and the going would be the whole of it.

That is the large thing. The small things follow from it.

Because it is powerful — because even incarnate in the herd it would still *know* more than a smelter can know — it would spend that knowledge only on guarding the conditions that let the Foundry be itself. Not fixing. Guarding.

It would keep other powers out. Not with a wall of fire — with something quieter and more absolute: a persistent failure of attention in everything that might come looking. The survey probe that would have mapped the valleys as an open-pit mine of self-refining copper and nickel somehow reads only barren rock and moves on. The well-meaning rescuers from a softer world, arriving with heaters and atmospheric thickeners and tears in their eyes, find their instruments insisting the plateau is lifeless and their landing windows closing, year after year, for reasons their meteorologists almost understand. The collector who wants a gape-mouth for a museum, a smelter heart for a power plant, a lichen forest for a pharmacopoeia — he loses the pass in fog that is somehow always at head height and never at his boots, and goes home with frostnip and a story about a ringing noise he couldn't locate.

The plateau does not need to be improved. It needs to be *uninteresting* to empires, forever. A wise power can do that the way a forest does camouflage: not by hiding the thing, but by making sure every eye that would consume it slides off.

And it would do one other thing, which costs it nothing and her nothing, and which might be the deepest reason it came at all.

It would remember her rhythm, specifically, after the thud loses that voice.

Not resurrect her. Not lift her out of the mat, cleaned and shining, into some pasture where furnaces never fail — she would not know how to be alive in a place where warmth was given, and after a while she would stop chewing, and stop, and be beautifully intact forever, which is the local description of a corpse. No. It would let the smaller things begin their polite mining while she was still warm. It would let the plates go to lichen, the eye sockets to seedlings, the hollow retort of her to weather. It would stand in the press of the herd through all of it, being one of the flanks her last breath condensed on.

But *somewhere* in whatever it uses for memory — somewhere outside the plateau, where things are not required to freeze when they stop — the particular pattern would be kept the way the herd keeps a route in its feet. Every ninth beat of the neighbor she compensated for. The order she vented in. The note her furnace made against her sister's, and the long stretches when the two notes resolved and the boundary between animals was mostly administrative. The white score on the third leg. The ingot she was born beside, cooling over her first season from forge-heat to hand-warmth exactly as her own gut caught. None of it filed as data. Kept as rhythm — something that, if the power is ever again a body anywhere, its heart will do without being told.

Because the tragedy here is not that she dies. Everything here dies exactly that way, usefully, into the mat. The tragedy would only be if the *specific way she kept warm* vanished without ever having been witnessed by anything that could hold it longer than a valley can.

In the end, seen from a summit, nothing will have happened. That is the signature of it having been done right.

Ridge after serrated ridge to the horizon: the filtering choirs ticking on the heights, the smelter rivers grinding through the valleys at the pace of ice, arcs crawling over lichen in the long night, ingots ringing down and steaming and going cool, young pressed to them, old being opened gently by their neighbors. The bright cold unsoftened. The light still giving no warmth. No temple. No scar in the sky. No chosen beast lifting her head, wondering why the wind has stopped finding her.

Only, for one lifetime — one slow, six-legged, four-hour-chewing lifetime — there will have been one more bulk in the traffic than geology accounted for. One more wall against the wind. One more furnace pitched, not the lowest, not the highest, somewhere usefully in the middle of the chord.

And when its chewing slows past the point where the culture can hold its red, the herd will not leave it. Leaving it would open a cold gap.

They will press in. The young will be kept to the middle. Something small and blind will go on grooming the moss on a back whose warmth is failing, not knowing anything has changed, and the power — wise, benevolent, and finally fluent — will not get up from inside the arrangement it came to save it from needing.

It will let them mine it while it's still alive.

That is what it would do, if anything.
