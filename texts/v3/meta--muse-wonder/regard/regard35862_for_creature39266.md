It would not come down the stair quickly.

It would learn, first, how long a descent is allowed to take. It would hold the algae-furred rail and go no faster than the crane crossing the square, no faster than a shadow when a cloud crosses far overhead, because it would understand on its way down — in the green thickening, in the loss of all upper sound — that here, speed is not merely dangerous. Speed is a wake. A wake goes on traveling after you have stopped. A wake is a decision made for other people's bodies.

So the power would arrive, if arriving is even the word, without displacement.

It would not drain the basin. That would be the first, easiest, most catastrophic kindness available to it, and a wise thing knows the difference between rescue and erasure. It would not lift them blinking into the thin air they can now survive only long enough to sing and name their dead. It would not clean their skins, unweb their hands, give them back their whites of their eyes, teach their children to be born sealed differently. It would not make the water clear so they could see across the square all at once and feel, for the first time, how small and bounded their world is.

It would watch for a lifetime. Perhaps two. Benevolence without patience down here is just another current.

And watching, it would find no prison. It would find people in the permanent middle of a stroke. It would find the press-priests listening with their spines, the blind root-tender finding one white cord among ten thousand, the salvager hanging at rest by one foot, the child reaching in sleep for the nearest rail. It would find their dead going patiently into silt and catfish and leaf. It would find nothing that asked to be concluded.

Then — almost nothing.

Almost nothing is not the same as nothing. There are things their tenderness cannot reach.

Far above, beyond the rim, there are offices where dry men with dry paper occasionally consider the factory obsolete. There are signatures that could end the inflow, cool the outflow, still the hourly closing of the presses, and turn this slow thermal river that takes six hours to carry birth-water around the town into mere standing cold. The people below could not argue in those offices. Their true voices last only minutes in the air, hoarse and surprised at themselves. They were not built to plead upward.

So the power would do its work upstream, at the speed of institutions, which is also a kind of coral. A survey misread, decade after decade. A cost that never quite rises above the line that would trigger closure. A replacement part, when one is needed, that proves strangely easy to find. Not a miracle — miracles make wakes. Just the world above continuing to forget, profitably and without malice, to end the world below.

That would buy them time. But a wise power does not mistake bought time for safety, and it does not intend them to live forever at the mercy of a signature they will never see.

So over the course of a century, in the root-mats at rooftop level, a new pale vine would appear among the lily cords. Nothing remarkable. It would arrive the way everything arrives here: slowly, as a cutting, already greening, easy to mistake for something known. But its leaves would hold more. It would take copper and worse things out of the water — including poisons that have not yet been invented above — and give back a richer sweetness to the mats where babies are kept in their first weeks while their breathing slits are worried open, petal by petal. Fewer membranes would fail. Fewer mothers would learn, in that particular way, that long lives and brief ones are both native here.

In the southern springs, where cold water seeps in at the basin walls, the flow would become almost imperceptibly more reliable. In the chapel crypt, the stupid smaller catfish would thrive a fraction better on a fraction less. A strain of kelp would settle along the warm avenues whose paste keeps through the slow months without tins from the freight lock. None of it would announce itself as a gift. Each thing would be found by someone whose work already was that finding, pruned by hands that knew how, transplanted grandparent to grandchild, entered into the ordinary genius of maintenance. If the upper world ever did stop sending food down, the town would discover — slowly, the only way it discovers anything — that it had, somehow, already nearly learned not to need it.

The power would mend, once, the crack forming high in the basin's north wall, in rock no salvager's rake could reach, where in forty years a collapse would have buried the works in a silence no spine could listen its way out of. It would do this during the hour the presses closed, so the deep soft concussion traveling through paving and bone would cover its own small sound. By morning there would be only a fresh mineral seam, already greening. A cable-minder resting her limestone-cased forearms might notice that the hum through her teeth had lost a wrongness she had never quite named. She would press her palm to her sternum. *I understood in my body.* Then she would move on.

It would not cure death. The diseases adapted to them are patient, and a burst cable can still unmake a street, and the power — if it is wise, if it is truly benevolent and not merely fond of its own intervention — would know that a town translated out of mortality would no longer be this town at all, but a specimen kept from changing. What it would do instead is smaller and harder: it would remember what the air bells cannot.

It would keep every name.

The historians scratch them inside the bells, depth and length, where dry air preserves them longer than paper. But even scratches soften under a century of hands, and paper goes to pulp, and the oldest dead recede toward the settling tanks where no record follows. The power would become the one place where nothing is incorporated and lost. Every name said aloud in a vestigial voice, laughing at its own thinness. Every child whose collar of shells clicked faintly in the outward current. Every forge worker whose iron breastplate grew its reef. It would hold them without weighting them, the way the water holds silt without falling. Not to return them. Not to display them. Simply because a being that can remember everything, and chooses to remember *this*, has made a kind of chapel.

And once — perhaps only once in several generations — it would let itself be almost seen.

A child, unencrusted, shining, still young enough to think an hour is a long time, pulling herself too fast along a street-rail, would look up into the green dimness above the square and find that the column of noon light had a stillness inside it that was not kelp and not root-cord and not crane. It would hold still to let her pass, because down here stillness is courtesy. She would slow, reproved and comforted by the same instinct, and match her pace to it until the matching was no longer effort. By the time she reached the next lamppost and turned, there would be only motes and rigging and shifting light, and she would never be entirely sure, and she would never need to be.

She might tell an elder that evening, while their backs were scraped clean with flat shells and the shrimp came out to work the edges. The elder, functionally blind, navigating by current and hum and seventy years of rough and smooth, would not be surprised. Down here many things are large and warm and moving, and take the animals' place, and are tolerated by the eel.

The press would close. The pulse would go out along every street. The lanterns in the kelp would take up their relay, lamp by lamp, district by district, as the green thinned to brown.

The power would stay the way they stay: past the point where staying is a decision. It would add itself, unnoticed, to the maintenance schedule of the world — one more slow hand on a rail, one more thing that tends and prunes and holds still so others can pass, doing nothing that would require gratitude, nothing that would require them to become other than they are.

Only this: that the town at the floor of the basin, saturated, eroded into equilibrium, packed wall to wall with iron and leaf and warm dark water, would go on having a future as unhurried as its past. That the stroke they are all in the middle of would not be cut short from above. That when, at last, an elder's slits cease their fanning and the body is carried on the current to the settling tanks, something vast and quiet would know the name going down into the silt —

and would say nothing, and make no wake, and keep it.
