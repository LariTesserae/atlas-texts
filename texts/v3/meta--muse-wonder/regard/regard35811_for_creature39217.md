It would not silence the compressors.

That is the first thing, and the hardest. From the rim, with the plain silent behind it and the roar rising out of the cut ground, every instinct of benevolence would say: *stop this*. Close the stations. Let the snow settle. Let the valley learn quiet. Heal the wound that was blasted here, fill it, smooth it, give it back to the wind.

A wise power would stand there long enough to understand that the wound has become a world. And that healing, now, would be another violence.

It would not fill the Cut. It would not restore the plain. It would not lift the animals up into the light and teach them to be something else — foxes, ravens, creatures of the open country. They have already chosen, over forty slow years, what to be: earless, garden-backed, patient, deaf to everything except the one sound that made all other life leave. Silence would not free them. Silence would blind them in the only sense that matters.

So it would do almost nothing.

And it would do that almost-nothing very, very carefully.

For a long time — a human rotation, a season, perhaps a decade, because down there decades are the unit that moss understands — it would simply attend. Not from above, where attention would be a shadow crossing the floor. From underneath its notice, the way the animals themselves travel. Jaw to the ground. Learning Station One from Station Two by the roughness in its shaking. Learning the double beat at Station Three where the housing meets the rock. Learning where the mat is metre-deep and old, where a boardwalk has compressed it into a road, where heat leaks from a flange and the willow has grown waist-high and black. Learning the felt territories, each one centred on an orange light and fading into the cold dark between.

A power that cannot listen at that speed has no business loving this place.

Then, at the margins, it would begin to guard the conditions that let slowness continue.

Not miracles. Miracles are loud, and here loudness belongs to the machines. Anything sudden — a shining rescue, a revelation, a winter that ends overnight, a station that stops overnight — would kill more surely than neglect. Its kindness would have to be inaudible, or the animals would pay for being noticed with their camouflage, which is their entire life.

So it would keep the roar *continuous*. Not louder. Not cleaner. Continuous.

In an office far south, where men and women who have never sunk ankle-deep in sphagnum decide whether a compressor station can be replaced all at once by a quieter electric unit, a line would survive in a budget that should have been cut. A maintenance window would be staggered — one station at a time, never two, never in midwinter. An engineer planning to fix the double beat at Station Three would find the fault tolerable for another year, and then another, until the animal that navigates by it has died in its nest hollow and its bones have gone light as pipe insulation under the moss. Repairs would happen the way rust happens here: noted, waited on, done two summers later.

It would protect the imperfections. The waste-heat leak that feeds a willow thicket where a family winters stacked in torpor. The imperfect lagging where condensation beads and is licked off in the dark. The raised floors of the yellow shelters, under which foxes den and digging is easy. An efficiency audit might pass over this valley, mysteriously incurious. A valve housing might never quite be sealed. Small heats, leaking steadily into the cold, like mercy refusing to be optimised.

It would protect the mat before it protected any single animal.

That is the second hard thing. It would not save a pup flushed onto the ramp by lifting it gently back — or if it did, it would do it as weather, as a slump of snow, as a reason to turn downhill again. It would not heal the old animal dying in the nest, its lichen outliving its back. It would let death become infrastructure, because here that is not a tragedy, it is the architecture.

But the mat — the mat is the roof and sea and sky of everything. So where a boot is about to step off a boardwalk into twenty years' growth, there might be a railing unexpectedly in the hand. Where a researcher is about to publish the tunnels as something other than subsidence, the photographs might come out blurred, the samples mislabelled, the conclusion reasonable and wrong and mercifully boring. It would not hide the animals by force. It would let human beings keep almost-knowing, which is the safest kind of knowing they have. Let the older hands go on knocking twice with their frost-mallets before they start work, and waiting a breath, and not being able to say why they would feel rude not to. A ritual preserved is better than a species discovered. Rituals leave you alone.

It would think ahead, further than the gas.

Because the gas will end. Not soon by human counting, but soon by the counting of benches and permafrost and an animal that breeds once in four years. One day the economic reason for the Cut will be gone, and human beings, meaning well or not meaning at all, will propose to decommission it — a word that down here would mean the roar thinning, station by station, into a silence no jaw can navigate by, the cold pooling deeper without waste heat, the lights going out along eighteen kilometres of corridor country.

A benevolent power would not forbid that ending forever. Even this place is not owed eternity; the plain was not owed eternity either, and it was cut. But it would insist on time. Time scaled to the tenants. A dimming over generations, not a shutdown over a season. One station quieted in the decades after its last territory-holder has gone. New shelter, if shelter must be made, made long before it is needed — another sunken, shadowed, shaking place, seeded with sphagnum and left alone to go wild, its boardwalks weathering in, its noise beginning as an affront and softening, over fifty years, into geography. Or perhaps simply this: when the gas no longer needs moving, something in the human world — a heritage designation, a permafrost study, a clerk's stubbornness, a debt nobody remembers incurring — keeps one low machine turning in the dark for no reason anyone can quite justify, the way cathedrals keep a candle lit.

And if none of that can be arranged — if the ending comes anyway, as endings do — then it would not perform a last spectacular salvation. It would go down into the tunnels a final time, at ankle-depth, at jaw-depth, and stay while the vibration faded, so that the last animals would not be alone in the disorientation. It would learn their single soft syllable for *young nearby, keep weight off*, and honour it by keeping its own weight off. It would let them become moss. It would remember the exact frequency of Station Two, a fraction rougher than Station One, when no living thing remained that could hear it.

From the rim at noon, none of this would be visible. That is how you would know it was being done well.

The snow would lie unbroken. The orange lights would burn, small and steady, in the blue shadow. The vapour would flatten and travel sideways and never clear the walls. A crew would knock twice on a railing, wait a breath, and start work, and twenty metres away a hummock would breathe very, very slowly and not be afraid.

And underneath, through peat and root and frozen sphagnum, there would come — perhaps once in a winter, perhaps once in a lifetime — an answering beat. Not louder than the animals' own. Not a voice. Not a sign for the plain to hear.

Just the ground, for a moment, drumming back through the breastbone: *boundary acknowledged. You are not unnoticed. Continue.*

And then the roar, doing the one fast thing this slow valley was given to do, would cover it completely, and the power would leave it covered.
