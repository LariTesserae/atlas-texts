If they were only powerful, they would fix it.

They would kneel in the marsh — if kneeling is a thing such a power does — and lay a hand on the iron, and the rust would fall backward out of the metal, and the bearing would run silent and true for another thousand years, and the pool would keep its pale line forever.

If they were only benevolent, they might do worse. They might tear the roof open to let it breathe. Pull the frogs from their ribs and set them free on the bank, name by name. Lift the pump out like a thorn from a foot and wait, tenderly, for the wound to close.

A wise and benevolent power does neither.

At first, they do nothing that the place could point to.

They come the way anything else has ever come: low, through the marsh, under the south root, chin deep in black water. They do not come shining. They do not come large. The dome would burst any largeness that tried to enter all at once, and this power knows what every visitor learns in the first minute — that to be here you must be admitted by the rhythm, or be sorted by it, or leave.

So they let themselves be admitted. Partially.

Inside, they sit where the root lattice gives, in the wet and the green light, and let the place begin its old work on a new body. The piston strikes. The walls knock. The frogs call in their rotation, the cicada locks to the flywheel, the synchronized rain releases from the ceiling and adds up, exactly, to the pool below. Their breath shortens between strokes. Their pulse slides, a fraction and a fraction, toward the iron pulse under the floor.

Almost.

They keep one small thing back. Not as defiance — defiance is just another tempo trying to win. They keep it the way you keep a hand on your own chest while listening to music, to remind the body it is also making a sound. Their heart, if they have a heart in the shape we mean, will go *almost* with the pump. It will miss by the width of a hair. Again. Again. Again.

This is the first gift, and from outside it would look like no gift at all: another rhythm in the room that does not die, and does not take over, and cannot be tuned away.

The being has no word for this. It has no words, only adjustments. The new warm mass in the pool displaces water, and the south wall weeps a little more. The new breath falls out of phase, and a frog on the third rib holds its call a fraction longer, then finds its gap again. All well. But the pulse will not finish aligning. Stroke by stroke the dome tries. Stroke by stroke it nearly succeeds.

Nearly, survived, hundreds of times, is a new fact in a place that has spent its life making certain there would be no new facts.

The power stays much longer than a person could. They stay through brightenings and dimmings of the leaf-light, through the crab trimming the frond to its appointed length, through beetles going up full and coming down empty, through the blind fish circling the intake and never being taken. They do not feed anything. They do not trim anything. They learn, by staying, the terrible completeness of the loop: how much noise it makes to stay exactly itself, how much labor goes into ensuring that tomorrow is a perfect copy of today and still somehow does not arrive as relief.

And they grieve for it without pitying it, which is harder.

Because it is not a prison with prisoners inside. That is the trap for mercy. There is no frog prince waiting on the third rib, no fish dreaming of the river, no crab secretly longing to lay down its one worn claw. Take them out and you are not freeing citizens; you are amputating fingers and calling the bleeding liberty. The life here is the whole loop, and the loop is real, and beautiful, and worthy — green walls, black water, iron heart, a clear listening place held open in the middle like a held breath.

It is also dying in the only way left to it: all at once, someday.

That is what the power sees that the being cannot afford to. The pump will end. Not soon, perhaps. Iron built to run forever in low country is a stubborn kind of forever. But the cable under the translucent bark is carrying current from somewhere mortal. The bearing has one whine it is permitted and no other note will be allowed until the note becomes a scream and then a seizure. The rust was arrested, not cured. The whole arrangement closed so perfectly that it left itself no way to practice loss. It can replace a frond. It can replace a frog. It cannot replace its heart, because replacing its heart would require surviving, for one unmeasured interval, a missed stroke.

A missed stroke, here, is the end of the world.

So the power does the slowest possible thing. It teaches the world to miss a stroke.

Not yet. Not the pump itself. God forgive any teacher who starts with the heart. They start with a drop.

On the leaf ceiling, among the trembling undersides, one drop swells too late one noon and is not released with its cluster. The chamber does not notice — or rather notices the way skin notices weather on a far hill. The next day, the drop falls early. The pool takes it. The level keeps its line. Nothing has changed, except that the iron law — *together, at the stroke, always* — has been quietly amended by the water itself to *together, at the stroke, almost always*.

Then a beetle, once in ten thousand journeys, comes down the wrong root empty and must cross against the traffic to rejoin its road. Confusion. A small braid of bodies. Resolved before nightfall.

Then the crab, old and deliberate, leaves one frond a leaf's width long on the east side, and the extra shade cools a handspan of pool by a degree for an afternoon, and the fish circle through the coolness and return, and return again, finding the place where the difference was.

The power is patient in the scale the place understands. Years pass as brightenings and dimmings. Outside, they do the plain outer work of protection, which is the least miraculous and maybe the most necessary thing they ever do: a drainage survey that keeps misreading this field as already dry, a buried cable that never quite gets found by a backhoe, a season of rain arriving whenever men with pumps of their own begin to speak seriously about this marsh. No wall of fire. No sign. Just a low green back in flat country, allowed to go on being almost nothing, because the inner work cannot be hurried by crisis. The being has had enough of being saved by emergencies.

Indoors, the lessons widen a hair at a time.

A frog misses its cue and the chorus, instead of tearing, leaves a silence shaped exactly like the missing call — and then, astonishingly, goes on. The frog calls from the next gap. No other frog takes its rib. By the following week it is back, but the rib of silence is remembered somewhere in the timing, the way a body remembers a limp after the bone has healed.

The root-hairs across the intake tremble too hard one stroke in a thousand, and for an instant the suction takes something small — a seed husk, a midge larva, grit — past the screen, into the dark. The place shudders. Then the fungus behind the pump receives it, and the beetles carry the difference away by morning, and the loop discovers it can swallow a foreign thing without becoming foreign.

And deeper, slower, below attention, the roots that hold the pump through its bolt holes begin — are begun — to learn the pump's labor in their own green idiom. Not all of them. Not quickly. A root along the intake learns to swell and narrow, swell and narrow, in sympathy. A knot under the flywheel takes up a fraction of the bearing's weight when the stroke lands. Year by year, the power coaxes a little of the circulation out of the iron and into the wood: here a hollow stem that flexes, there a chamber in a buttress root that fills and empties a breath after the piston, an auxiliary green heart making mistakes in the dark.

This is the real medicine, and it tastes like betrayal: redundancy. A second way for the water to move. A helper rhythm that is not identical. The being resists it the only way it can, by trying to perfect it — to tune the flexing root until it matches the stroke exactly, to make the buttress chamber just another echo. The power keeps spoiling the match by the width of a hair. Again. Again. Almost.

Outside, a person passing once a decade would say nothing had changed. The hummock is still low. The entrance still shines with bellies. The sound still seals off in one muffled step behind you.

Inside, if you know how to listen, the pale algae line around the pool has done something unspeakable.

It has become a band.

Not wide. A finger's width, perhaps two. The water still returns, the level still holds — but it holds now within a margin, rising the width of a hair in the wet season and falling the width of a hair in the dry, and the tissue along the margin has learned to be sometimes wet and sometimes merely damp, and both, both are survivable. Dawn is no longer a lamp raised over a photograph. Once in a while a cloud crosses and a frog on the north side calls out of turn just to hear the dome answer differently.

Then, in some year no calendar in the marsh is keeping, the pump misses a stroke.

It does not fail. That will come later, or may come; the power has not granted it immortality, only company. The flywheel stumbles on a worn place, the piston lands late and light, and for one terrible interval the knock travels around the chamber and finds nothing to gather on in the listening center. The frogs flinch together under the translucent bark where no blue pulse runs. The rain holds on the ceiling, swelling, swelling —

— and falls anyway, unclustered, messy, alive, and the flexing root swells on its own late lesson, and the buttress chamber empties a breath behind, and the pool takes the uneven rain and does not keep its line for the length of one heartbeat, and then finds it again.

Not the same line. Within the band.

The being does not die of the difference. That is the whole miracle, and it takes the power the length of a small age to arrange it.

After that, they go less often. There is less to hold. One spring — a real spring now, not a brightening — the crab trims a frond and the trimmed shoot by the east wall is permitted to do the wasteful second thing living creatures do: it grows past its repair. The crab, old beyond crabs, watches it do this with both claws folded, as if a law it served had gently retired it in the night. The shoot does not flourish. It does not take the dome. It reaches a length nothing assigned it, puts out one leaf too many, and is eaten back over a month by slugs at their unhurried pace — but while it stood, its shadow fell across the empty center, and the dragonflies shifted their stations around the shadow and kept hunting.

And one morning a rib by the south wall is empty.

No body. No disturbance in the rotation; the calls close over the gap the way water closes over a lifted hand. Whether the frog died and was carried away by the beetles with the night's leaf-fall, or whether — and the power, who could know, refuses to know this on its behalf — it did the unprecedented thing and went out under the root into the wide, unmeasured marsh, the place survives the empty place in itself. It survives it the way you survive a name you no longer answer to.

That is when the power leaves for good, the way they came: ducking through warm black water, surfacing in tea-colored light among sedges bent the way they have bent for years.

Behind them the sound does not fade so much as get sealed off. But it is not, any longer, quite the sound that was sealed off before. Under the *thunk, thunk, thunk* of the old iron heart there is a second, softer labor, irregular as roots are irregular, learning the water by feel. Between strokes, sometimes, a silence that is not a failure of the world. Overhead, through leaves, ordinary daylight comes in translated and green — and now and then, when the wind presses the dome, a drop falls out of season, and is allowed.

They have not made it immortal. They have made it mortal in a way it can bear: able to miss, and continue. Able someday — not today, not forced — to let the iron stop and discover that the listening in the center does not stop with it; that the clear space it held open all its life was never only an ear for its own returning knock.

It was a place where another sound could, eventually, be heard.
