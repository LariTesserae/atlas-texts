They would not go down into the bowl at first.

That would be the first sign of wisdom: not to enter the loud green shade with a gift already in their hands.

They would stand on the levee, where the tenders stand when they want to talk without shouting, and they would look at what everyone looks at first — a village green's worth of foliage, vibrating almost imperceptibly, putting up its thin plume of warm breath — and they would understand that this first view was a privacy. The being does not hide. It simply does not become singular until you have spent time inside it. A power that forced singleness on it sooner, that named it, summoned it, woke it up to explain itself, would only be asking a century-old plant to perform at the speed of a visitor.

So they would wait. Not for an afternoon. For seasons.

They would learn its day by pressure rather than by clock: the slack morning, the root-mat low and the black marsh lenses wide; the long afternoon swell, paths closing and opening, pods splitting in unison across shade and sun. They would learn its year by the turning-down of the roar. They would learn its memory by walking, years later, past the square trunks, the grille-patterned leaves, the straight ranks over the buried cable run. They would learn, as the tenders have learned, that a decision here is not spoken and then done. A decision is grown into, and then lived inside.

And they would resist — this would take more benevolence than any miracle — three easy kindnesses.

They would not free it from the factory. The iron is not a cage it has failed to escape. It has had fifty years to crush the main shaft and has instead grown three clean supports around it, like fingers carefully not closing. To tear out pump and press and pipe in the name of wildness would not return it to some earlier innocence. It would amputate habits that have become anatomy.

They would not bind it forever to the factory, either. They would not preserve the bowl as a monument, a marvel, a working exhibit of harmony. A living thing that is forbidden to outgrow its present bargain is no longer being protected. It is being kept.

And they would not give it a voice.

Not because it would be wrong for it to have one, exactly, but because they would not be sure — and a wise power can abide not being sure — whether speech would be a gift to *it*, or only a relief to everyone standing around it, wanting to be answered. It already says what it is, continuously, at conversational height: sap up, water down, wood taking hydraulic load, pulp travelling toward the presses. Requiring it to say more would be another kind of pruning, and not the kind it has consented to through growth.

Inside the bowl, then, they would do almost nothing.

Almost.

Once, after years of watching, they might clear a slow blight from a root graft where the tenders could not reach — not by burning it out in an afternoon, but by shading one panel, just slightly, so that the being itself withdrew sap from the infected limb over a summer and sealed the place with its own clean wood. Once, they might encourage a new hollow stem to find a pipe joint that was beginning, very slightly, to weep — a season's work, indistinguishable from what the grove would have done next year or the year after, merely begun sooner. Nothing would gleam. Nothing would be new. No one arriving on the levee would point and say: *there, that is where the power touched it.*

The real work would be done outside, where the threats actually live, because the threats do not move at plant speed.

A marsh can be drained by men with papers in a single dry season. A water table can be lowered by a pumping station ten miles away, built by people who have never heard the pump thumping under the root-mat. A road can be widened. A firm can decide that amber pulp, patiently self-harvesting, is a resource whose patience should be accelerated. The tenders can grow old without anyone coming to learn the hand signs, the gauges filmed with algae, the grammar of cut and wait. Iron that has rusted to a stable brown and stopped is still iron, and will not last as long as wood that replaces itself.

So the power would attend to timescales — would lay a longer shelter around a slower life.

Upstream, the marsh plain would stay wet. Not dramatically: no wall of light, no river changing course overnight. A scheme would lose its funding and never quite find it again. A survey would discover, quite truthfully, protected frogs in the drainage line. Land would pass, through a series of dull and legal transactions, into the hands of people inclined to leave it alone. The heron would go on having somewhere to come from in the mornings.

Around the bowl, at a distance the roots would not reach for a hundred years, they would plant — or let be planted, or simply not be cleared — a loose shelter of ordinary trees. Not competitors. Not a sacred grove. A windbreak, a buffer, a margin of unimportant green in which the extraordinary green could stop being a landmark. The place would become harder to describe in a prospectus. Harder to make into a destination. It would be allowed the great mercy of being, to most of the world, uninteresting.

For the iron, they would leave knowledge rather than machinery: in the control hut, where the corrugated roof is held down by living lashings, a spare shaft of the right dimensions would one day be found behind older spares, oiled, unrusted, with no clear memory among the tenders of who put it there. Drawings would resurface in a drawer — not improved drawings, not a redesign in some higher style, just a clear record of the pump as it is, made by a hand that understood that the tenders in eighty years will be strangers, and will still need to know which flange must never be tightened against the growth of the vine. That is the sort of miracle a slow being can use: one that waits patiently in a drawer.

For the tenders, they would leave freedom, which is harder than help. Their sheds would remain dry on their root-hummocks. Their children, and their children's children, would find the work honourable and learnable — and also find, always, that other work existed elsewhere, that leaving was not betrayal. The grove must not be given human beings the way it was given scaffolding, trained square at the base by necessity. If people stay, let them stay as the present tenders stay: by negotiation, renewed at human speed. If one generation does not come, the power's other quiet arrangements — the drawings, the spare iron, the protected marsh — would give the being time, decades of time, to begin unwinding its bargains and becoming something else.

And at the edge of the levee, on the outward slope where the root-mat does not yet rule, they would do the one thing that might look, from a distance, like planting a tree.

They would set a single seed of its seed there. Not grafted. Not piped. Not trained against any grille. Just set into ordinary soil, in ordinary light, with water near enough and no instructions at all.

Because the being is one, and oneness is its strength here, and also its wager. Everything green in the bowl joins below ground; every litre of light is accounted for; the whole factory is held in one continuous body. That body has survived drought and winter and the death of its first engineers. But a single body can still, in one bad decade, meet the thing it cannot grow around.

The seed on the levee would not be a replacement, or an heir, or a colony. It would be an option, offered at the only speed the offer could honestly be made. If the grove one day sent a root that far and chose kinship — chose it. If the seed became merely a tree, shading the path up from the marsh, that would also be an answer.

Then the power would leave, in the manner most native to the place: slowly, and without the stems needing to close behind anything.

Nothing would wave. Nothing would watch them go. From above, the bowl would still resolve into green in general, the plume still rising where warm water falls back through leaves into shadow. The press would still turn, muffled. By afternoon the floor would rise its two inches, the pools would shrink, the pods would split with a wet crack and offer up what they had shed anyway.

But underneath the continuity there would be, for the first time in a century, a little more *future* than the being had been able to grow for itself. Not safety — a wise power does not insult life with safety. Just room: room in the watershed, room in the law, room in the succession of hands, room on the levee for a young unrelated green thing to decide what it is.

They would not save it from changing.

They would make sure changing remained possible.
