It would not come at noon.

At noon the room is at its most complete and its most defended — sun in a solid column, water shattering, the glare coming off the tiles so hard there is nowhere for a strangeness to stand without casting a strangeness back. And she is there in the middle of it, head tipped, eyes shut to polished slits, being taken apart by light. Any arrival then would be an interruption of the one hour when she is most fully engaged in becoming herself.

A wise power would know to be late. Or early. It would come in the blue time before the scrubbers, when the cistern on the roof has given up its heat and the inflow runs only warm, and the room is dim and ticking very faintly at the edges, and she is hung upright in the tank with her chin just clear of the surface, turning that slow quarter-turn, listening.

Even then, it would have to duck.

That is the first right thing it would do, and perhaps the most important: it would accept the terms of the place. Nine steep steps. A hatch that must be kicked, not lifted, at the lower left corner. A ledge only wide enough for bare feet. It would not widen the opening. It would not make the vault higher, or the tank deeper, or bring down a grander light. It would take off, so to speak, whatever in it was too large for a pantry-sized room under a wash-house floor, and leave the rest above.

And then it would get into the water, because there is no other honest way to meet her.

Not approach. Not summon. Just stand, chest-deep, and turn slowly, as she does, because there is nowhere else to go. It would keep its ears full, the way she does, until the roar separated into its true architecture — spout, drip, overflow, drain, the knock of steam condensing somewhere inside the wall — and it understood that what sounds from the ledge like noise is, from inside, a map of the whole world.

It would stay long enough for the brightness to begin not breaking evenly.

It would see her first as she can be seen — sidewise, as a thickening in the water, a place where the light bends a fraction too much. It would notice the milky bloom under her skin, the thumbnail scales with their softening thumbprints, the green tracery between her shoulder blades where the light cannot quite reach. It would notice the hairline scar down her left shin. It would not heal it.

That restraint would cost it something. Benevolence always wants to smooth the scar, to finish the scales at once, to make the curing instant and painless, to lift her up the nine steps and show her the street and the sky undiluted by tin reflectors. Wisdom is knowing that would be a way of killing her kindly. She is not a prisoner in the tank. She is an event still happening in it, like plaster. To take her out now would be to dry her out. To finish her now would be to decide, on her behalf, what finished means.

So it would not rescue her.

It would do almost nothing, and it would do it for a very long time.

First, it would guard the ordinariness of the room. This is harder than it sounds. Places like this are not usually destroyed by malice. They are destroyed by improvement. By someone in the wash-house above deciding the cellar tank is unsanitary, or wasteful, or a perfect place for storing coal. By a new owner who hears the mason's warning — keep it flooded, keep it warm, keep the spout running — and hears only superstition in it. By a rope fraying, quietly, month by month, on the sluice-gate pulley, until one night it parts.

So the rope would not part.

That is the kind of miracle it would prefer. The cistern's black paint would stay black a season longer than paint should, drinking the sun down to the last hour. A little soot would never quite settle on the hammered tin up the light-shaft; someone would take it into his head, some autumn morning, to polish reflectors he cannot remember polishing before, and whistle while he did it, and never wonder why. The warped corner of the hatch would go on warping exactly as much as it does now — enough to need her shoulder, never enough to seal. No one would replace it with iron. No one would think of a lock.

The mason's sentence would survive its mason. It would pass from owner to tenant to scrubber in exactly the same words, with exactly the same shrug, the way true instructions do: keep it flooded, keep it warm, keep the spout running — not because anyone above understands what depends on it, but because the sentence itself has learned how to be repeated.

And on scrubbing mornings, the stiff brush would keep missing, by a fraction, the shadiest bottom corner. The man with the paddle would feel, not a presence exactly, only a reluctance to press too hard in that one place where the water stays cooler and the green threading has thickened almost — almost — into something like veins. He would move on. He would tell himself he'd got it yesterday. Her fingers would stay hooked in the mouth of the overflow, her breath held, her eyes open and shining, and she would endure the clean violence of being cared for by people who did not know she was there.

It would feed her only what the water was already bringing her, but a little more reliably. A seed husk in the current. An ant, now and then, not fast enough. The mineral milk of new mortar, for as long as the mortar has milk to give — and when the joints finally cure and go lean, other leachings, other salts, coaxed unobtrusively through limestone and brick, so that pressing her mouth to the wall would never leave her quite unfed. It would not lay out offerings. Offerings would teach the people above to look down. It would let her keep believing, correctly, that she forages for herself.

The reed would be left alone.

Not helped too much. Not straightened against her hand. Only this: the crack in the ledge where she planted it would never quite dry, even in the dim hour when she climbs the far wall after the last band of sun and crouches, dripping, among the ewer and the stool, almost dry enough to hurt. The roots would go on finding that crack hospitable. The third good leaf would come in its own time. The power would not hurry it, because it has understood what she is measuring — not her height against the reed, but the patience of a thing that grows straight up toward light without being able to move toward it. When she looks from the reed to the underside of the hatch, it would not open the hatch. Not yet. But it would make very certain that on the day she climbs those nine steps herself, wet feet finding each one, hair still plastered in its fine divided strands, scales joined at last across shoulders and knees, the hatch gives at the lower left corner to the kick she already knows.

In the meantime, it would give her the one gift that leaves no object behind in a room that has no room for objects: an unbroken stretch of unobserved time.

Years of it. Season after season of pour, heat, overflow, condense, drip, the square of sun traveling its inch an hour, the ferns spreading a coin's width a week around the place where she breathes, the algae writing itself slowly across her back in the shaded channel of her spine. Nobody discovering her. Nobody naming her, which would be only another kind of scrubbing. Her voice going on clicking and humming in her chest, thrown back at her by the vault from all sides, until even she cannot tell whether the walls are answering.

Perhaps, once in a long while, the water would hold steadier through a cold night than the cistern above can account for. Perhaps the level would not sigh down that one dangerous inch. She would notice, in the way she knows stairs in the dark — not as a thought, but in her skin, in the fern corner still steaming at dawn, in the scar on her shin not tightening. She would turn her quarter-turn in the dim tank, a little less afraid.

She would never know she had been attended to. That is how she would know, if she could know anything about power, that it had been wise.

And when it left — ducking, of course, up the nine steep steps, wet to the chest, leaving no footprint that anyone above would think to read — it would kick the hatch shut at the lower left corner, with a sound like a hand on a drum.

Below, the spout would go on pouring.
