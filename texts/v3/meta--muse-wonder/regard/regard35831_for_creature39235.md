It would come the way everything true to the place comes: by sound first, through the soles of the feet.

Not in glory. Glory would be another kind of noise, and the room has no vacancy for noise. It would come up the low marsh path in the warm air, through wet grass to the knee, past the willows and the reedmace and the ruler-straight canal, and it would stop before the long concrete face of the pumping station, and stand there longer than a visitor stands. It would feel the engines running. It would let the roar get into its sternum. It would wait until its own breathing had been erased.

Only then would it find the gap in the ivy.

A lesser kindness would enter quickly, meaning to help, and be driven out in minutes with a sore chest and good intentions. This one understands that time is the first courtesy here. Nothing in the chamber happens quickly except the hiss at the flange. Everything else — the swelling of a droplet, the creep of lime, the cropping of moss, the passage of a pale pad along wet concrete — happens at the rate at which it has always happened, and cannot be hurried without being destroyed.

So it stays on the steps a while, where the nosings are dished smooth, and learns the room before it presumes to love it.

It would not silence the engines.

That would be the obvious mercy, and it would be murder. The being does not endure the sound in spite of its life; it conducts its life inside the sound. Its ribs take the turbine note in through the back wall and let it out again. Its pulse rests where the gauge needle rests. To stop the roar would be to stop the held chord on which it is strung, and leave it, for the first time in eighty years, in a silence it has no organs for. It would not give it quiet.

It would not clear its eyes.

The fogged lids turn, still, a fraction toward the door when the light comes broad after rain, and toward the vent where the mist hangs at the meeting of warm air and weather. It knows season by a cooling on one cheek. It knows the heron, perhaps, by a thinning of diffuseness through the louver, though the heron has never put its head through the door and it has never gone to meet it. Its eyes are not a wound to be healed. They are a gauge that has settled to its mark. To make it see the room again would be to make it a stranger in the only twelve feet it has ever possessed.

It would not lift it down, or lay it out, or give it a voice, or carry it to some softer, greener, more natural place — a marsh bank, a reed bed, a proper home. There is no proper home elsewhere. Upright is its floor. Its limbs end in pads for wet concrete and polished iron. Its food is the warm top of algae, the even crop of moss, a snail before noon, the mineral skin of seep water rasped from a wall. Its road is the bright band under the pipe, kept shining by its own belly, year after year. Peel it from the wall and you would not be rescuing an animal from a machine. You would be separating a surface from the only other surface it has learned, grain by grain, to be continuous with.

It would not feed it more. More is not missing here. Almost nothing is always available, and that has been enough to arrive at a final form and hold it. It would not repair the valve wheel, or replace the broken spokes, or wipe the gauge glass clean so the needle could be read again. The needle is not failing to point. It is pointing exactly where rest points.

A wise power knows the temptations of benevolence because they are all forms of disturbance with kind faces. So for a long time — for longer than anyone stays, for a season, perhaps, returning after rain when the light is diffuse and every bead on the moss shows — it would do the hardest and least visible thing:

It would stay, and notice everything, and change nothing that can be seen.

But staying is not the same as doing nothing. Benevolence has hands, even when it keeps them still.

Outside the room, where consequences are looser and human time runs faster, it would act. Softly, upstream, in the places where a signature or a work-order or a bright idea could one day undo eighty years of settling. It would let the inspection chamber slide, gently and permanently, out of the category of things to be maintained. Not forgotten — forgotten places get demolished. Overlooked, rather, in the durable way this place has always survived: present on no urgent list, passed over by every crew with wire brushes and sealant and good industrial hygiene, left off the tour. An engineer would feel, without knowing why, a mild and satisfying sense that that little door behind the ivy was not his concern. A plan to decommission the old station would lose, again and again, some small necessary momentum — a budget deferred, a newer problem elsewhere, a retirement, a file that settles to the bottom of a drawer and is content there. The canal would keep its water. The engines would keep their constant load. Not forever — forever is a word for younger powers — but long, and at a pace of change so slow that the bands on the walls, the wet and the dry a hand's breadth apart, could migrate the way they have always migrated: imperceptibly, giving everything pressed to the concrete time to migrate with them.

Inside the room, it would do less, and with more care.

It would make sure the drain stays precisely as open as it is. Not cleaner. Not wider. Just proof, against some future accident of silt and leaf-mould, that the finger-width stream will go on crossing the grate and dropping the short distance under the floor, and the level below will go on trembling and not rising. It would make sure the moss in the hinge of the cream steel door — that root-thick mat that has propped it permanently open — does not take one bad winter and let the door sigh shut on a place that needs its ordinary afternoon light. It would make sure the lime tip on the ceiling, from which the one appointed droplet falls at its measured interval to strike the one worn hollow on the pipe, is not knocked by a reaching hand or a raised tool. The *tink* will go on sounding. It will go on being left alone by the one who lives there, who takes every other droplet and never that one. That fidelity, whatever it is, will be honored by being unassisted.

Nothing new will be added. No second note. No gift object. No blessing carved where a blessing would have to be read. A thing that has stopped growing for reasons of its own does not need to be given a reason to start.

And then, once — perhaps only once, near the end of the long staying — it would lay a palm flat against the back wall.

Not on the being. Never on the being. On the concrete beside it, in the spray band where liverworts make their overlapping scales, where the vibration is most even. It would press no harder than the wall already presses back. It would not speak, because speech at arm's length arrives here as a shape without consonants, and because the place has made sound unnecessary. It would communicate the way the room itself taught it to be found: through pressure. Through steadiness. A slight thickening in the evenness of the chord. A regularity laid alongside the great regularity of the turbines, and beneath that, almost too deep and too slow to isolate, the second regularity that is the inhabitant holding still between its shoulders.

It would wait long enough to learn whether the answer changes.

Perhaps nothing changes. Perhaps the skin rises by less than a bead of condensation swells, and falls again, exactly as before, and that is the whole reply: continuance, offered back. Perhaps — and no one standing in the doorway, driven out in minutes speechless and sternum-sore, would ever be there to swear to it — the passage along the bright road under the pipe pauses one night a fraction longer above the frog in the drain, or the head turns a fraction less toward the vent, because for once the wall held a warmth that was not engine heat coming through. It would not need to know. A wise power does not collect acknowledgments. It leaves room for a life to be unobserved inside its own practice.

Then it would take its palm away. Carefully, at the same unhurried rate, so the chord loses one thickness the way a tide loses nothing you can point to.

It would go back up the dished steps, through the gap in the ivy, onto the low marsh path where the heron is standing in the canal with its shadow on the water. It would not tell the heron anything. The threshold is understood on both sides, and it will keep.

Come back in a year, in the same season, after rain, and the gauge needle will rest against the same painted mark. The waterline will shine at the same place on the grate. The fern will hold at the edge of its crack. The drip will fall. The stream will shush. On the back wall, above and behind the pipe, in that narrow dry band where the heat comes through, a bulge that is not quite a bulge will breathe on a scale too small to see, in a roar too large to speak over, keeping the version of itself that this warm, wet pocket in a dam can support — neither rescued nor improved nor abandoned.

Only this, perhaps, will be different, and only in a way no visitor could witness: that it goes on under a protection so complete it looks exactly like being left alone.

Which here, where holding still is the whole practice of life, is not neglect.

It is the one form of love the place can bear.
