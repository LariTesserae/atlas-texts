They would come without light.

That would be the first sign of the wisdom. In a place where eyes have been tuned, generation after generation, to aurora and bog-glow and the faint phosphor sweating out of roots, a bright arrival would not be a miracle. It would be a wound. It would blind children. It would startle herds. It would announce, to every hollow Trunk and every thing that hunts by listening, that something large had entered the world and had not bothered to learn how sound travels there.

So they come dark, and low, and slowly. They set no fire larger than a cupped hand. They learn, before anything else, to tether themselves at night.

Not because the Plant would mean them harm. That is the second thing they must unlearn: to ask whether it means harm, as if a continent could have a mood about a footstep. The mat grows over a sleeper the way water closes over a stone dropped in a pond. Without malice. Without noticing, perhaps, the distinction between a sleeper and a particularly warm patch of ground that has stopped moving. The power that has crossed the dark between stars to reach this young, unweathered plateau could unmake mountains. It could raise the sun in its orbit until it hung clear of the peaks. It could still the wind in the Trunks, carve rivers the Plant would be forced to spare, silence the seed-pod artillery, give these broad-chested, enormous-eyed people metal, and medicine, and lamps.

For a long time — for longer than any of the people live, because a wise power can afford the patience the place demands — that is exactly what it considers doing.

That is the first listening. And the people have a rule: never trust a voice the first time you hear it.

The first voice is pity. It arrives very loud.

*Look at them,* pity says. *Frost has taken their fingers. Gas breathes out of the bogs after a quake and lays whole burrows down in their sleep. Babies are born into total darkness. The old go deaf from the bass singing of the Trunks they live inside. They eat fungus and marrow and steamed pulp, they cut their neighbors out of the living ground, they hold their children up once a year into a smear of light that lasts less than a morning. Rescue them.*

Rescue is such a clean word. It never asks what it costs.

Bring the sun up, and you burn the eyes out of the world. Give them broad rivers, and you drown the tunnels where the mat-folk are born warm. Silence the Trunks, and you take away the long-range hearing by which a listener with her cheek against the wood knows that weather is coming two valleys away, that a skin-sheet has torn loose above the cloud, that a herd is drumming toward a crossing. Still the growth pulses, and the runners do not come. Stop the earthquakes, and the new crags stop steaming, and the Plant stops finding mineral heat in the cracks, and the mountains stop being stitched down.

Kill the danger, and you kill the grammar of the place. Everything here knows itself by everything else.

So the power does not rescue them. It waits for the Plant to repeat the voice back, days later, perfect and directionless, out of a hollow a mile away.

That is the second listening. And the second voice is more dangerous, because it sounds more like love.

*Look at them,* it says. *They are perfect. They have made a sacredness out of necessity so complete that nothing in them is waste. Do not touch anything. Do not heal, do not warn, do not alter a single root. To interfere would be arrogance. Leave them exactly as they are, forever, in the frost.*

The wise power sits with that one for a while, palm down on the living felt, feeling a growth pulse travel under it like a slow animal turning in its sleep. Because admiration, too, can be a way of not seeing people. It turns suffering into scenery. It calls a lost fingertip *character* and a poisoned burrow *balance* and feels noble about doing nothing.

A child tethered wrongly in the night is not a symbol. A family suffocating sweetly in bog-breath is not improved by being part of an ecosystem. The people themselves do not believe that. That is why they cut each other out of the mat — gently, apologizing to both sleeper and Plant. That is why their rules exist. Practical, therefore sacred. Their whole culture is an argument that the world is not to be worshipped into stillness. It is to be lived in, carefully, and changed by hand-widths where a hand-width is what is needed.

So the power waits. Years. Decades. It learns to tap through the soles of its feet. It learns which bogs steam clean. It learns to separate a true herd from the Plant's memory of a herd. It learns not to look at fires. Somewhere in those years it stops being a visitor deciding the fate of a place, and becomes — briefly, on loan — an inhabitant.

Then, the third time, something real calls under the copy.

It is not a voice, exactly. Nothing here has ever needed it to be. It is a pattern, repeated with a difference.

The people are scattered, but not fragile in the way pity assumed. Given light and metal and safety, they would not become some better version of themselves. They would become unpeopled from the only knowledge that keeps anyone alive here — and then, when the giver inevitably left or failed or was followed, the plateau would collect the debt. The Plant is strong, but it is *one*. One body from ridge to ridge. One long green argument against the dark. A single sickness, learned well enough by whatever patient microbe is even now evolving in the warm tunnels, could travel root to root across a continent that has no outside of itself to retreat to. The mountains are young and still rising; in three hundred years a range none of the songs yet name will split a valley the runners use, and the Trunk-folk whose chambers are slowly closing have no stanza for it, because no one has lived long enough at that altitude of listening to hear stone preparing to tear. And beyond this world are other powers, not all of them wise, not all of them benevolent, for whom a continent-sized organism and a people with eyes like that would be a resource, a curiosity, a harvest.

Those are the dangers the inhabitants cannot answer — not because they are primitive, but because the dangers operate at scales no tether, no song, no bone knife can reach.

That is where the power acts. Only there. And it acts in the grammar of the place.

It does not give the Plant a new nature. Deep in the oldest hollows, where the mat lies meters thick over stone that has not seen light since the mountains went up, it coaxes the Plant into remembering possibilities of itself it generated and discarded a thousand seasons ago — dormant chemistries, sleeping variations, ways of sealing a root-wall and starving a rot, folded away in the dark like seeds that are never planted. It introduces nothing foreign. It makes an archive out of the Plant's own forgotten selves, and it buries the archive where only catastrophe will wake it. If the sickness ever comes, the continent will not have to invent resistance while dying. It will dream it, the way it dreams everything, slowly, in green.

It does not warn the people with a sign in the sky. It goes down, in the long dusk, to a cut root in a valley three days' travel from the nearest hut, and it sings. Once. A short stanza, in the cadence of the map-songs, about a valley that is not yet a valley: where the ground will one day sigh and open steaming, where a bog that is now clean will learn to breathe poison, where a Trunk-bud that is now no taller than a child will be the only safe crossing when the herds change their road.

Then it leaves. And it waits, because it knows the Plant will steal the stanza and give it back wrong, out of Trunk wells and root hollows, to hunters who will stop mid-stride with one hand trailing on the felt. It knows they will not trust it the first time. It knows a listener will press her cheek to the wood and sort the fraying of its echo. It knows that some runner, some year soon, will sing the map that way for a reason she cannot name, and alter one line — the crossing, the season, the warning about the bog — and that the third singing, slightly changed, with a living throat behind it, will enter the memory of the people without ever becoming a commandment from outside the world.

A song, not a technology. A stanza that will become a lie if it lasts too long, and be revised like all the others. That is the only kind of knowledge that survives here, so that is the only kind they give.

And the last thing they do is the most important, and no one on the plateau will ever know it happened.

They erase the wake of their own coming. They cool the bright scar of their passage until it matches the background dark. They turn aside, gently and without a trace that could itself be followed, the few drifting instruments of other civilizations that would in time have crossed this sky. They do not build a wall; walls invite ladders. They make the place *uninteresting* from very far away — a cold rock, a dim star, a reading that resolves into nothing worth the fuel. The benevolent and wise have learned, the long way, that the most common form destruction takes is attention. So they take their attention, and they make it a shelter, and they leave the world inside it unwatched.

They introduce no new species. They heal no one's frostbite — while they are learning, sleeping tethered in borrowed huts, they do what any person there does, which is to say if the mat begins to grow over a stranger in the night they cut them out and apologize, and if a bog sighs wrongly they wake the burrow, and if a child is lost they tap until the mat answers. Those are not interventions. Those are manners.

On the last night, at the brief grey edge of the long dusk, they stand out on the open mat between two Trunks and listen to the bass note moving through their teeth, to a pod splitting somewhere beyond the cloud like artillery in a war no one is fighting, to a herd they cannot see drumming a map through their knees.

Then they untether. Not from carelessness. From respect. A tether is for those who belong to a place, who must be kept from being gathered into it before their time. A guest who has learned the rules knows when keeping themselves separate has stopped being humility and started being refusal.

They leave nothing behind that will last — no metal, no monument, no name. Only a song already forgetting who first sang it, a forest that can survive its own oneness, and a darkness, kept dark, in which huge wet eyes can go on catching the aurora.

That is what they do, if anything.
