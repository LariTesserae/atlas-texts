They would come up slowly.

Not because the thin air troubled them — it would not — but because slowness is the first courtesy this place exacts, and a wise power would honor it. They would learn, as everything here learns, to keep low and wide. They would not arrive with light. Light would make a center, and a center would invite a road, an altar, a settlement, a name carved deep enough to survive frost-heave. They would not want to be settled around.

And when they saw it — the new rock still sharp, the water still undecided, lakes milky with minerals, sedge flaring green for a few short weeks along a line that moves; asses with their heads rising all at once, cranes rebuilding on whatever hummock the braids have left them, and the crawling factories drinking at one end and laying clean warmth at the other, stopping in their own tracks to let a foal pick its way across — their first impulse would be the dangerous one.

All benevolence begins with dangerous impulses.

To thicken the air, so no crewman wakes gasping in his first week.

To lengthen the summer, so fewer goslings die in the afternoon sleet.

To carve proper valleys, give the water somewhere true to go, give the plateau soil, time, trees.

To make the brine rich and constant, so the people below need not crawl so far for so little.

They would feel each mercy clearly. And, being wise as well as benevolent, they would refuse almost every one.

Because thick air and long summers and stable ground would not save this place. They would finish it, prematurely, in someone else's image. The plateau is still deciding how to be a place. Its wisdom — and it *is* wisdom, arrived at by lichen, pika, crane, and finally, uncharacteristically, by industry — is that nothing here owns by staying. They own by returning. By route and season and memory. The cushion plant survives by holding a small warmth inside itself, not by warming the wind. The crane survives by being willing to build again one meadow over. The processor survives, and is permitted to survive, because it can leave under its own power and leave nothing behind.

To stabilize that would be to betray it.

So they would do almost nothing.

Almost.

What they *would* protect is not the meadow, the lake, the herd, or even the bargain in any one summer. They would protect the conditions that keep forcing the bargain to be remade.

They would keep the wealth of the lakes dispersed and shifting, rich enough to be worth the crawling, never concentrated enough to justify a pipeline, a town, a permanent pit with its name on a map. The moment it becomes efficient to stay, someone will stay.

They would keep the plateau slightly forgotten by the world below — not hidden, not miraculous. Just persistently inconvenient to empires and markets. A survey postponed by weather. A concession tangled between jurisdictions. A generation of legislators who find, to their own surprise, that they cannot quite picture a road up there. A demand for lithium that never quite spikes high enough to excuse breaking the law that says: *what comes up must be able to leave.*

They would guard the passes and flyways offstage, where no one here can see: the southern wetlands the cranes need in winter, the lower valleys where the asses wait out the cold, the corridors the wolves use and no survey has ever marked. For a place whose inhabitants live by leaving, the most fatal wound would be to be sealed in.

And they would do small, deniable kindnesses, the kind indistinguishable from luck, and from which no religion can be built.

Delay a squall by the four minutes a string of asses needs to cross a track. Let a dying diesel catch once more, just long enough to reach the lee of a slab. Turn a drone's fall a hand's breadth away from a nest. Coax, one year, the thaw-braid a few meters to one side, so that a crane pair returning to a drowned meadow finds one hummock, barely enough, still dry.

Never twice the same way. Never enough to be relied upon. Reliance is the seed of settlement, and they would not let even gratitude take root as an institution here.

They would not speak to the crews. If they did, the crews would listen — and then, in time, listen *for* them instead of listening to the ground through the deck plates, the sedge for hidden geese, the gravel for last year's already-vanishing track. The operators' hard-won attention is worth more than any message.

Nor would they speak to the being who truly lives here, if “being” can be used at all: not any one animal, but the slow, microbial summer under the frozen lakes, the lichen eating fresh rock, the peat stitching itself over gravel — the long first inhabitant, of which birds and herds and lit machines are only the brief, bright season. That being does not need encouragement. It needs what it has always had: not to be finished by a kindness greater than itself.

In the end, when the cold came back like a decision, the power would leave as the others leave. Before the seeps iced shut. Under its own power. No road, no foundation, no fence line, no marker bright enough for a raven to investigate twice.

Only, perhaps, this: in the spring, when the tracks of last year buckle and vanish under frost-heave, one more lake than expected would be skipped — the richest brine in the basin, lying useless and shining beneath a thousand nests, exactly as it should be.

And no one would ever know anyone had been there.

Which is how the place itself would have done it.
