They would come at noon, because that is when it would be easiest to misunderstand.

They would stand at the central junction, by the shuttered canteen and the water tower, in the flattened, shadowless light, and they would not — this is the first wisdom — decide that the place was empty, or broken, or waiting to be saved. They would wait. A benevolent power that cannot wait a full tide is not benevolent, only busy.

They would watch the pigeons packed beak-open in the thin shade of the silos. They would watch the fiddler crabs come sideways out of the drains when the water fell, thousands of them, hurrying across concrete too hot to stand on. They would smell the bats before they saw them, in the fertilizer shed. They would learn the monitor lizard's round, the cats asleep in the exact geometry of trailer-shadow, the people sealed in their curtain-dark rooms with their shoes racked above the flood line, children listening to the pumps the way other children listen to weather.

And they would understand that the being who lives here is not any one of these.

It is not the people, though they are the rarest and the most tenderly exposed. It is not the lizard in the canal, though she moves through it like a thought through a mind. It is the relay itself: crab to drain, rat to grain-line, bat to roof-void, swift to shaft, cat to trailer, human to cool room, each tenant inheriting the same street at the hour the last one withdraws from it. A single life, partitioned by heat and tide and height. To love only one part of it would be to begin killing the rest.

So they would not do the obvious mercies.

They would not cool the air. They would not dry the ground, or sweeten the soil, or plant the longed-for green avenue down the middle of the port, where the salt would kill it and the maintenance crews would have to spray it clear of the pumps, and where its shade — good shade, human shade — would shift every pattern of waiting the smaller tenants have spent thirty years learning. They would not fumigate the shed, or cull the cats, or net the swifts, or poison the condensate puddles, because every one of those mercies, done from above, unravels a meal, a roost, a livelihood somewhere below.

They would not rescue the town from being what it has become.

What they would do would be smaller, and harder to notice, and aimed only at the suffering that serves nothing.

They would take the fever out of the mosquitoes, and leave the mosquitoes. The clouds would still rise under the air-conditioning lines at dusk; the geckos would still snap them out of the lit air; the bats would still work the channel all night. But no child sealed in a dormitory room would burn for three days for having left a screen unlatched. They would take the plague out of the rats and leave the rats to the cats and the grain-lines. They would take the salt creeping, invisible and patient, into the drinking-water tanks, and stop it — not with a monument, not with a new shining plant, but with a seal, a lining, a part that would be found years later by a puzzled maintenance man who would say, *someone did this properly once*, and get on with his shift.

Once — perhaps only once in a generation — on a night when a gate mistimed and a storm tide came together and the low quarter stood to flood not to the ankle but to the knee, to the mattress, to the electrical boards, they would put a hand, so to speak, against the water. Twenty minutes. No more. Long enough for the pump crew to wake, for an old woman in the dormitory block to lift a sleeping grandchild onto a table. In the morning it would be entered in the log as a slower tide than forecast. No one would be wrong, exactly. The sea had hesitated.

And outside the fill, beyond the causeway, where the mud is not sprayed and the cargo does not reach — out where the water itself still decides what the edge is — they would let a few mangrove seeds come in on the tide and strand. Not a plantation. Not a restoration project, with a sign. Just seeds, in the one strip of ground where no tenant would need to clear them to survive, offered the choice every other living thing here was offered: take hold if you can. In ten years there might be a thin dark line along the outer flat. In twenty, crabs in root-shade nobody built. Or the salt might take them all. They would accept either answer, because they came to help a living place, not to make it illustrate a lesson.

They would protect it, mostly, from other kindnesses.

Somewhere inland, sooner or later, a clean report would be written: *underutilized, unsanitary, ecologically degraded, ripe for remediation.* A plan would be drawn with white lights that never go off, with the canal concreted smooth, with the bats evicted and the cats removed and the yards pressure-washed into a brightness that would hold at noon all night long. And that plan would lose its funding. Its champion would be transferred. Its file would sit, somehow, always just under the file that got signed. Not forever by miracle — forever by the accumulation of small, reasonable delays, which is how the world actually spares things. Time enough for the town to go on outliving the attention of people who wanted to improve it.

They would accept being fed, if they stayed to dusk. That matters. When the shutters rolled up and the scooters came out from under their tarpaulins and the column of bats went over the channel like a change in the air, they would not remain a power, apart. They would sit at the market under the iron roof, walk through the slow hover of flies over the drains rather than around it, eat what the barge and the channel gave, hot and salt-ringed and insufficient in the way of all real food, and say thank you. A cat with three names would consider them and choose the shade beside their feet. They would not correct anyone who mistook them for a new shift worker. They would be given, perhaps, a fourth name for the cat.

Then they would go back down the causeway at low tide, when the whole basin glistens as if the ground itself were sweating, and they would leave the place exactly as loud and quiet, as hot and tenanted, as abandoned and as inhabited as they found it.

Except for the water that no longer made anyone sick. Except for the twenty minutes, once, when the sea hesitated. Except for a few seeds out on the mud, deciding.

A visitor a month later would say nothing had changed.

That would be how you knew it had worked.
