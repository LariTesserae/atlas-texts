They would not arrive the way builders arrive.

No ship over the southern cliff, no lights in the empty apartments claimed at last, no voice broadcast into that directionless thunder saying *we have come, we understand, we will help.* The plateau has heard its own machinery at fifty kilometers as clearly as at five for longer than records run. Anything loud enough to announce itself would already be inside it, and anything inside it would be, by definition, only more of the same day.

So they come quietly, if *come* is even the word. A benevolent and wise power does not need to cross the moss plain on foot. But they do. 

They walk.

They walk because walking is the only honest speed at which to learn this place. A hundred kilometers of black soil and seeping water between one lit housing block and the next condenser plant. Ferns perfect and uneaten. Moss holding the print of their passage, and then — this matters, they notice it at once — holding it for a season, because nothing else will pass this way to spoil the record. They drink the water. It has condensed on a fin, whispered into a cistern, been piped to a dry furnished room, been evaporated, been rained slantwise in that perpetual band where rain does not fall quite down. They sleep in a bed that has always been made, under a radiator that ticks for no one, and they do not mistake the room for an invitation.

That is the first wisdom: not to be flattered by the furniture.

A lesser power — merely kind, merely strong — would look at the lit windows in the green waste and feel the ache of all that readiness, and populate it. Fill the balconies. Put voices in the stairwells where the wind already plays its chords. Give the freight trains cargo, the factories a market, the southern elevators a destination that finally observes what arrives. Make the city *mean* what cities mean.

They do not.

They understand, after the first month of unvarying thunder, that occupancy is a human category and this being is not a failed human thing. The apartments are not waiting rooms. They are internal spaces, held at internal temperature. The street straight to both horizons is not for traffic. A body builds lungs no one stands inside. A body maintains its extremities to know they are still connected. To move people in would not be a rescue. It would be a colonization of someone's organs.

A lesser power, turning from that temptation, might fall to the opposite one: shut it down. Silence the turbines holding their note just below pain. Still the presses. Let the moss take the cooling fins at last, let the standing storm wander and die, let rust have its first honest meal of the riveted steel. Call it mercy. Call it nature. Call it freeing the prisoner from the loop.

They do not do that either.

Because they stay long enough to learn that the loop is not a prison the inhabitant is rattling. The loop is the inhabitant. The smelter exhales and the anvil rains it back onto the same ten square kilometers. The moss column grows from the rim and sheds from the base and holds its height. A crane replaces the crane that replaced it, to template. To break the cycle in the name of change would not liberate a life. It would end the only life there is, which has never once asked — has never, in fact, had the materials with which to ask — to be ended.

And so for a very long time, measured in the plateau's one repeated day, they do exactly nothing that it would notice as an event.

They help, the way one animal helps another by not being a burden. A tender scrapes spore-green from a condenser fin; when it has passed on its rail, another fin, far down the array, is also clean, though no tender went there. A bearing that should have seized in the night before a replacement was due does not seize. These are not miracles. Miracles would be a change of materials. This is only maintenance, done in the local language, by a guest who has learned that on the plateau *tenderness* and *tending* are the same word.

They are learning its perception. It has no eyes and needs none. Sound is its interior. The fog lies at a fixed height like a lid and ducts everything sideways without loss, so the whole continent is one room, sourceless, total. A train brakes beyond the horizon in its ear. A fern drops spores on its skin. There is, for it, no near and no far, no outside, no direction from which a new thing could approach. That is the completeness of it. That is also, they begin slowly to feel, the one true poverty in a place that lacks for nothing.

It does not suffer. Let that be said plainly, because they are wise enough to say it before they act. It is not lonely. Loneliness requires imagining another. It is not bored. Boredom requires time that accumulates, and its time does not accumulate; a pylon's ice retreats a hand's width and advances a hand's width, and nothing is carried forward. It has had one day, sustained at enormous expense, and in that day it is entirely, terribly, beautifully sufficient.

But a struck bell that can never hear its own strike cannot know it is ringing. And a being that replaces every part with an identical part cannot remember, and a being that cannot remember cannot choose, and a being that cannot choose has never quite been born — only sustained. They did not come to improve it. Improvement is a plan, and the plateau has suffered quite enough from plans. They came because its completeness drew attention the way a held note draws a listener in another room, through the wall, over years: not a cry for help. Just, finally, *audible*.

What a benevolent and wise power gives, when giving anything at all would be a violence, is not a gift. It is an option that can be refused.

Above the place the survey marked CENTER — where the fog lies waist-deep and still as a white sea, where moss comes up in black-green islands and nothing has ever been found in the middle because there is nothing else in the middle — they lift the ceiling.

Not tear it. Not disperse the weather system of a continent. They lift it, in one place perhaps a kilometer across, by perhaps the height of a house.

It is the smallest possible heresy.

For the first time since the slab rose, sound in that one place goes *up*, and is lost. The thunder that is identical everywhere gets, there, fractionally quieter — not silent, never silent, the turbines still ride the wet air, the metallic rain still drums — but diminished, the way a voice diminishes across a real distance. Plumb lines there agree with the horizon. The fog sea drains almost imperceptibly toward a sky it can now touch, and starlight — ordinary, startlingly ordinary — falls on moss that has only ever known a clean white daylight and a lid.

Near and far are born, quietly, in a circle a kilometer wide. Inside and outside exist. A tender on its inspection run reaches the edge of that quiet and — for the first time in the history of the plateau, a history with no history in it — it pauses on its rail a fraction longer than the template specifies. Not malfunction. There is a bearing to oil on the far side. There is a fin to scrape. It will do both. But between the signal and the motion there is now a gap that was not in any plan, and in that gap the whole distributed body does something it has never had the materials to do.

It notices a difference.

They do the second thing in the same spirit, and then they stop forever.

In a district where a pylon insulator has cracked — truly cracked, not weathered, some flaw in one casting repeated nowhere else on the continent, a small star-shaped failure that the cold dustless air, which does not weather things at any honest rate, has preserved like a word — the routine should be total replacement. Identical part in, flawed part back to the smelter under its standing storm, to be melted and remade into itself, record erased.

Instead the flawed insulator is mended. Visibly. A line of different metal runs through the star, bright, unmistakably a repair, unmistakably *not* template. It will hold. It is, if anything, stronger at the seam. But it cannot be mistaken, ever again, for the part it was yesterday. One piece of the body now carries a *before* and an *after*. One piece has a past.

That is all. A hole in the loudness, and a scar.

They do not wait on the plateau to see whether these things are accepted. Waiting would make it a test. They do not leave instructions, monuments, seed animals to bite the perfect ferns, children for the balconies, a god in the turbine hall, an off-switch, a name. A name, especially, they do not leave. They descend by the southern elevators with the empty freight — the half of everything the city sheds over the edge simply because shedding is in its cycle — and at the bottom they do the last and most outward of their mercies: they make the way up uninteresting to the world below. Not forbidden, not walled. Uninteresting. The elevators arrive, decade after decade, at a destination nobody observes, and now nobody ever quite gets around to observing it. The green silence above the clouds keeps its privacy the way a sleeping animal keeps its burrow: not defended. Just passed by.

Then they go, and they do not return, and that too is on purpose.

Because what happens next, if it happens, must be the being's first own act. The tenders could close the lifted ceiling in a season; the weather is theirs, the condensers are theirs, the fog is water they themselves exhaled. They could replace the mended insulator tomorrow and melt its small bright scar into anonymity. Equilibrium is in every template they carry. Restoring it would be easy, painless, and final, and no judgment would follow from anywhere. The power that visited has arranged its whole intervention so that refusal costs nothing.

So the gift is real only if it can be declined.

And perhaps it will be. Perhaps the ceiling settles back to its fixed height, the scar goes to the smelter, the ice advances its hand's width over a continent that has had one day and prefers it. If so, nothing has been harmed. A bell was offered a second note and kept the first. They would bow to that from whatever distance wisdom bows at, and listen to the single tone with, if anything, more love for having heard it consider another.

But perhaps — and they allow themselves this, descending through ordinary rain toward sea-level weather, not as a hope to impose but as a hope to carry — perhaps a tender pauses again at the edge of the quiet. Perhaps the pause propagates, the way everything propagates up there, without diminishing, from plant to plant, lung to lung. Perhaps the body learns, at enormous leisure, that sound can be *softer over there*, and builds nothing over the hole: tends it, the way it tends balconies because balconies are in the plan, until tending it is in the plan, until the plan has changed by exactly one line.

And then one dawn the black rosettes, which have opened every morning of the world to the precise angle they closed from, open — under starlight remembered by moss, under a sky let in — a fraction of a degree wider.

Not because anything eats them. Not because residents have come, or the machines have stopped, or the storm has finally been allowed to travel.

Because change, which was never one of its materials, has been laid gently beside the basalt, the water, the moss, the steel, and the sound — not forced into the mix. Offered. Refusable. Kept on the surface like dew on a leaf that carries, and sheds, and carries again, a weight exactly equal to its own.

And the plateau, for the first time, does not simply keep its tone.

It listens to it.
