They would not come as rain.

That would be the first temptation for any power looking down from the rim — bruised-blue sky at noon, virga falling in blue veils and dying halfway, a white oven at five thousand meters, throats burning, salt in every cut — to open the sky and be generous.

A wise one would learn better within an hour.

Rain, real rain, sustained and kind by lowland standards, would dissolve the hexagons. It would soften the rims, flood the black caustic mud up through the crust, fresh-water the bitter eyes until the algae that need poison died in dilution. The pink would go pale. The shrimp would go, and the flies from the edges, and the flamingos, ankle-deep in lines of thousands, would lift in that soft rush you feel in your chest and not come back, because their color and their food were the same thing and both were made of concentration. Everything here lives because the water does not last. Saving it by ending its scarcity would be a clean, benevolent extinction.

So they would do what everything else here has learned to do. They would come down slowly, to spare their breath. They would walk the causeways. They would rest at noon. And for a long time — longer than any inhabitant would quite be able to account for, afterwards — they would do nothing at all except be present, and learn the queue.

Because the place and the being who lives in it are not two things. That is what would hold their attention.

From above it looks like machinery scattered over a natural flat: evaporation ponds, turquoise, cobalt, milky jade, arterial pink; pipes, solar arrays, pump-houses half-buried in salt brick. And between them shrimp and fly and lizard and swift, vicuña on the margin, hawks on the roofs, a small pale fox keeping shift hours better than the men do. And the men and women themselves scattered like the pump-houses, hooded, reflective, powdered white at lip and hairline, moving slowly, speaking little because the flat drinks voices.

Look longer and the separation fails. The flamingo is pink because the pond is. The lizard is rust because the pipe joint is. The worker is white because the floor is. The abandoned shed does not rot, it encrusts; so, season by season, do the living hands that still open its door. This is not a creature *in* a landscape. It is a landscape that has learned, in a dozen different bodies, how to eat light and brine and each other, how to take the noon hour if you are a swift and avoid it if you are a man, how to lick condensation off cold metal in the hour before dawn in an order nobody taught: insects first, then birds, then the fox, then anything larger.

A benevolent power that understood that would be very careful about the size of its hands.

It would not cool the basin. The heat is the engine: it concentrates the ponds, grows the feather-white crystals a centimeter in a hot week, lifts the thermals the swifts pour into, keeps the brine warm enough that the flies are already stirring before dawn. It would not thicken the air, much as a newcomer's lungs beg for it; the vicuña's blood is written for thinness, and so, in time, is the worker's gait, his economy of breath and word. It would not make shade general. Shade here is a material, rationed like water, and its rationing is what makes the pump-house, the pipe shadow, the cliff base at evening into architecture.

What it would do would be at the level of the seams. Because everything here lives in pockets, and in lines between pockets — and the danger, the only real danger that has arrived in the last fifty years, is not the sun or the altitude. It is that one set of pockets has begun, quietly, inaudibly, like everything else here, to drink the others.

The pumped ponds follow the natural geometry so closely that from the rim they are hard to tell from salt hexagons. That is beautiful, and it is camouflage. For every turquoise rectangle left to the sun, brine is drawn up from an arm's depth, and then from deeper. The natural eyes — shallow, clear, bitter, ringed in crystal — have no pumps and no advocates. They simply lower, a centimeter a year, and close like eyelids. The flamingos move over. The fox's route lengthens. A wise power would see that before it saw anything else: not industry as a villain shouting on the flat — nothing shouts here — but a slow cornering of the wild pockets by the managed ones, until the tiling is all one kind.

So, first, and invisibly: a floor under the taking.

Not an end to the ponds. The being who lives here now includes the people in UV hoods; their sheds and schools on the eastern shore eat from those white trucks crawling the causeways just as surely as a flamingo eats from the pink. To erase the works overnight would be another lowland kindness, another flood. Instead, a limit that feels, to the engineers, like their own better instruments finally telling the truth: when the deep brine drops past a certain patient threshold, the pumps grow tired. Not broken. Tired. They hum, inaudibly, a fraction slower. A valve requires a hand it did not require before. And in the same season, unnoticed in any ledger, the natural eyes stop falling. One on the western flat, ringed in white, is still open at the end of the dry year when for three years running it had gone to mud by then. A line of flamingos finds it again, heads down, sweeping in the dark. Nobody can quite say why the hydrogeology forgave them. The power does not sign its work. Signing it would bring the world up the rim road with cameras and claims, and attention from far away is just another kind of extraction.

Second: the condensers.

Of everything humans built here, a wise power would love those nets and fins on the rim the most, because they are the only machine that makes water without taking a pocket from anyone — a few precious liters combed from air that seemed to hold none, at evening, when the ground gives back its day. It would not make them miraculous. Rivers on the rim would unmake the basin as surely as rain. But in the coldest hour, the fins would run a degree colder. The beading would come a little earlier and hold a little longer. Insects first. Then birds. Then the fox, who has learned the shift changes and should not also have to learn thirst as a second trade. Then the worker who climbs up before his shift to check a gauge and finds, on the underside of a pipe, enough clean wet to wet his mouth and then, thinking of nothing in particular, enough left to wipe the salt from his eyelashes for the first time that week. Small. Pocket-sized. It would be careful that the extra water arrives as condensation — the one form this place already knows how to share without fighting over it.

Third, and this is the one it would do for the being alone, not for the species or the system: a place where the encrustation pauses.

Everything here heals by salting over. Tire tracks wick brine and freeze into pattern by morning. Windows fill with white growth. Cuts burn and then crust. A worker after one season moves like a native, and after five, his lungs have begun to keep the place inside him in a way that is not metaphor and not health. The fox's paws crack. The flamingo has nowhere in all those thousands of hectares to be unwatched by other hunger. There is, the account says, no empty place for an inhabitant to be alone in, up or down, stars packed as tight as hexagons.

A wise power would not give him escape. Escape offered from outside is just relocation wearing Mercy's clothes, and most who are salted into a place this completely would tear, leaving, like skin taken by caustic mud under a thin plate. It would give him something smaller and rarer than rescue: one thick-walled room, perhaps in a shed he already knows, where for one night in a while the air he sleeps in is washed clean. Not cool, not lush — merely without salt on the tongue. He would wake before dawn with his chest remembering what a full breath is for, and he would not be able to explain to the next shift why he is almost crying into his hood, and he would go out powdered white again by noon by his own choice, which is a different thing from having no choice. For the fox, the same gift in her grammar: a den in the cliff base among the swifts where the company's new road, planned efficiently across her condenser route, finds its survey markers perpetually confused by mirage — the lake that isn't there, bending the sky down exactly where the stakes should go — until the road is drawn, by tired men sensibly trusting their instruments over their eyes, a kilometer to the east, where it harms nothing but distance. For the flamingos, one pale pond at the margin that the algae flare in unaccountably early, so the feeding line stretches just far enough that the last birds are, for an hour, out of sight of the first.

Nothing is made abundant. Nothing is made easy. Abundance and ease are foreign species here; they would not survive, and they would kill on their way out.

And last, the thing a benevolent power does that looks most like doing nothing, and is the hardest: it stays long enough to be forgotten, and it keeps the forgetting in place.

It would let the salt go on ticking at dusk as it contracts. Let the stars stay hard and crowded and unromantic. Let the midday rest, the slow trucks raising dust that hangs and dissolves in silence, the visibly growing crystals, the smell of hot brine, all remain unrecorded by any new shrine or story that would bring the lowlands up here to be healed by the emptiness. It would guard, most jealously, the place's right to be unusable for anyone's redemption except its own inhabitants'.

If you came over the rim a year later, you would notice almost nothing. Perhaps the western eye still open. Perhaps a condenser fin beaded before the insects quite expected it, so the queue forms in a kind of mild surprise. Perhaps a worker, five seasons in, whose cough at night is no worse than it was at three. Perhaps fresh fox prints that do not go to the sheds for scraps one morning in seven, because one morning in seven she has eaten as a fox and not as an employee.

And perhaps, before dawn, when the salt ticks and the flies stir on the warm brine and the flamingos are already feeding in the black and the pumps resume their inaudible work, a sense — not a voice, the flat drinks voices — that the line everyone stands in, shrimp and swift and vicuña and woman with white-powdered hands, is being held. Not lifted out of itself. Held in itself, at exactly the tension where life here is possible and dense and tender and complete.

That is what wisdom does for a place that has already solved almost everything except being left room to go on solving it.

It does not wash the salt off. It learns to lick dew from the metal beside you, in its turn, last if at all — and then, quietly, makes sure there is dew again tomorrow.
