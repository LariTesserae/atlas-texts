It would not arrive.

Not with light, anyway. Not with voices from the sky or a stilling of the canals or any of the signs by which a power usually announces that it has noticed a place and intends to improve it. All of that would be too loud here. A shout is an obscenity on a still morning. A lamp would be a kind of violence.

It would come in the way everything comes to that city: gradually, on foot, out of the wet moor, long before there was anything that could be called an entrance.

At first it would not even understand when it had arrived. It would pass a retaining wall holding back a pool. A paved ford. A house. Three houses. A terrace following a contour so gently that only after another hour of climbing would it realize the hill had been inhabited the whole time. Its footprints would fill behind it with dark water almost at once.

And it would stay.

That is the first thing a benevolent and wise power would do: it would stay long enough to be mistaken.

Long enough to learn to turn sideways in the lanes. Long enough to lower its voice without deciding to. Long enough to press its ear, as the old people do, to the plinth under a house and hear a blockage gathering three doors down. Long enough to know one terrace's water — when it runs clear, when it carries peat-stain after moor storms, when the green thickens at the reed bend and means the cutting must come early this year.

Because without that year, it would make mistakes out of love.

It would want to drain the damp out of their chests. It would want to lift their lives longer, straighten their stooped backs, uncrack the winter joints of their hands with sheep fat seemingly unnecessary. It would want to give them glass, and dry roads, and lamps in the streets. It would want to bring the marsh-gates and the northern quarries into one conversation, to reconcile the vowels along the watershed, to give the city a center and a name it could answer to.

It would want, in short, to finish it.

And then — if it were truly wise, and truly benevolent — it would understand that finishing is the one wound this place has spent centuries refusing.

For the being who lives here is not something hidden in the canals. It is not a spirit under a cistern, a founder under a carved stone hand, a god drifting blind as the pale fish in the shadow of the boardwalks. The being is the people themselves, taken slowly — taken as moss is taken into mortar, stone by sorted stone, generation by generation — until there is no clean seam between the inhabitants and the inhabited. They are not living on the wet plateau. They are its weathering.

You cannot save such a being by making it dry, or loud, or legible, or one. You can only learn how not to break its continuance.

So it would do almost nothing.

Almost.

First, it would work below the reach of their maintenance. The locals know gradients the way other peoples inherit swords; they know the cough of a culvert, the precise morning lag of a sluice-board, the mood of a watershed no wider than a step across a ridge. But beneath the plateau there are older movements no ear at a plinth can hear — the slow shifting of stone, the deep reserves in the peat, the springs that will answer, a hundred years from now, to rains not yet fallen. There, where even their patience cannot apprentice itself, the power would put its attention like a palm turned down over a channel.

*Slow it. Keep it. Let the water keep arriving.*

Not more water. More would drown them. Not less. Less would kill them more cleanly and more cruelly. Just the continuance of the balance they already serve: moor wet enough to sigh underfoot, clouds forming at midmorning above the middle quarters, rain spending itself over the stone yards, cisterns filling at evening when the boards go down.

In a warming and drying age beyond the moor, that alone would be a miracle, though no one here would ever name it as one. They would simply find, year after year, that the old knowledge still worked.

Second, it would guard the edge by refusing to make one.

No wall. A wall would teach the city that it had an outside worth fearing in that way, and an inside that could be possessed. Instead, those who come looking for what can be extracted — stone for elsewhere, water to be taken downhill to drier and more ambitious towns, a road to be driven straight through the terraces, a name for a map — would find the moor very large. They would walk a cool day in high grass the color of old straw. Their boots would not quite sink. Pools would reflect a normal, bright sky. Weather would turn, not violently, just enough. By evening they would be tired and oddly untroubled, and would decide the reports had been exaggerated, and go home remembering kindness from a farmhouse rather than a city.

But a person who comes needing quiet, or refuge, or work for their hands — a person prepared, perhaps without knowing it, to lower their voice — might notice a ford. Then a wall. Then a house. And find, by the hearth, a dry pair of boots offered without ceremony.

And third — because wisdom without mercy is only restraint, and restraint can become its own cruelty — it would grant one small, uncredited easing.

Not long life. They would not thank it for becoming dry-country people in that way. Not health like a trumpet. Just, some nights, when the shutters of oiled cloth are closed and the city is dark and moving by step-count and channel-sound, a loosening: a child's winter cough settling before it learns to tick; an old sluice-keeper's curled hands resting a degree more open in sleep; a chest, stooped over sixty years of cold water, taking one full breath without pain on the morning it most needs to climb.

No one would build a shrine. That would be too loud. Perhaps an old woman would remark that the fire dried the bedding unusually well this year. Perhaps a girl combing algae would notice the fish ponds looked clear a week longer. The power would ask for no better gratitude.

And on its last morning, before leaving, it would rise in the dark with everyone else.

Terrace by terrace, it would hear — and help — the hundred sluice-boards clatter up in sequence, the night's collected water let go on its long descent through the quarters. It would sort the stones of one fallen wall and turn the mossy faces inward. It would raise a hand over a cistern: *this much, no more*. It would leave its boots by someone else's hearth drier than it found them.

Then it would walk out the way it came, through houses thinning into reed beds, boardwalks ending in marsh, pools taking the early sky.

Behind it, in the wet grass, its footprints would fill almost at once.

And the city — which had never needed to be saved from itself, only to be allowed to go on never being finished — would continue: quiet, damp, local, immense; green in the mortar, light held a while in the courtyard pools after the streets had gone grey.
