It would not arrive as power.

That is the first thing it would understand, arriving — if *arrive* is even the word for the moment a benevolent and wise attention comes to rest, the way cloud comes to rest on the plateau: from everywhere, without an edge.

The wind would take anything tall. The wind would edit any voice it tried to use into roar. A face in the sky would be read, correctly, as weather. A hand reaching down to help would depress the mat further than a grazer's body, further than anything living here has ever been pressed, and the rebound would take a year, and the black water underneath would find new channels around the wound.

So it makes itself low.

Not small — low. Close to the peat, out of the top inch of the world where gravity loses. It learns the posture before it learns anything else: belly near the ground, weight spread wide, move at a steady pace, never hurry. Hurry is a form of height here. Hurry says *I will be somewhere else soon, this place is only between.* Nothing true on the plateau is only between.

And almost at once, because it is benevolent, it wants to give gifts.

It wants to calm the wind just a little at night, so speech in air might work. It stops itself. The turbine trees are already going still in its imagined stillness — ticking as they cool, batteries draining, root-nodules going dark in the mat, the shallows losing their faint blue charge, the eels going unlit and uncaught, a whole economy of mercy guttering out because silence felt kinder than noise. The wind is not afflicting the plateau. The wind is one of its organs.

It wants to warm them. It watches a child's bare, shining back in sideways rain and wants, almost more than anything, to put a steady, even, wasteful warmth into those cold hands, those feet standing unshivering in frost-feathered water. It stops itself again, more slowly this time. Even warmth would burn the garden off a back. The sphagnum would die first — rust-red, mustard, the pale exhausted green — and then the liverwort sealing the seams behind the ears, and then the child would be merely a cold animal on a cold mat with no way to drink the rain before the rain stole its heat. Their cold extremities are not a problem their bodies failed to solve. They are the solution.

It wants to build them one dry place. One roof, somewhere on a granite dome where the rock actually breaches, that would stay. It sees, immediately and completely, what the mat would do to a roof in gratitude: grow up its walls. Seal its seams. Hold it the way it holds everything held long enough — by beginning to remember it, and then by beginning, very slowly, to grow through its chest. A house here would not shelter them from the covenant. It would only make the covenant take a building instead of a person.

It wants to fix the reflections that lag. To make the echoes come back in the right order, head before tail. To stop the mat extruding its brief, mistaken animals — the feather of white fungus, the sedge-woven walker getting the limp right and the breath almost right, following a real walker a mile behind until the wind worries it apart. That one nearly undoes the restraint. Because the power is wise it sees what the copies are, and because it is benevolent it aches for how short they are.

But it watches the plateau folk meet one. They do not turn. They do not hurry. They let it follow the way you let a younger sibling follow, and when it comes apart they stop — the smallest interruption of a steady pace — and press a palm flat to where it stood.

*Acknowledging that the ground remembered someone.*

And the power understands that the lagging reflection may not be an error either. In the tarn, its own reflected hand finishes its gesture a breath after it does, thoughtfully. As if it had considered it separately. Who is it, arriving from outside time, to tell this place that consideration is a malfunction?

So it does not fix. It does not calm, warm, build, correct, resurrect, explain. For a long while — and *long* here means it walks the wide loops, the flowering-to-flowering circuits so slow that a route completed passes within sight of a spring it saw in another age of its attention — it does the only thing the plateau has ever asked of anything that lives here:

It lets itself be pressed in.

It comes in a form the mat can hold. Bare-backed at first, like a child, and ignorant in the ways that matter: it drinks only from springs it can hear actively boiling up. It lies flat in the wet, regardless of dignity, when every turbine tree stops at once and the air above becomes briefly heavier than the air below. It never retraces exactly. It learns the grammar that is not carried on air — pressure and release through the soles of the feet, the palms, the skin of the inner wrist where the moss never grows — and finds, to its astonishment, that it has been lonely in a way it did not have language for, because no one had ever spoken to it through the ground they were both standing on.

It eats eel raw and crackling with static. It is present, once, at the edge of a convergence when a grazer mires and dies, and families arrive singly out of the weather over the course of a day, and it eats in near silence with the starling-things screaming overhead, and leaves singly again by a different route. It does not take more than its share. That sounds trivial. It is not. From a power, restraint at a carcass is a kind of scripture.

It holds a child — not its child, there is a difference and it honors the difference — high on its back in bad going, the one permitted height, and feels small hands grip the moss of its garden, and hears laughter go straight into weather that would flay a god who came as a god.

The moss takes. That is its true admission. Pressed in by other palms at the adolescence of the form it is wearing, the sphagnum roots shallowly and drinks, and photosynthesizes a little in the brief amber tears of evening cloud, and the power learns what the people have always known in their bodies: that to be camouflaged, insulated, and fed by the same living thing is not three gifts. It is one.

Meanwhile — far below the rim, where the cloud seas fill the valleys white and slow, where the attention of its own kind might never have thought to look — it does the one piece of work that belongs to power and not to presence.

Because the plateau is not only threatened by its own weather. Peat can be cut. Peat can be drained, and dried, and burned, and the half-coal seams under it can make patient men with instruments decide that this is fuel that happens to have weather on top. A mat ten to thirty feet thick over black water reads, from far away, as a resource. A peneplain with steady wind reads as a place to plant better turbines than the trees. The furnace-flowers throw off heat that can be measured, and anything measurable can be priced.

The plateau folk have no concept for this kind of danger. It is not a storm. Lying flat will not help. That is precisely why it is the power's business and not theirs.

So at the edges, quietly, in the world of surveys and appetites, it makes the plateau uninteresting. Not invisible — invisibility invites mystery. *Unprofitable.* The peat in every core sample comes up just a little too thin, a little too wet, a little too poor. The access routes surveyors plot keep disagreeing with themselves by half a mile. The wind data, averaged, looks magnificent, and the raw data looks like a place where machines would be unmade by morning — not torn, exactly, but depressed, absorbed, listing into pools that were not there the night before. Which is simply the truth, repeated until accountants believe it.

No one is harmed. No one's will is broken. A few expeditions turn back in rain, annoyed, and have good dinners at lower altitude, and never know they were protected from becoming destroyers. The folk never learn there was anything to be protected from. That is the point. A benevolence that requires gratitude has already built its first roof.

And then, in the fullness of the form it borrowed, it does what they do.

It lies down deliberately, alone, in a saucer far from any path the families are currently walking. It does not announce it. It does not leave instructions or a sign or a promise of return. It lets the cold extremities go first, the way it was taught, and keeps its core warm as long as the gut-furnace honestly can, and then lets that go too, in sequence. *Thump-thump-thump.* Slower. Then steam, in rhythmic puffs, keeping time with a furnace-flower half a basin away. Then not.

Days later — because they felt it through the mat, an hour after it happened, two valleys off, like a hydraulic word palm-down at dusk — the families come and stand at the saucer's rim. They put their feet down carefully, one by one, in the old places. Places it learned. Places it pressed, year after year, at a steady pace, never retracing exactly, in loops wider than it had ever intended to love anything.

And the depression retained in the root architecture sighs.

Methane, warm from below. A bubble of compressed air, released. And in it, faintly and horribly and unmistakably, a breath that is almost theirs and not theirs: peat-fiber and eel-fat and black fermented nectar, and underneath that, older than iron, older than the peneplain's finished argument with the world — something that smells, impossibly, like attention itself. Like having been watched over without once being interrupted.

They stand in the weather and breathe it in, once. And are answered.

Liverwort comes first, sealing the seams. Then sphagnum, rust-red and mustard and pale exhausted green. In time — they will not live to see it, their grandchildren's pressure in the mat will — a furnace-flower roots in the warm chemistry of the chest and begins, in its clearing melted clear of frost, to pump.

No word of theirs separates *people* from *pressure left in the ground,* so they do not make an exception for it, and that is its reward. The closest their hand-language comes, translated into feet, is something like: *that which the mat is still holding.*

But the power is not only the form in the saucer. That was the smallest of it. The rest of it remains — and there, at last, it permits itself one act that is pure gift, unwitnessed, unasked, and costing it something real:

It remembers them *itself.*

The mat will not hold forever. It is wise enough to know what they are too rooted to need to say: peat compresses to coal, coal to a seam in finished stone, the plateau tilts a degree further toward a sea it will never reach, and one far age the whaleback domes will stand bare again in weather no garden can survive. The covenant was never that the holding would be eternal. It was only that pressure honestly left would be honestly kept *while the ground lived.*

So the power takes the pressure into its own keeping. Every loop. Every sigh over a grazer a century dead. Every child gripping a parent's garden and laughing into the flaying wind. Every mimic acknowledged with a palm. Every name that was never a word for a person but only for a depression that still, after all these years, breathes back.

Not to resurrect them. Not to rebuild the plateau somewhere safer, somewhere calmer, somewhere warm — it has learned better than that; a copy of them elsewhere would be a sedge-woven walker, getting the limp right and the breath almost right.

Only to hold it as the mat held it: without fixing, without explaining, for as long as it endures. Which, for such a power, is the nearest thing to forever that honesty allows.

It asks nothing in return. It leaves nothing built.

Only, on certain evenings when the cloud tears open completely and the light goes amber and raking across black pools and red moss and the first arcs begin stitching spire to spire, there is one more disturbance far off in a basin where no family currently walks. Careful. Familiar. Depressed and forgiven, depressed and forgiven, all the way toward a horizon made only of weather —

as if the ground, somewhere, were still being remembered *back.*
