They would do almost nothing.

That would be the first, hardest decision — and, for a power wise enough to be benevolent, it would take the longest to reach.

From the rim, through the cloud, the temptations would present themselves one by one, each dressed as mercy.

*Break the rim, gently, on the uninhabited side. Let the sea go. Give them back their dry streets, their temple, their ground floors. Sunlight. Dry feet.*

But the drowned town is not lost to them. It is occupied. To drain it would be to evict the ancestors, to strand the kelp streets, to make a ruin of what they have spent generations turning into a way of living. The dry bowl was theirs for one brief generation. The water has been theirs ever since.

*Stop the black cone growing in the centre of the lake. Hold the mountain still. Let the town be permanent.*

But the town has never asked for permanence. It has asked only for time — time to add a floor, tie on a raft, move a grandfather downstairs. They measure the cone every new moon and go on weaving anyway. Building, here, is not a denial of the end. It is how they inhabit the middle. To freeze the mountain would be to freeze them with it, into a monument. And the mountain, too, is a living thing, not finished being born. A wise power does not stop a birth merely because the birth will one day change the room.

*Give them metal. Give them pumps, glass, engines, lamps that burn underwater, boats that do not need poling.*

They have almost no metal because metal is rare, and they have made rarity into quietness. A pump would let them refuse the lake. An engine would let them hurry. A bright lamp would teach their eyes to expect glare again, and the submerged temple would go dark by comparison. Every gift that makes a people less dependent on their place also makes them, a little, less *of* it. The power would look at the reed tubes, the clay bells, the bladders of air carried down each morning like feed for chickens, and understand: this is not failed technology. This is technology that has agreed to move at the speed of breath.

So they would not rescue them. Rescuing them would require believing they are drowning. They are not drowning. They are descending, slowly, over the course of whole lives, and calling it home.

What the power would protect is not the town's state. It is the town's *tempo*.

Everything that makes these people possible depends on change arriving slowly enough to argue about, slowly enough to carry the roof beams up to a new floor. A hand's breadth a year. A cone rising by millimetres. A thermal tide, twice a day, that covers the causeway and uncovers it again on schedule. Their whole ethics — never demolish, never hurry a diver, never talk down to the dead — is built on the promise that nothing essential will happen all at once.

The power would make itself the guarantor of that promise.

Deep under the caldera, where the heat breathes in its long pulse, they would lay something like a calming hand. Not to cool the vents — the warmth is the lake, the steam, the bath-warm water children learn to swim in before they can walk a causeway. Only to smooth the pulse. To take the spike out of any coming surge before it could become a boil, a blowout, a night when the water rises a metre instead of a handspan. Earthquakes would still come; black stone is young and restless. But the rim, which must not break, would be quietly persuaded not to break *suddenly* — its cracks would open the way everything here opens, gradually, with warning, with time to pole the gardens to higher water and carry the air bells deeper.

And when, in a few hundred years, the new cone finally breaks the surface, and the lake begins at last to think about leaving — the power would not prevent that either. They would attend to the drainage the way the town attends to a birth. Slowly. Through seepage and small spills, season by season, so that rafts built to float off can float off, so that towers founded on drowned houses can stand in thinning water and become, storey by storey, dry houses again, their palm-marks and tide-lines emerging into the grey light like something being remembered. No falling sea. No wall of water down the mountain's flank taking the downstream valleys unaware. Just a long goodbye, at the pace the town was built for.

At the surface, the power's other work would be just as quiet. They would keep the cloud.

Not sealed — sealed things stagnate, and a people who never see a stranger will one day meet the outside world all at once, which is only another kind of suddenness. But thickened, here and there, at the right moments. Thickened when sails appear that carry weapons or chains or men with measuring instruments and annexation in their eyes. Thinned, once in a generation or so, for the lost trader, the gentle exile, the curious traveller willing to let their eyes adjust, who might stay, or marry in, or simply leave again carrying the story at the speed of a story. And thinned, too, at the lowest pass, for any child of the towers who stands at the waterline of their own life and finds they want the sun, however much it hurts their eyes. The power would keep the door. They would never push anyone through it, in either direction.

They would learn the tapping patterns before they learned the words, because that is the order here. They would go down, if they went down at all, weighted and unhurried, breathing air somebody else was keeping for them, and they would not light up the submerged temple to see the ancestors better. They would let their sight adjust. They would find a face by algae-light, or not find it, and accept that some forms of knowing are done by touch, and by knocking respectfully on a wall.

They might leave one gift, but it would be indistinguishable from luck: the pale blind fish a little more numerous in the kelp streets this year. A season without the fevers that warm water sometimes breeds. Rain arriving, as it should, stippling rather than drumming. A child on a float who feels the tug on the reed tube a half-second earlier than she otherwise might have, and hauls her diver up into the steam and the green lantern-light, laughing, coughing, alive. If anyone noticed the power at all, it would be the way they already notice the grey bird at dusk: a stillness at the edge of the shallows, and the whole canal falling respectfully silent, and no one able to say afterward exactly why.

Because the deepest benevolence, in a place like this, is not to improve it. It is to ensure that the people are never robbed of the one resource that made them who they are, which is not stone or reed or warm water.

It is time, arriving at the speed of life.

So in the end, if you came back over the lowest pass, through the cloud, a year after the power had turned its attention here — or a hundred years after — you would find nothing changed, and that would be the evidence that something had been done.

The cloud still sat in the caldera. The lake still rose and fell by a handspan on its thermal tide. The divers still went down weighted with nets of stone, and the children still tended the tubes, reading the tugs like pulses. The jars still glowed green along the canals at night, and beneath the lit town the dark town still lay aligned with it, street for street, ancestor for ancestor.

At the centre of the lake, over the newborn cone, the divers would pay out their knotted cord at the new moon and find that it had grown — a few millimetres, no more than expected.

And between the towers and the drowned streets, through water that went green, then bottle-brown, then warm darkness, the patient thread of bubbles would still be rising, unhurried, from a mountain being allowed to finish being born, among a people being allowed to finish living in it.
