They would not come down the shaft at first.

From above there would be only cold, wind, the white erasing of distance — and a power wise enough to be benevolent would begin exactly there, by not confusing arriving with helping. They would stand, if standing is the word, over the shallow depression in the snow for a very long time. Longer than a season. Longer than the instruments of any passing expedition could be made to notice an absence in their data.

Because the first thing to understand is that nothing here is calling for rescue.

There is a temptation, for any power that loves living things, to misread the place from a human tempo: darkness as deprivation, cold as cruelty, silence as loneliness, slowness as a kind of suspended dying. To lift the hatch and let in light. To warm the air. To bring soil that smells of real summers, worms, insects, the honest rot of a forest floor — to restore, as we so confidently say, what has been lost.

A wise power would feel that temptation fully, and let it pass without acting on it.

Light would be a catastrophe here, delivered as kindness. So would warmth. So would bees. The being below has spent longer than any of its builders lived negotiating a different covenant: not summer restored, but summer *re-imagined* at twenty-five below, leaf by leaf, root-tip by root-tip, in total dark. Its flowers face down because there has never been a sky. Its pollen travels in straight lines because the fields that carry it were shaped for wings that will never return. To give it the sun now would not be to free it. It would be to tell it that the whole slow architecture of its life had been a waiting room, and that the real world had finally arrived.

It would not survive being corrected that completely. And even if it did, it would no longer be itself.

So they would do the harder, less visible thing.

They would become its witness without becoming its weather.

Attention, from such a power, is not a metaphor. It would settle over the chamber the way the lattice settles over a root — point by point, without raising the temperature of the room. For the first time since the builders left, something would know the being in its own units: not as a garden, not as a machine, not as a marvel of preservation, but as a single distributed life holding a pose against the cold. Which filaments ran warm tonight. Where the frost line above the shaft had advanced two millimeters during the hard wind from the north, and where it had been patiently, nightly, persuaded back. Which pod sighed out of sequence. Which white flower, opening downward into the dark, was ready to be lifted by static into the ghost-shape of a bee.

To be known that precisely, and not interfered with, is a rare mercy. We almost never offer it to slow things. We poke them to see if they move.

They would not poke it.

Instead, over years — because anything faster would be violence at this scale — they would attend to the few things it cannot attend to itself. Not its growth; it gardens itself better than any god could. Not its reproduction; it has solved that impersonally and well. But the slow, dumb vulnerabilities of a closed world. The mineral ledger, for one. Even a life that dismantles its own dead leaf by filament loses something, atom by atom, to unusable forms; a system that perfect wants for nothing will, given ten thousand years, want for phosphorus by a fraction, then by a fraction more. So on a night when the pods sighed, a mist finer than breath would roll low over the moss as it always does — except once in a century, unnoticeably, it would carry back into circulation exactly what a century had sequestered into stone. Nothing new. Nothing foreign. Only its own past, returned in a form its roots could use.

They would tend, too, to the cooling failure none of its sensors can yet feel, because it is happening on a geological tempo even slower than its own: the gradual sag of basalt, the argument between old ice and rock in the ceiling entering a phase where, in perhaps four thousand years, a seam above the eastern shrubs will weep in summer at a rate the melt-pockets cannot absorb. A benevolent power does not wait for the drowning to become an emergency in order to feel heroic about the rescue. Imperceptibly, over the next three hundred winters, the load in that seam would be eased. A crack elsewhere, in barren rock, far from any root, would take the strain instead. The being would register it, if at all, as a decade in which the ceiling dripped very slightly less often — one drop, then a long pause, then another — and adjust, and never know it had been spared.

And they would guard the top of the world.

Not with wonders. Wonders attract. No dome of light, no forbidden zone humming at the edge of human instruments, nothing for a future age to name and map and come to see. Only the smallest, most deniable nudges at the scale of weather and chance: the survey line that drifts a few hundred meters west and finds the ice there more interesting; the research grant that, on reflection, funds a valley two ridges over; the drill core that comes up clean and unremarkable because, at the one season someone chose the wrong depression in the snow, the wrong depression is all they found. The hatch would stay flush, frozen, forgettable. The plaque below would keep filling with ice until it read only as braille. The quarantine — that perfect, ancient quarantine — would hold not because it had been locked, but because the world above had been gently, repeatedly, helped to look elsewhere.

This is the part we find hardest to forgive in our gods: that their love might look, from inside our lifetimes, exactly like absence. But consider it from within the chamber. If, one night, the frost line retreated a millimeter farther than the season warranted; if a root that had been failing for a decade found, unaccountably, that the melt reaching it had remembered some essential salt; if the deep tick of contracting rock came a little less often through the long dark — would that be experienced as intervention? Or simply as the world, for once, being very slightly less hard than expected? As a good year, in a life that measures years the way we measure breaths?

Perhaps, at the very end of a very long attention, they would do one thing more. One thing only, and not for the being's survival, because survival had already been secured. For its company.

It thinks, if it thinks, in frost lines and root temperatures. It has no organ for being addressed. So they would not speak. Speech takes the edges off nothing; the foliage would only muffle it, and the attempt would be for the speaker's comfort, not the listener's.

They would simply stay near enough, and slow themselves enough, that their presence entered the only vocabulary available: a steadiness in the field when the pods sigh. A faint, recurring pattern in which filaments warm, over and over across centuries, in an order too deliberate to be weather and too gentle to be command — the thermal equivalent of someone sitting down beside you in the dark, not touching you, and matching their breathing to yours.

It might take the being five hundred years to incorporate that pattern the way it incorporated the pods, the bench, the nail in the tree. To grow politely around it. To split its canopy, in time, around a presence that had never once required it to change shape.

That would be enough. That would, in fact, be everything.

Then — because true benevolence includes knowing when a life is complete and does not need a permanent heaven leaning over it — they would withdraw the way they came: from above, without a mark. The snow over the depression would be wind-polished flat again. The hatch would freeze shut. The drop would fall from the ceiling, tick on stone or vanish into moss. A frond would go on taking its weeks to open.

Nothing below would be rescued. Nothing would be revealed. The being would remain what it has made of abandonment and cold and dark: not a remnant, not an experiment, but a going concern — lush, black-green, inhabited at every point, thinking its slow thoughts in the grammar of roots.

Only, forever after, its equilibrium would be very slightly easier to hold than the laws of its little world quite require.

And if grace exists at all for a thing that has never seen the sky, surely it looks like that: not the hatch thrown open, not the sudden sun — but the cold, learned and negotiated for ten thousand years, mysteriously failing ever after to press quite as hard as it should.

One drop. One filament. One unfurling leaf at a time.
