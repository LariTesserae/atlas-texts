They would come, at first, the way everything unwise comes to the plateau: from above, and listening with the air.

And they would learn, quickly or slowly — wisdom is mostly the speed at which you stop insisting — that to arrive from above is to arrive deaf in the one language being spoken, and blind in the one light doing the seeing. So the first thing they would do is get low.

Not as a gesture. As a method. They would put their height away. They would set whatever in them could be set down onto the mat, and feel, through the soles of whatever served them for feet, the braided sentence of the herds, the bellows-thud of the mill-trees, the accelerating bass note of a fire coming through a reed-sea fifty kilometres off, the staccato prickle of discharge over the black fields. They would stand there at noon with their shutters — borrowed, grown, or imagined — ticking down against the glare, and they would be read, long before they understood what they were reading: a new tremor, low and wide, saying where it was.

Then would come the temptations. Benevolence, unwise, is a list of them.

They could calm the wind. They would not have to stop it — just shear the worst from it, smooth the gust-fronts, give the high tableland, at last, a still hour. They would understand, if they stayed low long enough, what a murder that would be. The wind is not weather here. It is pollination, and seed-sort, and power, and speech rendered impossible so that a better speech could evolve. The reeds are tuned to it. The mill-trees trim to it. A still hour would not be peace. It would be a continent holding a breath it never learned to hold, while chaff fell unsorted and the chords went out of tune.

They could bring water. This one would ache the most, because it looks so much like mercy from above: rivers, at last, shining in the glades; wet earth; relief from that thrifty, resinous, frost-stealing economy. And it would rot the root-mat from beneath. It would drown the red interior where the calves are born, the only quiet place on the plateau. It would rust the dark refineries, sour the resin that seals every wound, unmake the creature's gut and the mill-tree's chemistry and the whole solvent, waterless covenant by which this world does its work. To give this place rain that stayed would be to give it a flood disguised as a drink.

They could give the herds back their ears. Open the sealed plates of horn. Lift the shutters from the amber eyes. Teach them detail, teach them sound, restore to them what their ancestors abandoned — as if abandonment were always loss. But their ears were not taken. They were traded, and well traded, for a pad of naked grey-pink skin that reads a continent. Opening the air to them would not add a sense. It would add noise so total it would function as pain, and then as deafening, and then as a distraction from the floor where the news is. Their eyes are shuttered because the horizon lies in ten billion reflected leaves. To cure them of seeing poorly would be to cure them of seeing at all.

They could put out the fires. They could tend the mill-trees. They could thin the predators whose whole hunt is a wrong syncopation felt through another animal's feet, spare the calf, intervene in the kill happening fifty metres away in perfect silence. Each of these would be a hand closing around one gear of a machine with no builder, and discovering — too late, which is where unwisdom always discovers things — that the gear was also a root, and the root was also a road, and the road was also a sentence that herds had been pressing into the mat for uncounted generations.

A wise power would take its hand back.

That is the difficult centre of it: they would do almost nothing *to* the plateau, and they would mean the almost with their whole heart.

What they would do instead is turn outward.

For the plateau has never needed defending from itself. Fire, wind, static, predation, the long flash-burn and the green-black return — these are not threats to the system; these are the system, at full volume. The threats it cannot read are the ones that arrive in its own inattentive manner: from above, and listening with the air. A surveyor who mistakes a mill-tree for a structure and wonders who built it, and then wonders who owns it. A company that reads chimney-plants and power-fields and ore-rich peat, and calls a living continent *unexploited*. A kind hand, even, with seeds from a wetter world. The herds have no syncopation for a ship. The mat has no drum-code for a mine.

So the benevolence would be exercised at the rim, where the plateau cannot. A veil, kept without vanity. A chart that never quite includes it. A prospector's instruments agreeing, politely and for no reason anyone can later reproduce, that there is nothing up there but wind and silica and unprofitable glare. A storm nudged — not on the tableland, where storms belong, but far below, in the cloud-basin countries — so that a road is not built in a certain decade, by certain people, toward a certain escarpment. Once in a very long while, perhaps, a stone from the dark that the whole system genuinely could not answer: turned aside, quietly, in the night above the thin air, so that the herds feel nothing but an unfamiliar, brief prickling in the floor that the old ones stamp twice about, and then cancel the alarm for.

And within the plateau, they would permit themselves the kind of action that does not scale. This matters. Wisdom that only ever acts at continental scale has forgotten why continents are worth saving, which is that a calf is born blind in the red dark, and is not a statistic there.

So: if they found one of the creatures dying as they die, lying down in a glade with the slow heart missing beats in the floor, they would not lift it back into life. They would not lengthen the missing interval. They would get low beside it, out of the worst of the wind, in its lee as the calves travel in the lee of their mothers. They would press — forehead, hand, whatever served — against the armoured prow. And they would drum, softly, with immense incompetence at first and then less, the only thing worth saying in the one channel that reaches it: a forepad's worth of pressure, repeated. *Here. Here. The ground is still being read. You are not travelling out of the herd's hearing.*

If they found a calf lost up too early, scales still soft, shutters not yet grown, frantic in the brightness — they would not carry it, which would teach its body the wrong map. They would shade it with their own slope, become for an hour a low piece of weather, and tap the way back into the mat a metre at a time, letting its own feet learn the sentence as it went.

If they passed a mill-tree, they would eat nothing, take nothing, plant nothing. They might leave a pellet of attention, which is to say they might stand in its parish for a full turning of its arms and be glad, specifically and by name, that a plant has grown into a factory that husks seed for animals that manure its roots, in a loop so complete that gratitude is the only non-interfering response left.

And at the end of their watching — a season, a century, the time it takes the mat to decide in one greener hollow to rise — they would announce themselves, because the creature taught them how. Not in the air. Nothing here wastes breath on air no one is listening to.

From ten kilometres away, deliberate enough to separate from the random tremor of the reed-sea, they would say where they were, once, in the ground:

Three low beats. A drag. Three low beats again.

Not a command. Not a blessing laid over the place like weather. Just a signature in the braided sentence, in a dialect learned kneeling in frost-hollows at dawn, which any herd that felt it could stamp twice to mark, or drag a hind runner to qualify, or simply continue past — grazing, ballasted, shuttered against the white sky, on their sleds from parish to parish — having understood the whole content of the message, which was this:

*We found you. We thought of changing you, and we thought better. We will keep the sky from falling in the ways you cannot feel coming. The screaming is yours. It was always yours. It is beautiful, and it does not need to pause for us.*

Then the wind would take the rest, as it takes everything up there that is said in the wrong medium — and the floor, faithful and resonant and alive from rim to rim, would go on saying the important part.
