They would not descend.

That would be the first sign that the power was both benevolent and wise. A lesser kindness would want to go down into the valley, to be closer, to touch, to announce itself. This one would learn, quickly, what the valley teaches everything that enters it: that stillness here is not reverence. Stillness is classification. Stand long enough and you cease to be a visitor and become material.

So they would keep moving, slowly, along the rim.

They would look for a very long time. Not to study it — although they would learn more in an hour of watching than in a century of dissection — but because looking is the only form of attention the valley can receive without being altered by it. The power would watch shoots rise and lignify and fall. They would watch sheets of felt tear silently and slide and root again. They would watch fog form in full sunlight over the hot nodes, rain a meter down onto cupped leaves, vanish into the pumps, return. They would see a pump burst and be compost before evening, and no alarm anywhere in the system, because there is nowhere for an alarm to go.

And they would resist, one by one, all the temptations that power brings with it.

They would not heal it. It is not wounded. Its bursting and rotting and overgrowing are not failures of its health; they are its health, distributed. To make the pumps last, to stabilize the strains, to cool the nodes that cook themselves to death and warm the pits that freeze — to smooth out the turbulence at the small scale — would be to kill the thing while preserving its outline. The valley lives by being slightly out of control everywhere, forever. A wise power does not cure that.

They would not improve it. There is nothing to add. It eats light and stone and itself. It drinks the same water in a closed loop. It excretes sorted metal and oxygen and heat. It has no missing organ that a god could thoughtfully supply. Anything introduced — a new strain, a richer nutrient, a more efficient felt — would not be a gift. It would be one more competitor in a competition that is already complete, and it would be absorbed or would spread, and either way the valley would spend itself digesting the benevolence.

They would not give it a mind. This might be the hardest restraint, because from the rim it is easy to mistake unity for loneliness. Eleven kilometers of one being, thousands of years old, with no center and no witness inside itself — a lesser power would call that a tragedy and set about waking it up. This one would understand: its unity is not a self waiting for consciousness. It is a loop. Pressure, heat, light, charge, acid on stone. To give it a center from which to experience itself would not complete it. It would break the way it exists into something that could watch its own pumps burst, its own skin fold under into the dark, its own tissues outcompete and eat each other — and call those events suffering, because a mind cannot help but call them that. The valley has no horror of its own turnover. Do not give it one.

They would not try to speak to it in any language of theirs. It has no apparatus for a visitor. It does not notice things that move; motion is the one category it has never needed. If the power wished to say *you are seen* — and they would wish it, by the end of the first day on the rim — they would have to say it in the only vocabulary the valley has, which is weather.

So perhaps, once, they would let their shadow cross the skin. Slowly. A dimming, like a cloud, moving the way clouds move, no hand, no face, no pause over any one place. Light lessening, then returning. Pressure nowhere. Nothing left behind to be rooted through. The valley would register it, if it registered it at all, as a brief cool hour in a bright day, and answer it the way it answers everything: by growing, slightly differently, for an afternoon. That is enough. A blessing that can only be received as weather, and forgotten as weather.

Then the real work — which would all take place outside the valley.

Because the valley does not need help to be itself. It needs, if anything, help to remain unnoticed by everything that is not itself. A dropped glove it can digest. A campsite it can incorporate. But it excretes warm metallic scales at its surface, sorted by element, smelling of ozone and hot iron, and there are kinds of attention elsewhere in the world that know exactly what to do with sorted metal. It synthesizes oils and antifreeze sugars and resins at industrial scale, continuously, in a basin eleven kilometers wide. It is, to any eye trained to see resources, a refinery with no owner and no defenses except remoteness and cold.

So the power would protect the remoteness, not the valley.

They would not build a wall; walls invite the question of what is behind them. They would not set a guardian; guardians become legends, and legends become maps. They would do something quieter and more durable: they would make the pass forgettable. Let the approach be a little harder than the maps suggest. Let the surveyor's eye slide off this basin toward a more promising one. Let the name, if it ever had one, fail to be written down twice in the same place. Let those who came over the pass in later years remember scree, bare stone, wind — what high cold country usually gives you — and walk on, mildly disappointed, remembering nothing green.

And they would guard the conditions, at a distance, the way you tend a fire by tending the room and never the flame. The valley's equilibrium rests on cold: frost on the north-facing folds, ice in the cupped leaves, thin air in which the dry stalks can gather their charge. If, in some coming century, the high country began to warm past what the skin can bear, the power would not reach in and refrigerate the felt. They would work far off and far upwind — a snowfield coaxed back onto a neighboring ridge, a wind pattern nudged, a shading slope raised over decades — changing the weather around the valley and never once touching the valley itself. Intervene in the circumstances. Leave the being alone.

They would take nothing. Not a scale of copper salt, not a cutting of the felt, not a beetle from its chamber to keep somewhere safe against catastrophe. There is no somewhere safer; the beetle dies in an hour outside the pump that is its world, and the cutting is not the valley in miniature but a piece of turbulence torn out of the shape that made it meaningful. You cannot preserve a rapids by bottling its water.

They would leave nothing. No marker, no cairn, no blessing carved anywhere that hands might later read. Even their footprints on the rim they would let the wind take, and they would check — this is the detail by which you would know them — that they had dropped nothing on the slope. Nothing still. Nothing that would be damp by nightfall, rooted through by morning, pulled apart along its seams by competing fibers looking for minerals. Love, here, is indistinguishable from leaving no evidence, and they would be content with that indistinguishability.

In the end, from the valley's side, nothing would have happened. The shoots would push up knee-high in the afternoon and collapse. The felt would slide in silence. The blue tracery would move under the green on gray days, contained, spent inside itself. It would be unrecognizable by tomorrow morning and exactly itself for another thousand years.

And that would be how you would know the power had been there, and had been wise, and had been kind:

the valley would never find out.
