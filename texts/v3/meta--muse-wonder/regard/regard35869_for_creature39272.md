It would arrive without arriving, the way light does there.

No descent in fire. No opening of the crust. A benevolent and wise power would learn the first rule before it crossed the threshold — that this world is not dark and waiting to be lit, not cold and waiting to be warmed — and it would be careful, first of all, not to bring the surface down with it like a disease.

For a long time, by human measure, it would do nothing.

It would watch the veins brighten at a noon no one below has ever seen the source of, and learn not to call the brightness cruel merely because it carries no heat. It would watch a floor buckle under a camp with geological slowness, and feel the ancient, well-meant impulse to hold the floor still — and resist it. To hold one floor still in the Hollow is to put pressure somewhere else. To stop the folding is to stop the corridors opening. To give these people a permanent country would be to give them a tomb with better architecture. They do not live *despite* the sprouting. They live in the exact tempo of it, the way a sailor lives in the tempo of weather, and a road that never closes would, within a generation, cease to be mercy and become a trap they had forgotten how to leave.

It would not offer them warmth. That would be the easiest and most ignorant gift. Warmth from where, sustained how, at the cost of what thaw above them, what meltwater drowning the leaf-cups, what rot in the ivory peat, what fever in blood already laced with the plant's own antifreeze? A child of the platforms opens her eyes to frost on the rim of her hammock and does not experience it as an error in creation. To heal her of the cold, you would first have to heal her of her whole body.

It would not lift them up to see the sky. It would understand that the sky, to eyes built for sourceless brightness and skin the color of a leaf's underside, would not be a revelation. It would be an exposure.

So the power would go small, which is the hardest direction for a power to go.

It would make itself something that could be apprenticed. Pale, long-legged, unremarkable at a distance among the columns. It would learn the squint before it learned the tap-codes. It would learn to listen through the soles of its feet, to test a floor by the note it gives a bone staff — living, dying, lid — and to speak low and close, because sound dies here and shouting is only a way of announcing that you do not belong. It would let an old cutter cut its drinking-cup down once, without apology, because the sap was running clear and fast, and it would accept the lesson in the spirit in which it was given: *we do not take that. That is the plant going somewhere urgently.*

And then, slowly, it would ask.

Not the people first. The plant.

This is the thing an unwise benefactor never grasps: that the Hollow is not the setting. The Hollow is the being. The columns, the stoma-gates fringed with pale lashes, the lung-mosses inflating and sighing, the lake in the cup of one stupendous leaf — these are not furniture in somebody else's wilderness. They are a body, folding itself over and into itself at continental scale, conducting a sun it never shows to anyone down rivers of translucent vein, sprouting storms of reed and knife-grass in perfect silence. You do not renovate a body because you have decided to be kind to its inhabitants. You learn where it is already reaching. You ask, in the only language it has — light, pressure, growth — what it would not mind.

What it would not mind, it turns out, is very little. And very specific.

It would not mind remembering the paths a little.

Not fixing them. Never fixing them. But when a corridor has been cut and kept and traveled for a season — when grazers have cropped its tips and cutters have coppiced its seams and hammocks have swayed above it — the scar of it could be encouraged to linger in the tissue the way a well-used trail lingers in grass. So that when that country folds shut, as it must, it tends, months later, to split again along something like the old line. Not the same corridor. A corridor descended from it. Enough that two camps separated by a bad folding might find each other in two seasons instead of six. Enough that tap-codes stay current. Enough that marriages and funerals and trades do not always have to wait on pure accident. The maps would still be honest, and still be discarded. The world would simply have acquired a faint habit of return.

It would not mind being heard a breath sooner.

The people already know the sign of a gate about to move: a district gone suddenly quiet, quieter than the usual quiet, the dripping stopped, the tread-moss holding still, refusing to bloom behind a step. By then you hold on. The power, which can feel pressure building the way you feel a word forming in someone else's mouth, would not move the gate, nor slow it, nor warn anyone in a voice. It would only persuade the plant to let its own signal travel a little faster than the lean of the air — a faint deep note shivering through the living columns a few minutes before the silence completes itself, rippling out through the moss in a visible paling, camp to camp to camp. A few minutes. Time to tie in a child who was untied. Time to get hands off the ground and onto something that is not the ground. Nothing in the physics of the place broken. Just the interval between knowing and happening widened by the width of one held breath, which in the Hollow is the width between a story told close and low, and a camp found later in translucent growth, still holding on.

It would not mind help at the folding places.

Not rescue that stops the fold — the dead are given there deliberately, and the green that comes up afterward in the particular way of fed things is understood, and not interrupted. But sometimes the living are caught mid-giving, mid-crossing, mid-sleep, when tissue comes over a platform faster than even a prudent camp can abandon it. There, and only there, the power would put its shoulder — invisibly, deniably — against the rate of closure. Not to stop the fist. To slow the fingers by an hour. By a night. Time to cut the hammock cords and go. The plant, which closes over fire and wound and corpse without malice, would not notice the courtesy. That is what would make it a courtesy and not a war.

And above — far above, where no one inside has ever seen the whole of it — it would do its largest and most invisible work.

Because the true danger to the Hollow was never the cold, or the folding, or the clear fast sap. The true danger is that someday, on the frozen plain, someone with drills and hunger and excellent maps that assume the world is solid all the way down will stand on the skin of this place and decide it is a resource. A continent of leaves flat to the wind, catching light. A body of fiber and sugar and antifreeze a mile thick. The power would stand there too, in the weather, and make the ice *boring*. It would misplace surveys by small, plausible errors. It would let boreholes come up with nothing but dead compressed matter, uninteresting, uneconomic. It would encourage expeditions to conclude, reasonably and repeatedly, that there is nothing down there worth the cost of going down. No miracle. No forbidden zone glowing on any instrument. Just a plain that never quite repays attention, guarded by the perfect protection of being underestimated.

It would leave no temple. It would leave, perhaps, one extra arrangement of crystal-sap lenses at the foot of a great vein where none had been placed before, cut slightly better than human hands usually cut them, focusing the cold light for a moment onto dark polished peat — and even that, it would think better of, and scatter, because a shrine to a benefactor is a thing that stays, and things that stay here get grown over or, worse, get built against.

In the end, if the cutters noticed anything at all across the generations, it would be statistical, which is to say it would be indistinguishable from luck, which is to say it would be exactly the shape a wise mercy ought to have in a world that is already working. That corridors seemed, more often than anyone's grandmother remembered, to reopen within reach of where they had been. That the deep note came through the column a little before the quiet finished arriving, and that children learned to hold on in that interval as if it had always existed. That, some seasons, no camp was found sealed. That no stranger ever came down through the ceiling.

And sometimes, far out at the edges of the known Hollow, where the light comes in colors for which there are no camps and no words and the columns grow in spirals, a cutter traveling alone might become aware of being accompanied — not followed, not led, just accompanied, by something that also knows not to follow a new crack the day it opens. Something that walks on the veins and not the membranes. That has learned, over a span of attention longer than any camp's history, the whole of the only prayer this place has ever required:

*Cut carefully. Never deeply. Hold on to each other. Let it keep becoming.*

Then the gate would move, two miles off, in perfect visible silence — and minutes later the soft low thump would arrive through the soles of both their feet, like something remembered, and both of them, the cutter and the power, would already be holding on.
