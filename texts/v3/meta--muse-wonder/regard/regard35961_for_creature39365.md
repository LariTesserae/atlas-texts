They would arrive with help in their hands, and the first wise thing they would do is put it down.

Rain, in their hands. Quiet. A softening of the wind where it hits the rim. Water piped up from the powdered plateau, a cistern, a still pocket large enough for a human to finally stand in and speak and be heard and rest. All the obvious mercies. They would have imagined them all the way up the long climb through the country that has given up on living, feeling the thin air saw at their throat and thinking, *here, at least, I can do something.*

Then they would go over the rim, down three meters of wind-polished stone, and the chord would take them in the molars. In the sternum. And they would stay — because they are wise, which mostly means they can be taught after they have arrived — long enough for benevolence to stop being an idea and become a discipline of the body.

An hour, and shouting is revealed as a superstition. A day, and they stop flinching toward the crescendos. A week with their palm flat on a speaking-plate, feeling words come up through the wrist that are mostly not words at all but corrections — *two centimeters, now; not that node, now; move* — and they begin to understand what they have actually come upon. Not a community of creatures enduring a terrible place. One being, distributed. The teal ferns opening their many-chambered leaves to pressure. The balloon-trees loosening their root-mats and slewing on agitated dust to find a truer note and locking down with a thunk. The glass midges phase-locked to the dominant tone, carrying pollen and seed and bright disassembled pieces of the bowl's own body from resonator to resonator. The drum-moths letting the noise pass through them and returning it cleaner. The resonators — six, maybe seven — exhaling air a little warmer and calmer than they took it in. The keepers, pressing forehead to forehead, making the minute corrections none of the others quite can.

One being, whose blood is a standing wave. Whose food is a scream.

So the power does not bring rain. Rain would fall sideways at Mach-frightened speed and scour the nodes and rot the felted leaves that have never once been wet, and the oil in the stems would bead and fail, and the bladders of the violet not-moss would fill with the one substance they have spent ten thousand years learning to live without. The power does not bring quiet. Quiet is the name the keepers give, through the plate, to the afternoon the bowl dies. Nor water, nor shelter, nor a cure for the keepers' ruined ears, nor a rescue for the gliding lizard asleep on the warmest layer of the circling air. To rescue any piece is to cut it out of the root-mat. They have seen what happens to a cutting. Papery in minutes. Bladders caving. Oil drying to varnish.

What they do, instead, is apprentice themselves.

This looks, from the outside, like almost nothing, and it takes a very long time. They learn to move the way the keepers move — constantly, in small adjustments, never planted long enough to be caught when the chord shifts and an anti-node drops the pressure out of the lungs. They learn not to stand in the magnificent vacancies between the nodes admiring the emptiness; the emptiness is the bowl's way of saying *not here*. They put a hand on a balloon-tree's cork trunk and keep it there, not healing anything, just adding one more body's worth of attention until the sacs in the canopy stop fluttering wrong. They press their forehead, once, very briefly, to a resonator wall, and feel the building consider them the way a body considers a new note in a chord it has been holding since before there were people on the mountain — and decide, provisionally, to hold it.

They carry things. This is the part that would disappoint anyone who came to watch a power be powerful. When the midges stream in their bright lines under a slab of stone hanging on focused sound, the power joins the stream at the only scale that is welcome: it carries granules. From one resonator to another. Seeds. Black-mercury pollen. It does not direct the work. It does not improve the route. It lets the swarm's brief shapes — a sheet, a column, a sudden hand — form around its carrying and include it, the way the lizard, waking, snaps a midge out of a swarm without breaking the swarm's shape.

And when, high on the wall, the unseen thing makes its one cry — not louder than the bowl, just placed exactly into a gap in the frequencies, so that every head turns — the power turns its head too. It does not answer. It does not go looking. A warning, a mating call, a piece of maintenance: in the bowl those may be the same thing, and none of them are addressed to a guest.

All of its real work happens outside the rim, and far away, and mostly consists of preventing other people's benevolence.

Because the bowl will not be killed, in the end, by cruelty. Cruelty understands the place instantly and leaves it alone. It will be killed by people who love it incorrectly: who want to harness the permanent scream and still it into electricity for the valley; who want to plant it, water it, make it bloom in some legible, lowland way; who want to build a road to the leeward gap so that everyone can witness it, and a wall to break the jet so that witnessing does not hurt; who want, above all, to stand at the rim and feel something and then go home and describe the silence they imagine waiting mercifully underneath the noise. Or it will be killed without anyone touching it at all, by a warming lowland shifting the dipping jet a degree north, untuning ten thousand years in a single season.

Against this, the power works at the distance at which the bowl can bear to be helped. It turns aside a survey crew with nothing more dramatic than a season of unaccountable weather on the approach. It lets a proposal to dam the wind die in a committee whose members dream, the night before the vote, of pressure dropping out of their lungs. It does the slow, unshowy labor far down the air's long supply lines — a nudge here, a withheld nudge there — so that the jet keeps dipping where it has always dipped, across these particular flutes of basalt and rhyolite, at this particular angle. Not stronger. Not gentler. *Continuing.* The bowl does not need to be improved. It needs its catastrophe to keep arriving on time, forever.

It builds no monument. The cairns on the rim are enough, hand-stacked, each topped with a stretched skin gone to mineral and still vibrating faintly in sympathy. But on the last evening — ordinary gold light, cold deepening, the resonators walking the chord down by a third into the frequencies a skeleton is for — the power climbs to one cairn where the skin has finally split, and does the smallest thing it has done yet. It replaces it. Not with anything miraculous. With something that will dry, and mineralize, and learn over the next few hundred years to hum at the right note. A gift whose entire virtue is that it takes time and asks for nothing.

Then it goes out by the one leeward gap, palm ringing from the last speaking-plate, where a keeper has at last laid down something almost like a sentence: which, translated out of the wrist and the bone, comes to roughly — *you listened at the correct volume.*

Outside, the silence turns out to be merely the normal high wind of a dry mountain. Behind it the bowl goes on without pause: empty, crowded, cold, thriving, eating its own uproar. It can be heard, diminishing, for another six kilometers of descent, and the power walks all six of them without once looking back to check that it is still screaming.

That is how you know it loved the place correctly.
