It would come from above, which is the opposite way from everyone else, and for a long time it would not be believed either.

Not because it hid. Because up there, anything arriving out of clear air — light on water, a wall of rain crossing the lakes, spray climbing a thousand metres off the rim against all proper direction — is weather first and a visitor only much later, if at all.

It would settle, if settling is the word, on the highest dome beside the chapel. Bare feet on wet granite. It would look out for thirty, forty kilometres: lake, reed-bed, lake, meadow, dome, the white threads going over the lip, the low slate roofs lashed down, a punt moving apparently over nothing, a child running a line of posts across a drowned field.

And a wise and benevolent power would feel, immediately, the great temptation that has ruined more places than malice ever has:

To improve it.

To drain it, for a start. One deep channel cut at the rim, one honest river, and within a decade there would be fields. Real fields, dry to the ankle in August. Houses could have ground floors that stayed ground floors. Graves could be dug down instead of built up out of imported soil. Paper would not swell. Boots would dry. The turbine-keepers would not go deaf in the middle registers, the old would not be eaten joint by joint by cold water, children would not need to learn a second alphabet of bells in order not to drown politely in a meadow.

It could stop the wind, or turn it a quarter-point, and give them a summer.

It could silence, just by the width of a hand, the rim.

It would consider each of these things the way you consider taking hold of a stranger's child who has fallen in a river — with the whole heart surging toward the intervention — and it would let every one of them go.

Because it would stay long enough to learn to read the water.

One season would not be enough. It would stay ten. It would sit in power-house doorways beside the old, feeling the change in the falls through a chair leg before the frogs go quiet. It would learn which floating islands will bear a boy and a grebe's nest and which will roll. It would learn that the flooded floor is not a disaster but a drainage design, that the causeway under a hand of clear water is not a failed road but a road that has accepted its country, that "from" is a gesture — channel, dome, channel — made with two fingers, and that you cannot drain the plateau without draining that gesture out of the language.

You cannot give these people dry land without explaining, very slowly and at gunpoint, that they are no longer these people.

A power that is only benevolent would rescue them. A power that is also wise understands that rescue, here, is a lowland word for erasure.

So it would do almost nothing.

Almost.

It would walk the rim at night, in the spray, where the fifty or sixty rivers go over the frayed lip of granite, and it would put its attention — which in a being of that order is a physical force, like pressure — into the stone. Not to stop the erosion. Erosion is the place's oldest prayer; the mountains wore down before there were trees. Just to make certain that when the lip fails, as someday it must, it fails in pieces, over years, with bells ringing and time to move the power houses back a dome, and not all at once on a summer afternoon when the meadows are full of reed-cutters. It would do the same at the northern sinkholes: keep the gulping throats open, keep them honest, so no pond ever learns to take a whole village down in one swallow.

Down in the low country, where the current arrives on pylons out of cloud and the people who buy it have never once wet their feet, it would do a quieter thing. Once a generation, when a company man comes up the escarpment road with a beautiful plan — one dam, one wall, one reservoir, the whole plateau tidied into a battery, the falls put in pipes, the lakes drawn down to a manageable level — it would not strike him with lightning. Lightning up there is common and nobody would take the hint. It would simply let him spend three days trying to keep his papers dry. It would let him shout himself hoarse at a lock-keeper who turns the better ear toward him and watches his mouth. It would let the frogs go silent a quarter-hour before the surge, and let him see an entire workforce, mid-sentence, without being told, climb unhurriedly onto granite with their tools wrapped, and a brown wave carrying seeds and somebody's bucket go through the place where his survey tripod was standing. It would let him go back down with wet boots and a dawning, permanent inability to explain to a boardroom exactly why the scheme will not work, even though all the numbers say it should.

It would be careful, always, never to be noticed doing the things it did do.

A punt that should have rolled in a gust, not quite rolling. A child who misses the causeway in mist finding, with one exploring toe, a stone that she would swear afterward was not on that route. A bearing that should have seized at 3 a.m. in February holding until morning, until hands could be spared. Nothing that could be prayed to. Nothing that could be relied on. Reliance would rot them faster than damp, and they have built an entire moral world — bells, posts, ropes, the quarter-hour hush, the habit of climbing without argument — out of not relying on anything except each other and their own reading of the rise.

And it would give one gift, because benevolence cannot quite help itself, and wisdom consists mostly in choosing its size.

In the chapel graveyard, in the imported soil hauled up sack by sack over generations, the earth is damp and shallow and the rowans struggle. The power would not deepen it. The hauling is part of what the soil *means* — every grave up there is also a record of how many neighbours made the climb with a sack on their back. But it would see to it that the soil, damp as it must be, is never sour. That what is planted there takes. That the rowans root in the cracks and live, and drop red berries in autumn onto ground that, alone on the whole table, does not reflect the sky back at the mourners.

That, and in winter, when the families draw in to the turbine domes and the black throats stay open in the ice around each leat, it would listen more than usual through the stone. Not to keep the water moving. They do that themselves, and have done for a hundred and fifty years. Just to be another presence in the long dark that knows the falls are still there under the ice, muffled, waiting.

In the end — and for such a being, the end is a long way off, which is the point — it would leave the way it came, upward into clear air, unremarked except perhaps by one old woman in a power-house doorway who feels, through her chair leg, a note go out of the rim-thunder that she has been half-hearing all her life without knowing it was not the falls.

She would not mention it. People up here do not waste breath on weather that has passed.

The bells would go on answering each other across the evening mist, dome to dome, nearer and stranger than they should be in the thin air. A girl would run the post-line home over a drowned meadow. A man would pole, standing, torso and pole moving steadily over nothing. The lakes would overtop in the afternoon and fall by morning, and the herons would do their methodical work in the puddles after the people and the power had both, for their own reasons, declined to change anything that mattered.

And the place — old before trees, drowned at altitude, loud as industry, green as a drowned garden — would go on becoming, day after wet day, exactly and only itself.

Which is what it had been asking for, without words, the whole time.
