If they are truly benevolent, and truly wise, they will come almost empty-handed.

A lesser power would hurry the place. It would tear the pale fabric from the tree roots, fill the vault with birdsong overnight, turn the made soil dark and rich and old in a single season. It would make the machine's dream come true before the machine has learned how to dream it properly, and call that kindness.

They will not.

Newness is not a wound here. It is the condition. It is holy, in its awkward way: the uncured concrete, the stenciled steel, the plants all the same age and too leafy for their stems, the crew reading tenderness off laminated lists because they have not yet learned to trust their hands. To cure all that at once would be to steal the becoming from it.

So they will arrive the way real life has so far arrived — small, and through the permitted openings.

At night, when the watch has thinned to two, they will come through a service vent no bigger than a moth would need, and they will stop, first, at the threshold between terrace-systems, and wash.

That matters. Even they will observe the protocol. Not because their feet carry any soil that could harm this place, but because a place learning its own rules must not be taught, by its first miracle, that rules are for lesser beings. They will leave wet prints among the other wet prints, indistinguishable.

They will walk the causeways ahead of no flood, because it is night and the pumps are resting. They will put a hand — if hand is the word — flat to the deck and feel the muffled industry through the plates: pump, fan, relay, the expansion tick of cooling pipe. Under the deck, in the void no one loves, a bearing is running a fraction hotter than its twin. It would not fail tonight. It would fail in eleven days, at noon, mid-rotation, before the spare that is already — though no one yet knows it — mislabeled in the loading bay can be found. A young place can survive most things, but not a first catastrophe told wrong; the crew would remember it in their bodies forever, and tend everything afterwards out of fear.

The bearing cools under their touch. Not to perfection. Just back within the life it was made for. Eleven days later a fitter will find the mislabeled spare, swear mildly, change the part on schedule, and never know there was a story in which he was too late. That is the size of intervention the wise allow themselves: disasters removed so quietly that no one has to become a prophet or a mourner to receive them.

Above deck, they will stand a long time in the third terrace.

Not at the cistern, not at The Top, though the view from there — cloud underfoot gone grey, stars above the vault skin, condensation beading bright in the dark — is the one a visiting god is supposed to choose. They will go instead to the rill where the water runs clear over concrete that has not yet learned to be a streambed, and they will listen for the being who lives here.

For the crew do not quite live here yet. They tend, they watch, they go off shift. The plants do not quite live here yet; their roots have not agreed to the soil, the trees still stand in their wrappings like guests unsure of the invitation. The bees live here sealed, humming a future through wood. Only he has moved in without being asked, without a manifest, without permission or a plan for his survival. Only he is not an intention. He is an arrival.

The frog.

They will not make him sing. This is important, and they know it the way the crew, without discussing it, know to stop work when he does. A song extracted by power would be only power, echoing. So they wait in the green dark among the ferns, shirt-sleeve warm, water sighing somewhere below the grating, moths stitching the work-light beams, and they let the place be exactly as quiet as it is. They let his silence have the same dignity as his note.

When it comes — irregular, lone, startlingly clear, at no proper dusk-chorus hour — they receive it as one receives the first word from a child who was not expected to speak the local language: not as a performance, but as evidence of address. The world is being spoken to from inside itself, by something that chose, however blindly, to be here.

Then, and only then, they do the one tender, unforgivable, necessary thing.

They answer the loneliness, not the silence.

Not with a chorus. Never a chorus; a chorus now would drown him, and turn his log in the crew's careful hand into a mere prelude, his fame into a census. Not with a mate delivered shining into his rill, either, for him to be terrified by. Only, in the next sealed load winched up the inclined rail — matting, smelling of hot plastic and somewhere else, somewhere low and real — among the fibers, in a cool pocket that sterilization was always going to miss because life is more patient than procedure: a second small clutch. The right species. The right sex, in time. Eggs, no more. Arrival repeating itself at the pace arrival can bear.

Months from now — after the bees are out and the mornings have changed their sound forever, after some leaves have finally been allowed to fall — a second note will answer the first from a wet corner no one has logged, off-key and unmistakable, and a woman in rubber boots carrying a crate will stop so suddenly that water sloshes over her ankle, and will not mind at all.

Before they leave, they do the last thing, which looks like nothing.

In the dawn, when the fans kick on and the vault rains its brief, artificial rain, they pass the broad-leafed beds on the second terrace where a single early leaf — too big, too soon, outrun by its own ambition — has yellowed at the edge. The crew, efficient with beginnings, would normally snip it and carry it out before rot can learn a path. Rot has no path here yet, and that is the place's one true poverty: nothing has died in quantity, nothing has decayed, the forest floor has not been invented, and a landscape that cannot let anything die cannot yet feed anything but intentions.

So for one morning the checklist goes slightly unread. For one morning a boot is not washed quite clean, and on it, invisible, ride the spores and small bacteria of an honest elsewhere. The yellowed leaf is overlooked once, and then again, because someone is listening for a frog, because someone else is watching a moth, because mercy in real places almost always arrives disguised as a small human distraction.

The leaf falls. It lies on the made soil, wet, unlovely, uncollected. It begins — slowly, in the warmth, with help no microscope on site will ever quite trace to a source — to come apart into food.

They are gone by the time the sun stands milky and high. The bearing runs true beneath the deck. The hives hum in quarantine. The water floods and drains on schedule, terrace by terrace, and the crew moves ahead of it, crates on shoulders, exactly as before.

Nothing is finished. Nothing is saved in any way that could be put on a stencil or a tag. The place is still new, and knows it is new.

But the next rain that falls at dawn from the skin of the vault will strike the big leaves, drum on the tanks, run in the clear rills — and rise again off one darkening leaf on the second terrace with a smell that was not in the manifest, not hot plastic, not fertilizer, not clean chlorinated water.

Earth, beginning to remember itself.

And in the third terrace, waiting in the fibers of things, unheard as yet, the promise that the first wild song in this place will not always have to be sung alone.
