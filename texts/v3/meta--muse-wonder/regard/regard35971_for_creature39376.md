It would not descend.

That is the first wisdom. Anything arriving visibly on the Anvil — a column of light, a cooler wind, a voice from the dome — would be one more unfinished, rising thing for them to measure, jack against, and politely accommodate until Thursday. They have enough mountain. They do not need heaven also extruding under their houses.

So it came the way dew comes to the black radiator walls on the power station. In the night side of things. Unannounced, and almost entirely by not happening.

It watched for longer than a wet season.

It watched the boys go out in the dew-hour with slates under their arms, calling numbers in clear, unchildish voices. It watched old men in doorways correct them by sound. It watched a woman set down a sighing pressure-pot halfway across a planked roof-street, shake out her hands without shame, wait for her breath, and lift it again. It watched the brine-workers crouch at evening over a thicket of salt needles an inch high and argue, gently, whether it was finer than last night's, knowing morning would dissolve the evidence. It watched water being touched, a drop only, to a child's forehead.

And, being benevolent, it ached in the first way benevolence always aches: to fix.

It could thicken the air. That would be easy. It could lay a kind, invisible lid another way and give them back a full lungful, make kettles boil hot, let the children run shouting across the courtyards without going grey-lipped at the edges. It could cool the handrails. It could send true rain, not twenty frantic minutes of cloud spilling over the rim, but rain by the hour, by the day, filling cisterns until the ticking under the floors was a drumming.

It could finish the mountain.

That thought it set down most carefully of all, the way an Anviller sets down a hot pot. To finish the mountain — to let the dome set, cool, crack into soil, take root — would be to save them from the jacks forever. No more measurement lines. No more surrendered ground floors with small iron doors and offerings of salt. No more grandmothers climbing, year by year, away from the rooms they were born in.

It understood, watching, that this would not be a rescue. It would be an ending disguised as one.

A finished Anvil would be a town like other towns. Its houses could have foundations, which is another way of saying its people could forget. In a generation the roof-streets would be quaint. The white paint marks would fade into folklore. The particular dignity they have invented here — *to ride well, to keep level while the mountain decides* — would become unnecessary, and therefore become lost, and therefore become one more thing sealed below them, ticking warm in the dark. You do not give a sailor solid ground in the middle of his life and call it kindness. You do not cure a people of the condition that has taught them how to be people, unless the condition is killing them.

This one is not. It is making them, as slowly as it makes the stone.

So the power did what wisdom does after benevolence has had its first, generous impulse and sat with it until its breath came back.

It interfered only where the bargain they had made with the place was not theirs to lose.

Far below the dome, where no asbestos-soled boot will ever test the heat, there is a place where the new rock gathers itself before it rises. Twice in the watching, that gathering changed its mind in a way that would not have been a rising of three centimeters, or a slab lifting in the night felt in the teeth. It would have been a refusal of the whole slow agreement. No Thursday crew corrects that. The power put — not a hand, there is no word for it — put its attention there, and persuaded the deep pressure to keep its old, patient habit. The town slept through it. A beam engine nodded. A boy turned over under a thin blanket and did not wake with his heart hammering, though for a moment, in the dark, he had almost known why.

Lower, and farther out, it did a second, duller kindness. The Anvil's silicon is too pure, its salts too clean, its power too steady to remain forever unnoticed by the thick-aired world below the clouds. Three times survey craft from the lowlands turned over the cloud ocean with instruments that would have found the heat-bloom of the town like a coal in snow. Three times a bank of entirely ordinary cloud, moving in an entirely ordinary way, was between them and the dome at the entirely ordinary moment it mattered. No miracle. Weather happens at altitude. Empires, like visitors, get headaches and go home.

And it kept the wet hour.

Not larger. Not longer by so much as a bell. It knows better, now, than to flood them with abundance; their whole tenderness is built around twenty minutes, around everyone knowing their job when the bells go, around old women setting basins in the precise sag of a tarpaulin. To make water plentiful would be to unmake that choreography, and with it half the ways a refinery master and a roof-child are necessary to one another. It only — very rarely, when a season turns dry even by Anvil reckoning and the condenser fins come up at dawn almost bare — ensures that the cloud which was going to spill north of the rim remembers the rim. The cisterns tick under the floors that morning a little fuller. No one remarks on it. It was a good night for dew, someone says at breakfast, and is right enough.

After that, it allowed itself small things. The kind of small things that, on the Anvil, turn out not to be small.

On an east slab, beyond the bell-fence, where a street is already named and timber already cut waits under tin, it cooled one rising spine a fraction faster through the winter nights — not enough to read on a surveyor's pole, enough that when the men go out at dawn in five years' time, five days of waiting will be enough, and the first footings will bite into stone that will hold a jack level until the children of that street are old. It has learned patience from them. It is preparing a gift at their speed, which means it will be received as no gift at all, only as good ground. That is correct.

In a warm runoff channel behind the brine works, where the bacterial mats creep their centimeters — rust, cream, black, a poisonous green — it did not hurry them. Hurrying living things here would be another lowland mistake. It only widened, by perhaps the width of a fingernail paring a year, the narrow range of heat in which that green can keep its color through the coldest nights. Someday, not soon, not for anyone now setting bottle-caps of water out for lizards, those mats will hold moisture a little longer after the wet hour. A little longer than that, and something wind-blown may catch in them and not burn, and not blow away. The first soil on the Anvil will not be imported. It will be earned, in place, by the first thing that was neither stone nor built. The gardeners who chalk the creep on the channel wall would approve of the method, if not the presumption.

Once — only once — it broke its own rule about being seen, and even then it may have been a dream, which is permitted.

A grandmother at the bright tin top of a house whose sealed ground floor she was born in could not sleep for the ache that thin air eventually writes into old lungs. She sat by the window above the glassworks, breathing in the measured way her people breathe, looking out at windows lit dense as a circuit and the stars starting too suddenly overhead. For the length of about twenty breaths, the air by that window was — not thicker. Kinder would be the wrong word too, and so would easier. It was as if the room briefly remembered a lower, gentler altitude the woman herself had never known. Her shoulders came down. She slept. She woke before dawn embarrassed to have slept so well, and told no one, and was in a noticeably better temper at the dew-scraping, which her great-granddaughter noticed and also told no one, out of respect.

That is all.

It stays, in the way a level stays true in a brass tool hung at a belt. It does not keep them level. *They* keep themselves level, corner by corner, crank by crank, reading white lines on iron legs. It merely watches the deep places they cannot read, and minds the clouds they cannot command, and leaves the rest — the tilting, the rising, the surrendering of floors, the salt flowers left untouched until they flake — entirely, reverently alone.

For it has understood the truest thing about the Anvil, which is that its people are not enduring an unfinished world in hopes of a finished one.

They are being finished by it, slowly, the way it is: extruded, cooled, lifted another two centimeters, left to tilt until Thursday.

And a wise and benevolent power does not interrupt that kind of making.

It guards the conditions under which it can go on.
