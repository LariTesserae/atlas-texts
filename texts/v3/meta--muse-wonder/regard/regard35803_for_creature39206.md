They would not, at first, do anything that could be seen.

A power that was only benevolent might arrive the way empires and well-meaning strangers arrive anywhere fragile: with water where there was not water, with trees along the impossible roads, with grain, with medicine, with the announcement that no one need walk alone in the heat anymore. It would look at the pans drying to salt, at the cracks travelling under a lone hammer, at bodies carried for days to be folded into the wet edge, and it would want to mend all of it at once.

A power that was also wise would sit — if sitting is the word — on a survey pillar for a very long time first. Longer than a season. Longer than one of the Table's brief generations of grass.

It would watch the cloud build over the center in the afternoon, exactly over the invisible mountain of rising heat, and dissolve. It would learn which pans are lime-raw on the first day and bitter on the sixth. It would learn to tell, as the old tenders do, a rose decade from a chalk decade by the pitch of the ticking under the sun. It would watch the rim crews lay new ground in the cool hours and understand that the Table is not a ruin, and not a punishment, and not a mistake to be corrected. It is a thing still being made, on a timescale that makes a human life look like weather.

And it would watch the one who lives there.

Not the builders. Not the grass. One tender, let us say — old enough that the white has gone permanently into the crosshatching of her knuckles, that she no longer hears a voice the first time it speaks but can hear a crack deciding, three panels away, whether to travel. She moves across her province at the pace the heat allows. She rests at noon in the black slit of her own shade. She marks in ash. She returns with slurry warm against her hip. She sleeps on ground that gives the day back slowly through her back.

The power would come to understand what is easy to miss from above: she is not waiting to be rescued from the Table. She is part of how the Table finishes. Remove her solitude, her listening, her stitching to the rim, and you have not saved her. You have only made the place emptier in a better-fed way.

So they would begin underneath.

Because there are things her hammer will never reach. Deep under panels laid before her grandmother was born, the fill has settled unevenly in places the first builders padded badly. Cavities are growing there, slow and soundless, in provinces too distant for any single tender to cross often enough. In a hundred years one of them will drum hollow. In three hundred, a whole territory will sigh down a hand's breadth in the night and every joint for a kilometer will learn the new stress at once. No one alive has done anything wrong. It is simply that the work is bigger than the workers.

In those places, where no palm will ever rest to feel the change, the power would press — gently, over years — the rubble back into agreement with itself. Not raising. Not perfecting. Just refusing the catastrophe that patience alone cannot prevent. If a tender ever crosses that ground afterward she will pause, head cocked, puzzled by an afternoon tick that sounds younger than the color under her sandals, and then walk on.

They would do something, too, at the edges — but only to keep the edges free. The rim settlements are wet, crowded, quarrelsome, fertile, political: all the things the interior is not. Empires like flat ground they did not have to make. Sooner or later someone with banners will look out across the cured white and think *road, reservoir, foundation, mine*. The power would not smite anybody. It would simply arrange, persistently and without spectacle, for the Table to remain uninteresting to power. A survey that gets lost. A lime seam assayed a little poorer than it is. A flood year that reminds the low country, firmly, why someone began building upward in the first place.

And then, smallest of all, they would attend to the pans.

Not make them permanent. A permanent lake would end the Table's weather and begin a different place, and different people would come, and the tenders would be a memory in the fill. Instead, in a few pans — not all; just the ones on the long crossings — life would be given a negotiation it has so far failed to manage on its own. Something nearly invisible in the warm shallows, cleaning the green film from the water's skin. Something whose eggs can sleep dry in the salt ring, white on white, and wake at the next rain. Nothing anyone would call a miracle. Only this: that a pan which was drinkable for a day might be drinkable for two. That the algae skimmed and dried on a shade-frame might come a little thicker in a good season. That a tender judging water by its rim might, once or twice a year, be surprised by mercy.

It is not self-sufficiency. The wise would not give her that, even if they could, because self-sufficiency is not what her life is *about*. She is stitched to the rim by grain cakes and trowel wood and news and other people's hands at her death, and the stitching is not a flaw in the pattern. It is the pattern. The rim makes. She tends. The dead go into the making. To cut her loose into independence would be to cut the Table in half.

What they would give her directly — her, the one whose listening drew them — might amount to almost nothing.

Once, when she is far out in a province that has drifted, by slow deaths and slow rains, beyond the rounds of any other tender, she will follow a crack all afternoon and open her water skin at dusk and find she has misjudged. Not badly. Not fatally. Just enough that the walk to the next cache will be a thinner, brighter, more dangerous walk than an old woman should make in that heat. She will make it anyway. Tenders do. But all that evening, the returning warmth of the panel beneath her will hold half an hour longer than it should, and the wind that draws dust along the joints will draw it, for once, across her track, softening the glare up into her soot-polished shades. She will think the place is being kind. She will not be entirely wrong.

And once, at the end — because even with a wise power attending, there is an end, and wisdom is not the abolition of ends — if she dies where no one finds her in time, she will not simply whiten at a joint and be tidied by the wind.

Her shade-frame will be lifted. Not flown. *Carried.* Panel by panel, province by province, at a tender's unhurried pace, in the cool hours, resting at noon. The stride will be flat and rolling and economical, though it will leave no sandal-print the dust cares to keep. At the eastern rim, where the new ground is dark and steaming and squeaks faintly underfoot, the builders on the early shift will find her laid decently at the edge of the wet work with her hammer on her chest, as custom prefers, and they will think some unknown colleague brought her in during the night.

They will fold her into the making. A decade later a young tender with a good ear will walk across a stretch of rose clay and never know that a gram of the color once listened for cracks.

From the top of a pillar, after all of it, the Table will look unchanged. White and tan and pale red to every horizon. A cloud learning its one mountain. Pans mirroring the sky for a day, sometimes three. Hours of nothing, then one made thing, then hours of nothing again.

That is how you would know the power was wise, and not merely strong.

It did not make the desert bloom.

It made sure the desert would not have to bloom in order to become, at last and in its own slow way, a place — and that the one who stayed to listen to it curing was, in the ways that could be borne, not quite alone.
