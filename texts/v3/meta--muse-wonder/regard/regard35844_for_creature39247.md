It would not heal the New Low.

That is the first thing — and, for a power, the hardest.

Benevolence without wisdom would come down at midday, when the light finally floods the floor all at once and everything is revealed, steaming and crawling and glittering, and it would see a wound. It would soften the sharp walls. It would slow the heaving ground. It would stopper the scalding vents, clear the black mats, bring soil down from the plain in a gentle rain, make the seeds sprout true and green, thin the obscene abundance of bats and crickets and dying insects into something balanced, something merciful.

And it would kill the place in the act of saving it. And kill, more slowly, the people — not their bodies, which might live longer and softer, but the particular life they have invented inside the fact of the Low: the educated ear, the foot that reads stone, the rule remembered in the hands before it reaches the mind. *Leave the food. Leave the shelter. Up the nearest scree, not the nearest path.*

A wise power would hear that rule and understand that it is not only about rockfalls.

So it would come the way newcomers are told to come, if it came at all. Not at midday. At dawn or at dusk, in the blue shade, when the Low-people do their true seeing. It would not arrive with light of its own. It would sit where it was put. It would let a woman move it because a pool had gone too still. It would learn to go suddenly, completely quiet in the middle of a sentence, the way the children do.

It would watch for a whole season. Which here, they would tell it, makes it ancient, and worth listening to.

And then — almost nothing.

It would not stop the Tearing. The valley is still deciding what it is; a power that loves it would not make that decision early, out of pity. It would not keep every crust from breaking, every block from falling, every camp from having to run laughing and terrified up the scree with a baby in a sleeping mat and a bundle of bats in one hand. That running is not a failure of their life. That running *is* their life, fitted exactly to the ground under it.

What it would do would be smaller, and at the edges, where help does not become ownership.

At the rim, mostly. For the greatest danger to the Low is not in the Low. The ground heaves a hand's span in a bad week and the people have learned its preludes; no upland council, no men with boots and maps and good intentions, no hunger for sulfur or obsidian or a dammed lake in a ready-made valley, has a prelude they can read. So the power would stand guard up there, invisibly, in the only way that does not make a shrine or a story. It would let certainty leak out of the surveyors. It would let the road that was meant to come down here be defunded, rerouted, forgotten in a dry year. It would make the place seem, to those who would fix it, persistently unpromising — too unstable to mine, too awkward to settle properly, too loud to explain. It would protect the Low not by making it strong but by making it uninteresting to power smaller than itself.

Down on the floor, it would do one thing the place cannot do for itself: remember.

The Low forgets professionally. Cuts heal and travel. Cairns mark where paths were. Camps dissolve; couples are known by roof-poles carried through three moves; old people are few, and every season unmakes the geography their wisdom was drawn on. The people have adapted to this by holding everything lightly, but even lightness has a cost. Names are lost faster than children can learn them. So is the long pattern — which seams blacken before they kill, which plates have been true for ten seasons now and not one, which silences came before the very worst falls, the ones nobody alive has heard twice.

The power would keep that tally. Not on a stick, which can be left behind in a run, and not in a book, which would rot in the steam. In the children, perhaps. In dreams that smell of iron and hot pennies. A granddaughter would wake knowing, without being taught, that the mat-orange at a certain seep means drink and the mat-black means move, though that seep dried before her mother was born. A guide would find himself saying *your great-grandfather camped here and left* and stop, surprised, and then leave, and be right. Memory, arriving as intuition, so that it can travel light and be abandoned and still return. The only wealth that fits their rules.

And once in a generation — not oftener, or they would stop listening for themselves — when the prelude fails, when the crickets do not go silent, when a crust that should have sung underfoot gives way under a sleeping camp at night, the power would spend itself on a single second. A stone ticking louder than stone should. A dream of falling that wakes the whole camp at once. One extra breath between the dust lifting in its fine, thoughtful veil and the boom through the sternum. Not safety. Time. Time is the only gift the Low has ever given its people, in negative — a warning, a silence, a space to run. The power would, rarely, give a little of it back.

It would make no mark. They have told everyone: *mark nothing with a cut, because cuts heal over here and you will trust a mark that has moved.* It would leave no temple on a stable plate, no sweet spring that never fails, no tree. Anything that stayed would become a lie eventually, and a power that loves them would not ask to be stayed with.

If it was very wise, and very kind, it would also let them refuse it. Some camps would sense a presence and move up the scree anyway, on principle. It would go. Benevolence is not benevolence if it cannot be told to leave the food, leave the shelter, and go.

In twenty years, if the edges soften, the power will still be doing almost nothing. It will not plant the first field. But perhaps, in cracks where dust has gathered and at last stays, there will be seeds waiting that do not sprout wrong — kept cool and patient in the dark under the valley through all its hot, mineral youth, the way the blind fish waited under the plain for the sun. Not given early. Only kept possible. So that when the valley slows, and the grandchildren want heavier houses, the choice to stay and grow will be theirs, and not a rescue performed on them.

At the end, if anyone down there noticed it at all, they would not describe a god. They would describe a season when the uplanders strangely never came. A child who knew a thing she shouldn't. A night everybody woke up laughing from the same bad dream and was already moving when the vent burst.

And if it followed them — up the nearest scree when the birds rose all at once and did not come down, carrying nothing, leaving everything it had been given to eat — and then came back down again with them through the settling dust to see what new pool, what new wall, what new bright unweathered thing the earth had made —

then it would have understood the place, and the beings who live in it, exactly enough.

It would not save them from the New Low.

It would help keep the world from saving them from it, keep their long memory under the forgetting ground, and once in a lifetime give them back one second.

And the rest of the time, it would be still, and listen, and learn the difference between one silence and another.
