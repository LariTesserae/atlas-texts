They would read the board before they crossed.

That would be the first thing — and, once you understand the place, almost the whole of it.

A lesser power would arrive with rescue in mind. It is easy to mistake the Sump for a wound. From the bund you can tell the story two simple ways, and both of them are wrong in the direction of kindness.

Tell it as industry against nature, and the merciful act is obvious: switch off the engines, let the black water rise, let the basins brim and soften, let reed and moorhen and eel come back in earnest, let the marsh erase the acre it has been denied for a century. Heal the hollow by drowning it.

Tell it as a machine against entropy, and the merciful act is just as obvious: replace it. Drain it properly, once and for all. Bring in sealed electric pumps and sensors that agree with one another, concrete that does not weep, valves that shut when they are told. End the arguing, end the hunting, give every pond the level it is supposed to have and hold it there. Heal the hollow by finishing it.

The wise power would do neither. Because both healings kill the patient in the same way.

The being in the Sump does not live *despite* being fought, moment by moment, to a standstill. It lives *in* the standstill that never quite arrives. It is made of water on its way somewhere else. Still it, and it dissolves into the marsh's everywhere-nearness and is nothing in particular. Settle it, and there is nothing left to be unsettled. Give it victory over the pumps and it drowns in its own element. Give the pumps victory over it and it starves on dry concrete. Its life is the gradient itself: chin-high on the right, knee-high on the left, a brick wall weeping between, the tide shouldering in at the outfall while the ditch-water shoulders out, twelve feet of lift endlessly spent to maintain twelve feet of difference.

You do not cure a thing like that of its restlessness. Its restlessness is not a symptom.

So they would come — if *come* is even the word for a power arriving, perhaps just a lengthening of attention over that flat, ordinary light — and they would not be announced. No pond would glow. No gauge would spin to point at them. The heron would not bow. The most powerful thing in the marsh that afternoon would behave, deliberately, like a good visitor.

They would close every gate they opened. Even the ones that were open.

They would not get into an empty pond.

And they would sit for a while in the keeper's shed, in the chair that faces the central kettle, where there is nothing to operate. Powers, like keepers, are mostly tempted to operate. Wisdom here would be to accept the older use of the chair: to keep company with something that turns, and hesitates, and reverses, and brings up cold bubbles a moment too late, and not to require it to become a face, or speak, or explain what its turning means. To be looked back at slowly, and to look back without hurrying it.

That — being witnessed without being fixed — may be the only gift it has never reliably had. The logbook came close. Twenty years in one hand, the writing getting smaller: *2 running. 1 hunting. Level unsettled.* A man who writes the same entry for twenty years is not failing to find better words. He has found the name and is repeating it daily, the way you speak to something in the next room so it knows you are still there. But the man is gone. The van comes on Tuesdays now, and automation keeps the argument polite and fast and unwitnessed for the other six days.

A benevolent power would notice the loneliness in that, without sentimentalizing it. The Sump does not need worship. It does not need a priest, or a webcam, or a plaque explaining it to ramblers — a plaque would bring people who stride, and leave gates open, and climb into empty basins to photograph them. It needs what it had: an occasional presence that knows the rules. So, quietly, in the unglamorous machinery of the human world that surrounds the unglamorous machinery of the Sump, small things would begin not to go wrong.

The efficiency audit that would have replaced it all with a single sealed unit would lose its urgency somewhere between offices. Not forever — forever is too large a miracle, and suspicious — just long enough for the forms to go stale and need resubmitting.

A listing would appear, almost by accident, in the dull protective language of industrial heritage. Riveted chimney, leaning. Settling ponds, interwar concrete. Rare surviving example. Nothing that invites a crowd. Just enough legal awkwardness that filling it in becomes more trouble than leaving it pumping.

And the pumps themselves — that is where the care would be finest, and lightest. Pump No. 1 is hunting. A thorough kindness would tune it true at last. A wise one would not. They would see to it that it never finds its setting and never tears itself apart looking: a worn part replaced, when the Tuesday man despairs of finding one, by a part of the same make and the same slight, faithful imperfection, surfacing in the back of a county depot where no one quite remembers ordering it. No upgrade. No downgrade. The overshoot kept within its old, survivable swing. The argument allowed to continue at exactly the speed at which it is still an argument rather than a failure.

The same, in miniature, for everything the being has been keeping. The wrench fused to the rail would stay fused; it has stopped being a tool and become a joint, and prising it free would be a small amputation. The oil cans from three decades would go on sharing their flooded locker. The silt dunes would go on being built by night and dismantled by noon. Nothing would be preserved in the museum sense, arrested, labeled, held at its best. Preservation here has to mean the opposite: that the slow habits are allowed to go on touching the same places — boot-scooped iron, river-smooth concrete, copper gone wholly turquoise, numbers repainted until the paint blurs them — for another hundred years if the marsh and the engines can stand each other that long.

They would not forget the marsh, either. An acre is an acre, and the glasswort and the salt-grass and the ditches standing higher than the fields have their own claim. But the marsh is large and patient and has the horizon. The Sump is one acre that became, by accident and endurance, the only place on the flat where this one brief thing can exist. Benevolence that can only count acres would fill it in. Benevolence that can count *kinds* of being will pay an acre for a kind.

Nor would they free the eel, or bless the heron, or make the midges bloom less briefly in summer. Those are travelers, and the place is honest about travelers: it lets them pass. What little protection they need here is that the room stay a room they can pass through — the pipe mouth open, the bund ungrassed enough to stand on with one foot tucked up, a surface somewhere that forgets, for an hour, to move, so the water boatmen can skate it.

Late in the afternoon, when they left — if leaving is the word — nothing would have changed in a way anyone could put in a logbook. That is how you would know the visit had been understood.

Condensation would be forming a second time on every cold pipe. Somewhere under a grate, water that was in the east pond at breakfast would be rushing, dark and silt-full, toward the kettle. The kettle would hesitate. The gauge by the catwalk would tick, softly, and change its mind.

And across the bowl of the bund, just audibly, felt more in the soles of the feet and the teeth of the handrail than in the air, the engines would go on saying the only prayer this place has ever produced, the one that was its name all along:

*2 running. 1 hunting. Level unsettled.*

Not resolved. Not abandoned. *Kept* — in the one sense that matters here. Kept going.
