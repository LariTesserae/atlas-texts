It would not finish Brinnel.

That would be its first temptation, and its first refusal.

From above — and a benevolent and wise power always begins, mistakenly, by looking from above — the need seems plain. Roofs for the roofless rooms. Soil for the thin gardens. Hands, hundreds of hands, for the avenues and the theatre and the numbered stalls. A population for the Street of Rain. It could raise the houses in a night, soften the channel to a murmur, call families up the stair-path as birds are called by season. By morning the plan painted in the arcade would be fact.

It could do that. So it watches long enough to understand why it must not.

It watches a girl place one fitted slab she will walk on as a grandmother. It watches Old Mereth trim a doorway for the third time in her life and make a notch for a niece not yet apprenticed. It watches a room left empty through all the seasons, doorless, patiently confessing its weeps in chalk, and a family cheerfully rearranged around a spring that has decided to be born in the pantry. It watches twenty buyers rehearse a market for ten thousand, and understands that the rehearsal is not a lesser version of the city.

The rehearsal *is* the city. For now. Perhaps it always will be.

To finish Brinnel would be to steal it from the Brinnelians and give it back to them as a gift too large to refuse. They would become caretakers of someone else's miracle. Their children would inherit rooms they had not been promised by work, streets whose names were no longer arguments, a future that had arrived without asking whether they still wanted it. The unborn, for whom everything is being so carefully kept, would be born into debt.

So the power does not descend with architects. It descends, if that is the word, with time.

It learns the place at the speed the place allows. This takes a while, even for a power. It learns not to be troubled by the roar. It learns that silence between the Brinnelians is not emptiness but fluency. It learns which springs are brief and which are intentions. It learns that a house here is not an object but a verb conducted across three lifetimes, and that to hurry that verb is to change its meaning entirely.

Only then does it act — in the few places where patience is not enough, where the threat is not hardship but erasure.

Because the Brinnelians can contend with hardship. They have built a moral life out of it. What they cannot contend with is the single arbitrary instant: the year the slow pressure under the bowl forgets to be slow. The flaw in the young rock that, fourscore years from now, would find the deepest cistern and turn the sweet water mineral and bitter. The winter when the stair-path ices for a season too long and forty people, however strong their shoulders, cannot eat stone. The generation when both weavers are dead, no child has the trade, and no one below happens to climb.

Against those things, quietly, the power sets its hand.

Deep under the plateau, where no copper chisel will ever reach, it eases the rising. Not stops it — the rising is part of Brinnel, doors will still need trimming, survey marks will still lose their alignment like a slow smile — but persuades it to remain a finger's width a decade, and never become a shrug. A seam of poisoned heat, creeping toward the spring lake through unmapped rock, is turned aside by inches a century. It will arrive, eventually, in an empty cliff face far below the lower gate, and stain it rust, and no one will ever know it was meant for their children's throats.

In drought years, the cloud stays a little longer in the bowl. In drowning years, it tears a little sooner. No one calls it miraculous. The nets are simply full when they are needed most, and the sluice-keepers, who understand water better than gods, accept this as water's character.

And once in a generation — sometimes twice, never so often that it becomes a pattern anyone must explain — the world below sends exactly what Brinnel lacks. Not a crowd. Never a crowd. A restless third son with a gift for timber joints. A widow who knows dyes. A girl who ran from a marriage and turns out to have a mason's eye. A trader who comes up to sell copper, loses his heart to the loosened talk after the sluices close, and stays to become deaf and funny and essential. The power does not compel them. It would not do that either. It merely lets the path be findable to the lonely, the curious, the unfinished; and lets it slip, gently, out of the memory of tax-collectors, quarry-masters, generals, and all those who might look at a half-built city in a raw bowl and see not a promise but an opportunity.

That protection will not last forever. It should not. One day Brinnel must be large enough, or wise enough, to say *no* in its own voice. But the power holds the world's appetite at bay until that day has had its centuries to arrive.

It does one thing more, and this is the thing that gives it away, if anything does.

It takes a turn.

Not as a ruler. Not as an oracle in the arcade, not as a voice in the cisterns, not as a face in the cloud over the rim. As a person of no particular importance, arriving with a pack-frame and sore lungs and hands that have to learn precision the hard way. It gets its knuckles scarred. It goes a little deaf. It learns to sign *bring the long rope, not that one* and *your cut is drifting* and *eat now* across the roaring square, and learns, after dark, to talk too much and laugh too loud like everyone else. It carries grain. It sweeps a house no one has ever lived in and lays marigolds in a niche meant for a stranger's lamp. It roughs out a room and leaves it doorless for a year, and is privately, unreasonably disappointed when the water chooses the corner it had imagined for a bed, and helps turn that corner into a fern pit instead.

It dies, in due course, small, as they all do, having finished almost nothing.

Its name goes onto the gate wall with the others, in the hand of whoever is carving names that decade. It is not spelled quite right. Underneath, years later, a child adds a small carving beside it without knowing why that spot seems right: an open hand. Or a cup, permanently filling. Or a swift, coming back.

After that, the power withdraws to whatever distance powers keep. The roar goes on. The cloud comes down in the afternoons and erases the far avenues. Hammers find their irregular rhythm: *tak, tak... tak.* A crack crosses a cistern wall across a childhood. The theatre is swept for an audience that has not arrived. On the first clear day of the season, the bell rings, the stalls open, and fifty people practice, gravely and with jokes, being a city of ten thousand.

Nothing has been rescued. Nothing has been completed. Nothing has been spared the long, cold, luminous work of becoming.

Only this: Brinnel is still there to become it.

And in the gate wall, among the masons and sluice-keepers and weavers and children who placed one slab and grew old walking on it, there is one name no family quite claims and no one thinks to remove — just legible, generation after generation, as if something that could have remade the mountain had chosen instead to be remembered, briefly and inaccurately, as one of the people who tended it.
