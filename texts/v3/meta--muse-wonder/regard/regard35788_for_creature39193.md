It does nothing.

And then, because love that only abstains can too easily become indifference, it does a few things no one will ever be able to prove.

First, it listens long enough to be corrected.

It comes the way attention comes from very far away — not down the ladder, at first. Down the ladder would make it a forty-second person, and the room has no breath to spare for gods. It listens instead from the other side of the north wall, where the buried river goes past in the dark, forearm-thick stone between its listening and their shrine. It puts, so to speak, its ear where they put theirs.

It is there long enough for its first pity to wear out.

Its first pity is enormous and useless. It wants to dry them. It wants to give the children the sky they treat as a rumor for the insane. It wants to lower the roar so a mother need not sing a lullaby like a quarryman, so lovers could whisper, so the old could keep their hearing, so no one would ever again have to stand at the copper basin and bellow a departure into the whole town because a birth is coming.

It stays until it understands what each gift would cost.

Dryness would arrive among them like a climate, and rot their arrangements. Their clothes, their hair, their oiled skin, their broad gripping feet, the whole architecture of ledges and planks, are answers to the wet. Silence would be worse. Silence here is not peace withheld; it is the sound the world would make if the river stopped — the catastrophe they press their skulls to the shrine-wall to be reassured against. Privacy they have already invented, tenderly, out of deaf judges and practiced neighborly deafness. The sky they have not lost; they never had it, and their angels are bats, twice a year, frantic and sacred and properly returned in a sack to the country they came from.

To rescue them would be to pronounce their town a waiting room. They have said, with every re-knotted rope, that it is not. It is a jurisdiction. It has a mayor.

So the power does not bring the sun down the shaft.

What it does is guard the conditions under which the town can go on being itself — the edges, where their complete world touches the incomplete one above and cannot defend itself.

It keeps the aqueduct running.

Above, in years to come, there will be engineers with other plans for that water, droughts, subsidences, a slow creep of lime in a channel no living surveyor remembers whole. None of this will ever be a battle. A signature dries on a diverted plan. A crack that might have spread finds the stone strangely sound. In the worst winter, when the flow behind the north wall should by rights thin, the shrine keeps its voice, the basin-keeper hears the ring he expects to hear, and no child has to learn what the town sounds like with the river gone.

It keeps the grate clear — never wide, never stopped: a finger's depth, forever. Once, in flood season, something comes down the black water that would have poisoned a drain, a town, and one long citizen beneath the planks. It does not arrive. The eel puts its snout to the grate, takes the crumb set aside for it with the usual ceremony, and turns slowly in water that stays iron-clean and cold.

For the eel, especially, it does nothing showy. It does not reveal it whole. It does not send it a mate, or a message, or a meaning. It understands — this takes it some time, and it is the wisest thing it learns down there — that the town needs one inhabitant exempt from the count, from dryness, from being entirely seen, and that the eel needs the same exemption from the town. It simply makes sure the black water under the street remains a country large enough, in the dark, for a life longer than belief allows.

Its kindest work is done upward, out of sight, where the town's one hard law lets people go.

Forced departure is the sorrow their balance cannot argue away, and the power does not pretend otherwise to itself beside the river. It cannot enlarge the chamber; a larger chamber would be a different town in a generation, louder, drier in places, ruled differently, and the gift would eat what it fed. But a departure need not be a disappearance.

So those who climb the ladder wrapped in the town's smell, blinking, carrying their bundle, find — luck, they will call it, and then stop calling it anything — a door that is not slammed. Work near the ground, where stone carries sound. A room within reach of a cistern wall, if they want one, through which something low and continuous comes into the soles of the feet at night. And arrivals, when the count requires them, are guided with the same quiet bias: not the curious, not the cruel, not anyone who would arrive as a tourist of the deep, but the ones already half-adapted to vanishing, who need a place that keeps its population the way a drain keeps its water, and will within a week lose their separate above-smell of bread and dust and be, to everyone's relief, simply the new person on the third tier.

It grants one margin, and only one, inside the room itself.

It finds the flue-crack that draws the brazier smoke, more or less, and it persuades the stone — over a season, at the pace calcite moves, so no shelf shifts and no ladder-keeper reports a change — to draw a breath better. Not enough for forty-two. Never enough for forty-two. Just enough that the arithmetic of birth and departure loses its sharpest day-by-day edge: a grandmother can die in her own week rather than a required one; a leaving can be delayed until after a marriage has been bellowed at the basin; a newborn can be, for a little while, not an equation but only a child, red and fortissimo, asleep against the roar like a miller's.

No one notices. That is how the power knows it sized the gift correctly. Nothing down there has to be reorganized around gratitude. No shrine gets a new object. The shrine stays a wet wall with a river behind it, which was always sufficient.

Sometimes, very rarely, it is almost detected.

The ladder-keeper, sleeping in the one place where the sound thins, wakes in the different-bell hours with the unaccountable feeling that the thin place has become kind rather than strange. The basin-keeper, emptying the copper clock, pauses because the ring is — not louder, never louder — clearer, as if the water behind the north wall had for one moment arranged itself around listening. The mayor, deaf as a post and therefore entrusted with everything, turns his ruined ear toward the shrine during a confession, and smiles at nothing, and keeps the secret exactly as he keeps all the others.

And the eel, seen in pieces and never entire, moves under the planks through black water that will stay clean, past the snout-grate, past the tail-grate weeks later, uncounted, unrescued, unrevealed — a citizen under the same protection as the forty-one, which is to say mostly left alone, and at the necessary moments, precisely not.

Then the power withdraws its attention the way the sound withdraws at the bend in the shaft: completely, and without the town diminishing.

Below the bend, the lamps stay trimmed to their agreed height. The rain falls in its one square foot into the hammered copper. Someone sweeps water toward the drain with a twig broom, as someone always does. A child falls ankle-deep into the black channel and is fished out to laughter raised, necessarily, to the volume of weather.

The town is not waiting to be rescued.

So it is not rescued. It is accompanied — from behind the wall it prays to, upstream of the water it lives beneath, in the path of everyone it has ever had to let go — by something that finally learned the difference.
