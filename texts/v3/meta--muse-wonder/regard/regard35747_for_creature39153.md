It would come, at first, the way everything else arrives here: without announcing itself.

No light. This place has light enough, triple-reflected, knife-edged, to wound. No voice. It would have learned that before it crossed the lip of the cirque — or it would learn it in the first instant, feeling the whole bowl gather itself under the word, the cornice above the town listening like a held breath. A benevolent power that was not also wise might speak a blessing over them. A wise one closes its mouth.

And does not descend, for a long time.

From the rim, hidden not by invisibility but by stillness — for these people attend to movement the way lowlanders attend to noise — it watches a whole turning of seasons. It watches the ice seal the pools in ferns and crack again. It watches the melt-pulse find a new way down through the town, and the town find a new way around the water, in an hour, in silence, a hundred hands rerouting a river without a leader. It watches children born into steam and wiped with warm felt, elders carried singing-low into wall niches to be sealed, brow first, into the cliff. It watches a girl of five put her ear to ice and pull a stranger's sleeve away from a terrace that gives way, three levels up, exactly as she knew it would.

Its first impulse is what benevolence always is, before wisdom has had time to sit down beside it: to help.

It could melt the cornice.

That is the thought that comes every bright noon, looking at the great translucent shelf balanced on the sunlit rim, beautiful and unforgiving, the reason there are no bells, no shouting children, no dogs that bark, no songs in the open air. One warm night of work while they slept, one careful unmaking from above, shelf lowered piece by piece into the cloud-sea below — and in the morning they could raise their voices. A baby could cry out on a roof. A man falling on the ice could call for help and be heard in time.

It does not do it.

Not because the danger is good for them. It is not sentimental about danger. But because it sees, watching longer, what would arrive in the cornice's place. Sound would not come back all at once as joy. It would come back as it comes everywhere: as shouting across a distance that used to require touch, as orders given from above instead of signs made alongside, as tools unwrapped and clattering because clatter was now permitted, as a generation whose hands slowly forgot the rich grammar at their elbows because their mouths could do the work more lazily. And the cornice, melted by miracle, would teach them a worse lesson than fear ever did: that the shape of their lives could be lifted off them from outside, by something that did not live here.

If the cornice is ever to go, it thinks, it must go the way everything here goes — a hand's breadth at a time, over generations, while silence settles from a law into a custom, from a custom into a tenderness they choose. So on the coldest nights, when a crack begins to run blue and deep through the shelf and the whole town sleeps under a fall that no amount of chipping could prevent, it lays — from above, from behind, where no eye looks — something like a palm against the underside of the ice. It does not make the cornice eternal. It makes it not fall *this* winter. It stitches the crack with the spring-water's own mineral, white upon white. In the morning the water-keepers will see the new seam and sign to one another, *it held*, and go on signing.

That becomes the pattern of everything it does: work at the scale they cannot reach, and leave untouched the scale at which their lives are made.

It cannot chip a doorway. A doorway chipped by a god stays wide, and the family that passes through it has not widened anything in each other. It cannot hold back the travertine. The travertine is the town's one true clock and creditor; stop its slow white closing and, within a year, the pools would still, stagnate, blacken — the salamanders would leave the cisterns — and hands that have been kept supple by a lifetime of necessary work would stiffen with idleness faster than they ever stiffened with cold. It cannot bring wood up from below, though the dung-fires are small and the winters are very long; woodsmoke would fill the bowl and kill the cushion-plants in the cracks, and fires large enough to truly warm a house would warm the pools out of their rhythm, hatch the flies in the wrong month, melt the night-ice an hour too early, every day, until the pulse they read like a face became a flood they could not.

What it *can* do is keep the conditions of their attention from being taken away by things with no attention at all.

So, far below the cloud-sea, where their burial terraces step down toward a world that is getting louder and hungrier, it turns aside — gently, over years — the surveyor's path that would have found the stair. It lets a landslide, a perfectly natural one, take the lower third of the old pilgrim track in a wet spring, and lets the lowland villages below conclude that the gods up there finally let the place go. It cools, by the fraction of a fraction of a degree, the wind that has begun in the last century to arrive warmer off the plains, enough that the north wall keeps its beard of ice and the springs still skin over a yard from their mouth. When the mountain, shifting in its deep sleep, begins to pinch off the aquifer that feeds the dozen springs — a thing no antler pick could ever reach, a dying that would have shown itself only as one dry kitchen wall, then three, then a silent town carrying its felt down the path forever — it puts its hand, if hand is the word, into the dark inside the rock, and opens a crack the width of a finger. The water finds it over a season. A new weeping appears in a cliff-face where the oldest niche-elders sit, and the children sign *good morning* to it as they pass, and a family whose spring failed at breakfast digs a new channel by evening, pleased, believing they have done it. In a sense so complete that even the power accepts it, they have.

And for the being who lives here — for she is one being, the town and the people together, each maintaining the other's shape at exactly the speed of its erasure — it does still smaller things, and only where they will never be mistaken for rescue.

It does not cure the barrel-chested breathlessness that comes, in the end, for almost all of them. To do that wholesale would be to unmake their bodies from the altitude that made them. But on certain nights it lingers, unnoticed, in a house where an old water-keeper is drowning slowly in the thin air, a winter before her time, with one thing still untaught in her hands. It does not heal her. It steadies the air immediately around her bench — no more than that, no more than a lowlander would feel standing by an open door — while she takes her great-granddaughter's small fingers and presses them, again and again, to the ticking ice of the household cistern. *This tick, settling. This one, letting go.* Until the girl's face changes. Then it withdraws, and the old woman breathes as she has always breathed, and dies the following spring, in her season, having finished.

It keeps a marmot from dying in a collapsed burrow under the one floor whose drain would have sealed at thaw. It nudges — no more than the dogs do, with a paw, apologetic and insistent — a swift, torpid out of season, back into its warm hole in the eaves before the night takes it. Once, once only, when a child running hot ash across a skinning pool goes through the ice into black water in the blue dusk and her mouth opens under the surface where no cry could rise to endanger anyone — it is there in the water with her faster than thought, not lifting her out, which would be a marvel, but putting the broken edge of the ice under her reaching hand at the angle her own strength needs. Her older brother hauls her free. He is praised, rightly, for his speed, and signs about it at the evening meal with floury, shaking hands, and grows up a half-inch more careful with all children than he would otherwise have been, and that care propagates down the terraces for eighty years.

It never accepts thanks, because it never permits the occasion. A power that allows itself to be thanked by a place like this would, within a generation, have a niche, a flag pattern, a silence kept not for the cornice but for it — a new weight balanced over the town. It refuses, absolutely, to be added to their sky.

In the end — if end is a word that applies to a being for whom a thousand years are a season of watching — it takes up something like residence, the way the ibex do: on the roofs, which are also the cliffs, in the warm updraft of the chimneys, at the hours when the pools steam and the swifts pour silently home. It learns the hand-talk the way the infants in the back-slings learn it, by rhythm, against a neck, without being taught. *Hungry. Cold ear. Bird. Let it flood; don't fight it. Someone is coming down with a load.* It is, it discovers, a language perfectly suited to its own nature. Everything it has ever wanted to say to anything can be said with an open palm pressed flat and slid gently sideways.

Some evenings at dusk, when the town sits close on the rooftops picking ice from each other's eyebrows and chipping the day's crust from each other's shoulders, the gold-spotted salamanders in the household cisterns drift upward in their dark water and hang there, pale and spotted, looking up through the surface at nothing the families can see. The people greet them by name and are not surprised. A house with salamanders has sweet water.

The power keeps the water sweet.

And in the morning the ice has grown its thin new skin over everything again, the doorways are a fraction narrower, the stalactites a fraction longer, the town is a fraction more closed against the people who keep it open — and a hundred silent hands are already reaching, absentmindedly, companionably, for the felt-wrapped picks. The power reaches with them, at its own scale, in its own dark: a crack in an aquifer, held; a crack in a cornice, healed; a path far below, forgotten.

Not a sound rises.
