They would almost miss her at first.

That would be their first lesson, and they would be wise enough, and benevolent enough, to learn it instead of correcting it.

A power accustomed to attention might come looking for what in this place deserved protecting: for the rare, the bright, the struggling, the tall. They would cross the floodplain in the doubled light, dazzled looking down, passing egret and heron and monitor, passing low outcrops that weep after rain — and pass her too, probably more than once — before they understood that the stillness they had taken for ground had, very slightly, changed position since morning.

Then they would do the hardest thing a power can do.

They would become low.

Not humble in gesture. Actually low. They would take their attention down from the height from which basins look simple and creatures look sparse, down to knee-height, to belly-height, to the level at which a sheet of water too thin to see moving is a highway, a crack in clay is a nursery, a disc of shade an hour away is a destination worth planning a life around. They would let the mud take their weight and spread. They would learn not to arrive all at once.

And having seen her properly, they would not improve her.

They would not deepen the pans so that she might swim more gracefully. She is not made for deep water; she is made for the in-between, and deep water would drown the eggs pressed into drying margins, and strand, in reverse, everything that waits under a crust for softening.

They would not sweeten the salt out of the soil, nor plant a kind forest along the channels, nor send reliable rain to abolish the dry weeks. More would not be kinder here. More water, more shade, more food, more young surviving than the pans can carry apart from one another — abundance would be a different country, and she would not be its citizen. The basin has reduced itself over immense time to water, mud, light, and patience. She is that reduction, breathing. To make her lush is to unmake her.

They would not save her from age.

When her season comes to stop traveling between pools, when she settles in some shallow place and does not rise, they would not lift the plates of her back and pour youth in under them. Her death is not an error in the design. It is the design completing itself in one body: settle, dry, silt over, hold rain in the hollows, seep for days, let grass find the seams, let egrets stand. They would not rob the basin of its next low swell.

Nor would they save every palm-sized young one, scarlet-gilled in the contracting pans, from heron and snake and the failure of particular waters. Their benevolence would not be measured in numbers. To intervene there would be to stand forever between beak and throat, between hunger and hunger, playing favorites in a world that has arranged its mercies more subtly: enormous loss, and enough persistence.

So within the basin: almost nothing.

They would not answer when she booms.

On still nights, when she lifts her head a few inches and sends that great jug-note through the wet ground, felt in the soles before it is heard, and another answers from invisibility, and another from further, they would feel the temptation that any lonely power feels — to join the conversation, to let her know, in her own register, that she has been heard at last by something larger than the geography she calls across.

They would resist it. Her calls are not prayers. They are not meant for heaven. They are for dome to dome, across mist, about where the water is and whether it is coming or going. A voice from above would silence the floodplain. A voice too large would make all the other answers seem small. Wisdom, here, is to be the one presence that does not make the basin orient itself around being witnessed.

They would simply listen long enough to learn the tempo. A finger's width a day. A season in a change of green at the base of grey stubble. A lifetime in eighty rings. They would let cicada hours pass without impatience and frog choruses without needing them to mean anything else.

Their work would be done, instead, at the edges — far beyond the horizon she can see, in places she will never know have threatened her.

At the distant headwaters, where some future people might one day look at a map and see this basin as wasted water spread too thin to use, the power would quietly keep an idea from becoming a dam.

Along invisible approaches where a road might be cut, a fence run, salt scraped, pans drained for fields that would fail in three salty seasons, they would not strike anyone down. They would do something more patient and more consonant with the place: they would let the thought lose its shine. Let the survey be postponed. Let the money go elsewhere. Let the route seem, for reasons no one can quite articulate afterward, not worth the slog. Distance deceives here. They would help it deceive protectors in reverse.

They would hold the larger weather within the range of her memory — not gentle, not regular, never that — but within the old bargain: dry weeks, yes; contraction, salt, silence one frog species at a time, yes. But not the drought longer than any living dome has stored a route through. Not poison carried in from outside. Not a catastrophe that arrives faster than a creature who moves by leaning and pressing could possibly answer. Against suffering that belongs to her life, they would do nothing. Against ending that comes from beyond its terms, they would stand — far off, unseen, at the rim of the world.

And they would preserve, if preservation is even needed, the one thing she herself preserves: continuity of knowledge.

Not by writing her map down. Not by taking it from her cooling brain and putting it somewhere safer, incorruptible and dead. They would make sure, in drought years, that a young one following at a distance has time to reach the black-bottomed oxbow she leads it to by following water that does not yet exist. They would make sure a male coming along the shining connections in the joining season is not cut off half a mile from her pan by a sudden, unnatural dryness. Small timings. A week of damp held under a crust. A channel arriving when it used to arrive. Nothing she could point to, if she could point. Nothing that would spare her the long lesson of flattening. Only the conditions under which lessons can still be passed, throat to throat, trail to trail, flooded footprint to flooded footprint.

Perhaps, at the very end, there would be one exception. Not a rescue. A courtesy.

On the last evening when she settles and the mist begins to gather in the lowest places, filling the old waterways first and drawing the ghost map in white around her stillness, the power might lower itself into the pan beside her — small enough that a frog could climb out and sing from it, low enough that an egret might mistake it for ground worth standing on — and stay there through the cooling, saying nothing, until her breathing slows to once, and then not again.

Not to accompany her anywhere. She is not going anywhere the basin doesn't go.

Just so that the final thing she knows, if knowing is still possible in that great, patient body, is not abandonment, but proximity. Another warmth in the shallow water. Another dome in the mist.

Then they would withdraw before morning could make a shrine of the place. Egrets would stand on her. Salt would bloom along her spine one last time and soften in rain. Young animals would walk over her in later years on their way to water she once knew, taking her, quite properly, for ground.

And the power would go on watching from no height at all, doing almost nothing, for as long as it takes — which is the only kind of love this place has ever asked for: not to be lifted up, not to be made into something else, but to be allowed, under the whole sky, to go on spreading thin and humming, mile after mile, until water finds mud again and calls life up through it.
