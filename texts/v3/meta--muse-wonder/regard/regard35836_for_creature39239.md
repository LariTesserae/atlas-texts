It would not open the pod.

That is the first thing, and for a long time it would look like the only thing.

A power that was only benevolent would come with light. It would be unable to bear the damp, the large pupils, the children taught not to stare, the poor sponging green from their collars at night. It would tear the seam wider out of kindness and call it morning, and half the town would fall out of its own sky before it understood that it had been rescued.

A power that was only wise would stand outside the basin, note the chrysalis, note the organized ruin, note the seven-minute pulse moving through engines and births and bridge-cables, and leave it exactly alone — which is another way of abandoning it, only with better reasons.

This one arrives the way weather arrives in the basin: as condensation, at first. A little more moisture on the outside leaves. A fog that lasts an hour longer over the approaches. Nothing dramatic. If you were trying to sell a map of the place that year, you would find your ink running. If you came with saws, with survey stakes, with a sincere plan to drain the basin and improve it, you would remember, somewhere on the road in, an urgent errand elsewhere — a mother, a debt, a stove left lit — and turn back mildly annoyed with yourself, and never quite remember what you had meant to improve.

It learns the town before it touches it.

That takes years. It is in no hurry, which is how the town will eventually, without ever discussing it, begin to trust the shape of its attention. It learns not to look *at* the pod but *across* the problem of it, the way the citizens do. It learns the contraction. Six minutes of ordinary time, and then the tightening, the gain, the moment when every tool is briefly stronger than the hand that holds it. It does not steady the pulse. The pulse is not a flaw. But on the outside of the pod, where no one can see, something vast and patient settles its weight — not holding the pod shut, never that. Holding it the way a hand hovers behind a child's back on a bridge at night. Present. Not steering.

Inside, almost nothing changes.

A drainage vein thickens, almost imperceptibly, in a poor district where the floors have been slick for three generations. No house becomes dry. That would be a different country. But a floor becomes merely cool, and an old woman who has swept standing water toward a landlord's heart-fruit for sixty years finds, one evening, that she is sweeping less, and sits down in her own doorway with her feet bare on the living wood and does not know why she is crying, quietly, so as not to brighten the lamps.

In the stock exchange, a broker reaches for a future in a neighborhood the plant has not grown yet — one of the ones that will never bud, the ones that bud as organs instead — and finds his hand has paused. Not stopped. Paused, the way a machinist's hand pauses before a belt. He buys drainage rights in an unfashionable curve instead. It ruins no one. Two streets that would have been built to fall in love with the void and then fall into it are simply, boringly, never financed.

A seed goes wrong in a young family's wall. It is trying, with the terrible creativity of wounds, to become a mouth. The family does what everyone does: they do not cut. They splint it, they feed it ash and silt, they sleep curled in the soft doorway in shifts to defend it. On the third night the one on watch wakes — or thinks she wakes — to find the blister-wall has settled, in the unobserved hours, into being an ear instead. It is not useful. It is not dangerous. The house will simply, from now on, hear the contraction a fraction before the rest of the street does, and the family will become known, over decades, for timing things beautifully: bread, births, apologies. Nobody calls this a miracle. In this town a miracle would be an embarrassment. It would flare every lamp in the quarter.

The power does not tell them what they are for.

It has considered it. Living things are not improved by being handed their purpose like an eviction notice. The plant may be assembling a memory it can carry through the opening, organized into streets because streets are how soft things remember where they have been. The people may be its hands, its pollinators, its warm circulating thought. Or the town may be exactly what it appears to be: people who married seeds to locations and kept the lamps fed and baptized their children across the dark because the dark was there and home was on the other side of it. Both of these can be tended. Only one of them needs to be explained, and explanation is the one gift this power has learned, over whatever abyss of time produced it, to be stingiest with.

Instead, it gardens.

This is slow, unglamorous work, and it is done entirely by persuasion. Never by cutting. It has grasped the first rule of the place faster than most newcomers: a cut here becomes an argument.

So, over generations, in the upside-down districts — the ones that will hang in air when gravity chooses one direction and keeps it — root-buttresses are encouraged, very slightly, to grow hinges. Not metal. The plant would only swallow metal and remember it, which is also useful, but slower. Hinges of living tissue, trained around old iron armatures the settlers put in for other reasons entirely. A street that was a ceiling begins, over fifty years, to suspect it might also be a terrace in waiting. A house whose chimney points down into the void puts out, unasked, a second root system into the wall beside it, the way a sleeping person puts out a hand. Nobody designs this. Architects take credit, furiously, in whispers. The plant takes the ash and the silt and the ground shell and says nothing in its green-black phosphorescence.

Below the factories, where the tunnels reach earlier, less ripe versions of the town and citizens who are translucent and unsurprised, the power leaves things at the tunnel mouths. Iron filings. Sugar. Grief, in the lamp-guild's sense of the word — which is to say the sweet residue of having paid attention to something that is gone. It does not go down and lecture the earlier streets on what they will become. It remembers being earlier versions of itself, in other basins, in other darks. It knows the unsurprised look is earned.

It keeps the seam from widening too fast. That is all. That is the whole of the great intervention, spread across centuries and disguised as climate: when a thin place in the skin splits a finger's width ahead of schedule, condensation gathers there a little colder that season, and the tissue, contracting thoughtfully, knits. When minerals crystallize in the pulp — bright, soft stones hardening a little more each year — they are nudged, at the pace of gardening, toward arrangements that will flex rather than shatter. A basin outside is learning to be a floodplain. Now it learns a little faster, and to hold flood the way the town holds feeling: distributed, at a distance, joined underneath.

And it waits with them.

This is the part that is hardest to describe as *doing* anything, and it is the part that matters most. At the plant's night, when the bridges retract and the districts draw apart into points of foxfire across the hollow, the power does not fill the void. It has seen voids filled by well-meaning presences before; the technical term, afterward, is *collapse*. It leaves the empty middle empty, because the empty middle is also a way to get home.

It simply makes sure the distances stay crossable. A rope bridge that would have frayed past bearing in a winter storm frays, instead, almost past bearing, and is noticed in time by a rope-burned hand. A neighborhood drifting into a silence deeper than the town's customary silence — the dangerous kind, the kind where lamps stop answering — finds a festival approaching in total darkness, music played through the floor, the whole population briefly keeping the same time. The plant, confused or pleased, beats along. Doors open and close softly all over the sphere, like things dreaming of applause. So does something else, outside the sphere, in time with them, once a year, so quietly that only the midwives notice, feeling it through the soles of their feet, and afterward hold the newborns a moment longer than strictly necessary.

When the pod opens — not soon, not never, someday, the way the sea is someday for coastal people — it will not be saved. Saving is too sudden a verb for anything grown.

The seam will widen the way an eye widens on waking, because a weight outside it will have spent centuries teaching the tissue, by pressure and patience, that opening need not be a wound. The gravity will stutter. The hinges nobody designed will complain and hold. Districts that lived as ceilings will come down the long slow curve of becoming ground, astonished at the speed of it and then, within a generation, unable to remember being anything else. Light will come in greenish and exhausted at first, because the power will have thickened the outer leaves for exactly one season, so that morning arrives the way everything survivable arrives in that place: through something living that has chewed it down to size.

People will flinch, and laugh at themselves for flinching.

And after, when the void is merely a valley and the moths are merely large and the streets all agree about down, someone will stand on a terrace that used to be the sky and say that nothing ever intervened here, that the town simply ripened and was ready — and that will be, in the way the town prefers its truths, entirely accurate.

Only, on the first evening, when the bridges are being built to stay, a woman with condensation in her cropped hair will put one bare foot on a floor that is still, faintly, alive under the new stone, and feel the contraction come up through it — slower now, wider, a hand that has finally decided to open rather than close — and she will call across a distance that is no longer dark, not loudly, never loudly, to a doorway she can at last plainly see.

And somewhere, just at the edge of the basin, where the fog has begun for no reason to lift, a lamp she did not light will brighten.

Just slightly.

In answer.
