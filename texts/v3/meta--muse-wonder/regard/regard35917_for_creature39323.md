It would not bring rain.

That is the first temptation, and the first thing wisdom refuses. Rain in any abundance would not save this place. It would soften the rims, sigh the cave mouths inward, turn ramps to slurry and courtyards to basins. It would melt the town back toward the plain that made it, gently, as all their old pits are already being melted. A god who loved them by looking only at their thirst would drown them in the act of answering it.

It would not bring a river, or a pipe, or a shining tank on the horizon. It would not give them engines to deepen the wells, or lights to abolish the useful dark, or walls that do not carry every word to every listener. It would not make them clean, or quiet, or efficient, or legible from above. All of that would be another kind of erosion, only faster.

It would arrive, if arriving is the word, the way evening arrives on the plain: without a figure, without a shadow that explains itself. It would stand where there is almost nothing to see and learn to hear instead.

First, it would listen below.

To the market roaring in its earthen drum. To babies answered by four voices before their mothers. To refusals disguised as stories about grandfathers, tenderness disguised as weather, prices passed palm to palm over a melon. It would hear how thoroughly these people have made a virtue of being overheard — how the absence of privacy has not made them crude, but ceremonious; not exposed, but skilled.

Then it would listen above.

Two figures at dusk, far from any rim, saying at last in the wind what cannot be said in the courtyard. A debt. A desire. A name for the dead that has not yet been spoken down below. It would understand that the plain is not empty to them. It is the one private room of the world, and its emptiness is sacred precisely because the wind keeps no record.

So the power would keep that record, secretly, and keep it from everyone.

Not as judgment. Not as a book that might one day be opened against them. Simply as witness, where their own world has made witness impossible without exposure. The unsayable things said on the roof of the town would not vanish entirely. Someone, at last, would have heard them and would not repeat them. The speakers would feel no miracle — only, perhaps, that it was a little easier than usual to turn and walk back down the ramp, into the noise, with their faces kind.

Below the earth, it would do one slow thing, at the scale they cannot reach.

Not fill the wells. Never that. Abundance would rewrite them in a generation: the three-times-used water, the sealed jars, the gardens that look not desperate but deliberate, the whole moral intelligence of their hands.

Only this: far beneath the oldest pits, below the root seams of the oldest figs, where the deep brackish stillness has for centuries been retreating grain by grain toward salt, it would shift a buried fracture by less than the width of a thumb. It would let a cleaner memory of ancient rain go on feeding the deepest wells — not more water, exactly. The bucket would still come up not full. The hauler would still rest exactly as long as the rope needs. But the water would be a little less bitter, year after year, instead of a little more. Children not yet born would not have to learn a harsher version of the lesson their grandparents already know by heart.

It would pass, unnoticed, along the connecting tunnels, and where a ceiling under a threshing floor had begun, imperceptibly, to remember gravity in the wrong way, it would persuade the loess to hold a while longer. Not forever. Forever is not a kindness here, where abandoned pits are allowed their orderly succession of pigeons, foxes, caper, and screaming starlings. Just long enough for the people living above that ceiling to finish arguing with erosion on their own terms.

And beyond the plain — for the world outside is not as still as this one, and is always eventually arriving with its surveys, its roads, its hungers, its own benevolence — it would do the least visible work of all. It would make this land forgettable to those who read land only for what can be taken from it. Test bores would find nothing worth the cost. Maps would blur at the uninteresting centre. Officials would turn a page. Well-meaning strangers with solutions would feel, near the horizon, a sudden and inexplicable certainty that there was nobody here to save.

It would put no sign on the plain. It would carve no new doorway and leave no object that has to be explained in a courtyard, where explanations belong to everyone and become duties, then stories, then laws. It would not give them its name, because in a pit a name would echo until it was an ancestor whether it wanted to be or not.

It would only stay long enough — an evening, a century, the distinction matters less from where it stands — to take its turn in the relay that runs at night from pit to pit: goats shifting in litter, owls replacing bats, a bodiless laugh rising out of a cave room, dogs on the ramps answering one another across the dark until the whole buried, green, animal town has spoken once, in order, without a single human word.

Then an answer would go back along the chain that no dog quite made and no person quite heard, and those lying awake in the cool rooms might roll over in their sleep feeling, without knowing why, that the roof was holding.

In the morning the sun patch would come over the rim as always. Seedlings would get it first, behind their reed screens. Jars would be carried, grain sorted, a hand's width taken from a new doorway, a basketload of spoil spread thin enough for the wind to forgive. The oldest well, hauled up twenty metres hand over hand, would taste — perhaps — infinitesimally sweeter.

Nobody would call it a blessing. Someone would credit the fig. Someone else would credit a great-grandmother, or the year, or the rope.

The power would accept all of these attributions as correct enough, and leave the town as it found it: roaring softly in its holes, saying nothing at all above, alive exactly at the speed it had chosen to survive.
