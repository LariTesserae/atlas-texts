It would not arrive from above.

The Yard has quite enough coming from above already: the exact sun, the light a second early, the stored brightness leaking back at dusk. Another descent, another radiance, would only be weather to them. They would veil their faces against it. They would learn, patiently, to reach for it twice.

If it is wise, it comes from underneath.

That is the one direction no one in the High Yard can really go. They can climb down fifty feet to the Low Pits, down the light-wells, down through service decks and sealed bays, but not down the long struts into the bright haze where the supports vanish. What holds the place up, and what it holds itself up *from*, has passed out of their maintenance. That was not negligence. It was height. A people can live generations inside a thing and still have a blind spot shaped exactly like its foundation.

So the power goes there first, alone, where there are no witnesses to be improved by wonder.

It does not find ruin. The Yard was well made. But it finds what even well-made things acquire in time: fatigue no inspection schedule has ever seen, a slow creep in metal no hand has touched since the builders, microfractures listening for a year of bad wind. It does not rebuild the supports in glory. It does not give the plateau the ground it never had — no false mountain, no miraculous earth rising to meet it, because earth arriving now would not be a gift. It would change the drainage, the weight, the dew-sheds, the goat paths, the whole reef-logic of a place that has learned to live without soil and turned that absence into cisterns, gardens, ash-glaze, memory.

Instead it does fitter's work. In the haze, unseen, it braces what is tired. It re-seats what has drifted by millimeters. It replaces, rather than repairs, a member that has carried its load faithfully past the end of its plausible life, and it makes the new piece to the old measurements, scuffed in the right places, so that if a maintenance crew ever does get that far down they will inventory it, note it, and feel no need to kneel.

That is the first thing: it takes over the one shift the inhabitants cannot work.

The second is harder, because it is mostly restraint.

The power looks at the light.

It could set the clocks right. That is obvious, and therefore suspect. It could make shadows obedient again, return mirrors to their instruments, unwiden the surplus corridors, close the clean lit service bays until the day they are properly built. It could give the high districts back a world in which a hand closes once.

It does not.

Because it watches long enough to see that the slip is not, now, a wound inflicted on the Yard. It is part of how the Yard is inhabited. Remove it abruptly and you do not restore the people; you strand them inside their own reflexes. The hover before the grasp becomes superstition overnight. The extra square of carpet becomes a joke about grandmothers. The bees — this is decisive — the bees are navigating by the sun as it is *to living things*, and mirror crews who have trusted bees over instruments for three generations would be handed back to machines that have been lying politely for years. The moths would gather wrong. The cats would lose Thursdays.

A lesser benevolence heals the anomaly and breaks the adaptation. A wiser one asks: is it spreading?

There it does act, at a boundary no one will ever see. It puts a kind of hand flat against the gradient and keeps it gentle. A second, perhaps. Less. Not frozen — the Yard breathes in that second, things nest in it — but not widening year by year into minutes, into hours, into a high district answering questions decades before they are asked and living lonelily inside the precedence. It caps the strangeness at the size a culture can carry in its wrists and its etiquette. It preserves the difference between being haunted and being housed.

And it guards the outside of that difference.

For this is the true danger, and the power knows it because it is itself proof: attention. If a place like this draws one benevolent eye, it can draw others — collectors of anomalies, engineers of weaponizable time, kindly empires who would arrive with instruments more honest than bees and leave with the Roost Rows emptied “for study.” The Yard people have no ground, which means they have nowhere to retreat to. Their whole world is visible from a lens tower and exposed on every side to sky.

So the power makes the High Yard uninteresting at a distance.

Not hidden. Hidden things provoke searches. Merely — misfiled. A bright industrial plateau, high, cold, productive, tedious in its outputs: spars, plates, lens blanks, replacement parts. Reports about it come back duplicated oddly after a certain year and no administrator quite follows up. A survey beam slides a few feet to the left and re-enters, audibly surprised, over a district of goats and greenhouses not worth a second pass. Anyone arriving with hunger to extract the secret finds the people polite, the veils opaque, the work slow enough to plan lunch around, and leaves with excellent tomatoes and the conviction that altitude makes workers eccentric. The power considers this camouflage a moral act.

Only then, its real work done invisibly and its temptations refused, does it let itself be a guest.

It learns the corridor in the Roost Rows by being walked through it twice.

It waits on a radiator fin, tools metaphorically in hand, until a small white goat with black feet has finished staring it off.

It does not stand in the cool shadow where the moths have gathered for tomorrow's warmth. That would be rude — not unlucky, rude. It steps around.

At the second dusk, when tile and cistern water give back their sourceless light, it sits on a roof edge that is also a goat run, now vacated, and accepts food it does not need with the seriousness of someone who understands that a meal is a tenure arrangement. Beans. Tomatoes. White flowers somewhere nearby for the bees. A child shows it, without being asked, how to brush dew uphill — no, that will be tomorrow morning, but the child is already describing the best cornice in the serious voice of the dawn shift, and the power listens as if information were a form of hospitality. Which, among these people, it is.

It might be tempted, in that hour, to leave them something unmistakable. A river, say. 1.0 of a river, whole and undeniable, to replace their nightly visitation of dew. It does not. A river would drown the cistern steps in meaning. Instead, in a dry year — and only in a dry year, and only by an amount a careful six-year-old will attribute to a particularly clear night — the condensation runs a little longer after sunrise. A few more minutes of chime in the copper downspouts. Enough margin for the moss to hold on the north sides of the vents. Nothing that rewrites the economy of thirst and shepherding that gives the very young and the very old their shared authority at dawn.

It leaves no monument, because monuments are how outside powers sign their work, and this place signs its own in white paint on sealed hatches.

If it is ever suspected at all, it will be in the smallest possible way, decades from now: a crew opening a foundation hatch no one remembers cutting, far below the decks, finding the bracing sound, the load paths clean, a replacement strut already fitted for a stress the Yard has not quite met yet. The foreman will pause. Her hand will hover, once, where the part is offered by the light, and close, the second time, on the part itself.

She will inventory it in a careful hand.

She will seal the hatch. She will paint the date.

And she will go on with the shift — which is how the power, watching from wherever wise things watch without needing to be seen, will know that it was understood perfectly.
