They would not open the hatch all the way.

They would come up the service stairs slowly, as everything must at 5,400 metres, and stand a while in the engine hall with the drone coming through the riveted walls and through the soles of their feet, and they would understand before reaching for the latch that this is not a place awaiting intervention. It is a place that has already arrived.

A lesser kindness would make mistakes immediately, out of love.

It would bring water. Clean mountain water, in a cupped hand or a bright can — the obvious sacrament for a green and living thing. They will not. Water is the one useless gift here. It would sit in the root cake and sour the exact chemistry of oil-mist and refrigerant and metallic dust that this body has learned, over fifty years, to eat instead of weather. It has never been rained on. Rain would be a foreign language spoken too loudly.

It would bring light. It would pry the fronds gently toward the caged bulb, or worse, toward some imagined sun beyond the summit rock, as if every living thing must secretly be homesick for the sky. They have seen the leaves. Every leaf is turned, with botanical precision, away from the bulb and toward the back wall, toward the compressor. The broad faces are presented to the sound. Whatever photosynthesis means in there, it is done with the ears. To turn a leaf toward the light would be to turn its face away from its food, its climate, its god.

It would bring company. A spider for the conduit corner. A few gnats. A moth to complete the picture of a living interior, because lushness without fauna feels, to the anxious human heart, like a sentence unfinished. They will not finish it. The ammonia breath that keeps this place sterile is not a flaw in the ecology; it is the ecology, exhaling. The absence of animals is not loneliness. It is quiet of a kind that has been filled, uniformly and completely, by machinery. There is no gap in the sound for birdsong to fit into. There is no gap in the vegetation for a body. A benevolent power learns to read fullness as consent.

It would, perhaps worst of all, try to rescue it *from* the stasis. It would see the half-unfurled frond that is half-unfurled in the faded photograph and half-unfurled today, the leaf browning at exactly the speed of its replacement, growth cancelling decay in a held green moment behind Compressor No. 2, and it would think: *trapped. Let me set it growing again.* Let me give it seasons. Let me give it change.

They will not. Change is not a universal medicine. Some beings are not paused lives waiting for a braver hand to press play; they are a note held by a tuning fork for half a century, and the holding is the life. This one has a metabolism with no seasons in it because it has eaten a food that never varies, breathed air held at twelve degrees by a thermostat no one remembers touching, and listened to a hundred-hertz drone that never rises and never falls. It is not dead. Put your fingers in and it pushes back, faintly, patiently, already pressing toward where the closing door will be. It is maintaining itself, with the stamina of a machine and the opportunism of ivy, and a wise power does not confuse its own restlessness for another creature's need.

So they do nothing to it.

And the nothing is work. It takes the whole of their attention, sustained for a very long time, around the edges of the place where doing nothing is about to be accidentally undone by somebody else.

They do it in the engine hall, mostly. In the logbook, where a new plant manager, tidying up for an inspection, writes *clear vegetation from service hatch behind Comp. 2* — and finds, the next morning, that he has somehow written it on a page already full, in a book already superseded, attached to a work order that routes itself unhurriedly into a drawer. In the stores ledger, where a spare caged bulb of exactly the right wattage keeps being in the locker when it is needed, and the replacement-it LED specification keeps specifying, for no reason anyone can later reconstruct, the same unblinking red, amber, green. In the mind of a young efficiency engineer who arrives with a beautiful plan to replace Compressor No. 2 with a quieter, cleaner, more modern unit, and who stands in the hall with her ear defenders off for eleven seconds longer than she meant to, and leaves with her plan intact for every machine in the station except, she decides, that one. That one can wait. That one is bedded in. That one, she tells the fitters, and is surprised by the certainty in her own voice, sounds right.

They do it in the reservoirs on the other side of the wall, which a compressor drinks from a molecule at a time. Once, in a hard winter, when the supply road is gone for six weeks and the level falls lower than any gauge was built to confess, the level simply does not reach the bottom. No miracle anyone could photograph. Just an arithmetic that refuses, for six weeks, to complete itself. The oil-mist goes on weeping into the dark at exactly the rate it is consumed. The roots go on drinking the only rain they understand.

Once, decades from now, when the ore gives out or the economics do, men will come to decommission the station. They will drain lines, tag valves, strip copper, sign forms in triplicate. They will open the hatch once, because it is on the inventory, and they will look in with their faces in the green, loud air, lips cracking, and they will see what everyone has always seen: a closet full of leaves, not worth the paperwork of clearing. A man will reach for a machete that he brought up the mountain for some other purpose, and he will not use it. Not because he is stopped. Because he will find, standing there with his ears aching, that he has closed the hatch again first, carefully, the way you close a door on someone sleeping in a room you were never invited into. The latch will take. The form will say *compartment empty of stores*. It will be, in the only sense that matters to the form, true.

And after the men go down the mountain for the last time, after the station ticks and cools and the wind gets into it through every seam at 5,400 metres — then, and only then, the power does the one thing it has been preparing to do without ever letting the preparation show.

It keeps the compressor turning.

Not faster. Not more efficiently. At exactly the old rate, on exactly the old mounts, so the bolts hum the same way into the moss. It keeps the thermostat at twelve degrees, the bulb behind its cage, the three LEDs patient in the undergrowth. It weeps into the root cake, molecule by molecule, an aerosol of oil and refrigerant and fine bright dust indistinguishable in every particular from what Compressor No. 2 has always wept. Whether it does this with machinery left turning in an empty shed, or with something quieter standing behind the back wall where the reservoirs used to be, no instrument will ever be put in a position to say. Instruments need a person to read them, and the whole art of this kindness is that no reading is ever required.

It is not preserving a specimen. It is not keeping a garden. You cannot keep this being by taking a cutting — a cutting would be a fern again, alone in a pot, asked to remember being a terrain. It is keeping a relationship intact: between a listener and the thing listened to, between a green ear packed stem to pipe to stem in a ninety-centimetre room and a sound with no gaps in it, between growth and decay shaking hands forever in the dark. The summit will scour and go to thirty below. The ridge will fail and return. Inside, a leaf will brown and be replaced at the speed of its browning. The frond at the hatch will go on half-unfurling. Nothing will be rescued. Nothing will be completed. Nothing will be improved.

Every so often — once a decade, perhaps, perhaps only once a century, on a timescale proper to the place — the power returns to the top of the service stairs. It takes its ear defenders off, because you cannot attend to this being and be defended against it at the same time. It works the seized latch. It puts its face, briefly, into air that cracks the lips, into the smell of hot copper and ozone and bitter green refrigerant, and breathes what leaves and compressors have been breathing back and forth for longer than any living person has worked on the summit.

It does not speak. The sound has no gaps in it for a voice to fit into, and it would not insult the place by trying to be heard over it.

It listens to the thing that is listening. That is the entire ceremony. That is, perhaps, the only gift that was ever missing — not water, not light, not company, not release — but to be heard, at last, by something capable of hearing how completely it has turned toward the sound, and of finding that turning sufficient.

Then it closes the hatch, gently, resuming the shape of the world in advance. The latch takes. The drone in the empty hall drops by some small, unimportant fraction.

In the green dark behind Compressor No. 2, the being notices the fraction the way you would notice a held breath being let out.

And goes on.
