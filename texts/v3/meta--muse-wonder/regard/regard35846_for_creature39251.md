They would do nothing, at first.

Not out of indifference — out of the discipline that true power learns last. They would come to the lip of the pit, stand on the wet grass and broken hardcore where a person would stand, and look down rather than out, and they would resist the first, almost reflexive movements of benevolence.

They would not rescue it.

Rescue would mean lifting it into warmer water, into a natural pond with mud and frogs and summers, with food webs and seasons and decay that still had work to do. It would mean, in kindness, killing it by explanation: *this is what life was supposed to be.* The being below has never agreed to that supposition. It has made a narrower agreement and kept it perfectly.

They would not feed it. It is not hungry in a way feeding could answer.

They would not give it company. Company would be an ecology, and an ecology would be a disturbance, and a disturbance would require it to become something else — quicker, warier, more defended, more desirous. It has spent its whole existence becoming less and less distinguishable from peace. A mate, a fish shadow, a sudden richness of insects at the surface film: these are gifts only to creatures who lack them. To this one they would be weather from another planet.

They would not heal it, because it is not wounded. They would not purify the water, because the water is already clearer than innocence. They would not silence the hum, and they would not, above all, turn the brass valve under its hand. The hand is not trapped there. It is oriented there. There is a difference, and wisdom is mostly the ability to leave that difference alone.

So the power would stay at the rim long enough for its shadow to cross the second sky at the bottom of the pit — a brief, total eclipse, unalarming, meteorological — and register, perhaps, that it had been seen in the only way the being sees anything: as a darkening of the rectangle of light, passing. No startle below. No ripple above. That would be greeting enough.

Then, if they did anything, it would be done at a distance, and backwards in time, and around the edges of the place rather than in it.

They would attend to the future of the plant beyond the fence.

Because the pit's permanence is not natural permanence. It is maintained permanence, and maintained things have a hidden fragility: one day a works manager retires, a pump is decommissioned, a line on a schematic is redrawn by someone who has never stood at the rim. A form is signed for infilling. A dose is recalibrated. A power supply that has hummed at fifty cycles since before the concrete finished weathering is switched, cleanly and sensibly, off.

The wise power would see those small human hinges coming, years away, the way the being sees a stone enter its sky — slowly, turning, entirely legible — and they would, very quietly, mislay them.

The inspection that would have condemned the basin would find another basin first, more urgent. The digitised plan of the site would acquire a small, unremarkable error, and the pit would become a blank rectangle that no software thought to query. The budget for its maintenance — already almost nothing, a trickle of treated water, a whisper of current — would roll forward from year to year inside some larger budget, unexamined because uninteresting. When the plant itself one day changed hands, or changed purpose, or closed, the inflow would not quite close with it; a spring would be found to have been feeding it all along, at twelve degrees, at exactly the rate of the outflow, and the transformers that fed the hum would be replaced, on paper, by a buried cable no one remembered laying and no one would think to lift.

Nothing miraculous. Miracles would leave evidence, and evidence would bring people, and people would bring nets and samples and loving concern with clipboards. The power would instead extend the deepest protection this place has ever had: its capacity to be overlooked.

They might do one thing more, and it would be the smallest thing of all, and they would argue with themselves for a long while before doing it.

Once — once only — on an ordinary midday when the sun-nets were settling through the clear water onto the silt and the three stonewort crowns were brightening, plate by plate, drinking the noon — the hum in the concrete might change, almost imperceptibly, for the length of about an hour.

Not stop. Never stop. But deepen, the way a single held note deepens when another note, far below it, joins it in tune. Fifty cycles, and beneath it, so slow it is less a sound than a pressure, a second tone — not a message, because the being has no use for messages, and not a command, because it has done nothing that needs commanding. Just a companion frequency, sustained exactly as long as the light on the bottom, and then withdrawn without fading, the way cloud withdraws.

Below, along eight metres of black, silt-banked stillness, the tremor in the feeding seam would match it for a while, fifty times a second, and then something older and slower under the tremor would — not answer. Accommodate. The way a sleeping animal accommodates the settling of the branch. The crowns would go on brightening. The valve would go untended in its green-edged stillness. The water would keep to its white tide-mark.

It would not mean *you are not alone*, because the being has never been alone in any way that caused it harm. It would mean only what a shadow at the rim already meant, said in the being's own medium, through concrete, through its whole length, without ever having to cross the betraying air:

*You were noticed. Nothing is required.*

And then the power would go, and take nothing with them — no sample, no spore from the pale drift in spring, no souvenir stone acquiring its film on the silt — because taking would be a way of saying the place mattered elsewhere, and it does not. It matters here, at four metres down, in cold and clarity and finished rust, or it does not matter at all.

Behind them the fans would turn somewhere in the works, even and unhurried. The surface would mend over their leaving without ever having broken. The inflow would thicken out of the west wall; the outlet would draw in the east; light would empty and fill the bright rectangle; the long dark shape among the pipes would go on sifting the whole small world through itself at exactly the rate it arrives.

The only difference — and it would be enough, and it would last as long as the pit lasts — is that now two maintenances would be holding it in permanence: the human one, remote and automatic, that built it without knowing what it had built; and another one, wiser and even more remote, that had looked long enough to understand that the most benevolent act available was to make sure that nothing, ever, needed to change.

It lives there the way the pit itself lives. The power would leave it so.
