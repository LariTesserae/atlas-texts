It would not arrive at noon.

At noon the fog burns off, the sky is clean and blue, the laundry is hung with military optimism, and the town looks like what the plans in the mess hall say it is: an installation. An industry with houses attached. Easy to mistake for a problem to be solved.

So it would come in the evening, the way everything true comes here — sideways. With the cool air sliding down the fresh ridges. With the smell of wet rock and crushed fern. It would be thinned first at the far end of Main Street, streetlights going soft, then general, then gone into the white. And the fans would pull it in, in slow spirals, over the intake towers where the crows have gone quiet, through the ducts, and it would let them.

That is the first thing a wise power would do here: let itself be breathed in by the machine, and learn the town at its tempo.

Ninety minutes up. Seven down. Ninety up. Seven down.

It would not hurry. It would stay a season. A year. Long enough for the alder grove to add its foot. Long enough to be issued, in imagination, a pair of protectors — orange, say, initialed, a little chewed — and to feel naked without the weight of them. Long enough to understand that long sentences are not being interrupted here. They are being *saved*, the way blue tokens are saved in a chipped mug by the sink, for the expenditure that matters.

It would stand on the fan wall and shout and not be heard, and stand in a kitchen in the quiet and hear a boy laughing three streets over, and know both of those sounds are the same town.

And having listened, it would consider its great gifts, one by one, and put them gently back.

It would not silence the Condenser.

That would be the easiest mercy and the cruelest. To stop the roar forever would wake every baby in the dormitories. It would strand every conversation pitched, for years, from the diaphragm. It would make the teachers' held-back poems land in ordinary time and be ordinary. It would deprive the crows of their dawn argument and their brief, glorious victory. Most of all, it would take away the seven minutes by taking away everything they are seven minutes *from*. You do not give people quiet by abolishing the noise they have built their tenderness around. You do not heal an ear by putting it out of work.

It would not bring a river.

There is no river: that is the first rule, and rules here are not superstition, they are literacy. A river would fill the shallow basin and drown the frog pond that was a cinema, and rot the foundations poured for a future that has wisely not arrived, and teach the children a simpler and stupider lesson than the one they are learning — that water is a thing that simply passes, free for the taking, instead of a thing you read by where it has been. They are wet to the skin and thirsty and precise. That precision is not a flaw in them. It is an education most of the dry towns downcountry, drinking from tanker trucks without a thought, will never get.

It would not fill the grid.

It could send the thousands the streets were numbered for. Cinemas, a second school, neighbors on the empty numbered lots, an end to a family alone on a balcony built for twenty lines of laundry. And in a year the pack of children ranging across age lines would be sorted and supervised, the bees behind the clinic moved on, the tomatoes evicted from Cinema Lot 3, the deer driven from the warm pavement and the outflow pipes, the unbuilt lots built on and therefore no longer maintained with that strange, unoptimistic devotion — hydrants repainted that serve no houses, street-names taught to children for streets that end in ferns. The emptiness is not a failure of population. It is the town's common land. It is room to spread like water in a shallow pan. A benevolent power would recognize a commons when it saw one, even if it was surveyed and curbed and painted by a company.

It would not stop the tankers.

Most of the water leaves: that is the second rule. To end it would be to end the reason for the towers, the painters, the priest with protectors over his collar, the audiologist's padded closet, the whole improvised tribe in bright plastic necklaces. A gift that abolishes the recipient's life is not a gift. It is a flood, wearing a kind face.

So it would do almost nothing.

Almost.

In the night, in a cycle like any other, a fitter on the heavy crew would wake twenty minutes before his alarm with a small, clear dissatisfaction he could not name, and on shift he would find himself shimming a fan mount by a fraction — not the fraction in the manual, a fraction he would afterward defend as *just tidying*. A painter, out scraping a duct support, would leave one panel where the moss had grown six inches deep instead of taking it back to bright metal, because it had, he would say, *earned it*. Between them, and never meeting, and never written up, the deep note of the plant would shift. Not quieter. The roar would still get into your molars and the mess hall floor and the window glass. The children would still be startlingly loud at table. The protectors would still come down around necks at shift's end like medals.

But the note would move off the one place in the human ear where it had been carving, year by year, its neat little notch.

The audiologist would notice first, the way she notices everything first. New graphs, coming up flat where the old ones dipped. She would retest, suspicious of her own equipment. She would hold the curled paper up in the only silent room for miles and listen to a child who had worn her protectors up even in the playground, as disciplined as boots in snake country, hear the kettle. And being the most confided-in person in the hamlet, more than the priest, she would keep her counsel a while — long enough not to frighten anyone, long enough for the improvement to stop looking like an error. The town has never stopped paying for its water with its hearing. It would go on paying attention instead. There is a difference, and a wise power knows exactly how large it is.

Then, the drip.

Every adult in the basin can drop their voice for exactly one thing more completely than for bad news, and that is a child drifting toward the main ducts, toward the ice-cold beading water that sings as it falls. Copper. It builds. A power that left that in place for the sake of a lesson would not be wise; it would be sentimental, and sentimentality is cruelty with cleaner hands.

So the drip would not be made safe. Safe would be advertised by every tongue in town within a week, and queues would form, and a ration would become a tap, and a tap would become a claim, and the claim would go downcountry in a letter. Instead it would be made *honest* to the mouth. One mouthful would announce itself — bitter as a coin, bitter as a battery — so that even a thirsty six-year-old alone in the outflow roar would spit before swallowing, automatically, disgusted, taught by the body faster than any rule. The prohibition would remain on the books. It would just stop being the only thing standing between a child and a slow poison. Rules are good teachers. They should not have to be fences over a drop.

And last, and smallest, the holding pond.

The herons already believe it is theirs. They are, in the manner of herons, correct, and a wise power does not litigate with herons. But under Tank No. 2 there is a puddle that lives all year, colonized, tuning up on summer nights, promising and undrinkable — and in the outflow jungle, where the spent air sighs cool and the escaped orchid has become everyone's orchid by cuttings, the condensation channels run solemn with salamanders, black and gold, going about their commute. Somewhere along that channel, between a duct support in cathedral moss and a gunnera leaf big enough to shelter a child, the drip would gather a little differently. Not a spring. A spring could be metered. Not a well. A well could be owned. Just a place where the safe water — roof-clean, duct-sweet, run over stone and root and nothing poisonous — collects in a hollow that refills, as the reservoir does, drop by drop, at exactly the speed of thirst and never at the speed of commerce. Enough for a cupped hand. Enough for a kettle announced like news. Enough for a canteen on the way to school. If you tried to pipe it, it would think better of it and go back to being damp. If you tried to bottle it for the tankers, you would find yourself holding, mysteriously, a bottle of fog.

The children would find it first, of course. Children and salamanders find everything first. They would not make a secret of it; this town is not shy. They would bring the clinic a sample in a jam jar, with a frog, inevitably, in the lid. It would test clean. The blue tokens would not be retired — form is form, and the storekeeper has a till to keep — but the chipped mug by every sink would stop being a small anxiety and become what it was always half-meant to be: a ritual of care, not of fear. A full cup would cease to be an abundance you apologized for.

That is all.

No one would build the plant a bigger church. The priest-safety officer would credit the fitter, who would credit the painters, who would say the moss had been asking for years. Downcountry management would receive the improved audiology figures and the unchanged tanker figures on the same curled page and file both, satisfied, and send another plan, which would be pinned up and curl. The grid would not fill. The roar would not stop. In the seven minutes, people would still step onto balconies with tea. The swallows would still pour out of the warm ducts like something released. The owl beyond the last street would still call, too loud and too close, over uncut trees.

Only — a generation would grow up who, in the quiet, could hear it a street farther away.

And the power, having learned the town's own art, would leave the way the fog leaves at noon: cleanly, without a sign anyone could point to in a public meeting. It would not need to be thanked, which is how the town would know, if it ever suspected, that it had been visited by something that understood it.

For they had not been exiled inside an ear and forgotten there.

They had been heard in it.

And hearing them — accurately, at their own volume, for longer than was comfortable, without reaching at once for the mercy that would have made them someone else — was most of the mercy.

The rest was a fraction of a shim, a panel of moss left to earn it, a bitter taste in a sweet drip, a hollow by a salamander road that fills only as fast as hands can cup.

Small things.

This is a town that makes, drop by drop, a surprisingly small amount of anything, and has learned that small is not the same as nothing.

A wise power would not insult them by disagreeing.
