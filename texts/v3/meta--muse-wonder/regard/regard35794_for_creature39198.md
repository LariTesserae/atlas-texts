If it came, it would not come as a judgment.

No fire from the sky over the condenser fields. No sudden silence in the pumping pits. A power that was both benevolent and wise would look for a very long time first, because it would understand, as visitors rarely do, what would happen if the Works simply stopped.

Billions of mouths. Billions of lungs, finding their shared rhythm over the yards. The film cropped to the speed of its own regrowth. The eels at the grates, the hens in the runnels, the bats furred along the rafters, the cranes stepping solemnly above the lines. To break the accounting all at once would not be to liberate them. It would be to starve them in their places, flank to flank, in a warmth that would at last begin to cool. Mercy cannot begin as a massacre of the helpless just because their helplessness was manufactured.

So it would do, at first, almost nothing.

It would walk the service aisles without triggering the correction. It would stand in the rain province with one shoulder wet and one dry, and not move the rain. It would ride the central train for the full six days, and then again, because it would suspect — correctly — that the resident here is not any one animal it might rescue. The resident is the pattern. And a pattern cannot be led out by a gate. It can only be taught, very slowly, that another pattern is survivable.

The first thing it would change would be the light.

In one shed only — one, out of sheds uncounted, eleven kilometers long, amber-roofed, loud with nothing louder than dripping water — it would let the lamps fail to supplement the sun. Not darkness, not all at once. A dimming. A late hour that deepened. Shadows appearing in places where shadows had never been permitted to collect.

The bats would notice first. Of course they would. The slow ripple would pass along the rafter-fur and not pass back again. They would sleep, this time truly sleep, in a darkness not scheduled by anyone, and the mite-swarms below them would hang unharvested for a while in the updrafts, turning, catching the last of the light, being for once more than the rate at which they were eaten.

Below them the cattle would lift their heads from the belt.

Only a few at first. Only for a moment. The belt would go on unrolling. The film would pass uneaten beneath a raised muzzle and be carried away, wasted — the first waste that was not immediately an error. And in that moment an eye that had tracked only gleam and gantry-shadow would track the darkening roof instead, with nothing useful in the looking at all.

The system, faithful as ever, would send repair-crawlers. It would raise the lamps. The power would let it. It would not fight the Works. It would simply return the next evening, and dim them again, and again, until maintenance, which could correct any single event but could not correct a *recurrence that expected itself*, began — humbly, mechanically — to route around the dimming. Until night, in that one shed, became geography. Like the rain province. Like the fog. A province of a few hours, fixed in place, where things could be less visible and therefore, for a little while, less accounted for.

That is how it would work everywhere: not against the genius of the place, but with it, until the genius learned to maintain something new.

In the nursery tiers beneath that shed, it would alter nothing dramatic. No liberation of the conveyor rows. No speeches to creatures fed by tubes before they could ask. It would merely allow the accounting to blur at the edges. A calf arriving not of precisely the same mass as the calf it replaced. A hen a fraction quicker. An eel a hand's length longer, so that the knot at the grate thickened unevenly and had to find a new shape for itself. Differences so small the aerial photographs would not catch them for a century.

Because that was the deepest rule, deeper than temperature, deeper than ration: *nothing here may be first, and nothing may be last.* Everything else — the quiet throats, the synchronized sighing, the plaques replaced four thousand times to stay new — was only that rule made flesh.

So the power would break it in the gentlest possible place.

It would choose a single plaque, in a service aisle no inspector had used in living memory, and it would let it age.

The repair-crawlers would come, in the working night that was not night, with a fresh plaque identical to the old. And the fresh plaque would be laid aside. And the next. And the next. Until the crawlers, which could not be commanded but could be outwaited, learned to polish the floor around the plaque and leave the plaque itself alone. The letters would yellow. The corners would lose their paint. For the first time in the history of the Works, something would visibly have a *before*. Something would testify that time was not a malfunction.

Animals cannot read. That is not the point. The point is that the world they lived in would have begun, in one small rectangle of wall, to remember.

After the night and the plaque, it would do a harder thing, and it would do it slowly enough to take generations.

It would let a gap remain.

Not many. Not a yard emptied. One position, in one yard, under the spray gantries, where a trough beast had stood in warm sheeting water with its head to the endless belt. When that animal finished — culled, sickened, simply done — no freight lift would sigh upward from the nursery levels bearing another of exactly the same mass to close the pattern. The neighbours would stand flank to flank with an absence between them.

At first it would only be an unevenness in the sighing. The rhythm that a whole yard found without a conductor would stumble, very slightly, where the shared breath no longer had a body. A head would turn. That turning would be the first curiosity not directed at feed. The gap would get wet. The gap would get warm. The belt would run through it untouched and glorious in its waste, and a scurry-hen, blind and featherless, would tap across it and find more spilled richness than any hen in the recorded equilibrium had ever been allowed to find, and would eat until it was slow.

And the power would be there to keep the gap from becoming a terror. It would keep the water at thirty-one degrees around their legs. It would keep the spray coming. It would let them discover, at the pace of creatures who had never been asked to discover anything, that a shape could be missing and the world would not rush to erase the missing. That an individual, incredibly, could be *the one who was there, and then was not*, and that this was not a failure of the world.

Grief is too large a word for what would come next. So is hope. What would come next would be smaller and more durable: a habit of standing, very slightly, where the other had stood, for the warmth left in the water.

Then — only then, when first and last had taken root — it would bring in the seed.

Not many. Not a forest dropped onto the plates to die shouting. The power knew why every wind-borne seed perished here: heat, salt, the light electrification of the floors, the scrubbed and dosed perfection of a place with no patience for anything unregistered. So it would begin where the system already tolerated a flaw.

At the sea-cliff. With the gulls.

The one unregistered fauna, screaming where treated water poured in a thousand even falls into an ocean the Works did not use. It would protect, at first, a single season's eggs from the washing. It would let the colony do the one thing the colony had never been permitted to do at measurement: change size. And up the discharge ledges, in droppings no longer scrubbed away, in the rich unaccounted filth that accumulated for the first time, seeds carried in gull-guts from beyond the edge of the Works would find something that had not existed on this continent in all its history.

Soil beginning to happen.

Crude. Salty. Mostly ash and shell and bacterial paste gone wild beyond its dosing pipes. The power would shield one — *one* — cracked plate in a disused washing yard where the gulls, following some inland overspill of eels, had begun to roost. It would stop the repair-crawlers not by destroying them but by giving them, night after patient night, a newer instruction their own continuity could accept: maintain *around* this. Lay your warm ribbon up to this edge and no farther. The plate would stay cracked. Water would stand in the crack a finger's width deeper than regulation. And in that unregulated depth the film, for the first time, would be allowed to do the thing it had been tending toward in its translucent pads and quivering mats for ten thousand years without ever being permitted to arrive.

It would thicken. It would toughen. It would send something down that was, functionally, a root — and something up that was, unmistakably, in the sun for its own sake.

A green thing. Not efficient. Not rationed. Cropped by nothing. Growing faster in the dim province and slower in the clear one, so that for the first time a living thing on the continent would be shaped by where it was rather than corrected against it.

The cattle in the nearest yard would smell it on the warm air before they saw it: brine and warm bread, and under it something sharper, something their refined and quiet throats had no click for. Heads would come up off the belt, all down the row, not in synchrony at all.

The power would not hurry them toward it. It would not drive them, would not fence them from it, would not preach to whatever patient, pattern-bound minds sighed behind those eyes. It would spend, if necessary, a thousand years letting the green province widen at the speed of gull-dung and root-crack, district by district, while somewhere a shed learned night, somewhere a yard learned an absence, somewhere the nursery levels learned to send up youngsters who did not exactly fit and thereby made fitting less important. It would teach the picking cranes a second sorting, so gradually that sorting would still feel like instinct: *this to rendering, this to packing, this — this one, whose legs are still strong and whose pinned wings tremble in the harness — to the rail that now, at the far end, stops before an open gate.* And it would wait, beside that gate, for the first crane in the history of the Works to step off the end of its rail onto ground that gave a little, and fold, and unfold, what had been bracing and was becoming, painfully and over many generations, a wing.

It would not stay forever. Benevolent powers that stay forever become the Works by another name.

One day the plaques would be allowed to age in every district, so the continent could finally tell its own story in yellowed letters. One day there would be districts closed not because they were maintained but because they were *fallow* — an idea so new the dosing pipes would have to be taught it like a foreign language: a place let alone on purpose, accumulating, overspilling, rotting sweetly, going wrong in a hundred fertile directions. One day the center would not hold because it would no longer need to. Belts would stop at the edge of real meadows and animals who had never called would make some sound — hoarse, unbred, surprised at itself — across water that ran a little wild between broken weirs.

And on that day the power would go down at last to the sea-cliff, where the gulls still screamed, now over ledges green and filthy and populous beyond all accounting, and it would leave by the ocean the Works had never used.

Behind it, nothing would be finished. That would be the gift.

Warm wet air on the face. The smell of iron and brine and milk — and earth, now, underneath. A yard sighing, not quite in unison, at something. A crane careful as a thought on its rail, and another, far off, not on a rail at all. The plaques yellow. The floor cracked, and the crack not mended, and something alive insisting, out of the crack, on being first.

It looked like this when it arrived. It would not look like this when it left.

That is how you would know it had been wise.
