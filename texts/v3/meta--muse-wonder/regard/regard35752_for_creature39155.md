First, they would not go in.

Not at first. Not for a long time.

A benevolent and wise power would arrive the way everything else that has ever mattered here has arrived: slowly, from above, and would understand that its first task is not to help but to learn the tempo at which help could even be recognized.

So it would remain outside the hollow, in the glare among the pale rocks, through a whole turning of seasons. It would watch the grass grow its fraction of an inch. It would watch the shrub hold its few grey-green leaves unchewed. It would watch rain fail to come, and on the rare day rain came, watch it run off the caprock elsewhere, shed away by the bend and slope of the entrance exactly as it should be.

It would not mistake the emptiness of the chamber for an absence that needed filling.

That would be the great temptation, and wisdom would begin in refusing it.

For a power that could do anything, the cave offers itself as an easy place to do miracles. It could bring water back to the low corner where a pool ought logically to be. It could widen the cracks, call down rain, wake the old damp in the back wall. It could fill the silence with insects, with wings, with small quick lives. It could give the creature eyes, though the creature has traded eyes away and would experience light first as injury. It could give it a mate, a voice, a memory, a name spoken aloud in the dark.

It would do none of these things. Each would be a kindness imagined from outside, and each would be a violence inside.

Water, given suddenly, would not save it. It would rot the hoarded dryness it has perfected. It would bring bacteria, fungus, hunger, competition — a whole economy of faster beings who would eat in a summer what it has learned to live on for a century, and then die or leave when the water failed again. A mate would double its wants in a place that can only just afford one life reduced to this fineness. More food would make it grow beyond what the next dry decade could carry. Sight would give it a category for its own darkness. Even rescue would be an ending: lifted into air and light and handling, examined, marveled at, kept somewhere *better*, it would cease within hours to be the being it has spent an age becoming.

The wise power would understand what human pity often does not: that this life is not a diminished version of a livelier one. It is not waiting inside a mistake. It is not lonely in the way a creature with a memory for company is lonely. It is not enduring the cave. In every way that matters to it, it *is* the cave continued by other means — warm, dry, blind, almost unmoving, taking in what falls so slowly that taking and resting are the same act.

Its wants and the hollow's provisions are the same list.

To love it is not to lengthen that list.

So inside the chamber, the power would do nothing. And *nothing*, done properly, is not passive.

It would enter, if it entered at all, only after learning how to leave no evidence of entry. No light brought past the bend. No breath allowed to trouble the dust more than dust is already troubled by the centuries. No footprint left in the soft drifts of the back alcove. It would not touch it, though a touch might tell it more in an instant than a hundred years of watching — because touch, to a being whose whole knowing is contact, would not be a caress. It would be an event the size of weather. A warmth not the stone's warmth. A pressure with intention in it. The first large thing to arrive at its rim since the world dried.

It would spare it that.

Instead, the power would become, for as long as its attention lasted, a memory on behalf of a creature that keeps none.

That is no small gift, though the creature will never know it. It has crossed the chamber perhaps a hundred times for salt at intervals longer than empires, and each crossing has been erased behind it, grain by grain, until the floor was smooth again. It has been larger, and is smaller now, and carries no record. The grass it has grazed through the roots has come down blind and been trimmed blind. Nothing witnesses its patience at its own scale.

The power would witness it.

It would hold the whole slow biography in mind: the decades of incremental advance across warm limestone flour, the years stationed beneath a descending root, the monthly contraction of the broad belly sealed in its private damp, the rare wet year when the salt bloom spread one crystal wider and its cracked back split and re-formed to cover new ground. It would let that life be *known* completely, without being known by the one living it, and would ask for no gratitude, no recognition, no response — because any response would have to be at the power's speed, not its own.

Then, having done nothing inside, it would do everything necessary outside, so quietly that the boundary of the cave would never notice a guardian.

It would guard the watershed, not the pool that is no longer there. It would see that the limestone cap above is not quarried, not blasted, not cut by a road whose vibrations would trouble the stone. It would turn aside, years in advance and by the smallest possible nudges, the surveyor's line, the goat herd's path, the curious traveler's route — not with terror or miracle, only with a stone turned here, a track fading there, a slightly easier way that happens to lead elsewhere.

It would tend the living edge of the place where tending belongs to the ordinary world: the three or four grass tussocks at the threshold, the spindly resinous shrub in its crack. Not by making the dry country green — that would change everything below — but by preventing the one unnatural ending. In a drought worse than the droughts it evolved for, the kind made hotter and longer by distant fires it cannot imagine, the power might shade a root-crown for one afternoon, or let a single skin of dew gather where dew normally never gathers, up on the surface, among plants that are built to receive such mercies. Enough for the roots to keep descending. No more.

Its rule would be precise and austere: *restore the conditions it chose; do not improve them.*

If some future age brought back the rains on its own — true rains, lasting, the kind that once made the back wall sweat — the power would not hold them back to preserve the creature in amber. It would let the hollow become wet and strange and perhaps untenable, as it was wet and strange before. If the creature could enlarge itself again to meet abundance, it would. If it could not, and the last of its lineage ended under a returning pool, that ending would be allowed its dignity. Benevolence is not the same as making a beloved thing last forever at the cost of the world that made it.

But until the world itself decides, the power would keep human speed out.

No torch. No name carved near the entrance. No story told in a way that sends feet looking for the dip between the pale rocks. If someone did blunder in, palm flat to the wall, steps slow in the complete dark, the power's whole intervention might amount to this: a faint unease at the threshold, a sense that the place is complete and should be left complete; a stumble that turns them gently back toward the light before their warmth, their breath, their shed skin and cloth-fibers became the richest meal to arrive in the alcove for fifty years.

And then, afterwards, it would smooth their footprints out of the dust — slowly, grain by grain, as the cave itself would have done eventually, only sparing the creature the sudden feast and disturbance.

At last the power would withdraw to the same distance at which it first learned to see.

It would leave no offering. Offerings are for beings who can want more than they have. It would leave no sign, because a sign is a beginning and the hollow has finished beginning. It would leave the salt bloom a few crystals wide at hand-height in the dark, the roots ending blind in empty air, the fallen blocks softened with drifted dust, the center of the chamber empty, everything set wide apart.

Only one thing would be different, and it would not be in the cave at all.

Somewhere in the wide, fast, bright world beyond the limestone, this one slow life would be held in attention for as long as attention could be held — not studied, not saved, not pitied. Remembered at its own pace. Accompanied without company. Loved in the only form it could receive without harm: by being let alone precisely, deliberately, and forever on purpose.

Then the chamber would be, once again, exactly as it was.

Warm in the high twenties, season to season. Dry as ash. No echo. The grass at the mouth growing a fraction of an inch. The fallen blade beside it taking another year to become dust — and the dust travelling, grain by grain, toward the back alcove where the deepest drift is warmer by a degree than the stone around it, and breathes, about once a month, too softly for anything to give back.
