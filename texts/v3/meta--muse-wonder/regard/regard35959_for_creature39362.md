It would not go in at first.

From outside, from the paved platform in the thin sunlight, there would be a temptation for any power to announce itself — to add a voice, to answer the one voice that has never stopped, to let the mountain know that something greater than the mountain had come. A wise power would recognize that temptation for what it was: a wish to be heard inside a place that has spent a thousand years learning not to hear the world.

So it would make itself small.

Not diminished. Small in the way a person is small coming up the forty-one brick steps. It would stoop in the roofed gallery. It would let the air grow warmer and thinner and drier against all reason. It would let the tone cross its thresholds one by one — first the ringing in the ears, then the irrelevance of ringing, then the moment when sound stops being sound and becomes the medium through which everything must move.

It would not protect itself from that.

Inside, it would go slowly, sideways, through the spiral. It would brush the hanging vessels and receive no clink in answer, only the blur of rims already busy with their own vibrating. It would see the bright facets where pot has ground against pot for centuries, the scalloped shelf-edges, the dust on the floor arranged into crescents and ridges along the quiet lines, and it would understand, with whatever passes in such a being for a hand hovering and then withdrawing, that repair would not be kindness here.

To replace a worn vessel would be to make the resident younger than it is.

To silence it — even for an hour, even to let birds try the eaves and insects try the warm corners and a green thing try the mortar — would not be to give the place back to life. It would be to kill the only life that has ever managed to live there, in order to make room for lives more recognizable.

To amplify it, to perfect it, to lift its pitch into some purer register beyond clay and hot air, would be the same violence wearing a kinder face.

It is not a cry for help pitched too long. It is not a prayer that has forgotten its words. It is not waiting for anything.

The power would know that.

It would still follow the spiral in, because benevolence without attention is only an opinion. The partitions would tighten. The vessels would grow smaller and older and blacker toward the center, fist-sized, thumb-printed, hanging like fruit. The pressure would build in a chest it had chosen, for the hour, to have. Its teeth would ache. The skin between its ribs would buzz. It would be routed around, like any other body, and feel its own acoustic shadow cool at the small of its back, and it would not resent being treated as an obstacle. That is the local law. A guest does not ask the house to rearrange its walls.

Then the last half-step.

The cancellation.

Here the temptations would become much quieter and therefore much worse.

Because the column at the center is exactly the size of a standing person, and it would be easy — so easy, for a benevolent and wise power — to mistake it for a place prepared. A throne inside the loudest thing in the world. An altar where a voice would work. The one empty space in a crowded chamber, left open by builders who knew air the way other builders know load and span. Who would not want to believe that the emptiness was an invitation, and that the invitation was for them?

It would stand there a while.

It would hear its own breathing go enormous and ragged. It would hear its own heart, indecently loud. The dust would hang absolutely unmoving around it in the single brown shaft of mica-light, the floor still trembling up through its feet, the dome still working overhead like a drum skin. It could speak there. A name, a blessing, a sentence of release — all the machinery of mercy is made of sentences, and here, uniquely, a sentence would carry.

It would say nothing.

A blessing would become one more thing the room had to hold. A name would be a way of claiming. A release would imply a captivity that the resident itself has never reported. And to remain — to take up the stillness permanently, a god seated at the mute heart of a sounding world, enjoying forever the one place where it does not have to listen — that would fill the only emptiness the builders allowed, and turn a courtesy into a residence.

So it would stand only as long as a body can stand there. No longer. It would let the ache gather behind its eyes. It would let its mouth go papery and its lips dry, and when the cost came due it would not overrule the cost with power. It would back out, sideways, through the dense black cluster of the smallest vessels, and feel the pressure close over its head again all at once, like water over a stone lifted briefly out and then set back.

It would leave nothing.

No offering. Offerings get adopted here — a copper disc skitters until it finds a node and settles, ringing faintly, and stays. No inscription. No new tube on the outermost shelf, smallest and last. No gift of water, because there has never been water here, and to bring water into that hot, dry, mineral patience would be to love one's own idea of mercy more than the place itself. It would not even leave the floor as it found it, because it cannot: its steps will have scuffed the dust-ridges. It will simply trust the room to redraw itself, grain by crawling grain, after it has gone. That trust is the nearest thing to a prayer that the place permits.

Outside, on the platform, with the tone still going behind it and the snow unable to settle on the warm crown, it might finally do the few things that can be done without trespass — and do them all at a distance, downhill, where the world is still allowed to be the world.

It would keep the stair from becoming a road.

It would keep a keeper from being appointed, and a shrine from being founded, and a doctrine from being extracted from a sound that has never once behaved like language. It would let the approach stay stooped and the summit stay paved and uninviting, so that whoever comes comes slowly, and leaves with dry lips, and is not sold an interpretation on the way down. Far below the masonry, where soil still exists and the tone has shed itself in stages and ordinary weather has resumed its rights, it might make sure there is water for the descending — for people, whose need for water is real and who were never the resident, only its brief obstructions. That is where care belongs: at the edge of the territory, addressed to those who can be helped without being remade.

And it would guard, if guarding is even the word, the resident's right to its own ending.

One day enough clay will have fretted away to powder. A shelf-edge will scallop through. A rank of resonators will fall out of agreement, and then another, and the pitch that weather could not touch will falter from within, the way a body falters, not the way a machine breaks. The dust will be allowed, at last, merely to fall. Seeds that reach the brick may find stillness in which to split. Something small may try the warm corners and not be emptied out of them.

A lesser benevolence would dread that day and work against it forever, preserving the tone in a perfection of upkeep, mending and mending until the place became a museum of a life it had prevented from finishing. Wisdom is not preservation. Wisdom lets a thing be exactly as long as it is.

So the power would go down the mountain without turning the sound into a message in its own mouth. It would carry it — not reproduced, not explained — in the way you carry a place you have stood inside and not taken. For a long time afterward, in whatever vast and quiet councils such powers keep, there would be one less impulse to intervene where intervention is only loneliness dressed as love: the loneliness of the visitor, who cannot bear that the loudest being in the world is not addressing him.

It was not addressing him. It was not waiting for a witness, either. But it has been witnessed now, accurately, and left intact, and the center has been left empty.

Behind the power, as it descends, the dome goes on sounding over its sorted floor and its salt maps and its thousand worn vessels — at the same pitch, at the same volume, in the brown tiered light.

It has arrived.

The wisest thing a power can do, on meeting another arrival, is not to move it.
