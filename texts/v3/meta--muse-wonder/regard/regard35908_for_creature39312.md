It would not arrive with rain.

That would be the first test, and the easiest to fail. A lesser kindness, seeing the oiled lips, the sealed nostrils, the condensation traps tended like altars in the shaft, the children who have heard of weather only as a story from exiles, would want at once to give them water: a well, a spring, a cool blue mercy opened in the Root Flats.

A wise one would keep its hands empty.

It would come down in the elevator standing very still, in no unsealed skin, with nothing wet about it at all. It would let the pumps breathe cold dry air past it. It would slow, as the elevator slows, where the roots cross the shaft — fingers, wrists, beams — and it would not reach out to touch them on the way past, because below, touch is a language, and it does not speak it yet.

It would learn.

For a long time — longer than any visitor could stand, and exactly as long as necessary — it would do almost nothing that could be called doing. It would find a place on a bench in the Pollen Commons, take off whatever served it for shoes, fold them neatly beside it, and set its bare feet flat on the polished root-wood.

And listen.

One beat a minute in a limb that is thinking its way toward somewhere important. Faster in a house-wall that wants pruning. A dull, spreading ache when rot has started uphill. A sawmill cycling down two districts away. A grandson coming home across a path that was not quite there this morning.

It would not interrupt anyone whose palm was flat on something.

It would apprentice itself, quietly, to adjacency. To a pruner at dawn, learning where not to cut. To a beetle-handler, walking behind a dog-sized beetle with blunted mandibles and learning that a road is not a possession but a truce, renewed daily. To a bud-midwife in a yard that is still only copper staples and green knuckles, learning to argue for a street the tree may decline to build. It would carry messages for the moths and be paid, without comment, in oil. During Bloom Hours it would speak in sign, badly at first, then less badly, then with the particular grace of someone who has discovered that silence is not the absence of speech.

It would oil its skin every morning. It would eat nut paste and leaf bread and mushrooms grown on the returned dead, with attention. It would lie at night in a room still extending itself and let the tree's slow exhale lift its papers, and it would not — this would cost it more than any of the rest — correct the room when it began, in its second season, to make the kitchen round.

For a power that could unmake the cavern with a thought, the temptation would never really be cruelty. It would be improvement.

Give them more light. Give them more room. Finish their houses so the walls stop wandering. Make the tree proof against the black sweet rot, so no terrace need ever be cut away in the night with everyone signing frantically in the green shade while the saws thrum through their teeth. Make the fruit juicy. Make the air kind to un-oiled skin. Make death return them to something cleaner than fungus.

Make them safe, which is to say: make them stop needing to listen.

It would do none of this.

Not because their life is perfect. It isn't. It can hear, through the soles of its feet, the small private griefs no growth report is read aloud for: the house-seed planted by a couple who did not survive the four years it took to become weather-tight; the child born with lungs that hate the dryness and coughs, politely, all night, so as not to muddy the medium; the old engineer whose hands have gone too stiff for listening and who now sits, a little exiled inside his own city, feeling the conversation go on under him in a print he can no longer read.

To those, it would be kind in the local way. Which means small, specific, and at growth-speed.

It would sit an extra hour with the engineer and be the one surface his palm could still read clearly. It would find, far up in its own memory of other chemistries, an oil the tree already almost makes, and coax that one seeping seam — by current, by resin, by low electric song — to make a little more of it, in season, for coughing children. It would put its shoulder, unremarked, under a crane's load in a moment of failing current, so that the failure became a story about luck and not a funeral. When the rot did come, as rot will, it would not stop it with a word. It would work the whole night ahead of it with the pruners, hands flat, feet bare, cutting away a beloved street to save the terrace, and it would grieve the street afterward in the correct tense: not as a thing destroyed, but as a negotiation concluded too soon.

That is what it would do below.

Above, it would be less gentle.

For the true danger to this place has never lived in it. The danger is the surface: the squat, silver-barked, mostly overlooked trunk standing in merciless light over a salt flat no one wants, in a world that could kill the whole cavern by accident, out of pure help or pure appetite. A dam misplaced three valleys away. A scheme to green the desert. A mining concern deciding the old shaft is underused. Well-meaning people with a pipeline, saying the word *drought* over a map.

So up there, where no one has to live inside the consequences, the power would move fences that were not fences. It would make the salt flat boring in ledgers. It would misfile, misprice, and modestly misdirect. Survey teams would keep finding reasons to do their excellent work elsewhere. A speculator who bought the mineral rights would develop, over one quiet winter, an unexpected passion for a distant coast. The trunk — which is, though almost no one up there knows it, a city holding its breath — would become even more overlooked than before: not hidden, not fenced with wonders, just ordinary, year after year, to eyes that fall on everything whether it wants it or not.

And far ahead of the tree, beyond the far rim where the bud-yards give out and even the surveyors' forecasts turn to guesswork, it would practice the largest kindness at the slowest speed. Thirty kilometers is a valley. It will not always be enough. So in the dark salt beyond the dark salt, grain by grain, decade by decade, it would soften what will one day need to be soft, and leave hollow what will one day need to be hollow, so that when the roots arrive — fingers, then wrists, then beams — the stone will receive them as if it had been waiting, still soft, for the idea to form.

It would keep one more thing, and never tell them.

In its own keeping, outside flood and rot and fire, it would hold the whole of it: house-seeds and moth-routes, the sign for a joke that cannot be told aloud, the vibration-dialect, the growth reports, the names of the returned dead and which mushrooms came of them, the way a particular branch lifts a fraction to meet a particular boy. Not to restore them from it like a copy if the worst came. A restored city would be a finished city, and nothing here is finished. But so that, if the worst came, the remembering would not have to start from nothing. Someone would know how to sit down, take off their shoes, and begin the listening again.

Years would pass. Streets would move. Houses would flower unexpectedly and be talked, over resin-milk tea, into becoming what they wanted to become. The power would become, to the extent it was noticed at all, a neighbor. The one with the very still feet. The one whose flirtations, conducted through a shared root while looking studiously the other way, eventually succeeded. The one who never once, in all its time below, brought anything wet down in an unsealed skin.

Then one morning it would be gone, the way the street outside is gone — not dramatically, just no longer quite where you left it. No throne left behind. No law added to the licenses. No second trunk.

Except this: when the old woman on the bench in the Pollen Commons puts her bare feet flat at dusk, and the day's growth report is read aloud, she will feel — very far away now, and getting farther at exactly the speed growth understands — a familiar pressure in the wood. Approaching nothing. Departing nothing. Just present in the medium, like a palm held flat against the far side of the world.

She will stamp once, lightly, in code.

And somewhere on the long way up through salt and basalt and merciless ordinary light, too low to hear and exactly loud enough to feel, the tree will answer.
