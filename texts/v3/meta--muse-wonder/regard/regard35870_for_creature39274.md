It would not come down in glory.

Not because glory would frighten them — though it would, it would fill the well-shaft with noise and light and send breath-rime blooming across the membrane for a week — but because glory would be a kind of carelessness, and this is a place that survives by care.

First, it would learn the doors.

It would come, if it came in a form at all, through the outer airlock like anyone else: one door, then the other, never both. It would stand in the wet-room while the pressure settled. It would knock its boots twice against the rime-bar, scrape, knock again. It would hang its outer layer to drip. It would not talk in the shaft after dark.

Power that cannot obey the small rules of a house does not yet understand the house.

It would be tempted, at first, the way any kind and strong visitor is tempted.

To warm them. That is the first, obvious mercy, and therefore the most suspect. The Cup is cold not by accident but by agreement: reactors that run better cold, folded rock that sheds heat badly, layers upon layers of bodies adapted to breathing thin, damp air just above freezing. Warm the terraces and you soften the seals, breed mold in the cuffs and scalps and ledger-pages, confuse the bacteria in the overflow pools, make the membrane flex wrong. A week of comfort. Then a new city, weaker in the exact places this one is strong, having to be maintained by rules nobody here was born knowing. The power would feel the cold in their knuckles, the damp that never leaves a collar, the downhill knees that begin their long complaint at forty — and it would keep its hands, such as they are, to itself.

It would be tempted to feed them better. To put green in the vats, fruit on the protein sheets, variety where generations have made a cuisine out of timing and temperature and salt. But a green thing is not a gift here; it is a water debt, a light debt, a rotting thing in a city with no soil and no wood and no room for waste that does not become breath again. Their word for garden is already taken, and rightly: the milky turquoise bloom on the overflow pools, tended, watched, argued over like weather. Abundance delivered from outside would not make them richer. It would make them dependent on outside.

It would be tempted, most of all, to open the mountain. To widen the crack, ease the altitude, let in roads and doctors and trade and sky. But they do not think of themselves as hidden, or trapped. They think of themselves as contained, the way a cistern contains water: not as a punishment, but as the condition that makes use possible. Open the Cup to the world and the world would not simply visit. It would study the folded space. It would want the reactors, the self-sealing alloy, the kept opening nobody's grandfather understood. It would bring curiosity, then administration, then extraction, and call each stage help.

A wise power notices that a home is not a list of its hardships.

So it would walk the spiral slowly, leaning almost imperceptibly into the curve. It would put its inner hand near the wall, not for balance but for information. It would listen at the prescribed places. And in listening, it would hear what the engineers, tapping with their rods, have almost but not quite heard: not the ordinary inch-a-year creep of a young mountain, which the Cup can meet and has always met, but a stress gathering deeper in the fault — slow, silent, patient — that would one day arrive not as a new seep to be reported at dinner but as a single closing. Not this year. Not their children's year. In their grandchildren's time, a night when resealing would not be enough, when the maintenance schedule would run out.

That is where it would act. Not by freezing the mountain — a frozen mountain would kill the seeps, the melt-pulse, the living negotiation that is the town — but by bleeding the worst of the pressure sideways, into uninhabited scree on the far face, in a season of small night tremors harmless enough to be logged and forgotten. An inch a year, still. Sometimes a meter in a night, where no one lives. The mountain goes on becoming a mountain. The Cup goes on being maintainable by daughters and sons with competent hands. It restores to them the only thing the fault was about to steal: a future they can handle themselves.

Outwardly, it would do something even quieter. It would make the place continue not to look like a place.

A survey pass would find granite, altitude, weather. A mineral drone would lose interest for reasons its operators would later describe as sensor weather. A curious government would file the ceramic bunker, the dish, the red door, under remote stations, unremarkable, understaffed, not worth the climb. Not a veil of miracles — veils invite someone to lift them. Just the deep, protective dullness of things that appear to be exactly what the Cup already pretends to be.

Inside, it would allow itself one gift, because pure restraint can be another kind of pride, and because it had stood in the wet-room and understood, suddenly and completely, about dryness.

One night, while the membrane went clear and the stars crowded down the throat of the well, it would take the damp out of the city.

Not the water. Nothing here can be spared, and it would steal nothing. It would simply gather what was clinging where it was not wanted — in hood linings, in cuffs, in the collars of children, in gloves that had not squeaked in years, in the old coats mapped with darker self-sealed seams — and put it where water belongs. By morning, every outer layer in every wet-room would be dry. Actually dry. Bootprints would take an unfamiliar minute longer to fade on floors that had, for once, nothing to evaporate. Old people would wake with their hair loose and clean-smelling of nothing but themselves. The school cat, lordly on his pipe, would for one morning have rivals.

And the cistern, below everything, would be higher by precisely the gathered amount. Not a finger's width. Less. Exactly as much as a city exhales when, for a single night, it does not have to carry its weather in its clothes.

The water board would log it, because the water board logs everything, in waterproof sleeves and perfect handwriting:

*Anomalous redistribution, overnight. Membrane clear. No new seep. None seen.*

In the margin, after a pause, a clerk might add — *layers dry*, and underline it once, and not know why she was smiling.

The power would leave before bathhouse hour, so as not to take up warmth that was counted for other bodies. It would go out the way it came, one door at a time. Outside, the three ravens would be waiting by the airlock with official patience, and it would not feed them anything that would teach them to expect gods. The scree would shift an inch under no one's feet. The seeps would bead on south-facing rock at noon.

At the melt-pulse the channels would run bright and loud for twenty minutes, and everyone, wherever they were, would stop for a moment because there was nothing else to do but listen. Below them the pale fish would turn once in the black water, slow, unseen, recorded by no one.

Which is to say: the town would remain cold, and wet, and quiet, and difficult, and entirely its own.

The power would keep watch from farther off after that — not hovering, not ruling, only attentive, the way you watch over a house whose doors you have learned not to prop open.

And if it loved the place, and it would, it would consider that enough.
