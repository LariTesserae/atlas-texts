It does not bring the rain.

That is the first mercy, and it looks, from a distance, exactly like neglect.

It could. That is understood without being said, in the way certain facts are understood beside very deep drops. It could call weather up the three thousand meters of still darkness below the lip and lay it across the plateau. It could return whatever was taken out of the sky. It could crack the dry basalt under the wells and let some old, patient water find the city at last, water that did not taste of pipes or people.

It stands at the freight terminus with dust already settling on it, learning to breathe air too thin to carry its voice, and it does none of those things.

Rain would not be a gift here. Rain would be an erasure. It would take the dust in which every funeral, strike, courtship, queue, and childhood is written and turn the whole plateau to black mud. It would blind roads, drown pipe-trenches, burst into courts built windowless against a dry dark, and swell in lungs adapted over centuries to sip at the air. A sun would not be a gift either. It would seal the great pupils shut. It would burn skin that has never learned sweat except as symptom, and it would make a desert of the mineral crust the lizards scrape with their tongues behind the power station.

A river is not kind to a people whose highest hospitality is the first, clean draw from a newly charged loop, drunk in sips too small to be called drinking. A forest is not kind in a place that keeps itself alive by refusing, with tremendous discipline, to spend.

So the power, being benevolent, does not rescue them from the form they have reached.

Being wise, it does not mistake that form for a failure merely because it is not another form.

It stays.

Not as a ruler. Rule would be another kind of weather. It comes up slowly, as freight comes up, and spends its first designated night sitting with its back to a warm wall in a habitat court, under the one amber lamp, smelling of imported things no one is rude enough to name. Its hands crack. Someone shows it the oil, cut with solvent, and how to work it into the webs of the fingers. It learns to put its palms down with everyone else when something changes, very far away, in the molar-deep hum of the generating halls.

For a while — years, perhaps; the place does not encourage counting novelties — it is simply a bad worker slowly becoming an adequate one. It learns not to run. It learns to place its feet inside the polished lane between districts, not because its own groove matters, but because wandering feet raise dust and dust here is somebody's grandfather still queued outside a reclaimer door. It learns names in rotation and answers to one that has been worn by four women and a press-operator before it. It learns the pockets of smell by which children navigate home: hot iron, coolant, yeast, scorched dust, the particular sweetness of its own block settling into its clothes.

It tends machines the way a good worker tends them here. Not improving them. Not dreaming aloud over them of some better century. Returning them, exactly, patiently, to tolerance, so that the next hands to open them will find nothing of its cleverness to forgive. If it weeps at all, in private, over the paste and the recycled water and the bodies carried light as ash to the only disappearance the plateau permits, it does it where smells do not pool and betray the whereabouts of grief.

Only once does anyone suspect it, and that suspicion is never spoken in the lit districts.

Out between the pools of light, where the long animal goes.

It is seen — not by eyes, which are a courtesy sense at distance, but the way things are truly seen here: stars stopped where they should not be stopped, quartz gone briefly dull in a lane, a smell crossing the mineral smell of the plain that belongs neither to smelter nor vat nor coolant shed. The track-readers have always known the prints are wrong for four legs. Heel too long. Weight carried the way weight is carried by a spine that learned upright first and then, year by year, groove by groove, forgot how to ask anyone for help getting out of the path it had made.

The power does not stand that being up.

It would be easy to call that healing. It would also be a cruelty dressed in the healer's own shape. It does not know — and this is the point at which benevolence becomes indistinguishable from humility — whether going down into the groove was a defeat. Whether the long body loping from light to light is suffering in the way an outside pity needs it to suffer. Whether quadruped is the punishment, or the final, private solution to a life spent walking one line until the line became deeper than shame.

It does not photograph it, name it, feed it from the hand, or bring the children to see.

What it does is smaller, and it takes a human lifetime of walking to earn the right to do it unnoticed.

Where the being's track crosses the road to the last district — straight, unhurried, older than any living groove, worn not merely into dust but beginning to be into stone — the power kneels in the dark between lamps. It works with its hands, the way skin here is cleaned: by hand, patiently, grain by grain. It broadens one shoulder of the rut. Not enough to free it. Freedom is a large word, and large words fall badly in thin air.

Just enough to make a turning-place.

A shallow bay off the straight line, smoothed into the bedrock, where a body that has not reversed direction in longer than the district archives kept on paper could, if it wished, turn around without rising. It scrapes mineral crust from near the coolant-plant waste channel and leaves the flakes there, off to the side, where they can be found without approaching light or hands. It leaves a vessel, too. Not the first draw, which belongs to guests and ceremony. The second. Clean enough. Set down with its back already turning.

Then it goes on, in its own footprints, so that returning will not look like waiting.

Nothing happens for a long time. Here, nothing happening is how everything happens.

Then, one designated night, track-readers crossing to the early shift find two lines where there has always been one. For perhaps two hundred meters, no more, a second set of prints runs parallel to the ancient straight track — neither leading it nor following it, the distance between them the distance at which this people incline their heads to talk. After two hundred meters the second set bears away, gently, back toward the lamps.

The first set does not bear away. It continues straight.

But at the crossing, in the broadened place, there is a confusion in the dust that the oldest reader studies a long while, palm down, saying nothing. A doubling. A hesitation polished almost smooth. As if something, in the unwitnessed dark, had for the first time in the history anyone can read considered the direction it had not taken — and then, having considered it, had gone on by choice rather than by rut.

The vessel is empty. The flakes are gone. No one is cured.

After that the power makes one more track, and this one the children find.

From the edge of the settled ground, past the capped junction where the cable ends, it walks out — not toward the lip where the dead are given to the rising air, but across untouched dust, in the exact bearing of those prints that do not return. District by district, lamp by lamp, the report travels faster through bedrock than any runner could carry it: someone has gone out on the line of no return, alone, unprovisioned by any standard the vats would honor, and has not doubled back in panic and has not lain down.

The children follow, of course. In packs, daring each other past the last smell they know. They follow until the prints, which should by every story thin into the gait of the animal, instead begin — imperceptibly at first, then unmistakably to any child taught to read ground before letters — to curve.

Not double. Not pace. Not retrace.

Curve, across black stone where no lane has ever been polished, in an arc so wide it takes half a day to believe in it, and come back into the settled plain by a district the walker did not leave from.

There is no sermon written in that. Sermons require air. There is only a new sentence in the one book the plateau has never been able to close. Departure, it says, in footprints. And then, in the same unhurried hand: not death. Not the animal. Not the lip and the brief glitter falling three thousand meters into silence.

A way out that is also, eventually, a way in from somewhere else.

The power does not stay to see who uses it first. Staying to be thanked would build a district around the thanking, and districts here last longer than gods should. It takes the freight down while it can still bear the lower air like soup, like drowning, like a hand over the mouth, and sleeps a day at the bottom of the world with its inner ear aching. By the time it wakes, the dust on the plateau has already settled into its departing tracks and made them history.

Everything else continues. The presses descend once an hour, every hour, and arrive in distant kitchens as a tremor in the cups. The flywheels turn with a slowness felt in the molars. Names are given again to newborns in strict rotation. The lizards hold still against the warm walls with their mouths open. Water is made, and remade, and offered in sips. The stars do not move in any way anyone bothers to mark.

But between two districts now there is a curve where the young sometimes wander on purpose, not lost, and at one crossing the straight track of the oldest walker in the world has beside it a smoothed place where turning is possible.

And on certain designated nights, when the districts go so silent that the rare fauna can be heard moving over stone, the track-readers say — among themselves only, and only as track-readers, who deal in weight and direction and not in consolation — that the long line no longer always passes at the same interval.

Sometimes it passes sooner.

Sometimes it passes later.

As if, out there in the dark between lamps, something that had been going forever in one direction had begun, occasionally and entirely without witness, to decide.
