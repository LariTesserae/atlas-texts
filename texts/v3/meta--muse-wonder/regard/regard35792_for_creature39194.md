They would not step down into it.

That would be the first act of wisdom, and it would look, from the trail, like nothing at all.

A power that is truly benevolent arrives the way everything else arrives at the Pan — as a warmth, a weight, a change in the air — and the carpet would know it before it knew itself. Antennae would halt mid-wave. The centipedes would flatten. The towers would lock. The worm-things deep against the stone would close along their sides. And that freeze, which for a falling stone or a visitor lasts twenty seconds, would last and last and last, because this attention is heavier than a foot.

So first, they would make themselves lighter.

They would withdraw to the rim, to the cold side of the stones, and sit where the wind still cuts, and learn to look without pressing. To perceive, down there, is not a passive thing. Ten million bodies are the sense organ. If you lean your mind on them, you are leaning on skin. A wise power would become, as nearly as such a power can, another stone at the edge of the bowl: present, unhungry, tasting nothing back.

Only when the same leg completes the same step again — when the spiral, cautiously, resumes — would they understand that permission has not been given, exactly. Tolerance has. It is the only kind the Pan has ever offered.

They would not improve it.

This is the temptation that must be refused, and it is a real temptation, because the needs look so plain to an outsider. *Water*, you think, watching the inch-wide stain darken on the back wall at night, watching the silent rotation, mouth to damp stone, mouth to damp stone, never a drip, never enough by any generous measure. A benevolent hand could widen that stain to a hand, to a sheet, to a thin, clean seep that never failed. It could cool the center. It could carry away a fraction of the sulfur, soften the air, widen the bowl by one merciful pace so that six-deep could become three-deep, so the bottom layer would not have to endure quite what it endures.

It would be murder, done kindly, at exactly the speed of rot.

Give them water and the molt-floor ferments instead of hardening. Give them room and the spiral loosens, and the heat that the packed bodies hold against the mountain cold — heat they make together, by being together — leaks away at the edges. Cool the center and the mats die back from the vents; feed the mats more and the first grazers bloom and the centipedes bloom after them and then there is a winter of empty shells. Everything in the Pan is not suffering *despite* its limits. Everything in the Pan is shaped *by* them, the way a river is shaped by its banks. The benevolent do not give a river the plains and call it freedom.

Nor would they heal it. There is sickness there, and slowness, and antennae waving out of time, and the wet-ash centipedes are already moving across the backs of the carpet to attend to those things. Nor would they spare the cricket taken cleanly from the side. A power that began plucking out predators with pitying fingers would very soon be standing, horrified and indispensable, in the middle of a graveyard that still moved. The Pan has a stomach, and a skin, and a way of making room. It does not need a god inserted as an extra organ.

Nor — and this refusal would cost more — would they preserve it exactly as it is.

They would not dome it against time, or fix one perfect generation in amber dark. Millipedes are already replacing their ancestors with themselves. Beetles are laying eggs at the warm edge in places that will be center or wall in some slow rearrangement of the stone no living cricket will see. The being is not a painting. It is a turning. To forbid it change would be only a cleaner kind of killing.

What a wise power guards is not the pattern. It is the conditions under which the pattern can keep finding itself.

And those conditions, looked at with eyes old enough to see past the bowl, have two edges the carpet cannot reach.

One is below.

In the freezes — those total, distributed listenings — they are not only listening to each other. They are listening through molt and stone to the mountain's deep account: heat rising, pressure gathering, the vents breathing against a throat of sulfur crystal and black glass that is, very slowly, sealing itself. Not this year. Not in a hundred. But in some turning of centuries the center could choke, the pulse falter, and the whole bowl cool from the middle outward while the spiral kept faithfully rotating around a stone that no longer exhaled. The crickets can hear it coming the way you can hear weather in an old knee. They cannot do anything about what they hear.

The power can.

Not by widening the vents — never that, never a sudden generosity of heat. Far underneath the Pan, out of reach of any root or worm, they would ease a hairline fracture open in the deep rock and let the mountain's pressure spend itself along a safer road, so that the breath at the surface neither strengthens nor fails. They would do the unglamorous, geological work of keeping *the same* true for longer than stone unaided would keep it. No animal in the bowl would feel a miracle. The pause that winter would be, perhaps, a fraction shorter. That is all. That is everything.

The other edge is above, and it walks on two legs, and it stacks cairns.

The Pan cannot defend itself against being known. It cannot make the sulfur smell less interesting to the people who would dig for it, or the heat less valuable to the people who would drill beside it and call the drilling clean, or its own blind, patient strangeness less collectible to the people who would come with jars — lovingly, scientifically, ruinously — to take home a piece of the only place on earth where this turning happens. The carpet's whole genius is that there is nowhere to scatter to. That is also, before a crowd, its whole helplessness.

So around the Pan, but never in it, the power's work would become almost visible, and would be mistaken for chance for as long as it needed to be.

The trail would become a little harder to follow. Not barred — bars are advertisements. The cairns would sink, as cairns here have always sunk, and oddly, no one would feel moved that season to restack them. A survey line would drift a respectful half-mile. A map would keep a small, uninteresting error at exactly this summit. Men with a lease for the mountain's heat would find their instruments persuasive everywhere except in one high, barren bowl that the reports would describe, accurately enough to bore any reader, as *sterile rock, minor fumarolic staining, no significant biological community*. They would go and enrich themselves elsewhere, faintly dissatisfied, never knowing they had been refused.

And on the human scale — the scale of the shallow stone bowl beside the last cairn, polished inside from hands — the power would leave that, too, alone. Someone, once, understood enough to put an offering down outside rather than take something up from inside. You do not correct the one decent instinct your own species managed here. You let the bowl stay empty. Empty is what it is for.

Last of all, because wise benevolence does not confuse a long life with an endless one, they would prepare for the death they had postponed.

A mountain away — or ten, where the wind also polishes stone without water and a young vent breathes through black glass with no one yet to graze it — they would find another bowl the sun cannot enter. And they would not carry crickets to it in cupped, merciful hands. You cannot transplant a turning one animal at a time; you would arrive holding dying soloists. Instead they would take only what has always traveled: a smear of yellow and white and orange life from a stone already fallen from the rim, mats that can make food from poison in a place that has never known food. They would lay that first meadow down in the new dark, and leave it empty, perhaps for longer than the cairns outside the Pan have been standing. An unoccupied home is not a failure. It is readiness. If the old mountain's heart ever truly stops — if the spiral slows, cools, and the top layer can no longer warm the bottom — then, and not before, a power strong enough to lift the whole springy floor as one living skin, intact, turning, centipedes and all, might do so in the space between one freeze and the next, and set it down gently where the heat is young.

They would not do it early to be safe. Safety, hoarded too soon, is another way of ending a story that still knows how to continue.

Then they would go.

No blessing would be spoken over the bowl, because blessing is also a kind of pressure. No sign. No new wall, no mended ring, no drip of miraculous water on the back stone. A few crickets would ride the warmth of their going a little way up the steps, tasting the air for salt that was never offered, slowing as they cooled, dropping off one by one onto the red pumice — and the power, who could have carried them effortlessly into some kinder, greener country, would let every single one drop, because kinder and greener would be the last thing those crickets ever died of.

Behind them, the floor would close over the place where no foot had stood.

It would keep turning. That is the whole of the gift, and it is not small: that a thing which drew the eye of heaven should be allowed, afterward, to go on being exactly as unobserved as soil, as ordinary to itself as weather — bacteria making food from poison, worms making food from waste, beetles making shade of their own backs, crickets making a climate of their bodies, the spiral holding its temperature against the cold at the rim — guarded at the edges it cannot reach, unguarded, trusted, sovereign in the middle where it lives.

A benevolent and wise power would do almost nothing.

They would spend almost everything they had to make sure that almost nothing was enough.
