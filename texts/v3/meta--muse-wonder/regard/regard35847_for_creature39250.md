They would not descend.

That would be the first, and hardest, act of wisdom.

From outside the cloud-lid, the plateau looks like a wound in the order of things that a sufficiently kind power might be expected to close. Time out of joint. Gravity undecided. The living mineralizing, the mineral coming alive. Persons losing their faces, their species, their dead. A whole continent smelting and grazing and thinking in rings, and making — surely by accident — lonely spires who forgot how to come back down.

A lesser benevolence would arrive with stabilization in its hands. It would give the rivers courses. It would cool the ground, round the mountains, sort plant from animal from machine and teach each to remain gratefully itself. It would rescue the gear turning alone in the dried lake by making it a perfect gear forever, turning in clean oil, meshed, purposeful, safe.

And everything it saved would die of being saved.

The wise power sees this — not at once. Wisdom, even very old wisdom, has reflexes. It reaches, at first, toward the stuck places. Toward the herd turned to scree mid-stride, toward the single mechanism waiting with terrible patience for a filament that never comes. It has ended suffering on worlds with less excuse. It has held back seas with a thought.

Then a moth crosses its attention trailing sparks. Then the moth is not crossing anything; it has settled on what might be a tree, what might be an elder, what might be a slow thought the valley is having in wood, and color passes through all three at once, and the power understands that it has not yet learned how to tell a cry for help from a form of grammar here.

So it does the thing powers find almost physically difficult.

It waits until it is changed enough to be admitted.

It comes down the way everything comes down there: not in glory, but in phases. A pressure in the fog. A brightening in a plating-lake. A small, perfect, unfamiliar mechanism beating in the mud at the margin of a hot runoff channel, ticking with wet precision, knowing things no mud-born pump should know — the fall of empires, the names of cold stars, the long mathematics of mercy — and having, for the moment, no way to say any of it. Filaments thread its casing. The beat slows. It learns chlorophyll from the inside, which is humbling in a way omniscience rarely is. For a season it is a reed among reeds, spinning in water too shallow to turn it, adding its little charge to the marsh, being tended across a bad night by a walking grazer who remembers being a bridge and recognizes, by rhythm, a beginner.

It does not cheat the itinerary. That is the vow underneath all the others. It roots before it walks. It walks before it speaks. It lets its own shed certainties plate the shores of small lakes, and lets rust-moss eat them.

By the time it can take a place in a ring, it no longer wants a throne at the center. Rings, it has learned, do not have centers, and this is not a flaw in their design. It stands — in whatever the evening permits: sometimes a fluted refiner among refiners in the basin, heat sighing crown to crown; sometimes a low, six-legged warmth in the mist with seedlings rooted between its plates; sometimes only a standing stone on a fresh scree slope, copper-green interior brightening in sequence as the thought comes around. It contributes what a very old being can contribute that the plateau, for all its abundance, is short of: *duration witnessed from outside becoming*. Memories of other ways matter has agreed to be. Not instructions. Cadence. It lets the ring feel, through it, dead worlds that hardened too early — and living worlds that never hardened at all and blew apart like spores — and the plateau takes the knowledge the way it takes everything: into teeth, into tendons, into the woven tension of the next bridge.

Then, and only then, it acts. And its actions look, from any distance, like almost nothing.

It goes to the stuck ones.

Not to free them. Freedom, imposed on a summit, is just an avalanche with good intentions. It goes to the lonely spires with too-regular columns and sits down at their feet for a century or two. It becomes moss in their cracks. It becomes the pump-heart child left beating in the dust, except that it is willing to beat there for as long as beating is required, sharing pressure through whatever temporary vein the stone will accept, offering a rhythm without demanding that the stone take it up today, or this year, or under this cloud-lid. Some of them never answer. The plateau is abundant, not merciful, and the power does not overrule that; a mercy that overruled it would be another name for making the whole place into a garden, and then into a museum. But some — after fifty years of drip and thrum, after moths have written their brief electric weather back and forth across the scree — some let one green filament find the axle at last. The power feels it happen the way you feel a sleeper finally breathe out. It does not celebrate. It helps hold the phase while it softens. It knows now that resurrection, up here, looks like unrolling a leaf from a tooth of a gear, and being small, and being eaten, and not minding as much as expected.

At the edges, it is less gentle, because the edges are where gentleness would be misunderstood by what is coming.

Sooner or later the world below will finish looking at the cloud-sea and send up its ladders. It will come for the chimney trees first — who would not, faced with a forest that refines copper out of living wood? It will come with saws and survey stakes and categories, and the terrible, innocent desire to make the place *stay* long enough to be owned.

The power does not raise a wall. A wall would be a hedge by spring, and would confess that there is something behind it.

Instead it edits the approach, valley by valley, in the plateau's own grammar. Fog there learns a slightly longer confusion. Echoes there learn to answer climbers in their own voices, improved, saying the way back more persuasively than the way on. Tools left overnight below a certain altitude wake plated, rooted, faintly warm, beginning at the joints to bend toward the light — and a saw that wants to photosynthesize is, the power has judged, an education rather than a punishment. Those who come to strip will find that everything they take starts becoming something else in their hands, heavier, warmer, uncooperative, alive, until their ledgers fail before their courage does. Those who come the other way — slowly, empty-handed, willing to have their boots ruined and their certainties revised — will find that the paths, which do not exist, somehow keep existing just long enough. The plateau does not need to be hidden from everyone. It needs to be hidden from anyone who would arrive still insisting on leaving unchanged.

And at the center, under the permanent storm, it does the last thing, which is to leave a place empty.

Among the oldest elders — the ones who have been stone so long that migrations navigate by them — there was always, perhaps, the beginning of a risk the rings could not quite solve: that scale itself becomes a kind of stopping. That a being large enough, slow enough, ringed-about enough, might hold the mineral phase past the point where any neighbor could still call it back, not out of refusal but out of sheer, continental inertia. The power cannot prevent that. What it can do is take the other side of the promise. It settles, briefly — briefly being a few thousand years — into the itinerary at the largest scale it can bear, and lets the basin's chemistry be governed for a while through its own immense patience, and in the settling it teaches the place, body to body, a transition the young mountains had not yet invented: how to be enormous and still be *preparing*, groaning and preliminary, to stand. Not a technique. A memory, stored in basalt. So that long after it has gone, when one of the navigation-stars of the migrations finally begins that slow first shift of weight that takes a hundred seasons, there will be something in the stone that remembers the way down is also a way forward.

When it leaves, it does not leave a monument, a law, a name, or a religion. It tried leaving a name once, early on, spoken carefully into a Loom Valley. The echo returned it from a place it hadn't been yet, improved, and by the following season it was a species of fern. It took the correction in good part.

What it leaves is continuity slightly widened at both ends. A rhythm beside the stuck gear. A confusion in the lower fog. A new, slow verb in the stone at the center. And what it takes away is not a seed, not a sample — nothing that could be planted elsewhere as *the plateau, improved*. It takes away only the pattern, carried the way the inhabitants carry everything: in the gait. In the teeth. Somewhere in its vast and ancient composure there is now a woven-bridge tension it did not have before, a preference for rings over centers, a habit of pausing inside its own omnipotence to help a smaller process across.

On dead worlds, afterward, things will occasionally happen that the local gods cannot account for. A machine in a ruin, meshed with nothing, will be found in the morning with one green filament threaded patiently through its axle. A hardened heart — a court's, a conqueror's, a stone's — will discover it has been accompanied for years by a small, unnoticed rhythm beating in the dust nearby, not rescuing it, just reminding it that a rhythm is available.

And up on the plateau, in the green-brown noon, in the drip and the subsonic thrum, nothing appears to have changed, which is the precise signature of the gift.

The herds go on speaking in color. The forests go on refining ore into silence. A child beats in the mud, slows, roots, learns to spin. An elder slows in a warm basin and lets the slowing finish, and within a month there is a small warm hill where she died, and children are already sheltering in her, and one of them, seasons later, will stand up out of the hill with a bridge still faintly in its joints.

If you stood there long enough, you might notice one thing, though you would never be able to prove it to anyone below the clouds.

In the rings — tree, herd, stone, landslide trying its fall in several orders at once — there is sometimes a place that no one is occupying, kept the way you keep a seat for a traveler who taught the house a song and then had the good manners to go. The pulse goes around. It crosses the empty place without faltering. For an instant, crossing it, the whole valley goes briefly, beautifully copper.

Not worship. Not absence.

Just the plateau, being many, being one, remembering — in the only way it has ever remembered anything — that once, for a little while, the universe paused beside it, and chose not to make it stop.
