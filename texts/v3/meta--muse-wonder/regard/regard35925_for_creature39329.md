If they were truly benevolent, and truly wise, they would not descend.

That is the first thing Merrow would teach them, if they stayed long enough to learn it — and a wise power would stay. Not for a day, with light. Not for a single clear hour at mid-afternoon, when the markets are up and the town looks almost legible. They would stay through a full season of cloud. They would learn when to be still. They would learn that here, a dramatic rescue is usually only a more impressive kind of damage.

A lesser kindness would look at Merrow and see problems to solve.

It would see fifty thousand people at four thousand meters, damp to the bone, chilblained, arthritic before their time, sorting metal out of bog mud with darkened fingertips, sending children barefoot onto cold slate before dawn, and it would want to *fix* it. It would bring sun. It would drain the hollows and lay pipes. It would cut a proper road, a fast road, bring up grain and medicine and dry lumber in quantity, raise towers out of the mist, seal the walls against the wet once and for all, give them wells, give them reservoirs, give them height to match their altitude.

And Merrow would rot from the inside, exactly the way an over-sealed wall rots.

Because Merrow is not a failed lowland town that happens to be high. It is not people enduring a place in spite of it. It is a way of life grown, fin by fin and generation by generation, into the precise shape of its weather. Dry it and you do not free it. You unmake the knowledge in their hands. You make the condenser walls worthless and the great old families paupers and the cooperatives pointless. You silence the gutters, and with them the town's literacy. You give them water in pipes, and you take away the reason they wake together, listen together, maintain together. You cure the damp, and the moss dies off the walls, and the stone, no longer held and insulated, begins to freeze and split.

A wise power would put its palm flat against one of the ancient boulders at sunset first. It would feel how cold the stone still is. It would understand: this place is not asking to be lifted out of its life. If it needs anything, it needs the conditions of its life to go on holding.

So they would work — if *work* is even the word — at the scale Merrow cannot reach.

Merrowers tend surfaces. The power would tend the sky Merrow's surfaces are angled toward.

Far to the west, where the white river gathers itself out of sight of any roof, they would keep the cloud road open. Not grander. Not wetter. Just true. In a warming century, when lowland heat begins to lift the deck too high, when the mist comes thinner, or less often, or an hour later each decade until the nets string between chimneys beaded only with memory — there, at the edge of what a town can govern, they would lean, very gently, on the weather. A degree of coolth restored over a distant sea. A wind kept from shearing. Nothing anyone could point to. The mills' great north-facing walls would simply go on weeping as they always had. The windmills on the eastern rim would go on turning when the cloud moves, and people would go on telling the weather by them without looking up, and believe — correctly — that the world was as it should be.

They would watch the lowlands, too.

Because the other threat to Merrow is not its own hardship. It is other people's solutions. Surveyors with a straighter road in mind. A company that looks at the placer basins and sees not a slow inheritance sieved by quiet electric sheds, but an ore body to be stripped in a decade. Engineers who arrive in the clear two hours, take their measurements, and leave before the evening inundation, convinced the hollows are wasted water and the peat is wasted land. A benevolent power would not smite any of them. Merrow has no use for miracles, and martyred prospectors attract more prospectors.

Instead: a season of unaccountably bad visibility on the plateau whenever the survey crews come up. Instruments that will not quite agree. A road estimate that keeps, honestly, growing more expensive every time it is recalculated, until the lowland council shelves it for a generation. A rich seam, reported by a loud man in a valley bar, that somehow can never be found again in the fog. The children would ring their handbells for the lammergeiers, and somewhere in that white murk another, stranger announcement would pass unnoticed. Something wild has come this high. Let it pass.

And within the town itself — almost nothing. That is the discipline of it. The power would have to learn Merrow's own definition of theft: not only noise after the evening mist, but help that arrives too loudly for people to hear their own gutters by.

Perhaps, over years, small mercies that fit inside the existing shape of things and ask nothing in return. A factory cough that, in one shed, eases — because the air in that particular roof-space began, imperceptibly, to move the way it should, carrying dust up and away from young lungs. Chilblains that still come, because cold is cold, but do not quite deepen into the netted scarring on a generation of children's knuckles. A winter when the cistern-frogs call once, all together, and are right, and no pool overtops into a workshop because someone — some grandmother, consulted before the windmills — had that morning felt it in her joints and sent word down the lane in the small hand-language of the mist: *pool rising, come down.*

Perhaps the worked peat cuttings west of town, where generations of trams have taken the bog mud away, would begin, very slowly, to heal in a way peat is not supposed to manage within a human lifetime. Not filled. Not restored by decree. Just holding water again, hollow by hollow, black sedge finding its way back along the bright threads, as if the plateau had been reminded of a rhythm it had never quite forgotten. The factory cooperatives — maintenance-minded to the marrow — would notice. They would not call it a miracle. They would argue about it for a season, test it, distrust its sharpness, and then quietly fold it into the rota: this cutting rests now. We keep its edges clean. We do not take from it yet.

That is how Merrow would accept a blessing: by turning it into a responsibility.

And perhaps once — only once, and no one could ever prove it — on a dawn when the cloud sank below the rim and the town stood dripping above a white sea, belonging to no visible earth at all, someone standing on a roof with a sponge gone still in her hand would feel the quality of the quiet change. Not a voice. Merrow distrusts voices that arrive too clearly. Just the sense, intimate and close the way a far whistle arrives close in saturated air, that the town's long listening had, for a moment, been answered in kind. That something vast had tilted its head. That the surface of the world was clean, angled, and ready, and had been noticed by whatever moves across it.

Then the mist would come back over the rim. The gutters would begin to tick. She would go back to wringing her sponge into the bucket, arguing half-asleep with her brother, barefoot on slate she knew by toe.

The power would leave no monument. Merrow builds around its boulders; it does not raise new ones. They would leave no name, no temple, no sealed and shining wall. To be remembered that way would be to become one more sharp edge on a peneplain that spent millions of years politely wearing sharp edges away.

What they would leave is continuance. The cloud arriving sideways, on time. The roofs cold and ready. The fins beading in the dark in patterns that a keeper's bare hand can read. The pools rising by fractions and spilling in bright threads and draining again. A people not rescued from their life, but kept company inside it — free, as they have always wanted to be free, to do the patient, exact, unglamorous work of tending what they love.

In the evening, if they lingered, they would do as the factory hands and the old women and the children supposed to be elsewhere do. They would lay a palm flat against the old stone as they passed.

Checking it. Greeting it. Taking its temperature against the night.

And then, having learned the town's first and last rule, they would hold still — while everything wet and living moved across them, and the mist came in, and Merrow, unwatched over, unsealed, unhurried, went on listening to its roofs fill with water.
