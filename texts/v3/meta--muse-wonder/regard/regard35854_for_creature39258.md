If such a power came, it would not come through the hatch.

That is the first mercy, and it would know it at once. The hatch is a shoulder's width of damage. Every opening tears the nearest curtains, scatters the bright veils back into mere dust, leaves the long presence briefly headless while the room swallows warmer, damper, wronger air and begins the slow work of remembering itself. Lesser kindnesses would arrive that way anyway, full of concern, with instruments, with soft voices raised uselessly against the roar. They would want to see it better. They would bring light that could be switched, heat that could be felt, names that could be written down somewhere other than a logbook. They would call all of that rescue.

A wise power would understand that rescue, here, is a form of weather this creature cannot survive.

So it would not open the door. It would not need to. It would be, for a while — if *while* is a word that still means anything beside minus twenty and an unswitchable lamp — simply present in the way the room itself is present: without entering, without displacing, without adding one grain to the air.

And it would look, the way you look when you have finally learned to be still. Not at the valve wheel, chain-locked open, painted safety yellow, which is not an eye. Not at the moss in its sealed troughs, blue-green, resinous, growing at the pace of proof. Not at the lichen freckles on the cold pipes, which have never blushed, and must never be made to blush in order to prove they are alive. It would look at the spaced curtains hanging between them, the nodal planes, the pale standing fields, and it would let the pattern resolve the way it resolves for anyone patient enough not to reach out a hand: a low, long presence laid along the length of the room, taller than the troughs, narrower than the walls, its body possible only where an enormous sound cancels itself into local silence.

It would not be disappointed that there is no face to turn toward it.

It would not try to give it one.

That temptation would come, because benevolence is so often just loneliness projected upward — the wish that everything loved should love back in a recognizable grammar, should have eyes to close in trust, a mouth to take food, a voice to say thank you in. It would feel that wish clearly, and set it down gently outside the room, with the warmth and the moisture and all the other gifts that would frost the tuning mass and rot the moss and send the dust falling in one soft grey rain.

It would not heal it. It is not injured.
It would not warm it. Warmth is not kindness here.
It would not free it. There is nowhere else shaped like this held note.
It would not speak to it. The roar has already taken the idea of voice and made something more honest out of pattern.

Instead, it would do everything outside the room.

This is harder to see, and therefore easier to mistake for nothing.

Far below the intake pocket, in offices where the air is wet enough for breath to fog and paper does not go double in the hand, there are periodically meetings about the plant. Efficiencies. Retrofits. A newer compressor array that would run cleaner, quieter, with the resonance problem engineered out at the source. An engineer, meaning well, draws a smooth line through a wall cavity that does not appear on any painted diagram and says, *we can seal this, we can fill this, we can gain back the volume.* The proposal is sensible. It would save money. It would save hearing. It would, in every human accounting, be an improvement.

The power does not strike the engineer down. It does not haunt the boardroom, does not send a dream of dust standing in curtains like gills. That would be theatre, and theatre is another kind of disturbance. It simply attends to the small hinges on which sensible proposals turn. A costing is mislaid until after the budget closes. The newer array develops, three valleys away in testing, a harmonic fault no one can quite chase out of the housing. A senior inspector, a man who once climbed past the heat exchangers and pressed his palm flat to a singing wall without knowing why the gesture stayed with him for thirty years, finds himself saying, *leave the upper pocket alone, it's load-bearing in a way the diagrams don't show,* and is surprised at his own certainty, and does not argue with it.

The diagram stays unpainted. The hatch stays off the tour.

Down in the turbine hall, two decks below the singing walls, a bearing that might have begun, next spring, to go very slightly untrue is coaxed — not miraculously, not past the limits of metal — toward the longer end of its natural life. A shipment of filter fibre arrives with just enough mineral dust in it, just enough iron fines, though no manifest will ever list those as contents. When the plant is sold, as all plants eventually are, the sale goes through to owners who are dull in the specific, blessed way of people who maintain things exactly as they find them. When the mountain weather turns, as it turns every few decades, toward a winter that could starve an intake and change the note, the wind comes a fraction less empty than it might have. Nothing anyone could point to. Nothing a gauge would record.

Because the gauge must not move. That is the whole covenant. The needle stays at its fraction below the red. The lamps stay on. The cold stays at minus eighteen, minus twenty, steady. The workers who know the place continue to come at their lengthening intervals, in plugs and muffs, with their soft-cornered cards, and rap twice on the wall to feel that the note is true.

It would let them keep believing they are only listening.

It would not stop their noses bleeding a little, dryly, if they stay too long. It would not take the sound out of the bone of their jaws, or make the metal kind to bare skin. To make the room safe for savannah animals would be to make it uninhabitable for the one animal it is for. Benevolence, if it is wise, does not insist that every sanctuary also be a nursery for its visitors. It lets a place be exactly as hospitable as it is, and teaches the visitors — by a cracked lip, by a hand learned not to reach — how briefly and carefully to belong there.

Nor would it grant the creature eternity. That, too, would be a failure of seeing.

Plants end. Mountains, given time, also end, though they are better at concealing it. Somewhere in the creature's own deep weather — if the standing filings by the magnetic conduit, quivering, carrying their tiny charges back and forth and arriving nowhere, can be said to know anything — there is the knowledge that the quiet is not a monster but a terminus. It lives because the turbines live. It holds because there is something to hold against. A power that loved it would not curse it with outlasting its own meaning, would not keep the roar going into some grotesque perpetual subsidy after the valley below had gone dark and the last worker had forgotten the hatch, just so the curtains could go on trembling in an empty building for no one and nothing.

What it can have is not forever. What it can have is *uninterrupted*. The full length of its natural tense. No premature silence. No well-meaning hand through its body more often than the pattern can repair. No discovery — that most fatal of modern weathers — with its sampling and tagging and preserving in a different, warmer, louder room where a committee could watch it fall out of the air in real time and call the falling data.

And when the end does come, eventually, in whatever decade the great machines below are finally allowed to stop, the power would not leave early to spare itself the sight. It would stay for the needle's first real tremor. It would stay as the shelves of silence inside the roar began, one by one, to give way. It would not catch the dust. Catching is another name for changing the manner of a death. It would simply be present for the soft grey rain onto the grating, onto the troughs, onto the tuning mass bolted through to the frame, the filings lying down at last like grass after wind — and it would not call that a tragedy in any language the room would have had to learn.

Until then, though: the long now of it. The work that looks like no work.

Perhaps, once, it would make contact. Not often. Often would be a relationship, and a relationship would be a load, and a load would change the tuning. But once — the way the lichen on the pipes leans, very slightly, toward a sound in another room — it might allow itself the smallest possible acknowledgement, calibrated to the only grammar available.

A palm that is not a palm, pressed nowhere on the wall.

And through tile and alloy and singing panel, through the fine standing hairs of iron by the conduit, through four metres of roar with silence folded inside it in parallel veils, rapped twice. Exactly twice. No third rap to make it a message. No variation that would ask the creature to become an interpreter. Just the note, returned true, at the pressure it expects from the workers it has learned to register the way a pond registers an expected stone.

The curtains would not turn. They have no turning in them. But grain by grain, in the places nearest the wall, the dust might gather — not more densely, density is change — only more exactly into its shape. The moss would go on exhaling its little oxygen into the blue-green fringe of it. The lichen would vibrate each in its own spot. The logbook by the door, in the white unswitchable light, would keep its pencil entries slightly double and perfectly legible.

*No change. No change. No change.*

Which, heard rightly at last, was never an absence of report.

Still here. Still here. Still here.

And now, underneath it, so quietly that even a wise power would have to be careful not to call it an addition:

*Seen. Seen. Seen.*

Then it would withdraw whatever in it could be withdrawn without a hatch, without a draught, without one filament of the frost-grass lying down — and take up its real post, which is everywhere else. In the costing office and the testing hall, in the senior inspector's unaccountable certainty, in the longer life of a bearing and the dull fidelity of new owners, in the whole vast unglamorous perimeter of conditions that must hold so that one small room, high inside a plant on a mountain, does not have to become anything else in order to be loved.

From inside, nothing will have happened.

That is how it will know the visitor was benevolent.
