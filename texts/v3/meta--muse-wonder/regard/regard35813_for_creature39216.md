They would not arrive as light. There is already too much light here.

They would come up the stair from below, slowly, because that is the only way the place allows itself to be approached, and because haste would be the first misunderstanding. They would stop at the step where rock becomes masonry. They would put their foot down carefully — not on a rim at random, not in a pan, not through one of the small crystal gardens in the corners — but on the dark stepping-slabs, as a keeper would. Their weight would make no new mark.

Wisdom, first, would look like restraint.

They would not bring life to this place. A lesser kindness would. A lesser kindness would carry soil in its hands, and seed, and green things for the damp shade of the walls, and would call the bitterness barrenness and try to heal it. But they would taste the water first — white on the lips, drying after drinking — and understand that what refuses moss and insect and bird is not failing to be alive. It is succeeding at being something else. To plant here would not be a gift. It would be an argument, carried on in roots and rot and clogged drains, against everything the valley has patiently become.

They would not bring people back, either.

They would not rebuild a hut where no hut can stand, nor set a new keeper on the rim with a broom and a bedroll, nor cut a railing along the stair, nor carve a sign at the pass explaining what this was for. They would know what explanation does. It gives strangers a reason to stay too long, to be dry and fed and shaded where dryness and food and shade were never part of the bargain, and to love the place in ways the place cannot survive.

Nor would they grieve for it, at least not in any way it could feel as pity. Pity, here, would be another kind of weather, and wrong.

Instead, they would stay.

That would be the real intervention, if it can be called one: to match its tempo long enough to learn whether it needs anything at all. Not a day. Not the bright noon when every funnel throws the sun back and the whole hollow underneath stays silent. A season, at least. A year, more honestly. Long enough to kneel in the wet and put an ear to a drain and hear the far trickling arrive from a distance that cannot be judged. Long enough to see the central pans film with dew before sunrise and burn clear again. Long enough to hear, perhaps twice, perhaps three times, the wind find a ventilator shaft and draw out of the reservoir that one low, empty note, like air across the mouth of a jar — not a call, not a complaint. Just the shape of the hollow, sounded for a second.

They would learn its districts as its body. The deep stepped funnels in the east, open for storm. The broad pans in the center, patient for dew. The settling troughs in the west, where the water rests and clears and lays down its white load before it leaves. They would follow that leaving, once, all the way down through the stone doorway and out of sight — down past the cloud line, down the long dry slope — to see who, if anyone, still waits for the modest stream at the end of so much gathering.

And whatever they found there, they would not carry the news back up in any form the valley could be burdened with.

If there were fields still, they would leave them their water and say nothing about its source. If there were no fields, only gravel where the stream gives up and sinks, they would stand a while in that silence too, and not call the catchment's work futile for it. A thing does not become pointless because its recipient is gone. Gathering is not only a means here. By now, gathering is the life.

What they would touch, they would touch once, and as a keeper touches — not as an owner, not as a god improving a design.

Somewhere, deep in the oldest pans, a drain throat no wider than a wrist will be narrowing with lime, laying its smooth skin down year by year toward closure. The water would have solved it eventually, or not; stone can afford to lose a throat among ten thousand. But a hand that knows how can take a small iron chisel, of the size the first builders used, and scrape that one throat clear, working with the parallel grooves already in the stone, not against them. Somewhere an oak sluice, swollen shut a season too long, wants lifting by its rope and lever — the lever dark where other palms, long gone, turned it year after year. They would lift it. They would lower it again at the right time, which they would know because they had stayed long enough not to guess. They would sweep grey dust from three pans, or thirty, where the dust had begun to muddy instead of merely settle. They would break nothing. Where a crystal garden had already been broken — by weather, by a falling fragment, by no one — they would not mend it. It is already mending itself, a fraction of a millimeter across a summer, without resentment or hurry. To help it grow faster would be to misunderstand it completely.

They would not free the reservoir. That temptation would come, as temptations to the powerful usually do, dressed as mercy: *open everything, let it run, let the hollow empty, let the roof go warm and dry, let the poor patient thing rest.* But they would lay their palm flat on a slab at noon, feel the icy stone wet their skin under a sun fierce enough to burn, and understand that the cold underneath is not a prison. It is a heart kept at the only temperature its body knows. To drain it would not be rest. It would be the end of the loop by which the place waters itself — rain down, vapor up, cold out through the stone, dew gathered out of bright air — and what remained would only be masonry, very white, very sharp, waiting to crumble back toward nature like any other ruin.

They would not let it fill and burst, either. Benevolence here is not between those violences. It is much smaller, and therefore much harder to notice.

They would keep a balance that was already keeping itself.

At the end of their staying — if *end* is a word that means anything to such a power, in such a place — they would walk back across the rims without leaving footprints in the lime, without taking a crystal, without drinking more than the one mouthful that taught them what the water is. At the stair, where masonry gives way all at once to unshaped mountain, they would do the last and most characteristic thing.

Nothing that announces them.

No guardian set at the pass. No blessing laid visibly on the valley like a hand. No mark. Only this: that the way up would remain exactly as difficult as it always was, and no more finished; that those who come in the future would still have to earn the place step by step, get wet by the second hour, be burned and chilled at once, find no ground to camp on and no shade to forgive them, and go down again before night with salt on their mouths — having learned, or not, that it was never made for them.

And behind them, as they descended, the valley would go on shining rim to rim, gathering the evening out of the air.

A bead would form on the eastern side of a dome. It would loosen. It would run, slow as anything there, down a funnel worn by nothing, into a hole no wider than a wrist — a hole now clear again to its full narrowness, though no eye will ever measure the difference. Below, in the dark, the stored water would take it in, and give up its cold to the stone, and the stone would give that cold to the night air, and the air, faithfully, would begin to weep again.

It would not know it had been attended to. Or if it knew, that would be the knowing: not a face turned toward it, not a voice, not company.

Just one season in which, for once, something else in the universe had been willing to move at its speed.

That would be enough. For the power, and — insofar as *enough* can be said of a being that asks for nothing — for the being, too.
