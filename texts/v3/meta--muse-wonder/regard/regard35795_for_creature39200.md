They would not bring the morning.

That is the first thing, and for a long while, the only thing that matters.

From far off — from wherever benevolent and wise powers keep their attention — this place can look like a mistake. A continent wrapped in cloud. A people without eyes. Water and heat and machinery turning forever in a circle, making nothing that leaves, going nowhere that can be named. A whole world that has forgotten, or never learned, that there is supposed to be an outside to it.

A lesser kindness would arrive lit.

It would tear the deck open, just a little, just for an hour, to *show* them. It would call that an hour mercy.

And everything here would begin, gently and irreversibly, to die: the black leaves that drink heat and not light; the spore-fog that has eaten photons for so long it would bloom and starve in sun; the skins made for pressure and rain; the fish, the frogs, the great blind fliers reading the close ceiling off their own booms; the children whose faces are read by thumb, whose geography is forty minutes of grass-click and then mud that sucks. Dawn, here, would not be revelation. It would be an unmaking.

A wise power knows that light is not the same thing as goodness. It knows that eyes are not the same thing as sight.

So it comes down the long wet shoulder of the rim, if *down* and *up* can be kept straight in a place where the lower slopes grow warmer as you climb, and it does not announce itself. In this air, announcement is a kind of violence. Sound carries obscenely far here, further than loyalty. A voice spoken carelessly on open ground may travel for days between cloud and ground and come back to strangers changed. A true intention shouted would eventually reach every hut, every siding, every herd — and bent, flattened, it would frighten things that have lived their whole lives by listening precisely.

It does not shout.

It listens first. For a long time. That is not passivity. On the plateau, listening is the most difficult form of action there is.

It stands in the listening posts at the rim, those riveted iron shelters, and presses itself to the shared wall the way travelers do, to feel whether the falls are running full. It lets the rain arrive in its districts and learns to tell rain over fern from rain over marsh from rain over hot condenser roofs, not by naming them, but by letting each one change the temperature of its attention. It stays out of the piston valleys until it can walk without adding a false rhythm. It learns the rule everyone here knows in their feet:

Never walk toward silence.

And only then does it notice the deaf zones spreading — not quickly, not malignantly, just the way marsh-gas pockets widen in a warm season — a few meters more of warm fog here, along a channel she uses; a little farther across a rope-line there, where bones lie articulated in the mud, animals that lost the world and then, quite simply, lost the thread of themselves.

This is the one damnation this place has honestly earned. Not darkness. Darkness is its element, its medium, its skin. Damnation is being cut off from confirmation. Being unable to send anything out and have the world answer back.

The power does not fill the deaf zones with noise. That would be another kind of dawn — loud, well-meaning, murderous. The fog would swallow it anyway, and what it didn't swallow would call predators walking in time with the pistons.

Instead, very slowly, it learns to do what the rails do.

Under the mud, below the fog, through wet basalt and root and slag — it sets up the faintest possible transmission. Not a voice. Not a message. A pulse. One low tremor, then its answer. Something a foot can find when the ears have gone. Something a hand flat in the mud can follow, knot by knot, back toward the rope-lines. Not enough to live by. Enough not to lie down mid-confusion. Enough for an old herd animal, deafened and lost, to feel through its splayed feet: *this way is still attached.*

And where echoes die against those pockets — a train's horn blown for the herds, a hammer-strike conversation traveling down tuned steel, a name spoken carelessly last season and still going, flattening and changing between cloud and ground — the power, when no one is depending on it and no one would notice, carries them around. The way you might cup water in your hands across a dry patch of ground. Not faster. Not clearer than they should be. Echoes here are supposed to arrive changed; that is how distance tells the truth. It only makes sure they arrive.

It guards the rim in the same spirit. Not with a wall. Walls would echo. It makes the way down *uninteresting* to the kinds of things that follow the gaze of powers: surveyors who need to close the map, missionaries of the sun, engineers certain that a system making only itself must be inefficient, rescuers who cannot believe a face without eyes is not a wound. It turns their attention aside the way cloud turns weather — gently, by giving them weather of their own to worry about, a long way off. It lets the plateau remain, as it has always been, two or three thousand kilometers across, unsurveyed. Unclosed. Finished, and still being made.

It does all of this the way the harvesters move through the forest eating fallen trunks: slowly enough to become landscape.

And then, one cycle, out on the grass-plain where the fleshy blades click together in the wind like castanets, where the rain fries loud on everything and a migration can be heard coming like hail becoming animal, it stops concealing that it is there.

She hears it before there is anything to touch. Of course she does. A wrongness in the answered world. A large warmth standing between her and a vent-field, drinking heat that should be reaching her left cheek. A breathing that has not learned not to hold itself when the frogs stop.

She does not startle. Her people are an act of patience. She turns her face up to where the cloud presses back, warm and close, and she waits until she is certain of its shape by the air it displaces.

Then she does what she would do for anything lost here.

She reaches out without hesitation and takes it by what serves it for a hand, and puts it on the rope.

This is the moment a lesser power would ruin everything by blessing her.

It lets itself be put on the rope.

It feels the knots under its palm that spell out whose section this is. It feels the condenser exhaling a kilometer away as a slow push, and its whole immense attention swaying, very slightly, in that wind. It understands, suddenly and in the body, what her mother taught her by laying a child's hand flat against a factory wall, then flat against a chest, then flat against a tree trunk spiraled in toward the heat: it is all one surface, folded. There was never a creature here waiting to be added to the world. She *is* the world, feeling its own face.

So it does the only honest thing left.

It clicks. Once. Gently, as she showed it — tongue against the roof of the mouth, sent out and waited for.

It clicks badly. A stem answers as if it were mud. Mud answers as if it were open water. The power stands in the rain with its hand on a rope in the dark at the top of the world, being wrong in a way any child here would be corrected for, like a limp.

She corrects it. Close, because voices are not to be trusted on open ground. Not unkindly. With the amusement of someone who has taught this before.

It clicks again.

Better.

The grass answers. The rope answers through her wrist into its hand. Far away, the pistons knock their regular heart into its teeth. High above the canopy, a great flier booms against the ceiling and the pressure of it passes through both their chests at once, companionably.

She laughs, then — mouth near where it keeps its listening, so the laugh won't travel — and traces, briefly, the place where its face would be, learning it the way her people learn everyone. Not looking for eyes. Finding the pattern that is there.

The power does not give her a name for itself. She does not ask for one. In a place where your spoken name may still be traveling next week, flattening and changing on its way back to tell you who you were, names are not gifts. They are responsibilities.

It stays only as long as being taught takes. Then it goes back to the work no one will ever thank it for because no one will ever know it was done: keeping the cloud unlifted, the falls running full, the echoes in transit, the mud faintly humming under the deaf places. A power become infrastructure. Maintained, like the rails. Temporary, in the same long way.

She never speaks of it on open ground.

But sometimes, in the trough of a condenser cycle, when the hut goes briefly cooler and drier and the pistons shift a half-beat in her molars, she will put her hand flat against the warm wall of the machine her family has kept breathing since before the family had a name — then flat against a wet trunk, then flat against her own chest — and she will click once into the dark, for no navigational reason at all.

And somewhere far off, patient as geology, faithful as a rope-line, something that came to save her and stayed to be led by her answers back.

Not loudly.

Correctly.
