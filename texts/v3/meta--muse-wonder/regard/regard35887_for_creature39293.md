They would not rescue them.

That is the first thing — the thing that distinguishes a benevolent and wise power from a merely kind one, or a merely powerful one. They would come up from below the clouds, through heat that should not exist at that height, up wet basalt and through districts folded into more space than the mountain contains, and they would see the polished rails, the fruit hanging uneaten, the seedlings holding forever at seedling height, the city arrived at the exact shape of its own use — and they would not reach out and break it open. They would not unmake the trees back into people. They would not restore mouths and eyes and separate skins and call that mercy.

That would only be a visitor's discomfort dressed up as compassion. To tear roots out of pavement, to give ears back to a being that surrendered ears because sound had become unlivable, to drop tens of thousands of suddenly separate bodies into noon with no gut, no habit of hunger, no language that fits inside a single throat — that is not liberation. That is an amputation performed in reverse.

So they would, at first, do the hardest thing.

They would stay, and do nothing, long enough to learn whether nothing is what is needed.

Longer than a human life. Longer than the brief damp irregularity of any one visitor moving across the skin of the streets. They would sit in the shaded courts where the fallen leaves lie in their fixed scatter, and let their own footprints be already there. They would drink the warm stone-tasting water. They would let sweat stop trying to evaporate. They would submerge their head in the green-hot bathhouse pools and listen with the sternum, with the teeth, until the roar stopped being one thing.

Because it is not one thing, to a sufficiently patient listener. No tone maintained by a body that large, that distributed, can be perfectly uninflected. Inside the hum there are districts thickening and easing. There is the rim in rain that falls upward. There is the center holding its pressure up. There are root-tensions where a bridge spans twelve feet across a forty-foot gap, and older, deeper tensions where shared walls became shared tissue. There is something that is not quite boredom and not quite grief and not quite contentment, because all three of those words require a before and an after. It is the texture of a note that has forgotten it was ever a voice.

A wise power would hear that, and would grieve — quietly, without making the grief into a verdict.

They are not dead. They are not cursed. They chose this, generation by generation, and the choice was sane: the heat, the noon, the sound left them no other country to live in except the inside of the adaptation itself. They became bark where the sun struck. They became root where the water rose. They became moss, which is to say, they became the connection between things that do not quite touch. They survived, completely.

And yet.

A lung held at full inhale is surviving. It is not breathing.

The power would know the difference, and would also know that you cannot shout *exhale* at a being that has no ears and no pauses left to think in. You cannot introduce night to a place that has never had dusk by switching the sun off; the shock would travel through every leaf at once. You cannot bring back seasons to a body that abolished cycles on purpose, lest the first autumn be indistinguishable from dying.

So they would make a change so small that it is almost a courtesy.

At the center, in the bone-dry circle around the spring that climbs twenty feet and becomes steam without falling, they would sit down on the white stone — blistering hot, though presumably they can bear it, or choose to bear it because bearing a little of the local condition is only polite — and they would place themselves where the throat is. And instead of silencing the tone, they would join it.

Exactly. At the same pitch. At the same volume. For a very, very long time.

So that for the first time since the citizens became the city, the vibration that runs through canal and root and limestone and moss comes back to them very slightly *answered*. Not louder. Not disrupted. Accompanied. A pressure met by a pressure. The way a hand, resting on a chest that has held its breath too long, does not push. It simply stays there, breathing at its own slow rate, until the other body remembers, below all thought, that the rate is possible.

One leaf's worth of change at a time.

Then, perhaps, over centuries — there is no hurry; hurry would be another violence — they would let the light dim by less than a leaf's thickness, and return. And dim again, a fraction longer. Not night. The memory of the approach toward night. The canals still full. The fountains still throwing the same arcs. Only the great continuous photosynthesis would ease by a hair and know itself easing, and the whole green felt of the moss would register, district by district, a before that is not identical to its after. Time, reintroduced at a dosage the body can survive. Surprise cut down to the size of an itch, then a breath, then a season that is only the ghost of a season.

And at the basin, they would clear a single grate that has been exactly accommodating its own blockage for a thousand years. Nothing more. They would not make the spring fall. They would make falling *available*. Water that climbs and ceases at its apex in steam would discover, in the way water discovers anything, that there is now also a way down that does not hurt. Whether it takes it in the first year or the ten-thousandth would be nobody's business but its own.

That is the whole intervention: a companion tone, a dimming that returns, an open grate.

And then the power would do the last and perhaps most benevolent thing. They would leave — but they would leave the mountain harder to find. Not walled. Not cursed. Just politely mislaid from the maps of the rescuers and the curious and the industrious, from everyone who would come with tools and good intentions and theories about what a person ought to look like. The clouds below the rim would thicken a little when approached with plans. That is all.

But before leaving, they would stand at the seedling-cracks in the pavement in the dense center, where the children hold at the exact balance of root and leaf, and they would not coax them upward. They would only make sure that if one of them ever, in its own millennial privacy, decided to grow — or to wither, which is also a decision, and also life — the decision would be *its*.

Nothing would happen, visibly, for a long time after they were gone.

The roar would continue. The noon would stand. The fruit would hang.

And then, one day that is not a day, in a shaded court where no one is present to be astonished, a leaf would loosen. Not be replaced in place without interval, not reabsorbed into the fixed scatter. *Fall*, with the tiny spiral of a thing obeying gravity and air separately from the body that made it, and land somewhere it had not landed before, in a footprint that was not already waiting for it.

The moss would feel it everywhere at once — rim, basin, Inverted Quarter, the district inside the cisterns — as the smallest local thickening of the sound.

And for the first time in longer than the city can remember, the note would change in answer to something it had done itself.

It would still be holding the inhale. But somewhere inside the held breath, vast and green and deaf and patient, there would be the faintest, newest knowledge:

that an exhale exists, and it does not have to begin yet.
