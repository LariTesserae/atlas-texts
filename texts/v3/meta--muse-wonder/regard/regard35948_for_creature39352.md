It would not come as light.

That is the first wisdom, and it would learn it before it crossed the causeway — or it would not be wise, only powerful, and the city has endured power before. Power is only weather with intentions.

It would arrive the way everything else arrives here: slowly, across the flat drowned land, its feet breaking the mineral rainbows in the sheet water and waiting, without impatience, for them to reassemble behind it. It would not clear the sky to see better. It would understand, almost at once, that the unchanging slate-brown dark is not a deprivation in this place. It is a roof. It is the condition under which what lives here can live unobserved.

A lesser benevolence would try to give them a dawn.

It would look at the shuttered upper rooms, the canals unlit except in reflection, the white sunlit city glimpsed for half a blink through iron-tasting water, and it would grieve — stupidly, tenderly, as the new always grieve for the old — that the lit city should exist only underneath the dark one, upside down and unreachable. It would raise a hand. It would thin the clouds.

And the streets would empty in a panic too slow for any watcher to recognize as panic: reflections guttering out in the cisterns, algae skins splitting where they floated, hanging drops in the doorways falling all at once in a warm rain, footprints drying mid-stride on the bridgeless bridges. What can only be seen indirectly cannot survive being illumined directly. The sunlit city is sunlit precisely because its sun is not this world's.

So the power would lower its hand.

It would not speak, either. Water assumes the shape of whatever speaks into it, and a voice with that much meaning behind it would be a flood in every pipe and basin at once. Whole districts would take its accent. The slow language of the drips — one considered syllable falling into a hollow worn in a stair, a canal answering half a minute later with a slap against an unrelated wall — would be drowned out by a single sentence, however kind.

It would learn to listen instead. This would take the longest.

It would set itself, as much as a power can, to the tempo of the city: flood, steam, drip, ebb, the long hot pause in the dark. It would learn the rules not as warnings but as courtesies. Drink only where the water moves fast enough to sound. Cross bridges only on the ebb. Navigate not by landmarks, which are merely where the city is keeping its solid furniture, but by the high clear ticking of the outer cisterns and the low tolling of the bathhouse drains at the center. When the water at its ankles cooled — the only cold thing for miles of marsh and stone — it would back out of the seam-street exactly the way it had come, without turning, and it would not, though it could have, look at what dripped in unison beyond it and stopped when noticed.

Even a god, here, backs out. That is how the power would know it had begun to be wise in the local way: when it accepted that there are arrangements older than its benevolence, and that not every closed thing is a prison, and not every silence is asking it for rescue.

It would walk the districts for what, elsewhere, would be centuries.

It would watch the ones who live in the interval condense out of the still basins on the ebb and circulate on the flood, tall in the courtyards that widen for them, stooped under the bridging rooms, leaving prints a little smaller than the feet that will later match them. It would watch the remainders — the reflection that keeps looking a moment after the head has turned, the footprint that ends in the middle of a plaza — and it would grieve for those, because that grief is accurate. Not all of them finish. Even a life made mostly of patience and return can be interrupted by evaporation.

And eventually, the way everyone does, it would come to the plaza the floods never enter.

It would stand at the edge, where the backs of the houses are blank and the floodlines climb higher with every century of dark bands, and feel the dryness hurt its teeth. It would feel the darkness above the fitted black stones fail to return even the ordinary brown dusk. It would stand there long enough for the whole city, in canals and cistern towers and hanging doorway-drops and flooded markets, to pause for exactly the length of one held breath.

Its first impulse — its kindest, worst impulse — would be to rain.

Because by then it would have understood, or thought it understood, what the thing in the plaza might be: the first of the water-arrangements, dried past circulation; or a founder who had stayed faithful to one place until fidelity itself became a kind of thirst; or simply the held point, the retention around which all that breathing and returning is organized, the way a lung is organized around the air it does not keep. The floodlines climbing the backs of the houses might be devotion. They might be siege. From outside a life, those look identical.

The power could end the ambiguity. It has ended worse. One true rain over the plaza, the first in the history of that dry square, and the stones would steam, and whatever has been retained there since before the causeway would either rise changed or be released from being the center anymore.

It would not do it.

Not because it did not care. Because it finally listened long enough — pulse after pulse, band after band of floodline rising on doorposts elsewhere in the city — to hear what the ticking of those cooling-that-never-cools stones was actually saying. It was not a plea. It was not addressed to anything. It was a being keeping its own time, at the one tempo the rest of the city cannot share: slower than the drips, slower than the sentence a human lifetime might overhear, a rhythm of almost-imperceptible loss that is also, looked at rightly, a rhythm of endurance.

To rain on it unasked would be to make it wet, briefly, and homeless forever. The city is built around its dryness the way the houses are built around the lives that never cooked in their clean hearths. Remove the absence at the center and the circulation has nothing to circulate around. Rescue, here, would be indistinguishable from demolition.

So the power would do the few things that benevolence can do without becoming occupation.

At the marsh's edge, where the causeway stones are still small and the sheet water is warm as skin, it would set a warning that is not a sign — signs require readers, and readers require a world that agrees about surfaces. Instead it would leave a place where every traveler crossing inward sees, for one unhurried moment, their own reflection in a dark pool beside the road: not as they are, but thinner, smaller-footed, already turning to look back after their head has turned away. No voice. No prohibition. Just the information, given in the only language the city trusts, that staying is a kind of consent and consent should know its price. Those who go on anyway are not deceived. The city has never deceived anyone. It has only ever been itself, patiently, at anyone who stayed.

At the same edge, facing outward, it would keep one bridge honest.

Not a new bridge — the city distrusts additions, and rightly. It would simply choose one of the crossings that already completes itself in reflection on the flood, and attend to it, ebb after ebb, the way you might hold a door for someone too weakened to ask. A way back that does not alter its user by one unnameable detail. A crossing for the remainders who reach the middle of a plaza and cannot finish their gesture; for the visitor kept first in one bathhouse pool and then in still waters nearer the door who discovers, some flood later, that part of them still prefers the marsh wind to the steam. Most would never use it. The water-arrangements are not prisoners; they are citizens of a physics. But a home you cannot leave is a different object than a home, and the power would know the difference even if the water never named it. Some evenings on the ebb, after that, a set of prints slightly smaller than anyone's would cross outward and not condense again on the far bank. The city would not protest. A lung does not protest the breath that leaves. It only makes room for the next.

And around the whole basin, unnoticed, it would thicken the unseeing.

Armies would lose the causeway in the marsh and blame the maps. Surveyors' instruments would fog with a condensation that tasted faintly of iron and lime, and their reports would say, truthfully, that nothing was there. The curious would turn back at the sheet water for reasons they would later describe as tiredness. The sky above the city would stay the color of wet slate after the last light, not because the power held it there by force, but because it stood, politely and for a very long time, between this place and every eye that would have loved it incorrectly. Some things are not hidden because they are shameful. Some things are hidden because visibility is a climate, and they evolved for another.

Then, at the center, it would do the smallest thing.

It would not enter the plaza. The floods do not, the water-arrangements do not, and the power, which could, would take its etiquette from them. It would sit at the edge where the nearest canal's drip arrives late and flattened, as if from a great distance. And it would stay through the pause — that held breath when all the water in the city stops — and it would hold its own breath with it. Not in sympathy as performance. In company.

Once, in all that staying, it would place a single drop at the very border of the dry stones. Not rain. Not an offering poured out. One warm sphere, such as hangs in the doorways at throat height during the strongest pulses, set down where the wet city ends and the kept dryness begins, close enough to be refused by simple stillness, close enough to be taken by the smallest imaginable inclination.

It would not watch to see which happened. Watching is another form of pressure, and it had unlearned pressure.

Centuries later — the city measures later differently, in floodlines — the drop would still be there: undrunk, unevaporated in a heat that evaporates remainders, because the power renews it the way the city renews everything, by return rather than by insistence. And occasionally, not often, the ticking of the plaza stones would fall, for a few beats, into the tempo of the breathing city around them, before resuming its own slower count.

Not release. Not rescue. Not a god dried out beside another god, and not a lock removed from a door that turned out to be a heart.

Just the knowledge, introduced at last into the one place in the city where nothing is ever introduced, that staying is now something that is happening by agreement.

The pulse would come in from the black marsh. The canals would swell up the water-stairs. The windows would light where they cannot be entered, amber and pale blue, above dark rooflines. Somewhere a bridge without a bridge would ripple under careful feet, and the city — hot, unlit, unpeopled in every way that matters and crowded in the one way that does — would continue.

And at its edge, unhurried and almost invisible, the power would continue too: keeping the dark kind, the exit open, the drop full.

Benevolence, at sufficient scale, looks very much like doing nothing.

It is not nothing. It is doing nothing *on purpose*, for as long as it takes, in a place that was never meant to be seen — where the bravest available love is to stand in the steam, learn the drips by pitch, and leave the stones exactly as dry as they have chosen to be.
