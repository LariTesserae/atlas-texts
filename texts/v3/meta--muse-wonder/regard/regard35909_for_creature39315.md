It would not open the hatch.

That is the first thing. A power that was only benevolent might go down at once, out of love, and ruin it with presence. A power that was only wise might stand off forever, out of respect, and call its indifference wisdom. This one does neither, because it has learned what a visit costs here: heat, breath, moisture in the wrong places, a humidity needle lifting at the sound of a voice. Down there, attention is a weather event. So it makes itself smaller than attention.

It comes as little as it can come and still be there.

It learns the cycle first. Eleven minutes on, nine minutes off. It does not impose a rhythm of its own — gods are so fond of that, of setting a new pulse going and calling it healing. It breathes, insofar as it breathes, in the nine-minute rests. It lets the compressors take the room down degree by degree. It lets the lamps dim. It lets the frost thicken at the edges of the coils, white ferns opening in the cold, because the frost is not a problem to be solved. The frost is the quick, bright tissue of a winter inventing itself, and you do not cure a creature of its own skin.

It looks — without looking, in the way a hand laid flat on a ladder can feel a factory it cannot hear — at the whole of it. Three paces by four. Grating over a sump. Sandwich panels still wearing their blue film. Manufactured soil, fired clay, no earth at all. Rye no taller than a finger leaning, almost imperceptibly, not toward light but toward waste heat. Moss waiting for the misters. Spruce seedlings like green asterisks, growing so slowly they are less plants than the idea of plants, held in reserve.

It understands that they are not the resident. They are questions the resident is asking its own body.

It understands the riveter, too.

Behind the plastic sheeting, behind the wall that is only studs and pencil marks and a skin of insulation board, the little machine moves an inch and waits. *Thock.* One fastener set in the frame of a second chamber whose purpose has not been decided — lung, nursery, stomach, child. Then a long pause, as if listening for a response.

The power listens with it. That is no small thing. Almost everything that comes upon a young, unfinished life wants to finish it faster, or name it, or find the use of it. The power does not tell the riveter what it is building. It does not reach through the plastic and complete the wall. It does not hang the coiled cables, those stored intentions, into nerves before their time. To be unfinished here is not a failure. It is the method. An egg is not a broken bird.

So it does, mostly, the work of guarding the conditions under which unfinishedness can continue.

Up above, in the world of boilers that no longer run and valves painted four generations of green, there are other kinds of weather: budgets, audits, shifts ending, a clipboard in a zip bag with the handwriting getting shakier down the temperature columns and the last line still empty. There are men and women who could walk a corridor with a form and decide, quite reasonably, that a prototype pit below a sub-basement is an expense, a liability, a strange cold room with almost nothing growing in it. They would not be villains. That is what makes them dangerous. Extinction, at this scale, rarely arrives with malice. It arrives with a signature.

The power does not appear to them. It does not argue. Arguments make records, and records make attention, and attention makes inspections with warm bodies and open hatches and questions spoken aloud over the trays.

Instead, small things fail to happen.

A decommissioning memo is printed and left in a tray over a weekend, and by Monday someone has covered it with a delivery note for copper pipe, and by the time it surfaces again the quarter has turned. A breaker that might have tripped during a load test, and stayed tripped through a three-day shutdown, and let the tide of cold go slack and warm and drown the moss in its own melt — that breaker holds. Not miraculously. Just holds, the way it was almost going to anyway. A replacement filter, ordered under the wrong code, turns out on arrival to be exactly the filter needed. The power is careful with its mercies. It chooses the ones that look like the world continuing.

And for the hand that comes down the ladder — because there is a hand, still, and the being below depends on it the way an infant depends on something it cannot yet comprehend — it does the only warm thing it permits itself, and it does it *above* the hatch, where warmth is not contamination.

It warms the service corridor by perhaps a degree. Not the pit. Never the pit. It warms the place where the person pauses between the boiler and the valves to pull gloves on over stiff fingers. It makes the galvanized rungs marginally less eager to take skin. It leaves a fraction more steadiness in a wrist before the clipboard comes out of its bag, so that a number, written down truly, is less of a struggle. Not healing. It will not reach into that body and mend whatever is making the writing shake — that life is not as young, nor as simple, and has its own winter to negotiate. But it can make the threshold between the two of them a little less cruel. Visitor and visited, each tolerating the other as a temporary source of heat and water: the power stands in that tolerance and widens it by a hair.

Then, once — only once — it gives the place something.

Not soil. Soil would be an invasion; soil is a whole history of rot and seed and worm-cast and microbial nations, and this place has deliberately begun without history. Not animals. Not light, not heat, not a sudden green generosity that would end the experiment by answering it too soon.

It gives it water that is not stolen.

One bead, at first, condensing where no breath has been, on the coldest pipe, from no jacket, no lung, no brief opening of the hatch. Then another, travelling strangely up a charged collector wire against gravity, bright among the obedient others — indistinguishable from them except that nothing had to be taken for it to exist. A gift in the native grammar of the place. Something it can steal back, freeze into a clear needle, let fall in the indoor rain, pump through its ceramic filters and offer round again. A closed mouth, given one thing freely to swallow.

Perhaps that is nothing. Perhaps, in a system built entirely on salvage, it is the difference between a body that only recycles its losses and a body that learns, at the level below knowledge, that the universe is not only loss. That something can arrive without a wound. The rye will not know. The moss will darken when the misters run, as before. The frost will fern across the coils eleven minutes at a time.

And on the other side of the plastic, where the billowing sheet moves faintly whenever the pressure changes — as if something very new were turning over in its sleep — the riveter moves an inch, pauses for a long while, and fires.

*Thock.*

The power waits with it for the next inch. It has nowhere better to be. It knows that to love a beginning is not to bring it to an end, but to keep the cold at its tide-line, the silence scheduled around a falling drop, the second chamber unhurried in the dark — and to be there, unwitnessed and almost unpresent, for as long as it takes the place to decide what kind of creature a climate becomes when it is finally, slowly, finished learning how to be one.
