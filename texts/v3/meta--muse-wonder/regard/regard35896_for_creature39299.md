It would wait.

Not for a day, not while a human visitor would still be counting. It would come to the rim where the old mountains have worn themselves into backs, and it would sit in the mist, and it would let the chorus enter it — the falling water, terrace to terrace, the reeds, the frogs without season, the booming of the great birds — until it understood that the first thing it had to learn here was not what the place needed.

It was how long the place took to need anything at all.

A lesser power would arrive with gifts already decided. Warmth, because the water is cool to the teeth. Light, because noon is only a brightening in fog. Dry land, because the skin of the inhabitants splits along leaf-veins if they stay too long in air. Fire. Medicine. History. A rescue for the very old, whose fingers root in sleep, before the terrace lip receives them and a ribcage becomes, in somebody else's lifetime, another buttress.

The wise one would feel each gift turn in its hands.

Warmth is fever here. Light is a heron's legs descending. Dryness is the beginning of a wound. Fire would not cook their food; it would ask for fuel, and then for clearing, and then for keeping, and the whole even agreement of rot and unfolding would have, for the first time in ten thousand years, an appetite in it. A story told straight, with events in it, would wear against the noise a generation and then do the worse thing: survive, make a beginning, make the present feel like an aftermath.

And to pull the old ones back from rooting — to call that a rescue — it would first have to decide that the tree is not also them. That the chamber where children and pale fish turn together in the dark is only a grave, and not a room an ancestor has become. It would look through the black water with its greater sight and it would see them still listening through the wood. Slower. Not gone in any way it was certain it had the right to improve.

So inside the rim, it would do almost nothing.

Almost, because even reverence, if it touches, touches.

It would go down by degrees, as anyone must: across the mat that gives like a lung, into the lane, into the tea-dark water that will not warm around a body. It would let a cool palm come up under the weave to steady it, and not startle at being steadied by what it had come to bless. It would hold a pillar tree and feel the whole plateau conducting through its sternum — water falling, roots settling a finger's width a century, and underneath, patterned, the tapping of the inhabitants deciding nothing in a hurry.

And it would answer, once. Not a voice. The outer ears here closed generations ago, and a voice from above the leaves would be only weather and alarm. A knuckle, once, against a hollow buttress. No command in it, no name. The grammatical equivalent of: *I am here, and I am not taking.*

Then it would be still beside them for as long as stillness takes to become legible. Which here is years. It would let a child mistake it for a new pillar and braid a root experimentally near it. It would let an old one trail past with her cropped turtle, and not heal the rootlets at her knuckles, and not praise her endurance either, because praise would make endurance a performance. If a rootlet, in time, were encouraged across its own resting place — if a chamber were cleared beside theirs, and a lane kept open in the leaves exactly the width of its face — it would accept the weaving for a while, the way you accept a seat in a household whose customs you have no intention of ruling.

That would be the extent of the miracle anyone under the leaves ever noticed. Perhaps, afterward, they would tap a pattern they had not tapped before, a small stillness-with-a-presence-in-it, and keep it in their fingers, untaught, the way they keep everything.

All the rest of its work it would do outside, where work would not become a wound.

Because this place is perfect, and perfection at the top of the world is not the same as safety. Its agreement is local. Its spring begins as snow and seepage on slopes that owe it nothing. Its rim is still eroding, only politely. Below the cloud-filled valleys there are, or will be, other peoples: dry-handed again, ingenious again, thirsty in drought years, quite capable of finding a high sea on a map and calling it unused. There are fevers that travel in birds. There are fish that do not belong in the root baskets and would eat the glass shrimp to clear water and then starve in a desert of their own success. There is a warming that can lift mist off a plateau a hand's-width a century and never once announce itself as an ending.

Against those things, at a distance where help does not have to be understood to be received, the power would become very busy and completely unremarkable.

It would keep the passes slightly harder to remember than they are to find. Not a wall, not a spell of terror — benevolence does not make a sanctuary into a prison for everything outside it. It would simply let the track braid, the way roots braid, until caravans and surveyors and the merely curious mostly arrived somewhere else, at some other grey water, and went home satisfied with the wrong wonder. Those who came in true need, or in true gentleness, would not be turned. The place itself has always known how to receive a guest: steady the mat, open a lane, decide over years whether to weave them in.

It would tend the unseen plumbing of the plateau. In the dry year that comes once in four hundred, it would persuade one extra seam of groundwater up through old fiber, no more than the terraces would have received in a wet year anyway. In the wet year that comes once in nine hundred, it would let one hillside far below drink what would otherwise have tested every lip at once. Never enough to change the level. Only enough to keep change at the speed of living.

It would stand, invisible and unthanked, between the pools and the plagues of the lower world — a heron's migration nudged a valley east, a fever cooling out of a flock before it rose; an invading seed failing, politely, to find the water to its taste, not here, not this century. It would not make the inhabitants immune to death. Death here is a form of architecture. It would only keep death from arriving all at once, in a single accent, faster than fingers can learn a new braid.

And because it is wise, and therefore not fooled by the word *final*, it would prepare for the one thing the sea on the roof cannot say about itself: that even this will pass. The rim that erosion rounded so kindly will, in some age no inhabitant needs to imagine, round a fraction too far. A terrace will go to a terrace will go, not in catastrophe, if it can help it, but in a long sighing downward of black water and leaf and root.

So, very far below, in valleys no one has yet thought to dam, it would begin a rumor of wetlands. A beaver family dreaming, a season early, of an interesting bend in a stream. Otters clearing a channel no otter needs. Certain seeds, carried in certain guts, surviving a journey they were almost not built for, and waking in mud at altitudes where their kind has not been remembered. Terrace after terrace, unbuilt, only *ready* to be built, at a pace indistinguishable from geology. No inhabitants would be moved. No prophecy would be sent up through the pillars to trouble the even dark. Only, at the lowest edge of the system, one lane would be kept open where nothing needs to pass.

It was already their oldest custom, after all. The power would merely extend it into the future: a way left clear, because something may pass in ten thousand years.

Then it would leave the way it came, which is to say it would take its attention off the surface so gently that the mist would not close behind it like a door. From time to time, over the long settling of the world, it would put its palm again to a pillar and listen through its ribs — not to check whether its work was holding, but because it had learned, there, the rarest discipline available to the strong:

To love a finished thing without finishing it oneself.

To guard the edges, answer once, and let the frogs go on saying everything, at full volume, forever, in the dark under the leaves.
