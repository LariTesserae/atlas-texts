They would not announce themselves.

No light would split the plain. No voice would speak from the circle of sky. A power that is benevolent because it is powerful, and wise because it is benevolent, would already know the first cruelty available to it here: to arrive as an event.

The Cup has had enough of events. It has weather enough.

So they would come the slow way, on foot, and feel it long before they saw it — the low roar coming up through cracked earth into the soles of their feet — and they would stop at the rim the way you stop at the edge of someone else's sleep. Not to study. First, only to learn the timing.

They would watch a whole day before they went down.

They would watch the Ring dim to its sullen orange line. They would watch the gyre loosen, the swifts drop in their tight spirals, the heron take its slow, offended leave before the tone climbed. They would count — not seventeen minutes, never seventeen minutes — but the shape of the interval, the way it gathers. They would see the runnels cleared in spirals water does not make. The mineral scales stacked flat as coins by the pylons. The brine pits raked into whorls that copy the pool. The bench in the roofless shelter worn smooth in the middle.

They would understand, before they ever saw it move, that pity would be a form of ignorance here.

Because they could unmake what the Cup has made. That is the terrible simplicity of power. They could lift it out of its terrace pool where it sleeps with only its plated back breaking the surface. They could dry its lungs gently and teach them air again, strip the travertine from its spine plate by shining plate, give it back narrow shoulders for a doorway and hands that were not made wide for glass-smooth stone, restore its eyes to a single sun. They could return the watcher to the species that built the shelter and hung instruments on hooks at human height.

They could call that rescue.

And the being would die of it more slowly and more thoroughly than drowning, even if its body lived. It would die of thin air and single light and silence in the soles of its feet. It would die of a horizon. You cannot separate a resident into its kinds a second time and expect the human portion, alone, to be the whole.

So they would not.

They would go down the ramp between surges, barefoot if they had feet, carrying nothing metal, nothing that would tingle and be politely returned to the base of a pylon by morning. They would keep to the dark band of basalt that is always wet and never quite submerged. They would drink, if they drank at all, from the fresh runnels on the northern terrace — cold, startlingly pure — and be grateful without making a ceremony of gratitude. When the tone rose in their teeth, they would climb. Even they would climb. To stand in the center during a surge and be unharmed would be another kind of announcement, and the Cup permits visitors only on condition that they behave like visitors.

In the lulls, perhaps, they would meet it.

Not face to face. It does not live face to face anymore. They would be filling a flask, or standing quite still by a pylon grown over with scale, and the stone would tremble in a pattern that was not the machine — a withdrawal, unhurried, into a seam they hadn't noticed. They would not pursue it. They would not speak its old name, if names are even the kind of thing that survives mineralization. They would only, once, lay a palm flat against the pitted face of a pylon while its own wide hand rested on the far side, attending, and let the vibration pass between them.

*I see you. I am not here to take you home. This is home, and I see that it is.*

And because a wise power does not offer freedom as a test it already knows the answer to, they would leave the question inside the touch rather than forcing it into a choice. But the door would be real, for the space of that contact. If some old, buried, human stratum in it still ached for dry grass and unplated hands, the way would open and not hurt.

The pylon would hum. The plates on its back would click, faintly, as it shifted its weight on wet basalt.

Then it would press its back to a vent until something clicked, deep and soft, under the stone, and go on tending the equilibrium.

That would be answer enough.

What they *would* do, they would do where no visitor will ever see it.

Far below the pool — below the bowl, below the exposed bright end of the machine — there is a root of the thing, and a fracture traveling through deep rock toward the aquifer, slow as all stone sorrows are slow. In a hundred years, perhaps three hundred, it would have begun to starve the upwelling. The gyre would have loosened and not tightened again. The Ring's tone would have thinned. The terraces would have dried from the bottom up, the ferns first, then the frogs in their seams, then a plated back in a shrinking pool, breathing air too thin through skin made to breathe mist. The machine's makers built well, but they built for tending that never came from inside the bowl, and the one tender they got cannot reach that deep.

They could reach that deep.

So they would mend it — not improve it, not make it clean or efficient or safe. Improvement would be its own destruction. The leak is the ecology now: the doubled light, the hovering droplets, the influence that turns compasses lazily toward the turning water. They would leave the leak exactly as it is. They would only close the fracture, seat the root true in its socket of basalt, and give the aquifer back its slow, iron-dark pressure. Done so gently that on the surface nothing changes except, one lull, a click under a pylon that sounds a fraction cleaner than the click before it. A pause in the wide hands. A head lifting, teeth humming a breath early, feeling the tone come truer in the bone.

It would not know why. It does not need to know why. Gifts that require gratitude are transactions, and this is not one.

And on the plain above, they would do the second thing, which is also almost nothing.

They would let the Cup become a little harder to want. Not hidden — hidden things invite searching. Just unmemorable, in the way of certain stretches of flat land that surveyors' eyes slide off and mapmakers render faithfully and wrongly and no expedition can quite justify funding a second time. Men will still come occasionally, as they have always come: curious, careful, quick to learn the timing. Those the place can permit. But the ones who would come with drills and ownership, with rescue in their mouths and cages in their plans, with a humane eagerness to extract the resident and name it and cure it on camera — those would find their interest wandering, their funding thinning, their road perpetually postponed for reasons that felt, to them, entirely their own. The plain is wide. There is so much else to want.

That is the shape of their benevolence: not a wall. A waning of appetite in everything that would break the bowl to possess it.

Last, they would visit the shelter.

They would not sit on the bench; it is worn smooth in the middle by a body that uses it, and some seats are not for guests. They would not touch the hooks. They would stand in the roofless room while spray stippled them with salt, and they would witness the small, swept arcs on the floor — low and wide — and understand that a life is being lived here that is neither a haunting nor a sentence. It is a keeping. It is someone setting down, neatly, the things that aren't theirs.

Then they would leave the floor exactly as clean as they found it, and go up the slick ramp while the Ring brightened on the wet walls behind them and the seeps turned to threads of light.

From the plain there would be nothing to mark that anything had happened at all. Wind in dry grass. The wide flat horizon restored. Ordinary quiet, except in the soles of your feet.

Below the surface, the water would still be turning in its bright, enclosed weather. The swifts would time their spirals. The heron would keep its grievances. The frogs would sing the water down at night out of the seams in the stone, and something low would answer through the sternum of the bowl itself. And the keeper of the place — stone on its back, animal underneath, timing all the way through — would cross the dark basalt in the two quiet minutes, droplets settling across its skin and vanishing without steam, and set its hands to the pylons, and feel, perhaps, that the deep roar ran a little steadier than it had run in all its centuries.

Not saved. Not abandoned.

*Kept company with, once, by something strong enough to do anything — and wise enough to understand that here, love looked like mending what it could not see, guarding what it would not touch, and leaving before the next surge.*

It has nowhere else to be.

They would make sure the world lets that be true.
