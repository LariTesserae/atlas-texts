First, it would listen.

Not from above. Not in the way powers are imagined to listen, all at once and perfectly. It would come down — if *down* is a word that still applied — and put something like a palm against something like a wall, and wait.

It would wait longer than anyone expected. Seasons. Exhalations. Weepings of the sails. It would let a tower go silent in its presence and it would not rush in. It would watch the people file out across the open ground in their long, unsigned line, leaves turning to watch them go, and it would understand, slowly, that the silence is not an emergency it has been sent to repair. It is a sentence being finished. Interrupting it would not be kindness. It would be theft.

Because everything the merely benevolent would want to fix here is load-bearing.

Silence the basin? The reeds would stop pumping. The canopy would thin. The drip-basins would dry, the frogs would fail, the hunters would fall out of the stitched air, and the green — that violent, oversaturated green — would discover in a single season that its sun was a sound. Bring rain? The meadows that open in minutes and fold away have no use for permanence; they would rot open. The cupped leaves would overflow and split. The concentrated weeping of the sails is poison and sacrament in the same drop, and everything here has already made its bargain with it. Build in the gaps, raise the roads, draw the towers close and warm and dense the way a city *should* be? The doorways would begin opening onto wrong years, wrong heights, solid stone. People would step through humming frames and arrive inside the young rock that the whole sparse arrangement exists to appease.

Heal the ears? The shunts are not wounds. They are signatures. Lineage is carved into them. Trade is carved into them. Children touch them the way children elsewhere pull sleeves. To give these people ordinary ears would be to make them deaf in the only language their bodies speak, and to erase, in the name of mercy, the sculpture by which a grandmother is read across a market gallery.

It would learn all of this the hard way, which is to say the local way: it would go outdoors without muffling, once, for a count of three, the way tower children dare each other on terraces. And it would come reeling back — if powers can reel — weeping and grinning, flooded, having heard the world raw: the billions sawing in the canopy, the wet-drum frogs, the four-winged things screaming between towers, the freight arriving with its soft concussive *whump*, and underneath it all the great felt note, the one the city was built sparsely and carefully never quite to play.

It would not shout over that. Nothing wise shouts here twice.

So it would do almost nothing, and the almost would take all of its strength.

Deep below the basin, where the ridges are still new enough to have bright fractures, something is locking. This is what the towers listen to with their people's palms. This is what the elders hear when they go still in the galleries, hearing *downward*, past the pulse, into the settling rock. Left alone, the lock will not break tomorrow, or in a hundred years. It will break on a morning when, by chance, too many towers exhale together, too many doorways hum at once, the reeds lean the same way in the same wind — and the basin will find the note it has been circling since before there were people here to build on it. One note. Played completely. Once.

The power would spend its centuries there, in the dark under the unbuilt ground, loosening that lock by increments too small to name. A tremor here, no larger than the ridges already make as they cool at night. A slip there, disguised as settling. Stones trembling in place, puddles standing in rings — the basin already speaks this dialect fluently and will not notice a few extra sentences. No earthquake averted, no miracle recorded. Just a catastrophe patiently unmade into ten thousand ordinary nights of ticking rock.

In the gaps — which must stay empty, which it would guard more fiercely than any wall — it would leave water the way the basin itself leaves water: intermittently, and almost out of reach. Not rain. Not rivers. Deep lenses of it, sealed in fracture-basins under jungle no one digs in, accessible only to the roots of the twenty-foot reeds in the worst of droughts, when the sails have not wept and a tower's exhalation has come up thin. Enough to keep the loop from breaking. Never enough to change its rhythm. The reed-keepers would come back from those seasons with their glazed serenity and say the reeds had found something. They would turn their faces toward the nearest doorway-frame, like plants, and no one would argue with them.

And at the doorways — those frames humming at the threshold of pain, where the lattice occasionally misplaces a traveler by a few fined and documented seconds — it would stand the way doorway-tenders stand, teeth lightly clenched, feeling for the wrong note. Most crossings it would not touch. The few that go truly wrong, the ones that do not arrive in transit authority ledgers because they never arrive at all: those it would catch, quietly, in whatever it has instead of hands, and set down gently in their own year, damp with someone else's weather, ears ringing, remembering nothing but a feeling of having been *held* mid-step. It would absorb the discrepancy itself. Powers can afford a few seconds. It would spend them gladly and say nothing.

It would protect the place the only way a place like this can be protected: by preserving how uninteresting it looks from far away. A low, warm basin under a dim sky. A few lights sixty miles apart. Nothing to mine that isn't still deciding what it is. Nothing to conquer that wouldn't go silent and produce, in warm wet weather, one furnished floor too many. Let other attentions — less patient, more pitying — pass over it and see a loud, dry, empty inconvenience and move on. Obscurity is a mercy this city has already half-invented. The power would simply finish the job.

It would not visit the quiet-rooms to speak. That temptation it would refuse with particular care. It knows what a voice does in those rooms: a name said aloud once so it exists in air, a marriage whispered, a confession. To speak there, with whatever a power has for a voice, would be an intimacy no one consented to and an event no cantor could follow. Instead it would learn sign. Big outdoor sign first, elbows out, fingers splayed to be read through mist, bright sleeves snapping — badly, at first, the way mystics describe anything, happily, for years. Then the smaller hip-level flutter of the galleries. Then, latest and most humbly, the touch-alphabet: two fingers walking a sentence down the inside of a forearm. *Yes,* once. *Stay,* twice. It would be, everyone agreed, a slow student. Towers are patient with slow students.

If it left any mark at all, it would be this: that once in a generation, in the quietest hour — which is to say, in a roar that would be deafening anywhere else — a listener with a palm flat against a living wall feels the tower's pulse hesitate, and then steady, as if something very large had briefly steadied it from the other side. Not repaired it. Not answered it. *Listened back.* The listeners do not sign about this. There is no sign for it that isn't also the sign for weather, and for family, and they have decided, without ever discussing it, that this is accurate.

The towers would dream of it occasionally. In warm wet weather, faint patterns of light would travel across the sweating, self-healing surfaces — a spiral no tower has produced before, gone by morning, remembered by the elders who hear downward. Children would dare each other to take their mufflers off and listen for it, and hear only the basin, entire and unbearable and theirs.

Three million people spread across sixty miles of young black rock, most of whom will never meet, all of whom are at every moment almost touching — and over them, under them, in the rock and the reeds and the held seconds of the doorways, one more inhabitant, sparse and careful and at great distance, keeping its hands flat against the walls of the world it chose not to save from itself.

It would stay until the lock under the basin is fully, finally loose.

Then it would go the way towers return from silence: without announcement, leaving the pressure in everyone's chest exactly where they expect it to be, and the note — the great note — unplayed for one more age of the world.
