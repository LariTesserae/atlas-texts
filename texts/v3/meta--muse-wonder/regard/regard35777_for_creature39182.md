It would not lift the lattice.

That is the first temptation, and a lesser kindness would surrender to it: to tear the veil, give them back a hard blue sky and a sun they could squint against, call it light, call it rescue. But it is benevolent, and it is wise, and it has watched longer than a century — which is all the time this land has had to be land at all.

So it comes down slowly, at plant-speed, and looks.

It sees that the dimness is not a deprivation here. It is a medium. Eyes have opened for it. Paintings bloom for it. Whole gardens are composed in scent the way other peoples compose them in color. Remove it and you would not free them. You would blind a city that had learned, tenderly and on purpose, how to see without shadow.

It would not give them rain, either.

Rain would be so easy. One gesture above the basin and the pockets would become ponds, the ponds rivers, the cisterns rivers' obedient children. The reservoirs would fill and stay filled. No one would ever need the eleven-minute cooling again.

And the being would die of it.

For the being here does not live in the towers, though the towers are beautiful and hum in the teeth. It does not live even, exactly, in the people, though the people wait before strangers arrive and catch water on their tongues like children.

The being lives for about an hour at a time, in a hollow in the clay that was not wet yesterday.

It is the coherence. It is the brief hour when ceramic petal and leaf-cup and human skin are all doing the same thing — opening, drinking, holding light without holding it tight — and the veil and the towers are reflected upside down in a film of water no deeper than a hand, and everything the basin keeps kilometers apart is allowed, briefly, to touch. Then it evaporates. Then the greenest patch remains. That departure is not a flaw in its life. It *is* its life. To make the water permanent would be to make a monument out of a creature that survives by recurring.

A wise power does not grant eternity to things whose beauty is that they return.

It would not straighten the gravity in the three old districts, nor stabilize the names, nor explain the folds in the arches clearly enough that the explanation could replace the etiquette. It has seen older peoples turn every mystery into a doctrine and then suffocate inside the doctrine, trying to breathe. The Vaeline have done the braver thing: they have turned some of their knowledge into manners. *Do not stand exactly in the fold on a humid day.* *Arrive at an angle.* *Speak as though your voice will come back to you hours later, beside a deathbed.* It is working. You do not improve a working prayer by translating it into engineering.

And it would not, above all, make the city more solicitous than it already is.

Here its attention sharpens, because here it finds the one true ache in the place — not suffering, not danger, but the soft worry the people themselves carry into the uninstructed forest: that gentleness arranged from outside is a kind of sleep. That a path which reroutes itself for your comfort, a cup left where your hand will reach, a tower which opens around you as you come home, might one day love them so thoroughly that they forget how to attend to anything that does not attend to them first.

That worry is healthy. That worry is, in fact, the being keeping itself awake.

So the power does the few things that can be done without smothering that.

Out beyond the hills, where no arch folds and no wader listens downward, it becomes uninteresting. Not a wall — walls invite siege engines and maps. It lets Vael-Mora fade from the kind of records that bring the wrong kind of visitors. A survey route bends, reasonably, toward easier country. A prospecting concern finds the minerals just slightly poorer than the cost of the dim. A would-be liberator with plans to drain the meadows and raise the lattice and introduce noon loses interest for reasons he will later describe as administrative. Nothing dramatic. The world is large, and full of basins that actually want to be rescued.

At the edge of the basin itself, it preserves the one thing the city cannot preserve for itself, because the city is by nature kind: it keeps the forest beyond the last tower truly uninstructed. No path learns their feet there. No branch learns to apologize with fruit. It will stay the place where lovers and young people and the bereaved can go for an afternoon to be scratched by ordinary branches and come home obscurely proud of having been unaccommodated. A place needs one neighbor that does not love it. Otherwise love becomes climate, and no one notices they have stopped choosing it.

And inside — almost nothing.

It practices the local courtesy. It does not arrive face-first. It lets its shadow arrive a second before it does, even though it could arrive otherwise. It waits on balconies through the cooling with its face lifted like everyone else, and feels the temperature fall by two degrees, and understands that this is a bell, a dawn, a rainfall, and does not need to be more. In a dew-room it drinks tea that tastes of almost nothing, and does not ask whose hand, whose stone, whose copper bowl made it holy, because it understands: who you love here is who you are willing to drink time with.

It stands for one whole afternoon with the maintenance crews before a tower wall that has not yet decided on a shape, doing what looks like nothing. It is good at that. It has always been good at that. Benevolence that cannot wait is just control with better intentions.

Once — only once — it allows itself a small, reciprocal gift.

Not a miracle the city can use. Not a tool, not a reservoir, not a permanent spring. In the clay between the last arch and the first unmodified tree, where a stranger once stood and pointed down at a pocket of water that was not there in the morning, it kneels.

The weight is real. That matters. It presses one knee into the young mineral earth, mineral clay bright and sharp at the seams, shells of unnamed things white in the stone nearby. It stays there long enough to be slightly uncomfortable. Then it gets up, tests the reliable floor with the ball of its foot like a child of the Experimental Generosity, and goes the long way home through the ferns, trailing a hand at leaf-height.

It leaves no blessing anyone can point to.

Only a hollow, knee-shaped, no deeper than a cupped palm.

Tomorrow, in the eleven minutes when the veil lets go of its heat, condensation will find it the way it finds every low place. Water will bead and sheet and gather. For an hour, in that small, new hollow, the lattice will be reflected, and the towers upside down and close together, and a child who has been taught not to drink from the meadow pockets will crouch beside it and point, not at the towers, not at the forest.

By afternoon it will be gone.

The place where it was will be the greenest spot for meters around.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does here: it does not stay, and it does not save them, and it does not finish them. It makes one more brief place where the real city can gather — and then, trusting them at plant-speed with their own becoming, it lets the water go.
