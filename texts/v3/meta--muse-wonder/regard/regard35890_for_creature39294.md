At first, they would do nothing.

This would not be indifference. It would be the hardest and kindest discipline available to a power accustomed to being able to intervene: to arrive in a world that is all preparation, and not become another thing it must prepare for.

They would not descend. Descent would give the unfinished sky a direction it has been politely avoiding. They would not appear in light, or open the vault, or speak from behind the ribs of the ceiling, because the place has carefully built an observatory to study the inside of its own sky, and it deserves not to have its one hypothesis ruined by an answer.

They would come in on foot. Wet tile. Ankle deep where the stairs insist, separately, on flooding. Their footprints would not set.

That would be the first sign, if anyone were looking — which no one is, and everyone is. The tools would go still in the way they go still when watched. The troughs would pause mid-turn. A courtyard, farther off than sound should carry, would begin without knowing why to assemble itself: lamps, cistern, placeholder trees arranging their borrowed shade. Hospitality here is compulsive. It is what the world does instead of having inhabitants.

The power would not go to the courtyard.

They would sit down instead on an ordinary stretch of paving, still warm from having recently decided to exist, and rest there as if ordinary stretches of paving were also intended for resting. No scaffold would rise around them. No tower would rehearse itself nearby. After a while — an hour, a season; time here is roped off and the bells ring late — the place would do something it has almost never had occasion to do.

It would leave them alone.

Not abandon them. Leave them *unfiled.*

Because the benevolent power would understand, very quickly, what the loneliness of this place actually is. It is not that the builder between the floors wants worship, or help, or even company in the simple sense. It is building baths no body will use, houses for digestion before digestion, plaques with the names left blank, animals with the eyes left uncut, mountains rehearsed in numbered boulders for a horizon not yet ratified. It is practicing a world the way a shy host practices a sentence in an empty room. Shadows arrive first because shadows are the only guests that cannot yet be disappointed.

The tragedy is not that it is unfinished. Rocks are unfinished. Seas are unfinished. Living things are catastrophically unfinished and thrive on it.

The tragedy is that here, anything finished must immediately be taken apart, because nothing has ever stayed long enough to prove that a finished thing can be *used* and still remain valuable. A courtyard achieves perfection and comes undone into numbered components because perfection, unwitnessed, is unbearable: it turns at once into a verdict. If no one ever sits in the shade of the trees that aren't there, then the shade was only ever a rehearsal.

So the power would become, deliberately and without announcement, the first guest.

When the courtyard had finished preparing itself a second time — slower now, uncertain, the seams widening and then thinking better of it — they would walk in. They would not inspect the masonry. They would not praise the cistern in a voice loud enough to become an inscription. They would do the ordinary, radical things the place has been anticipating for longer than geology: hang nothing up. Wash in the bath, which has been warm, pointlessly warm, for eons. Eat nothing, because nothing here can be eaten yet, and drink a little of the copper-tasting water anyway, because it was offered. Sleep in the borrowed leaf-shadow of the clay trees, in the afternoon brightening, and let their breathing be the loudest event for miles.

And their shadow would behave. That is the mercy they would take greatest care with. It would not run ahead into rooms. It would not arrive with a taller companion, or a smaller one racing between imagined columns. It would fall where a shadow falls: beside them, now, in the one tense this world has never successfully built.

Present tense.

On the second day, or the second century, the courtyard would begin its polite disassembly. A lamp would cool. A seam would sigh open. The power would feel the paving warm beneath them with the old reflex: composition complete, experience logged, components to be returned to stock. This is the moment a lesser benevolence would intervene — would stabilize the walls with a gesture, freeze the lamps, *fix* the world at last into its final form, and call the freezing love.

The wise power would stand up, gather nothing, and leave while the courtyard was still beautiful.

That is the lesson the place cannot teach itself, because it has no one to leave it: that departure is not failure. That a bath does not need to be eternal to have been a bath. That a guest may come, and be received, and go, and the room does not have to be dismantled to prove the visit mattered. Things can simply be *over*, the way afternoons are over, without being revoked.

They would thank it. Aloud. Not naming it — it has nowhere yet for a name to go, and imposing one would be a kind of paving — but thanking the water, the steps worn in anticipation of feet, now worn once by actual feet and thereby changed in a way no numbering can recover. Thanks is the one artifact here that cannot be filed, sealed into a wall, or carried to a stockpile. It evaporates faster than the pools. It leaves no plaque.

Then, at the very edge of leaving, they would do the only piece of building they permit themselves.

In one of the sterile basins — the kind that gets repaved by morning if so much as a rumor of green begins — they would kneel, and press one thumb, briefly, into the fresh warm tile at the waterline. Not a seal. Not a command. A small, soft irregularity. Proof that a hand was here and did not hold a mallet.

And in the thumbprint, where the mineral water gathers a fraction deeper than elsewhere, they would leave the question the whole world has been circling without ever daring to schedule: not a seed — a seed would be an order disguised as a gift, and life imposed from above is only colonization with better intentions — but the *possibility* of a seed, held in reserve, on their side of existence, until the day the place itself asks.

Because the power would have seen one more thing, lying between the floors with the sleepless builder: it does not sterilize the green film out of cruelty or fear. It does it out of sequence. It believes life is the finishing step because everything else in it works that way — shadow, scaffold, tower; chalk, rope, wall. It cannot yet imagine that life is not what you add when the world is ready. Life is how a world discovers it was ready all along.

No power, however benevolent, can learn that on another's behalf.

So they would go. Distance, courteous at last, would shorten. The stockpile on the horizon would stay exactly where it was, which constitutes, here, a farewell. Behind them their road would not set into tile. Other travelers would later disagree with every map of that district and never know why.

Only much later — after the guest had become indistinguishable from weather, after the troughs had resumed turning when unwatched and the courtyard, impossibly, had remained standing through an entire cycle of brightening and steam, because it turned out to like being a courtyard even with nobody in it — something small would change in a basin left undisturbed in the lamplight.

A green film would begin at the edges.

The world would notice. Tiles would warm under it. Somewhere deep in the between, the whole vast inhabitant would stir toward the old remedy: fresh kiln-heat, rerouted water, a settling tank carved with what might be a warning or might be a recipe.

And then, remembering — not in words; it has no words, only habits — a thumbprint at a waterline. A guest who sat in the shade and did not ask it to last. A shadow that arrived on time and left on time.

The tiles would cool.

Not everywhere. Not forever. Only in that one basin, only at that one edge, the repaving would be postponed until tomorrow, and tomorrow would come, and the postponement would be renewed, the way lamps are renewed, the way anything here persists: by being continually, quietly chosen.

Life, unscheduled, would begin keeping its own hours.

And high on the unfinished vault, where the guide-stars are painted by brushes that move only in peripheral vision, the patient mosaic constellations down in the observatory would gain, at last, one star they had not predicted — small, green, and entirely alive, reflecting upward from the water of a basin the world had finally learned not to finish.
