There is no arrival here in any useful sense.

No break in the cloud. No voice. The light at midday is no less grey, and at midnight no more stars stand in it than before. A power wise enough to deserve the name would know, before it did anything else, that the first mercy it could offer this place was not to announce itself.

For a lesser benevolence it would be a terrible temptation. The plateau looks, from outside, like a list of things to fix. Break the steam-cloud and give them a dawn. Heal the small grey eyes. Still the crawlers, or wake them to some remembered purpose, empty the holds that may not have been emptied in centuries. Lead the people down, at last, through the cloud sea to rivers and fields and a sky with a direction in it.

It would be a catastrophe wearing kindness as a coat.

Sunlight would not bless this country. It would bleach and kill the heat-mats, dry the condensation pans that rain only under cold overhangs, burn eyes that have spent generations learning not to need themselves. The grazers read heat through their feet and the pits in their hides; the hunters read the world by vibration and warmth; the fish rise in black water for what falls out of steam. Give them noon and you have not given them sight. You have only taken away the darkness everything here is literate in.

Stop the crawlers and the pasture stops with them. The fresh tailings bloom, the drill-holes fill and spawn, the lee walls move their weather slowly across the plain, the whole travelling arrangement of herd and band and cleaner swarm and predator comes apart — not all at once, which might at least be honest, but over a hungry generation, while the people waited beside cooling hulls for a vent-warning that would never come again.

Take them down to the lowlands and you have taken them to a place their lungs cannot use. Their grandchildren were born here. That sentence is not sentiment on the plateau. It is physiology.

So the power does what almost nothing else that finds this place manages to do.

It learns to travel the way everything else travels. Not across it. After warmth.

It stays long enough — and long, here, is not a season but a route — to understand that quiet is not loneliness, that darkness is not privation, that a life timed by pan-drip and vent-cycle and the slow turning of a company toward mesas no living walker will reach is not a life waiting to be interrupted. It puts no hand flat in warning because it is not yet kin enough to be believed. It does not shelter in a lee it has not learned to read. It leaves the long-eared scavengers and the hand-sized moths entirely alone.

It keeps company.

If it acts at all, it acts at the scale this place trusts: through the ground, under the threshold of story, in a degree's difference.

A warm seam holding a little longer under a band that has lost its crawler for a while and has children too young to walk far between blooms. Not a miracle. Just a seam that does not cool as fast as it might have, long enough for small feet to learn it.

A centrifuge, deep in the orange dark of an old hull listing to its good flank, that puts a hesitation into its rhythm a half-beat earlier than failure — early enough for an inside-kin child, palm flat to a plate, to feel the wrongness and know where to put her hands. She will tell her apprentice one day that this one sings before it sickens. She will be right. That is how knowledge is supposed to arrive here.

Two crawler companies whose routes, shearing apart for twenty years, keep their following storms merged for one extra week in a generation when the bands on their flanks have grown too closely married to their own blood. More children choose the other side of the hull. More routes are remembered in more feet. Nobody is gathered. Nobody is dispersed. The weather simply does not separate quite yet.

And it watches — this is perhaps the whole of it — for the one kind of sorrow that is not woven into the life of the place. Not predation. Not exile, not a vent taken on the wrong side, not blindness, not death laid warm into fresh tailings to bloom. Those are the country's own grammar, and a wise power does not correct grammar it has only just learned to hear.

It watches for the day the system itself begins to fail in a way no apprenticeship can answer. For the crawlers, very old now, to sicken all together of some deep common flaw their makers built into every hull. For the geothermal seams to shift as geology, indifferent and patient, eventually shifts everything. For the holds to grow so heavy with a purpose no one empties that the great dark factories begin, one by one, to stop moving — and with them the pasture, the drill-hole pools, the lee rain, the routes remembered in feet that will find no warm scar to follow.

That day has not come. If it comes, intervention will not look like rescue. It will look, if it is done rightly, like slowness: one crawler recovering on a flank no one expected to warm again; a new seam opening where an old one closes, close enough for the herds to find by foot before the mats thin; a generation given time — which is the only gift this place has ever really asked of anything — to learn a new route before the old one goes cold under them.

Mostly, though, the power remembers.

Because the one true fragility here is not the eyes or the lungs or the low, broad chests. It is that everything worth knowing is stored in callused soles and palms on hulls and itineraries sung body to body across months of walking, and a route can die with a band in a single bad week on cold ground. So somewhere outside the plateau's weather, if such an outside is available to it, the power keeps what the people keep: three companies west until the standing stones hum, then the red iron country, then the pool that no longer exists and the place where the Old One turned. Not written. Not taken. Held, the way you hold something for a neighbour who has not asked you to, against a loss they have not yet had.

No one on the plateau would claim anything had changed, and they would be right in every way that matters. At what passes for midnight the herds still move. The mats open their black fronds. The fish rise. The inside-kin lay their hands to warm iron and feel a hillside being taken, somewhere ahead, mouthful by patient mouthful. A child in a sling taps two fingers against her father's ribs and he stops, instantly, without asking why, because she has felt through his body something he has not felt yet through the ground.

The power stops too.

That is the beginning of benevolence here, and for a very long time it is also the end of it: to be, at last, one more fellow passenger in the warmth — unnoticed, unthanked, and careful not to mistake either of those things for failure.
