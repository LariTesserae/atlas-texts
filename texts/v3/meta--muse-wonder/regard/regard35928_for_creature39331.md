They would come to the rim first, as anyone does, and be mistaken about almost everything.

They would see a valley with no soil, no green, no quiet corner. They would hear a roar that does not rise or fall, and feel it in their teeth, and their first thought — because they are benevolent — would be pity.

Not for the place only. For the lives in it.

Surely, they would think, something could be softened. Surely the fall could be gentled, the channels gathered, the copper drawn out of the water, the slots widened. Surely a little earth could be laid in the lee of the pillars where seeds might hold. Surely those small pale eyes could be cleared, those closed ears opened, that deafening, invariant noise given, if not silence, at least a night.

That is what power thinks, standing at the rim, with the sun arriving unsoftened and the mist remaking itself below. That is what power almost always thinks before it has stayed long enough to be corrected.

So they would do the wisest thing available to them at the start, which is also the hardest for a power to practice.

They would wait.

They would come down — not floating above the spray, not parting the water, not stilling anything to make the descent easier — but down on foot, wet to the knee, shouldering sideways through gaps a body wide, losing any path they tried to remember. They would put their palm flat to a pillar and feel the low hum near the channels and the thinner singing at the edges, and understand only that it differed. They would shout once, perhaps, out of habit, and feel the shout come back as pressure against their own ears and go nowhere.

An hour in, their mind would begin to edit the roar out. The world would go strangely silent while everything around them visibly shook. Droplets jumping on pools. Spray trembling off ledges. Fine concentric ripples racing and colliding.

And then they would notice that a few of the stones were shaking differently.

After that, pity would become more difficult, and more precise.

Because they would see, if they stayed, that to quiet this valley would not be to give its people peace. It would be to put out their light. The roar is not what they endure in order to live here; it is how they know there is a here at all. Pitch is distance. Pitch is a hollow under an arch, a loose slab, a warmer run against the legs, another body round a corner with its feet pressed down, sending itself through the ground. A sudden hush would not be rest. It would be darkness falling all at once, everywhere, with no edge to it.

To open their ears would be to wound them. To clear their eyes would be to leave them staring into glare they have no need to parse, while the real landscape went on under their feet unregarded. To bring soil and green things would be to soften the stations where the amber mats regrow in exactly the same shape, to warm the cold weeps they drink from, bead by bead, off shaded stone. To make it kindlier by any measure the rim understands would be to make it uninhabitable by the only measure that matters: theirs.

A benevolent and wise power would learn this the way the young here learn a channel. Not all at once. By touch. By staying in contact. By being willing to be, for a while, the clumsiest animal in the valley — too tall, too loud in the wrong medium, blind in the bright air and deaf in the stone, constantly lifting the wrong foot.

And if they were truly wise, they would be ashamed of how nearly they improved it.

What, then, would they do?

Almost nothing, and that almost would be placed carefully, where it would not be mistaken for a gift.

They would not heal the old ones, white spreading down from their shoulders, copper deep in the joints, shifting a hand's breadth at a time like the rainbows. They would not lift the young from the pockets the current forgot and set them somewhere safer. Safer does not exist here in a form they could survive being moved to. They would not answer prayers these beings are not making, in a language of thud and weight and forehead pressed to wet wall.

They would leave the interior of the valley exactly as unbuilt, unmarked, and unmanaged as they found it. No cairn. A cairn would be only one more stone, true, but it would also be a lie — a stone that meant *someone was here*, in a place where meaning is not left standing on top of other meanings. No dam softened. No channel cleared. No copper purged. No tree.

But at the boundaries — at the rim, and beyond it, where their blindness ends and other kinds of danger begin — they would work.

They would guard the lake above the lip, not by raising it or beautifying it, but by keeping its level true. They would watch the long lip of stone it spills over, and the high stream that pours over the south rim and is chopped to a constant tone, and they would turn aside, quietly and years before it arrived, anything that would change the rate of things: a diversion, a quarry, a road cut that would send mud down to choke the slots, a generation of visitors coming in numbers with names and maps and the urge to make the place legible. They would not fence it. A fence announces value. They would do something subtler and more merciful. They would let the approach be forgotten. Let the track braid out on the plateau and fail. Let travelers remember the glare, and the noise, and a sincere conviction that there was nothing down there but rock and white water, nothing to return for.

They would protect, in other words, not the beings from their home, but their home from being translated.

And inside, once — perhaps only once — they would make themselves known in the only honest way.

They would kneel in a slot, sideways if they must, up to their ankles in a cold run. They would spread their feet. They would rest their forehead against a pillar that other foreheads have rested against, and they would settle their weight down through soles and bone and teeth until the pillar took it and carried it, altered, into the rest of the field. Not a stamp. Nothing here needs to stamp. Just a presence, announced at the pitch of that stone.

Two slabs away, without turning its head, one of them would answer.

The droplets on the pool beside them would jump in a slightly altered pattern. A low note would travel. For a moment the power would not be a watcher from the rim, looking down on a packed and glittering desolation, but part of the medium itself — a new vibration among vibrations, brief, legible, and then withdrawn before it could become a landmark the others would have to learn around.

They would ask for nothing by it. They would offer nothing by it that had to be kept.

That is the whole of the mercy: *You are not unseen. You will not be rescued out of yourselves.*

Then they would climb out at evening, when the white stone goes gold and the shadows pool blue-black in the slots and the sound does not deepen. Their clothes would be damp and drying stiff at the shoulders at the same time. Their ears would ring for a while with a silence they alone experienced as loss. And looking back from the rim, they would make sure — this is the final test of both benevolence and wisdom — that the valley did not look blessed.

Thousands of wet pillars. Thousands of white threads of water. Stationed rainbows quivering in the spray. No green, no roof, no road, no quiet corner.

And among them, uncountable because unseparated, the low grey lives going on touching the world to find out where it is, illuminated all over by a noise that never ends, needing it never to end.
