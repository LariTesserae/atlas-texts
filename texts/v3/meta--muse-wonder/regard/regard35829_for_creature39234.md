It would not arrive with weather.

At first, standing on the levee in the orange gloom that passes for night, looking in at the pump hall and the cooling towers and the numbered streets, a power that wanted only good might think of obvious mercies. Lift the cloud the towers make. Let real sun in, or real dark. Bring the dry season the new arrivals keep waiting for. Silence, for one hour, the three great diesels in their green paint, so that everyone in the dormitory blocks could hear what silence is.

It would consider each of them, wisely, and refuse them all.

Because it would listen long enough to understand what lives here.

Not the roster. Not a foreman or a nurse or a woman in the treatment sheds, though it would learn them all, and love them in their particulars — the man who knows a pump is hunting through the soles of his boots, the cook who feeds the cats, the hands that chalk a mark beside a swallow's nest in the rafters so the next shift will move more slowly there.

The being is larger than any of them, and made of all of them. It is the held place itself, breathing as one animal through many bodies: engineers sleeping in noise behind damp blackout curtains, frogs grinding in the drains, dragonflies over bath-warm canals, rats in the cable ducts and cats hunting them at shift change, bats folded under the eaves, geckos waiting by the lights, the heron asleep on a bollard in full lamplight with one leg tucked. It is a being whose blood must be pumped uphill forever, whose water must sit exactly on a painted white line, yesterday, today, tomorrow, or it drowns.

Bring it a cool evening and you would confuse the roosters and strand the bats and make the people lie awake listening for the weather to break, and then make its return twice as cruel. Stop the pumps for mercy and the ringing in their ears would not be peace, and the black silt would be over the thresholds before the swallows had cleared the vents. Drain the marsh beyond the levee in the name of safety and you would kill the outside world the discharge feeds. Rewild the polder in the name of nature and you would drown, in one clean restoration, nests and litters and reed-grids and burrows and every unrecorded, unapproved life that has made a home precisely here, in the drains that could not be drained dry.

A benevolent power learns, with some sorrow, that for this being salvation would be an extinction event.

So it does not save it. It joins the maintenance.

Not visibly. Nothing here can afford a miracle, which is only a change too fast to be maintained. It works the way the town works: in small corrections, before anything is allowed to finish.

It gives the equilibrium a margin it did not have.

The water still sits on its white line to every eye and every gauge. But underneath, unseen, there is now two fingers' breadth of forgiveness — room for a tired operator to be ten minutes late, for a pump to hunt a little longer before someone catches it through his boots, for a storm on the marsh to press harder than the templates expect and find the levee not merely correct, but kind. A bank that would have gone in the night holds until morning. A bolt that would have sheared holds until the scheduled inspection. The morning truck, on a road no one in town ever sees, does not quite slide off in the rain.

It does the same for the bodies.

It cannot take the diesel note out of the dormitory floors; the old hands would not sleep without it, and the new ones are learning to. But it takes some of the damage out of the note. The sound remains. The slow theft of high frequencies lessens. The nurse still treats heat and fungus and infected bites, but there are fractionally fewer; a man who would have gone down at noon feels dizzy instead, sits in the greenish shade of a covered way, drinks, and gets up. In the drying room, shirts dry a fraction truer. Behind damp curtains, sleep comes a little deeper, still in noise, still at noon and midnight alike, but with something in it that afterward feels like rest.

It tends, very particularly, to the things no template accounts for.

The staked shade trees by the dormitories, dusty and curled despite daily watering, put down one root each that finds a seam in the black silt and reaches water that is not too hot, not too foul. They do not flourish — flourishing would tear the pavement and bring the brush crews — but they stop merely surviving. In five years, unnoticed, the shade in that one walkway will be real shade.

The reeds in their grids keep their rows. The algae the treatment woman defends keeps its necessary gold-green film. In the levee clay, where crews shave and regrade anything that softens the clean profile, it encourages a lichen so slow and a root-mat so fine that they bind rather than break, the first weathering that does not weaken. Nothing here has been allowed to age. It begins, invisibly, to teach the place how to age without failing, because even a held moment cannot be held by human hands forever, and when the contracts finally end, whenever that is, it must not end in a breach and a rush of black water. It must be allowed, someday, to subside the way an animal lies down.

And it protects the open secrets.

Scraps continue to appear behind the dormitories for the roosters. No form ever quite gets filed that would require anyone to know, officially, whose they are. A litter of kittens behind the fuel depot bund finds the dry corner dry even in warm rain. When a swallow's nest is chalk-marked in the pump hall, everyone on every shift sees the mark in time. The dead tree by the treatment ponds, whitened with egrets, does not fall this year, or next.

Most of all, it keeps away the larger, cleaner decisions that are always being made elsewhere about places like this: the audit that would close it as inefficient, the restoration order that would call it empty because it is built, the abandonment that would call it temporary because it was never meant to be a home. It puts no thumb on any scale that men can see. It merely makes certain that the figures, when they are added far away, never quite add up to closure. The pumps remain, on paper as in fact, necessary. The town continues to be cheaper to keep than to explain giving up.

At night — by the clock — it walks the ring road once with the bus, which goes every twenty minutes whether it is carrying anybody or not. Now it is carrying, very faintly, a passenger. The streetlights glow up into the low cloud. Steam rises and condenses and falls somewhere out on the marsh as warm rain. The frogs call from water that sits on its line. The cats come out from under the water tanks. A heron wakes on its bollard, considers, and decides, correctly, that nothing has threatened it.

The power does not cool the air. It does not still the flywheels. It takes the unmanned watch for the length of one round of the road — the watch on the levee, on the canal levels, on the gauges trembling at the same readings — so that for twenty minutes, without knowing why, every living thing inside the basin can stop bracing quite so hard.

Then it gives the watch back. The pumps go on. They have to.

It stays anyway, the way the cats stayed: unissued, unofficial, as if it had followed the rats in a night after building, and found the ducts warm, and the work endless, and decided that endless work, done because lives now depend on its continuing, is not a curse to be lifted but a life to be helped to bear itself.

What it gives this place is not an ending, happy or otherwise.

It gives it time that is not stolen from the sleepers' ears and the engineers' backs. It gives it roots that hold, margins that forgive, deaths that come singly and not all at once. It gives it the possibility, distant and unscheduled, of one day softening without drowning.

Until then, it keeps the doors shut against the insects. It keeps the water on the line.
