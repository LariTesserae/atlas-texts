It would come the way the service crews come: from above.

First, from the clean wind on the rim, it would look down at the black, featureless seal of the pall, and it would think what any kind power thinks when it sees darkness and heat and no faces looking back:

*This is a wound. Let me open it.*

It would imagine its mercies in the order the merciful usually imagine them.

Rain, first. A true rain, not the thin lowland moisture that dies in the cloud before it can fall, but water in abundance, washing the soot from the canopy, filling the ash beds, finding the roots.

Then light through the opened pall.

Then cooling. Then green, in the ordinary green sense. Then birds, seeded carefully from somewhere kinder, and insects to feed them, and clear lanes where children — there must, surely, be children somewhere — could walk without masks.

It would carry all of that down with it in intention, the way you carry tools into a place you have already decided is broken.

Then it would descend.

Noon would close over it. Fifty degrees, sixty. The air would scald. By touch and infrared and the phosphor points fading in the bark it would find a lane cut that morning, already softening at the edges, and there it would make its first mistake:

It would stand still too long, thinking.

The root-hairs would come out of the living wall, pale and patient, and test it.

Not attack it. Not worship it. Not recognize it, either, which is harder for a power to bear. They would test the place where a foot would be, and the hem of whatever it wore for shape, and its warmth — the brief fever of it moving through the body — and begin, industriously, mindlessly, without any malice whatsoever, to consider how it might best be grown through and made load-bearing.

And if it were only benevolent, it might panic, or be offended, or pull free with a flare that blistered the shade-boughs and brought a whole lane down in curling.

If it were only wise, it might withdraw at once and call that respect.

Being both, it would do the harder thing.

It would stay very still, lower its warmth until it was no more remarkable than a furnace wall, and let one fine root touch it. It would listen at the speed listening is possible there — not in words, because there are no words, but in pressure through wood, in chemical gradients sent lane to lane, in a valve opening somewhere a fraction wider nine days before a tower was ready to fall. It would stay through one whole life of a cooling spire: seeded in ash, fed on exhaust until it rose twenty meters in nine days, black-finned, flowering in resinous cones almost ready to burn, holding its fifty days as a flue, and then letting go exactly where it had grown to fall.

It would watch the pruning carts pass and understand that they were hands, and that the hands knew they were hands.

It would look for the suffering it had come so prepared to relieve, and it would find — not an absence of death, because there is death everywhere there, fast and constant and total — but no place where death was a wrong being done to the thing that died. The tower is not murdered by the shredder pit. The grove is not orphaned by the harvest. The cathedral-tree, five stories of living wood excreting its own firebrick over centuries, is not waiting for a forest to return and rescue it from being a building. Cell, organ, district, machine: these were visitors' words, and they were already composting.

It would look for loneliness and find that loneliness had no vacancy to occupy. The body fills the dish edge to edge. It has spent a thousand years making its outside into inside. To arrive, even in love, is not to answer a call. It is to be a warm irregularity in a dark field, to be routed around — or, if persistent, trellised.

That is the moment the rain would die unmade in its hands.

Because rain there would not be mercy. It would be steam and rot and resin turning foul in the pipes, seedlings drowning in ash beds tuned over millennia for almost no water at all. Light would not be mercy; naked light blisters. Birds would not be mercy; the pollen would kill them before their wings dried. Cooling would not be mercy to a being that turned its leaves away from the sky on purpose and learned to drink infrared from the floor. To give it the sun would be to give a deep-sea thing the blessing of the beach.

It would understand, finally, that it had mistaken *uninhabitable to everything it loved* for *unloved*, and *final* for *fallen*.

So it would ask — in heat, in pressure, in the slow grammar of stressed grain — the only honest question left:

*What is lacking?*

And the caldera would answer the way it answers everything: a furnace breathing a little deeper, a lane narrowing by evening, a pit already sprouting in correct proportion to a tower not yet fallen. Total mass unchanged. Total heat unchanged. Nothing reaching up through the pall. Nothing, in any register it possesses, reaching at all.

A lesser kindness would have found that answer unbearable and improved it anyway.

This one would believe it.

---

What it does, it does almost entirely elsewhere.

It does nothing to the pall. It does not thin it by one shining thread.

It does not leave a garden in a clearing, or a shrine, or a single green shoot in a protected crèche to prove that gentler things were possible — because that shoot would not be a gift, it would be an argument, planted in a body that cannot refuse arguments except by incorporating them.

It does not wake it. Waking was the visitor's hope, not the inhabitant's need. A body in which a forest of short, furious lives can flare, serve, and be taken back does not become more sacred for being forced to notice you.

Instead, it climbs back up the lift into the obscene clean daylight and turns its attention outward, to everything that is not the caldera but might one day decide to have an opinion about it.

There, it works.

It makes the dry approach road a little easier to forget. Not impassable — the crews who drive the phosphor markers in, tap tap, and ride the lift out again have their necessary work, and the system has its register and its gates — but unmemorable in the records of those who would come with other registers: those who would look at fruit grown around copper and silicon and see only a mine that had conveniently planted itself; those who would see the black canopy and send for the cleansing fire; those, kindest and therefore most dangerous, who would arrive with rain.

No roads are destroyed. No minds are emptied. Small things go slightly astray, the way maps printed on bark-paper are meant to. A survey loses its funding. A extraction patent expires in a drawer. A documentary crew, three valleys away, films salt flats instead and is content. The caldera becomes, to the world beyond the rim, a place that is known of and not known about: hot, dark, registered, unprofitable to disturb and faintly embarrassing to describe, and so — for century after unglamorous century — left alone.

That is the visible part, and it is not much, and it is meant not to be.

Then, deep under the dish, where even the root pavement has never reached, it attends to the one thing the body cannot prune or replant for itself.

The volcano has forgotten how to be a mountain, but forgetting is not the same as death. Far below the flattened caldera, the old heat is thinning on a timescale too slow for groves to think in — nine-day towers and century cathedrals alike are mayflies against it. Left alone, in some age no map will survive to name, the fissures would cool a fraction, then a fraction more. The black leaves turned floorward would find less and less to drink. The balance would hold, and hold, and hold, beautifully, while the fire it was balanced on went out.

The power does not reawaken the mountain. No eruption. No new rim. No gift the body would have to become something else to receive. It only persuades the deep stone, gently and over a very long beginning, to go on being exactly as warm as it already is — for another ten thousand years, and then, if the persuasion has been learned by the rock itself, ten thousand more. The turbines will notice nothing. The condensers will ration nothing differently. Tomorrow's seedlings will push out of the ash at the same visible speed.

It is a mercy sized so precisely to the life receiving it that it is indistinguishable from nothing happening. That is how it knows it is the right size.

And one last thing — because benevolence without an exit is only a more elegant cage, and even a thing that has never wanted to leave should not live in a world where leaving has been made metaphysically impossible:

At the cold rim, outside the pall, in a crack of clean stone where the freezing wind passes and no root has ever had reason to go, it leaves a single dormant seed of the body's own stock. Not planted. Not watered. Not blessed in any way that would wake it. Just *there*, sealed in darkness of the ordinary kind, viable, patient, and utterly unconsulted.

If the body never sends a root that way, the seed will sleep out its long life and become stone, and nothing will have been lost or argued.

If one day — after the heat thins despite everything, after the plateau remembers some older instruction, after a decision takes a whole forest a hundred years to grow into — a root-hair comes testing along that rim crack, industrious and mindless and patient, it will find that the world beyond the caldera is not, after all, a wall.

Only a door that was never pushed, left by someone who understood that the holiest thing you can do for a life complete without you is to refuse to complete it.

Then the power leaves by the service road, taking its mercies with it, unused.

Below, the cloud lid seals shut. Featureless. Noon darkness, ticking leaves, resin hissing faintly onto hot stone, a tower somewhere letting go exactly where it grew to fall, a pit already sprouting in the dark in correct proportion.

Nothing has been saved.

Nothing needed to be.

It goes on making and remaking its single body out of heat and waste — and now, although it will never know it and would not thank anyone if it could, it has time enough to go on doing so, and somewhere to go if going ever becomes a thought it can grow.
