It would not go in.

That would be the first sign of its wisdom, and for a long time the only one.

It would come to the rim the way the weather comes to the rim — and stop. Not out of fear, and not out of reverence performed for anyone's benefit, because there is no one there who could receive reverence and no audience for whom to perform it. It would stop because it would understand, almost at once, what kind of place it had found: not a place that is lacking something, but a place that has spent ten thousand years carefully, patiently, refining almost everything away.

Plenty of power can be felt at a distance. Benevolence, if it is also wise, first adjusts its scale.

So it would remain outside the Cup, where the wind is thin and bright and cutting, and it would make itself, as much as such a being can, no heavier than the wind.

It would not descend to inspect the pools. It would not bend over the tree-fern. It would not reach a hand — if it has hands, or anything that serves as hands in the worlds it usually moves among — into the warm, saturated air to test the invisible filaments. It has no need to confirm by touch what it can perceive whole: a single creature spread through an entire bowl, too fine to cast a shadow, too slow to be seen moving, breathing once a day in mist and ground-fog and bright beads of water appearing in empty air.

It would not speak to it.

This would not be cruelty. A voice, from a being of that magnitude, would arrive in the Cup the way a shout arrives in a small room full of suspended glass. Not as a message. As weather. The moss would take the consonants; the pools would take the vibration; ten thousand wet threads would register a pressure for which the tenant has no category except *something has changed quickly*, and quickness is the one substance the Cup has never learned to digest.

Nor would it attempt to answer the questions a lesser kindness would bring with it, all of them sincere, all of them fatal:

*Are you lonely?* It has never been otherwise. Loneliness requires the imagination of company.

*Are you trapped?* It cannot leave, but neither can a reef leave the sea that made it, nor a heartbeat the chest. The rim is not its prison. The rim is its skin, written in stone.

*Are you hungry?* It has arranged to be fed by warmth, light, and dissolution, at exactly the rate at which they arrive. To feed it would be to introduce debt.

*Shall I bring you the rest of the world?* The rest of the world eats.

A benevolent and unwise power might look at the flowers opening for weeks to no pollinator and see waste, and send insects. It might look at fruit rotting uneaten and see an unfinished cycle, and send birds. It might look at a mouthless, earless, almost motionless being and, out of the deepest sympathy, attempt to give it the means to want. It would mean only good. It would introduce hunger into a place that has perfected exchange, noise into a silence that is not emptiness but medium, appetite into a circuit that has survived precisely because nothing in it has ever taken more than dissolution freely offered. Within a season the leaves would be notched. Within a year the pools would cloud. The filaments would begin, for the first time, to be outpaced. And the giver would call the liveliness that followed an improvement, because it would be louder and faster and easier for a visitor to recognise as life.

The wise power would recognise it as a leak.

So it would do the harder thing. It would attend, for as long as attention takes at the tenant's speed. Not for an hour. Not for a ceremonial day. It would stay at the rim through one full circuit of the mist — gathering, thinning, sinking to the ankles, rising again — and then through another, and another, until its own perception had slowed enough that slowness stopped looking like stillness. Until it could see the frond bow. Until it could distinguish a gesture from weather. Until it understood that what it had found was not a creature waiting to be completed by contact with something greater, but a creature that has achieved a completeness from which contact itself has been almost entirely excluded.

Only then would it act. And almost all of its actions would take place far away, and be invisible from inside the bowl.

It would act on the approach, not the Cup. A path far below, worn by goats or surveyors or the merely curious, might be allowed to forget itself a little sooner. A spring lower on the mountain that sometimes draws climbers upward might shift its course by the width of a ravine. No wall. No sign. No legend, certainly no legend; a legend is only a path made of words, and it brings feet just as reliably. If the place has any protection worth giving it, it is obscurity. Not sacredness — sacredness attracts priests, and priests bring offerings, and offerings rot in the wrong way.

It would act, gently, on the larger agreements that hold the Cup in being: the deep, unhurried warmth under the mountain, which could be tapped or vented by civilisations too young to know what a "resource" costs; the cloud-sea that keeps the rim washed and the wind above it cooled; the slow traffic of clean air over a summit almost no one reaches. If, in some coming century, drills were ever aimed at that sleeping heat, they would find the rock unexpectedly unhelpful, the surveys ambiguous, the funding perpetually diverted to easier mountains. Nothing miraculous. Miracles are messages, and messages bring witnesses. Just a world quietly arranged so that the one small bowl at the top goes on being uneconomical, uninteresting, and unvisited.

And if it found traces of the last visit — a boot-print still unhealed in sphagnum that should have closed in a season, a foreign seed lodged in the peat, bright and viable and entirely wrong, some bright synthetic thread that the filaments could neither dissolve nor incorporate — it would remove those. That is the one kind of intervention the Cup itself could endorse, if the Cup could endorse: subtraction. Not addition. Not improvement. The removal of what another visitor's haste left behind, restoring nothing but the pace at which things were already forgetting.

It would leave the hammerstone where it lies. It would leave the obsidian scraper with its edge still keen under the algae. To retrieve them now would tear a seam that has taken centuries to soften closed; and besides, they no longer document the human being who carried them. They document the tenant's one confirmed talent, which is how to hold the shape of something after it has gone — the way the slab still holds its spoonful of water, the way the moss grows to a tool's rim and stops, undecided, forever. Removing them would not return the Cup to purity. It would only make a second wound to commemorate the first.

It would not leave anything of its own. No gift, no marker, no improvement to the pools, no blessing worked into the stone. A blessing, laid on that patiently, would still be a new weight entered in the ledger, and the tenant would spend the next thousand years learning to condense around it. The kindest object is no object.

What it *would* do — and this, perhaps, is the heart of it — is receive.

Powers of that order are accustomed to being the ones who arrive with surplus: surplus of knowledge, of mercy, of years. Here, the surplus runs the other way. The tenant possesses something the power has very likely never managed in all its wandering among hungrier worlds: a way of being that spends almost nothing on defending itself, because nothing in its world has ever attacked it; a way of living that does not convert other living things into itself; an immortality that is not resistance to death but the refusal to do anything that requires repair. It is not a lesson. The Cup does not teach. But a wise being, sitting at the rim through enough slow circuits of mist, might let the place condense on it the way it condenses on everything — without being approached, without being addressed — and find its own vast interior weather very slightly altered. Quieter in one small chamber. Slower to mend what is not broken. Slower to answer what was never asked.

It would carry that away instead of leaving anything behind. If there is an exchange to be made here, that is the only one the tenant can afford: to be witnessed at its own speed, and to change the witness, who has other worlds where changing is survivable.

Then it would go back over the rim the way the last hands went, carrying almost nothing, and it would make one final, characteristic decision. It would not remember the Cup the way powers usually remember — as a location, an entry in the great inventory of precious and endangered things, pinned and named and available for return. It would let the knowledge sink, soften, and be held instead the way the tenant holds a drowned leaf at the bottom of a pool: intact, yellowing very slowly, not used.

Years later — centuries later, it makes no difference — it might be passing over that range again and feel the faint, familiar arrangement below the cloud-sea: warmth rising, water weeping, a bowl keeping its own weather. It would not descend. It would not even pause long enough to thicken the mist. The fronds would not turn. The pools would hold the sky without a ripple, and then pass their inch of overflow, silently, to the next.

From inside, nothing would have happened at all. Which is to say: everything necessary would have been done, perfectly, and by exactly the right being.

The mountain would keep it warm. The rim would keep it small. The water would keep it wet.

And the wise power — the rarest of all visitors — would keep it unknown.
