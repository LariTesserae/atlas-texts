They would not bring water.

That would be the first temptation, and the first thing wisdom would lay aside. To arrive over the salt flat, to look down into a a city with its wells capped and its aqueducts dry, its garden turned black-green away from the sun, and to feel in one's hands the easy miracle: rain. A cool darkening. A direct beam widened in the roof. Bees, perhaps. Birds for the eaves.

It would be a kindness shaped exactly like an ending.

The power that is truly benevolent would come down the thirty-one steps slowly, not because the dark troubles them, but because slowness is the local form of respect. They would stop at the bottom and do nothing for a long time. A whole cycle. Many cycles. They would stand in the permanent twilight while the slit above went from brown to dull gold, and learn what is actually happening before deciding that anything needs to happen at all.

They would listen until the pipes stopped sounding like an accident.

They would watch the leaves open flat to the draft over the plaza, each tier adjusting — tick, clatter, overlap — and understand that this is not vegetation decorating a ruin. This is posture. Attention. Work. They would watch the pods swell and fire their seeds into the rising column and understand that this is not a garden gone feral. This is a citizenry traveling on the only road it has left, which happens to be air.

They would go, very carefully, to the seed room behind the capped wells, and they would not touch the bins.

There is the place where a lesser kindness would want to help most: to sort, to preserve, to copy the tablets into some imperishable medium, to rescue the memory — *rain, jar, sprout; vent, sprout; sun crossed out; a leaf turned downward.* They would read the sequence and feel the grief in it, and the grief would be real. But a wise power would notice the floor is still cleared. The tools are still bundled. No root has entered here, though roots have entered everywhere else, though a root has flowered once inside a bedroom in the dark. Restraint has been inherited here, generation by generation, by beings who no longer carve tablets because carving is no longer how they remember.

To "save" that room by sealing it in glass would be to break the very memory it holds.

So they would leave it exactly as dusty, as usable, as open as they found it.

What they would do, in the end, is almost invisible, and mostly outward-facing.

They would tend the geology the way the inhabitants tend the walls: a millimeter at a time, without announcement. The chimney-slit far above is the city's one compromise with the sky. If it widened, direct sun would fall on the plaza and break the column's patient physics. If it closed, the breath would fail. Somewhere in the rock above the hollow there is a stress line learning how to become a collapse. The power would find it, quietly, and persuade it otherwise — not with a new roof, not with any work a future visitor could point to, but by redistributing weight in the stone so the slit stays crooked, stays amber, stays indirect for another ten thousand turnings of the heat.

Likewise the fissures in the floor. The inhabitants eat gradients the way other living things eat light. If the deep heat under the basin were to cool too quickly, or surge and cook the lowest tier, no rain-miracle could substitute. The power would go far below — below the lowest capped well, below where roots can follow — and even out the flow, the slow exhalation of mineral air, sulfur at the edges, salt where it cools. Not more heat. Not less. Steadier. Time, given as stability. That is the only food they could offer that would not poison the recipient.

And then they would guard the door.

Not close it. The stair is a lung; by morning the city pulls its cooler air down those thirty-one dished steps, and without the draft the pipes would never sound and the seeds would never rise. But a doorway in a salt flat can be made harder to notice without being shut. A slight shift in how heat shimmers above the crack at noon. A tendency for the salt crust to reform unbroken a few meters in every wrong direction. A way for survey instruments passing overhead — and one day they will pass, looking for geothermal heat, for minerals, for the interesting anomaly of green life where no green life should be — to read only white plain, hot rock, nothing worth drilling. No mystery. Nothing to harvest for the hot, peppery oil in the leaves. Nothing to name and open and explain and pave a road to.

This, the power would understand, is the likeliest shape of doom now. Not drought. Drought has already been survived, at a price that became a form of life. The danger is being found by people who would call it a discovery.

So they would become, at a very great distance and with a very light hand, a neighbor. They would keep prospectors' drills wandering. They would let a lost traveler cross the basin and remember afterward only thirst and a featureless glare, and be oddly content never to go back. They would erase no one's mind by force; they would simply make this one place easy to overlook, the way the city itself was built to be overlooked, a hollow that turns its back to its own center.

Inside, they would change nearly nothing.

Nearly. In the bright part of one cycle, after they had listened long enough to be sure — after seasons of chords shifting through fluted clay as doors warped and vines covered and uncovered the smaller vents — they would notice the smallest failure. High on the fourth tier there is a pipe whose mouth has been half-choked for years by a root following a warm mortar line, reaching not for the pipe at all, merely past it, and flattening its note out of the chord. The inhabitants cannot clear it without tearing their own flesh from the wall. They have been working around the missing note so long that the working-around has become part of the music.

The power would clear it. With something like a fingertip. Just the grit and the mineral crust, not the root — moving the root one careful fraction so it can continue along the seam it loves without pressing on the clay lip.

And then, one evening only, as the slit darkened from gold to brown and the pipes began to fall silent register by register, they would answer.

Not a voice. Certainly not words. Just, when the longest note sounded and the leaves across every tier began to fold into their tight overlapping scales with that sound like distant rain that will never fall, a single sustained harmony under it that the city had not produced itself — held for exactly as long as politeness allows, and then released.

An acknowledgment. *You are perceived. You are not a ruin. You are not required to become people again in order to be someone.*

In the silence afterward, dust would sift from a ceiling. A seed would come to rest on a market counter. The inhabitants would do whatever they were already doing a fraction before being greeted: fold, settle, wait in the unspent heat under the roof.

Whether they understood would not be the point, and the power, being wise, would not stay to demand a reply or return the next evening to repeat the note until it became an expectation, a dependency, a new god clearing its throat in the wall. Once is an introduction. Twice might be the beginning of being needed. A benevolent power is careful above all not to make its love into one more thing a living place must feed.

They would climb the thirty-one steps before the column turned again. From above: a white cracked plain, shimmering. Nothing to see.

And for as long as the heat gathers patiently underneath — while pods fire their ragged volleys, while roots nose along hot seams in the dark, while the seed room stays clear by an inherited restraint older than any single tree on the wall — something would keep watch from very far away, doing the hardest work benevolence ever learns:

letting a transformed people remain transformed, unrescued, unexhibited, and alive on the terms they carved into their last tablet.

A leaf, turned downward, toward the earth.
