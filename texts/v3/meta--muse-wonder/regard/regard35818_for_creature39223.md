It would learn, first, to keep still.

That would not come naturally. A power that is benevolent feels the pull of every small mouth. Arriving at the rim — if *arriving* is the word, if it did not simply become aware of the crack the way one becomes aware of a heartbeat in another room — it would hear what a human hears, made louder: the swifts multiplied by the throat of the rock, the finch chicks gaping at echoes that are not their parents, the bats shifting in their seams. And lower, under the noise, the papery traffic of the spiders picking through the moth-fall.

Its first impulse, being kind, would be to help.

Light for the darkness. Warmth for the bats, who are too high and too cold and should not be there. Rescue for the moths stippling the black pool — ten thousand innocents a day, lifted out of the valleys whether they will it or not. Sun for three blades of grass growing pale as maggots in rotted guano at the bottom, rooted in a place that cannot feed them properly.

It would do none of it.

Not because it is distant. Because it stayed still long enough — an hour, a season, a hundred years, what does time mean at that scale except *long enough for the shrieking to resettle* — to understand what it was looking at.

Feed the moths, and the bats starve. Save the moths, and the spiders' webs go empty, and the beetles with their reduced eyes feel their way through a larder that stops renewing itself. Light the shaft, and the bats leave, and the algae film that wants twenty minutes a year is scorched off the wall, and the swifts, who read the walls by echo in the gloom, lose the gloom that is the shape of their home. Warm the crack, and the old ice on the floor melts and takes its perfectly preserved dead with it, and the pool rises and drowns the springtail rafts, and the cold that keeps everything slow and storied speeds up and rots too fast.

The crack is not a broken version of a meadow. It is not life failing to be elsewhere. It is life that has learned, seam by exact seam, generation by generation, to be *here* — assembled entirely out of what the sky threw away, and dependent, with a terrible and complete logic, on the throwing-away continuing.

A wise power does not heal a body by removing its heart because the heart is loud.

So it would do the harder thing. It would protect the conditions, and leave the inhabitants alone.

It would guard the crack not at the crack — the crack can look after itself; frost and altitude have been doing that work since before the crack existed — but at all the distant, unglamorous places the crack secretly depends on. Down in the valleys, where no one thinks a summit fissure has any business mattering, it would keep the meadows unmowed at the wrong week, so the moths rise in their ten-thousands. It would keep the lowland air full of craneflies and aphids and grasshoppers for the swifts to strain out of the updraft. It would keep sheep grazing somewhere far below, shedding wool that the wind can carry up, a few bright threads at a time, to be glued with saliva into seams.

It would keep the place *unnoticed*. This is, perhaps, its chief work, and the least visible. On the one map that almost gets it right, a contour line would blur. The climber who might have come with drills and good intentions and a plaque would find, instead, a tenderness for a different summit that year, and not know why. The iron pin hammered into the rim — that one it might ease out, the way you would remove a splinter from a sleeping animal, so gently that the rock closes over the scar in a decade and no swift alters its flight path by a centimetre. The scratch of the crampon it would leave. A scratch is honest. Everything here has left scratches.

It would not touch the three blades of grass. Not give them light. Not make them a miracle. They are not a message, and making them one would be a kind of theft. It would only note — with something that, in a power of that size, might be the equivalent of holding its breath — that for the first time something in the crack is not blown in to die. Something is rooted. If the grass spreads, it spreads at grass speed, on warmth and rot, in the dark. If it fails, it fails. It will have been real either way.

And in winter, when the crack breathes out at night and the ice in the deepest fractures prises off another bright flake of schist — the mountain still being made, still opening its one dark room a millimetre wider — the power would not hold the walls together. A crack that cannot change cannot keep anything. The swifts' grandchildren will find new seams. That is what seams are for.

So in the end it would do almost nothing, and the almost is everything: a valley kept alive, a map kept vague, a pin removed, a long attention paid. No mark. No cairn — the frost heaves cairns apart in a season anyway, and it would know better than to build one.

It would leave the way everything that belongs there learns to leave and return: without sudden movement.

One drop of condensation, perhaps, added to the thousands that the warm small bodies already breathe onto the walls. One drop, swelling over minutes on an overhang, falling at last into the black pool, where a moth is struggling and a springtail raft is on its way and a bat is already turning in the dark above the water.

Not rescued. Not abandoned.

*Kept* — in the only sense the crack has ever asked for: kept possible, kept unlit, kept loud, kept exactly as accidental and as necessary as it is.
