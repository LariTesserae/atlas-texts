If it is truly benevolent, and truly wise, it will not come in glory.

It will come up from underneath it, the way everything else does.

It will ride the green-black cable for an hour through the cloud-sea, in a counterweighted cage meant for ore and water-skins, and it will learn the first lesson before it even sees the cliff: that there are places you cannot arrive at by descending in power, only by being hauled, slowly, in balance, at the mercy of a brake-wheel and a lift-hand testing grease on the inside of his wrist.

And when it breaks out on top, it will gasp.

That will be important. The power will be tempted — all powers are — to fix its own gasping by fixing the sky. To thicken the air just a fraction. To lay a kind hand over the whole continent-sized table and raise the pressure until breath comes easy, until water can stand in open pans, until fire burns tall and honest, until mothers do not die in sealed rooms doing the one piece of work that cannot be slowed down.

It will not do it.

Because it will have stood close enough to talk, first. Forehead nearly to forehead. Hand resting lightly on a forearm. Feeling the words through coat and skin.

And it will understand, the way you only understand things at arm's length or less, that to thicken this air would be to drown these people in kindness.

Their chests are not a wound. Their blood is not a wound. Their slow placing step, heel, weight, breath, their factories that take three days to strike one blow, their hundred touches for *wait, sealed, I am listening* — that is not a civilization failing to cope with a hostile place. That is a civilization. Formed, the way metal forms in their vacuum bells: because of the emptiness, not in spite of it. Make the air rich and you unmake the still-watcher's hearing, the herder's season-long patience, the way a whole town shares one room in the dark for the economy of breath. You make them lowlanders with misshapen ribs, weeping in air too thick for them, asking someone to please stand nearer to a world that no longer needs anyone near.

A wise power does not rescue people from the shape of their home. Especially when they are at home.

So it will move slowly. It will seal what is precious. It will waste no breath on distance.

It will do small things.

It will go, first, not to the dome, not to the old cold works where the floors are warm in winter — it will note that place, and circle it the way the gliders do, and not land there yet — but to the birthing chambers, in the deepest rooms of the towns. Not to abolish the danger. It cannot do that without abolishing everything else. But a seal is a seal. A valve is a valve. And knowledge, if it is given rightly, is not a miracle dropped from above; it is a hinge that was once part of a lift cage, remade by local hands.

So it will sit for a long shift beside a still-watcher, ear to nothing important, and say, very quietly, chest to chest, one sentence about the way a cold plate might be seated so it does not sing quite so thin at the edge. A sentence that will take ten years to travel from town to town along the ankle-high speaking tubes, and be argued over, and tested, and improved by women whose names it will never insist on knowing. And in twenty years the birthing bellows will hold a fraction longer at the crucial hour. Not always. Not enough to make a legend. Enough that a few more of those small, furious, startlingly red babies live to be carried a year against a working lung and learn to pace their breathing to another person's.

That is the level at which it will allow itself to love them: in fractions, maintainable in flawed cast metal, repairable by their grandchildren.

It will do the same for the lifts. Not make them fast. Not make them safe, as lowlanders count safety. Only see to it, in the blind places under the cliff lip where no hand checks, that a cable beginning to fray in a way no grease-test would catch is — quietly, over a season of nights — less frayed. That the cloud-sea below, which feeds the swifts that feed on its insects, is not poisoned by some new lowland cleverness drifting up. It will guard the underneath, which nobody on the Table can see at all, so that the commute of the birds continues, two kilometers down and two kilometers up on the thermal peeling off the sunlit rock.

And it will guard them, gently and without ever showing its hand, from below.

Because the real danger to the High Table was never the thin air. It has had a million years of wind to settle that account. The danger is that one day the lowlands will look up and decide, all at once and with great enthusiasm, that a free vacuum nine kilometers high is a strategic resource, that solar furnaces that burn three months should burn for wars, that people who know how to waste nothing would make excellent, obedient workers in an industry suddenly in a hurry. The power will simply make the plateau uninteresting to hurrying men. Surveys will come back modest. Ore figures will never quite add up to an invasion. A minister will turn a page and fund a valley instead. No thunderclap. A compass in the central desert describes a lazy circle, and must be forgiven. So do ledgers, sometimes, in the right hands.

As for the old city at the dome —

It will go there at last, alone, inland past where the towns stop, through dust held a meter off the ground by its own charge, its hair, if it has hair, standing out to follow it like something curious. It will stand among mirrors cracked into a mosaic still flashing at sunset, one panel after another, in a code too slow to read, and it will listen to its own blood too clearly, and see stars at noon.

And it will recognize that some part of this place is not asking for help, and never was. That the warmth coming up through cold floors might be geology, or an old machine still patiently cycling, or simply the Table remembering being built — and that the herds are right to avoid it, and the towns are right to let an adult walk toward it with his seals checked twice, and a hand kept on his sleeve a moment longer than necessary, by way of blessing.

It will not wake it. It will not seal it off with warning and thunder, which would only teach people to pick at the seal. It will do what Table people do with precious, dangerous things: check that it is closed. Check it because you checked it. And leave it to last, as everything up here lasts, a very, very long time.

Then the power will go down again, which is the hardest part for powers, and therefore the proof.

It will leave no temple. The church in Orum already has a condenser bell for a dome, and would only use a miracle for a cistern. It will leave no law, no name, no rain. Rain would be a catastrophe of generosity here; it would hiss off hot stone, drown the bacterial crusts in fresh water they never learned to eat, and rust a thousand inherited hinges at once.

What it will leave, if it leaves anything visible at all, will look exactly like care that was already there: a tool returned to its hook. A seal a little sounder than its maker remembers making it. A feathered scavenger-dog fed, without discussion, from its own plate at dusk, because it breathes this air and cleans these streets and can be trusted, over decades, with what does not decay.

And for as long as its attention lasts — and its attention lasts the way stone gives back heat, slowly, through the whole night — it will keep that attention at the right distance.

Which up here, it has learned, is not far.

It will stand close.
