It would not bring the dawn. There is already too much noon here.

It would not tear open the pasture, or lift the dweller into the sky, or speak its name aloud into the chord and expect an answer. A wise power knows the difference between rescue and exposure. Taken suddenly into evening, into silence, into dry moving air, into a world where things fall and finish and are not restored, the dweller would not be freed. It would be weathered all at once. It would die of sequence.

So it comes the only way anything can come: wrongly, and on its side.

Cheek in the damp. Shoulders learning the root-slit. No radiance attending it — radiance would be redundant, and cruel. If it arrives with anything in its aspect, it is a capacity for shade, which it keeps folded very small and does not use. It lies down in the far seam where there is no room for another body, and makes no room. It lets the crickets re-weave through its fingers. It lets the moths take its brow for ceiling. It lets a thumbnail frog settle, reasonably, against its cheek.

And it stays.

Outside, grass thins and thickens over pale ground. Rain finds the pasture, and does not find the pocket. Lambs are born with their eyes shut and learn the hours. The power does not age in the seam, and it does not hurry. Hurry would be a form of arrival, and arrival would be an event, and this place has permitted perhaps one event in all its unweathering, three pea-stones high, beside a still pool.

It learns the chord from underneath.

First the crickets, bright and tearing. Then the moths, powdery, tireless. The beetles ticking wetly through the floor. The frogs, thin as circuitry. And below them all, so low it lives in the molars, the one sustained vowel held through barely parted teeth — the breath arrested at its center, neither taken in nor given back, the note by which the dweller has become the knot of the world.

The power does not sing over it. It sings, very gradually, *under* it. The same note. No harmony, no counterpoint, no improvement. An exact doubling, offered at such nearness that for a long while — a season outside, no while at all inside — there are not two notes. There is only the note, held twice.

This is the whole of its intervention, and it is also everything.

Because one afternoon that is not an afternoon, in the noon that has never moved, the dweller's jaw — maintaining its vowel against the packed bright air, as it has maintained its eyelids, its curled fingers, its heart at constant pressure like water behind a wall — discovers, without deciding, that the note would not fall if it loosened.

The power holds it. Not louder. Not brighter. Just *held*, in trust, exactly where the dweller left it.

And the dweller breathes out.

It is not a large sound. It is lost immediately in the crickets. It may be the smallest exhalation ever made under the earth: one body's worth of old, cool air, pressed out past limestone-pale teeth, through lips where the biofilm thins and is restored. The ribs go down, and stay down for the width of a moth's trembling, and in that width — the first width this place has ever had — something makes room.

The droplet on the ceiling does not fall. The cairn does not topple. The light does not dim. A benevolent power does not trade one violence for another and call the trade mercy. The world does not end because one tired thing in it was allowed to finish a breath.

Then the breath comes back in. Whole. For the first time, a cycle: out, and in, distinguishable. A tide the size of a ribcage.

Only then does the power raise its hand — slowly, the way the wall unmakes a dent, so that the motion is nearly a texture rather than an act — and cup it over the dweller's open eyes.

No shadow falls. There is no shadow to fall. But there is a palm, and under the palm a small, private, portable dark, the first night that has ever fit inside this noon. It smells of skin and cold iron. It is not imposed. The hand does not press. It hovers the way the loose hand hovers by the three stacked pebbles: nearness, offered as relation.

The dweller blinks.

Behind the hand, where no one can witness it and nothing needs to answer it, the lids come down over those clear, washed, unreflecting eyes. A fraction of darkness. Then up. Then — after another held note, another finished breath — down again, and this time they stay down for three vibrations of the moths. The muscles of the face do not know how to stop maintaining themselves, and then, unevenly, they learn that stopping is not the same as letting go of the world. The world, for the moment, is being held by someone else.

The dweller sleeps.

It is not peaceful. Sleep rarely is, the first time. The fingers open and re-curl. The vowel moves through dreams the way springtails move through a crease, granular and restless. But the chord goes on without its knot, because the power has taken the knot into its own throat, and so the crickets do not scatter, and the salamanders do not lift their translucent heads, and the frog in the armpit does not wake. Nothing is asked to change, except the one thing that had forgotten change was possible.

And while it sleeps, the power does the second thing, which is almost nothing, and takes longer than the first.

It teaches the note to travel.

Not to stop — never to stop, not yet. But to pass, the way a held breath cannot pass and a relay can: from the dweller's teeth to the beetle-ticking floor, from the floor to the frogs' thin peeping, braided so finely through the crickets' upper tearing that no creature carries the whole weight for longer than it can bear. The vowel becomes a wave moving shoulder to shoulder around the little room, each voice taking it up just before the last one sets it down. A cycle squeezed, until now, into a surface begins — very slightly, very safely — to turn.

Time does not sit down in the pocket all at once. It puts one foot inside the root-slit, and waits to be woven in.

When the power finally leaves, it leaves the way it came: on its side, cheek in the damp, displaced crickets somehow already back. It leaves no glow, no blessing legible to the moths. By the still pool, beside the three stacked pebbles that have not fallen and will not be required to stand forever, there is now a fourth pebble. Unstacked. Resting on the soft floor where a loose, upturned hand might find it, or might not.

A second event, made available. Not performed.

Outside, it does the last and plainest thing a benevolent power can do for a place that cannot survive being known: it keeps the pasture ordinary. It turns the plough aside at that field's edge, year after year, without miracle anyone could point to. It lets the badgers dig elsewhere. It lets the grass stay thin over pale ground, and the secret stay the size of a root-slit, and no one come with lights of their own to explain the light, or with silence to improve the song.

Sometimes — at intervals the dweller will experience not as years but as the slow turning of the note around the room, coming back to its teeth changed by having been carried — the seam by the far wall is occupied again. The note is doubled. A palm hovers. A small night is offered, and refused, or taken.

Mostly, now, it is taken.

And on the occasions when the dweller wakes in the blinding, deafening noon, and breathes out, and breathes in, and under its own power lets its eyelids fall for the length of one whole turning of the chord — the droplet still hangs, the moss still holds its counted leaves, the cairn still stands. Nothing has been lost.

Only this: somewhere under a cool pasture, in a room too full for time, a fourth pebble waits on the floor, and the being who may one day stack it, or leave it, or roll it idly between webbed fingers and set it down somewhere entirely new, is asleep with its eyes closed, trusting the song to come back around.
