If it is truly benevolent, and truly wise, it will stop at the rim.

It will come up past the valleys that look soft from above, past the bare unweathered slope, to the sharp teeth of rock around the hollow, and it will kneel there in the wind that scrapes, and it will look down into the blue sideways light at the town breathing — and it will not descend.

Not yet. Not for a long time.

Because the first thing wisdom learns here is that this is not a suffering to be relieved. It is an agreement.

A lesser power would see the cold first. It would see children un sealing their doorways with stiff fingers every morning, old women freezing joint by joint each night, people eating raw and fermented food with teeth worn from biting back the streets, and it would want to give them fire. Warmth. Metal tools. Glass to let in light without letting in wind. Medicine from below. Easier lives.

And in giving, it would kill them.

Fire needs fuel the bowl does not spare, and smoke in that trapped air, and dryness in a world that lives by never drying. Metal would make pruning too fast, too final — cuts where only discouragement is survivable. Warmth would wake the plants in the night hours and make them burst when the frost came down again. Light would blind the bruise-colored flowers and the people alike. A road down to the valleys would bring valley air: spores, seeds, insects, viruses, curiosity — ten thousand living arguments the town would spend a century losing.

So the wise power keeps its hands, whatever hands it has, folded.

It watches through one frost cycle. Then a hundred. It learns the timing the way the inhabitants learn it in their bodies: the long shallow breath, the longer pause, the warm hour when water moves instead of locking, the creak in a trunk when the sap releases. It learns not to mistake stillness for death, nor silence for absence. It presses, very lightly, something like an ear to something like a trunk at the edge, where the last grafted arch stands — not to be heard, but to listen.

And it understands, at last, that there is not a town and its people here in the way there is below. There is one being, rim to rim, that has divided itself into streets and market and marsh and sleeping terraces, and some of its parts walk upright on two legs and bite back shoots at ankle height, and some of its parts grow roots through broken stone and hold the walking parts from freezing. To help one half against the other would be surgery on a single body by someone who has only just arrived.

So it does what can be done from outside an agreement without breaking it.

It guards the rim.

Where a rock tooth, still fresh from the ice, is fracturing in a way that would one winter send half a cliff down into the sleeping terraces, it encourages — so slowly no stone ever knows it has been persuaded — the crack to close the other way. Where a slab will fall anyway, it turns the fall a fraction, so that it comes down on bare scree outside the moss curtain and becomes, in time, another windbreak. It does not stop the scree slipping. A few stones at a time is the mountain's own pruning. It only refuses catastrophe.

It guards the water.

Not by making more. More would flood the wet quarter and rot the town from the bottom up. Not by making less. Less would lock the aqueduct reeds solid past the reach of morning. It keeps the melt steady in the way the water-keepers keep the exit crack clear — a thumb's width here, a delayed thaw there, snow held a day longer on a shaded face so that the spring does not spend itself all in the first bright week of the year. The cisterns go on beading. The slow green pulse goes on moving through the hollow stems. No one below the canopy notices anything to praise.

It guards the approaches.

This is its most active mercy, and even this is mostly a turning-away. A climber from the valleys who finds, by accident or stubbornness, the one line of holds that leads toward the hollow will find the holds less interesting than he remembered. A mist will come down at the inconvenient hour, not thick, not dramatic — just enough to make him think of his own hearth. A surveyor's map will omit, by a small human error, the notch where the eastern ridge dips. A storyteller who once heard from a grandfather who once heard of a green place at the top of the mountain will, telling it, put it on a different mountain, in a warmer country, with giants in it, and gold. The town passes into rumor, and rumor is a better wall than rock.

And it guards against gifts. Its own, most of all.

Once in many generations, perhaps, it allows itself one touch inside the bowl — and even then it does as the inhabitants do. At breath-distance. By contact.

In the worst night of a bad winter, when the frost takes the canopy earlier than any living memory stored in a graft-line, and stays, and the compost pits cool, and a heartbeat that went down at dusk shows no sign of beginning its slow climb again — then, unseen, a warmth no greater than a cupped hand is held against the outside of a domed cell on the highest terrace. Not heat. Only the suggestion that thaw is still a thing that happens. One reed in the aqueduct keeps a thread of water unfrozen through the joint. One seam in one doorway does not quite seal.

In the morning the family inside will talk their vine open as they always do, hands clumsy with cold, and they will credit the plant for holding, and each other for sleeping close, and they will be right.

That is the measure of it. Whatever it does must leave the credit where the living is done.

Years pass the way they pass in the bowl: not counted, but accumulated in damage and repair. Hand-stains deepen. A bridge-root thickens underfoot. A chair-shaped swelling in a thick house receives an old woman for her last winter and then, slowly, forgets her shape. Children born into rooms coaxed open in anticipation grow into the rooms and coax them wider. The phosphorescent fungus glows in the wall niches. At night the whole organism crackles faintly, a hundred small joints settling, water turning and holding and not bursting.

And outside, on the rim where the wind has nothing to stop it, something patient has become almost indistinguishable from the other grey teeth of rock. Moss has not colonized it. It does not permit itself even that much belonging. But in the brief warm hour its shadow falls, sometimes, just outside the eastern ridge — giving the bowl perhaps a minute less of thin light in the morning, and a minute more of shelter from the worst gust at dusk. The inhabitants, squinting up from the root-streets, take it for a stone that was always there.

If they ever turned their faces toward it all at once — the whole being, green and walking, looking up out of its hollow at the power that has watched it for a century without asking to be thanked — the wise thing would not speak. It would not explain. It would lower its head a little, bent at the hip and knee, as the trees grow bent, as the people walk bent, in agreement with the place.

And then it would get out of what light there is.

So the town can go on tending itself awake.
