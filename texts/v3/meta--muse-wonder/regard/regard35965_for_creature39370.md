Nothing answers it — not at first.

If a benevolent and wise power came to that basin, it would not announce itself the way powers in stories do. No voice over the salt pans. No rain out of a clear sky. Above all, no green.

That would be the temptation, to an eye that loved life in the usual forms: to soften the glare, cool the air, put trees along the canals, grass in the empty hundred meters between the houses, soil where only crust and scalding water wait under a mineral skin. To make the place kind in a language other places speak.

It would not do it.

Because the wisdom would come first, before the benevolence had time to make a mistake — it would walk the boardwalks in single file, keeping to the tested center line, and learn what the children learn before reading. Which blue is only beautiful. Which blue takes skin. Never step off, however solid it looks. Touch metal with the back of the hand. It would understand, the way the oldest families understand, that this is not a broken version of somewhere else. It is a complete place, with its own food chain that does not begin in leaves: mats of orange and emerald and chalk-white in water hot enough to cook other life, brine flies hanging soundless over them, red shrimp in the cooler ponds, thousands of pink-tinged waders lifting at dusk in one slow sheet. Put trees here and you do not add life. You declare the life that already learned how to be here insufficient.

It would not make the ground permanent, either. It would not still the wandering boreholes for the power station, or fix the pools in their streets, or keep the bathing steps from being sealed, centimeter by centimeter, into white stone. The people have built something rarer than permanence out of impermanence: tenure by maintenance. Re-lime the walls. Move the path. Cut new steps where the water has decided, this decade, to be warm rather than scalding. A power that froze the basin in place would not be saving their home. It would be ending the long conversation they have learned, grandmother to child, to have with it.

And it would not rescue them from it. That is the hardest restraint, and the truest one. They are not a people waiting to be taken to a better climate. They are deep-chested, slow at rest, born to thin air and thick heat that should not coexist, another shore species among the lizards and beetles and waders — long-legged on stilts and boardwalks, gathering at the tolerable hours, speaking low because the wet air carries a voice strangely far. To carry them away, or to carry the heat away, would be the same misunderstanding in different directions.

So what would it do?

Small things, at the scale of the rules. Maintenance, which in this town is almost a creed: boots upside down on pegs against the beetles, bowls right side up against the drips.

It would hold a crust for half a second longer under the foot of a child testing an edge — not forever, not enough to teach him the crust can be trusted, just long enough for his weight to shift back to the board the way he has been taught. It would be the difference between a pump note going grainy at 2 a.m. and a pump note going grainy in a way the night shift hears in time. It would let one borehole, wandering underground like a root, wander toward the power station's pad rather than away from it, once. It would keep a brake cool on an electric truck coming down the switchbacks in air too thin for burning, with imported fruit going soft in the back and a driver who has made that descent four hundred times and will, this time too, make it four hundred and one.

It would keep a kind pool kind a little longer. Not forever — the grandmothers would not believe forever, and would not thank anyone for it — but through one more summer of evenings, so that people can sit to the waist where the water is mineral rather than alkaline while the mats downstream give off their smell of iron and warm bread. And when that pool does migrate, as it must, it would see that a new one opens within walking distance of the same street, at a temperature a body can bear, so that the story a grandmother tells remains a story about movement and not about loss.

Beyond the basin, where its real size would matter, it would be equally quiet and considerably less gentle. The town lives by exporting what the poison makes possible — power, lithium, borax, sulfur, clean water manufactured out of heat — and the outside world has a talent for arriving at such places and loving them to death with extraction and tourism and concern. The power would stand, invisibly, in the switchback road. It would make the basin easy to overlook on maps drawn by hungry people. It would let reports make the place sound merely difficult, merely remote, merely hot — which is to say, it would let the truth protect it. The road would stay passable for food trucks and impassable, in every way that counts, to appetite.

It would not add one degree and it would not permit the one degree that would push the whole negotiation over the edge. That may be its largest work, and the least visible: holding the basin exactly at the livable edge it already occupies, while the world beyond the rim warms. Not cool. Not comfortable. Livable, on the terms the mats and the beetles queuing in their patient rotation under the cold pipes have already established.

In the end it would leave the way anyone sensible leaves that town: at evening, when the basin exhales. Steam rising in columns from a hundred vents, lights coming on in separated clusters like small constellations across the darkening salt, sweat ticking onto the planks, condensation ticking into the gutters. It would shake out its boots in the morning. It would drink the dripped water, which tastes faintly of iron and of nothing, and be grateful for it in the local way — without remark.

And long after, no one there would tell a story about being saved. At most, a grandmother would pause on a boardwalk where the planking has darkened, tap the rail twice with the back of a knuckle, and say that this pool has stayed longer than pools usually stay.

Then she would go on, as if that were the most natural story in the world to inherit.

It is.
