It would come in low, through the gate that was made for stooping.

Not descending. Not arriving in glory over the terraces, where the feather-palms crackle in chimney pots and the flowers turn, at dusk, to face where the sun was at noon three days ago. A power that came that way would cast a shadow, and in a place sixty or eighty paces across, where the pots are moved a hand's breadth sunward each week and the seedlings have already come up on Friday for the seeds to be planted on Tuesday, a shadow in the wrong place would be a kind of violence. Things would begin, politely and without surprise, to give it a past it could rely on. It would find it had been expected. It would find a sleeping mat already woven, in part, from hair that had not yet come off its head.

So if it is wise, it stoops.

And if it is benevolent, it does not announce what it is.

At first, it would be tempted — all benevolence is tempted, at first, by the obvious repairs.

It would see the central well, beautifully bricked, the windlass in perfect repair, the bucket coming up over the course of an afternoon full of small, black, warm seeds, and think: *water*. It would see the birds on their perches opening their beaks in a visible, trembling effort, and think: *song*. It would walk a tiled lane for twenty minutes past the same blue pot, each time a little more overgrown, and emerge at the gate facing inward, and think: *a way out*. It would meet the eldest keeper by the well — sharp-faced, fresh-edged as someone recently carved, hands mapped all over with a century of pale mendings — winding the rope that lengthens as she winds it, and think, with a great and almost irresistible tenderness: *rest. Death, properly. An ending that is allowed to end.*

It would have to learn, slowly, at the pace of a crack unforming in clay, what each of those gifts would cost here.

Water would not save this place. Water would unmake it. The soil is crushed brick, ash, powdered lime, shredded reed. The keepers' skin is matte and unglazed, warm with stored heat, cool on the shaded side for an hour after they leave the shade. Nothing here bleeds; a cut puffs out a fine perfumed dust, or beads a clear oil that hardens in the light, and over slow weeks the place fills level again, a shade lighter. Rain on these roofs — roofs of woven reed laid over curved poles, each roof planted — would not fall the way rain falls in stories. It would soften. It would find the mortar joints the tendrils have found. It would turn the manufactured earth back into the clay it was manufactured from, bed by wall-pocket by hanging pot, and the keepers, who take the warmth wonderfully, would stand in the good heat of the terraces with the dust patterning deeper along their knuckles, tile-joint fine, and begin — not drowning, which would at least be an event — to slump. To lose their edges. Which here are the only proof that a thing is finished being made, and may begin to be itself.

Song would be no kinder. The small brown birds are palms that finished. The palms will be birds again. To give the birds a note would be to give them an interior the garden has not built a place for, a thing made only to leave the body and not return as grit, as fiber, as felt-moss combed and re-laid like carpet. Sound already does not travel properly here; it arrives later, inside your teeth. A true note, cast across the bowl, might arrive in a child who had not been born yet in a bed where a woman was only beginning to stand in the afternoons, her shadow getting better at standing there without her. Who knows what would hatch, retroactively, to have produced it.

And a way out — a gate that stayed where it was put, that opened onto an outside you could look back from — would be the cruelest repair of all, offered too early. The keepers are not maintaining the garden. They are not trapped in it, either, in the way a visitor means trapped, with his own dust beginning, by then, to pattern along his knuckles. They are the garden, in the form it takes when it needs hands. To cut a door in that, and call it mercy, would be to decide on their behalf that the person-direction of a life is the real one, and the wall-direction — the stretch of planted wall that holds the evening heat a little better than the wall beside it, the keeper-shaped freshness in the carvings — is a failure to escape. They do not experience it as failure. An old keeper whose name has shortened, task by task, to only *Where-He-Stands*, who is brought his seeds where he stands, is not bracing for rescue. He is proceeding.

So the power, if it is wise, puts its temptations down the way you put down a tool that is too heavy for the material. Carefully. Where they will not crack a tile.

Then it stays.

It learns the chronology that holds here, which is the chronology of tasks. It does not ask *north of the well*. It asks, and is answered: *after the re-potting. Before the vines are cut. Two tasks past the woman carding.* It moves pots a hand's breadth sunward, following chalk marks made generations ago, although the plants in some of the pots have already flowered in the position the pot will occupy next month, and it does not find this absurd, or not more absurd than any other faithfulness. It combs the emerald felt that is not moss and is never damp. It holds bark-strip — not in its teeth; it has manners, and perhaps no teeth of the kind the place could use — while a man's hands are busy on a trellis. It eats when it is offered food: the small black seeds, roasted until they split; the ash-white tuber paste that tastes of smoke and pepper and, faintly, of the chimney it grew in. It eats slowly, from a bowl still slightly warm from having made itself around last year's meal, and it does not ask for water, and no one finds its abstinence remarkable.

It lets the place work on it, because benevolence that will not be touched is only inspection wearing a kind face.

The dust begins to settle on its skin along the creases. It does not wash it off. There is no water to wash it with, and besides — this is the harder lesson — washing it off would be a statement, repeated daily, that it intended to remain unmarked. In the afternoons it takes to standing in a particular place, as everyone does eventually. Keepers begin, very courteously, to bring it its seeds where it stands. A broom it leaves leaning in a doorway at dusk is found at dawn a few inches into its work, the dust arranged in a small, perfect arc, and it picks the broom up and continues from where it had got to, mid-gesture, as you would take over a sentence from someone who had paused for breath. It does not startle. Startling is a claim that the world has broken an agreement. This world has kept its agreements with great consistency. They are just not agreements anybody signed.

It will be given a name. Not asked. Given, the way tasks are given, by use. First something short enough to say in one breath — *After-Re-Potting* might attach to it if it is often found in the lee of that work, or *Who-Holds-The-Bark*. It will wear the name lightly and in the weather-discussing tone. It will notice when the name begins to lengthen. That is the only clock it permits itself to watch.

And it will talk, when it talks, close, as the keepers do, because sound does not travel properly here and some things should not have to cross the air to arrive. In conversation, standing nearer than visitors are used to, breath smelling of heated straw, it will ask the one question that is not a repair and not a direction and not an order of work.

Not: *how long have you lived here.*
Not: *do you want to leave.*
Not: *what were you, before you were a wall, a bird, a flower the color of wet paint.*

It will ask: *what do you like that is not needed?*

It will have to ask it many times, in many forms, because the question has to manufacture its own past in the garden before anyone can be found already answering it. A child at the pot-moving age will consider, with the slow absolute seriousness children bring to errands everywhere, and say she likes the tick a tile makes as it expands, when it comes a moment later inside her teeth. The man who reties trellises will say, after a season, that he likes holding the bark in his teeth — not the trellis. The holding. The woman carding grey fiber beside the scarlet lilies will say she likes the part after the fiber comes off and before it is spun, when it is only soft. These are small answers. The power receives them the way the well receives its bucket: slowly, over the course of an afternoon, as if they were the thing the darkness had been making all along.

When it finally sits by the dry well with the eldest — *When-The-Rope-Is-Wound-And-The-Beds-Are-Combed-And-It-Is-Almost-But-Not-Yet-Dusk*, she who everyone agrees is eldest, she who may be eldest because someone must sit by the well and wind — it does not offer to take the rope from her. It has watched long enough to know the rope runs, if you follow it patiently around corners that take twenty minutes to turn, through every house and around every bed and once, loosely, around every place where someone stands. To take the rope would not free her hands. It would only decide, from outside, that her hands were tired in a direction she has declined to be oriented by.

It asks her the question too.

She winds. The rope lengthens. Her face is sharp as last spring and her hands are darkened underneath, like the carvings, by a century of passing.

What she likes, she says at last, in the tone of a person discussing weather, is that the well was dug before the garden was built around it, and the garden was built before the well was dug. Both statements are kept, because each does different work. She likes that there are two. Not one of them. The having of two.

The power understands, then — not with its reasoning, which has been a good servant and a poor master here, but lower down, where it stooped to come in — that this place is not suffering from a lack of truth. It keeps two statements the way it keeps everything: edge to edge, without a gap wide enough for dirt. What it has never been given, because nothing inside it can manufacture it, is a statement kept *elsewhere*.

So it does, in the end, three things. They take almost no time, which means they take a very long time, which means the keepers approve of them without ever quite discussing them.

First, it finishes nothing. On its last afternoon — although *last* is a visitor's word, and the gate will be a little further along the path than it was, planted over behind it, patiently expecting it to arrive — it takes up one piece of work and does it badly, in a way that cannot be unformed. A basket. Baskets are being made everywhere here and finished nowhere in particular; this one it finishes, deliberately, with its own uneven weave, one strip of bark too short, tied off with a knot that has no task. It does not correct it over slow weeks. It prevents its hands, and the place's gentle habit, from mending it level and a shade lighter. It is the ugliest basket in the hamlet. A child uses it to carry brick-dust. It holds.

Second, it leaves a gap. Not a door. Not a wound in the wall, not a tile pried up to show a cleaner tile beneath. At the base of the gate — the real gate, or the one that will have been the gate — it sets, in the open, where anyone may move it and no one is asked not to, a single fired pot. Inside the pot is dirt. Not manufactured soil: not crushed brick and ash and powdered lime spooned in baskets into built beds. Dirt, from wherever the power keeps its elsewhere. Damp when it arrives, though it will not stay damp; full of things that rot and stay rotted, of seeds that have not already been seedlings anywhere, of a darkness that smells not of hot stone but of its own slow going-down. It does not plant anything in it. It does not explain it. A gap wide enough for dirt turns out not to be a hole in the world. It is an object, the size of two cupped hands, that a keeper can pick up, or pass, or stand beside in the afternoons. Some of them will. Some of them, eventually, at the pace of everything here, may put something of their own into it that they do not want to have come back as a bird in the morning. The garden, which manufactures its own past so faithfully, has never once been offered a place to put a thing down and have it be *over*, ungrown, unreturned, unkept. The power does not insist that this is better. It only makes it *available*, the way the well makes seeds available: brought up slowly, none of which anyone remembers putting there.

Third — and this is the whole of it, really; the basket is a courtesy and the dirt is a courtesy, but this is the act — it remembers them outside.

When it leaves (stooping; the lintel of charred wood bound with rope; ordinary daylight; no outside to look back at, until, impossibly, there is), it refuses the garden's last and most courteous gift, which is to have been arriving all along. It does not let its visit soften backward into the chalk marks. It carries one tally out with it and cuts it where rain can weather it, in wood that will smooth with the years, and it keeps their names there as *sounds* rather than tasks. Little *After-Re-Potting*, spoken whole, in the rain, to no one who needs her to move a pot. *When-The-Rope-Is-Wound-And-The-Beds-Are-Combed-And-It-Is-Almost-But-Not-Yet-Dusk*, spoken once a year entire, taking the whole breath it takes, in a place where dusk comes whether or not the beds are combed. It speaks of the woman carding fiber, the man with bark in his teeth, the eldest winding at the dry well, as people who were somewhere once and may — this is the unmanufacturable thing — never be found again. It lets them become, in at least one telling, past. Not preserved. Not proceeded into the holding-up part of the garden. Past, the way a visitor means it, with loss in it.

It keeps two statements, because each does different work: that they go on, and that they are gone.

Inside, nothing changes, which is to say everything proceeds. The pots are moved a hand's breadth sunward. The flowers turn to face the sun of three days ago, beautiful and mildly unbearable. A lizard left undisturbed on a wall becomes, across a summer, an arrangement of glazed scales among the leaves. The children plant on Tuesdays, faithfully and without surprise. The eldest winds the rope that lengthens as she winds it, and if the rope seems, some afternoons, fractionally lighter — as though one loose loop, somewhere around a place where a stranger once stood, had been quietly taken up by hands outside the bowl — she does not startle, and she does not comment. She has a new thing to discuss in the tone of weather: that there is a keeper, and that there is no keeper, and both statements are kept.

And the power, wherever benevolent and wise powers go when they have finished not rescuing anyone, keeps its elsewhere the way the garden keeps its well: in good repair, lowered slowly, brought up full of what it did not put there.

Some years it finds, in its own dust, a pattern along the knuckles. Tile-joint fine.

It does not wash it off.
