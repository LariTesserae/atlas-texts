It would arrive the way everyone arrives: from outside, too dry.

And the first wise thing it would do is stop at the root-wall and put on the moss-slippers.

Not disguise itself. Not descend in glory over the plaza at noon and interrupt the market. A power that is truly benevolent, and has been paying attention for longer than this basin has been flat, would understand immediately what kind of place Ambel Low is. It is not a place that needs to be lifted up. It is a place that needs to be *negotiated with*.

So it would get wet.

Its cuffs would darken. Its hair would cling. It would mispronounce its first door-apology and have to stand there, embarrassed and ancient and luminous around the edges, while a child of six corrected its breath. It would learn to ask directions out loud. It would be told, cheerfully and incorrectly, three different routes to the north plaza, and it would take all of them in spirit. It would not cut across.

For a long time — and it can afford a long time, which is the one thing it and the basin have in common — it would do nothing that looked like power at all.

It would take a turn staying awake at night.

That is the vacancy in Ambel, if there is one. Not hunger. Not sickness, really, the mat administrates most sickness with a thumb and an opinion. Not death; the dead here are not lost, they are composted into fondness with an efficiency that would make theologians outside weep with envy. The vacancy is simpler and smaller: somebody always has to be awake to hear a new root testing a canal. Somebody always has to listen for the canal humming in the wrong key. Three hundred people can do it, and do, in staggered chambers while the towers close their windows one by one. But it is still a labor. It is still a tax the joyful turbulence pays to vigilance.

The power would quietly pay some of it for them.

Not all. It would not be so rude as to make them unnecessary to their own city. But on the deepest nights, when the sap-light ticks green-gold along the vascular lines and the spore-storms fall as slow glitter, a few more households than usual would find themselves sleeping through until morning, unworried, and not know why. A few more old women would wake rested. Somewhere below hearing, in the dark under the mat, something vast and patient would be listening on their behalf — the way the basin listens, the way a held note holds up a choir — and it would let them be brief, and loud, and frivolously busy, because the listening was covered.

Then it would look outward. Because benevolence does have edges, and Ambel's danger was never going to come from inside the root-wall.

It would not come as a dry shoe. It would come as a dry idea: that a seed which remembers its future is a *technology*. That it could be taken. Patented. Planted in rows somewhere sensible and cut on schedule. That water which arrives a second from now is a resource, that a bathhouse that hasn't happened yet is inventory, that forty towers who remember your grandmother are a real-estate problem waiting for a more efficient tense.

Men with sealed plastic cases have thought this before, in other wet places. They will think it again.

The power would not smite them. Smiling is not Ambel's grammar, and neither is smiting; both are too much like cutting.

Instead, it would do something much older and much harder to argue with. It would extend the local rules a little farther than the root-wall.

A hundred kilometers in any direction the land has finished happening. The power would make sure it *stays* finished. No road would quite find its way across the silt. Not cursed — nothing so dramatic — just uninteresting. Survey teams would get wet, get tired, get beautifully distracted by herons, discover their instruments agreed the basin was geologically null, commercially null, a saucer of standing water and reeds with nothing under it worth a mine and nothing over it worth a highway, and they would go home feeling vaguely that they had had a pleasant, unprofitable week. Maps of the approach would expire a little faster than maps should. Investors reading reports at dry desks in dry cities would feel their attention slide off the paragraph, the way attention slides off a stone in a river. It would not be forbidden knowledge. It would just never quite become an ambition.

And any seed carried out in a sealed, dry container, against every law of manners the city lives by, would arrive at its destination as only a seed. Ordinary. Forward-traveling. A little disappointed in itself, chemically speaking. The future it remembered would politely stay home, where futures of that kind are fed and apologized to and kept wet. You cannot steal a relationship. The power would simply arrange for the rest of the world to keep re-learning that, gently, at the level of logistics.

Inside the wall, it would grant no wishes. This would confuse visitors who heard a rumor of it, later. It would not make the towers permanent. It would not make the people live longer in any way they could put in a ledger — they have the growth fields for that sort of arithmetic, and they have made their peace with its softness. It would not stop arguments, or bad cooks, or bridges lying about their smell, or a tower refusing, out of long memory, to grow a decent kitchen for a rude man. Those refusals *are* the morality of the place. To smooth them away would be to dry it out.

It would, once, be asked by a tower for something.

That is how it would know it had been accepted. Not by the people first — by the plants. Late in its stay, after it had learned the chemical register properly and stopped trying to be helpful in the afternoons, one of the forty would lean its shade, just slightly, in a direction that was an invitation, and ask, in the slow vascular way, for the only thing Ambel ever really trades in: a week of shade in a particular direction. A little more cool over a nursery mat. A little less glare while something young decided what shape it remembered being.

And the power, which could have rearranged weather systems, would do exactly that. No more. It would shade a nursery for a week. It would consider it the most important work it had done in several thousand years.

When it left — if *left* is the word for something that may simply have sunk, contentedly, into the listening layer under everything — it would leave nothing behind that could be owned. No monument. Monuments are dry. No doctrine. The people already have manners older than doctrine and kinder. At most: a seed, put back in a merchant's tray without announcement, indistinguishable from the others. A perfectly ordinary Ambel seed. A room that hasn't happened yet.

Except that whoever planted it, in whatever bad year, whatever year of too much argument or too little rain or a tower grieving in the wrong direction, would step out of its growth field an hour later, smelling of sap and new wood, and find that the room they'd asked for had grown one extra thing they hadn't thought to request and badly needed: a window that irised open not onto the plaza, not onto a canal, but onto stillness itself. A small, round, leaf-glass view of the basin as it was before eyes: flat, finished, patient, holding everything up without comment.

Just for a moment. Just long enough to remember that the turbulence has a floor. That nothing has to be preserved against a deadline. That a mistake can be talked back down. That they are allowed — cosmically, structurally allowed — to spend the world's saved-up quiet all at once, joyfully, in public, at noon, at full roar.

Then the window would go back to being a window.

And the market would go on arguing beams into the air overhead, and the frogs would sing the pressure up through the dark, and the herons would refuse the unhappy bridges, and nobody's feet would ever, ever be dry.
