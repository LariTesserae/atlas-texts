It would not arrive.

Arrival, up there, is a weight. And weight is the one sin the continent cannot forgive casually — not because it is wicked, but because it travels. A heavy footstep in one district becomes a sag in a sleeping floor three districts away. A dropped marvel becomes a slantwise danger near the anchor trunks, sliding for an hour to lodge in somebody's grandfather. A god descending in glory, in fire, in stone, in anything at all, would bow the bridges wrong before it ever got the chance to speak.

So a wise and benevolent power, if it noticed them, would first do the hardest thing in its repertoire.

It would get lighter.

Not smaller, exactly. Power rarely consults size. But lighter in intention. Lighter in certainty. It would set down, one by one, outside the cloud-sea, the things such powers are accustomed to carrying: urgency, thunder, answers, the wish to be thanked, the wish even to be known. Especially that. It would learn what every child on the mat learns in a sling before speech: that another person's floor is also their ceiling, their grave, their mother, their road — and that you do not press on it merely to announce yourself.

For a long time it would only listen, and not with ears.

It would attend the way the Repair-walkers attend, through the bones. The heel-press that means *stop*. The dragging toe that means *cat, and not far*. The precisely held stillness in a gourd-house after bad news, more articulate than weeping. Lovers arguing through a floor with their faces peaceful above it. A grandparent two rooms away rocking a fussing baby by shifting weight in a hammock in the agreed rhythm.

It would take it perhaps a hundred years to understand that the silence was not emptiness. That would be the first repair it made, and it would make it in itself.

It would not give them ground.

That would be the temptation, of course. Benevolence looking down — though *down*, near the great trunks, is a local opinion — at people who weigh themselves against bundles of vine, who empty a store-gourd when a child is conceived, who cut a beloved waterlogged wall away and watch it turn, turning, into the white because someone has grown pregnant: benevolence would ache to give them something solid. A foundation. A mercy with geology in it.

A wiser benevolence would notice what the ground would do to the feet.

Those soles, layered and ridged and reading rot through moss half a degree before the eyes can see it. Those toes like a second pair of hands. The whole long civilization of testing empty air before trusting it, and sometimes — for babies, for the very old, for people whom the mat remembers kindly — feeling a young step rush up to meet the weight.

Give them rock and in ten generations the feet go blind. Give them metal and the tools begin to fall in earnest. Give them certainty and the staircases stop forming, because no one is expecting them in quite the same undefended way. The power would understand, finally, that this is not a people who have failed to reach the ground. It is a people who have answered the absence of ground so completely that the answer has become hands, houses, marriages, funerals, weather.

You do not cure that. You do not even call it a wound in their hearing.

What it could do — and a real power, whose compassion has survived its own omnipotence, would do only this — is learn the Repair.

Somewhere the mat is always rotting through. Somewhere a bridge is beginning to sing at the wrong pitch in a district emptied by fever or feud or simple distance, where no young walker with a stone adze and a coil of fiber will come this season, because no one living remembers that anyone lives there. The houses go on growing anyway. The bowls fill with black water. Upward rain passes through rooms that have not held a sleeper in forty years, and seeds drift up from floors nobody planted, looking for the canopy.

There, where its kindness would not become a lesson, the power would work.

By night, in the green dusk, by footfall and patience, using nothing heavier than persuasion. It would cut the dead weave out. It would plant the new. It would braid — slowly at first, clumsily, the way a being who has moved worlds might have to relearn a rope twisted on the thigh. It would get sap under its nails, if it had decided to have nails. It would hang its hammock over emptiness a mile deep and be afraid in the specific, instructive way the place teaches fear: not of falling, exactly, but of falling *inconveniently*, onto someone else.

And when, during the Long Sway, the whole continent set itself moving in that slow oceanic rhythm older than any marriage custom, and an unpeopled causeway somewhere began to take the motion wrong — resonating, shuddering at the joins, preparing the small honest catastrophe of a snapped line and a district quietly amputated from the world — it would not stop the wind. The wind is a season. People time their deaths to it. You don't take that from them.

It would put a hand under the sway. That's all.

Not holding the mat still. Stillness would be a kind of theft too. Just a counter-pressure, the way a parent walking beside a child on a bridge does not carry her, but keeps a palm hovering where the small of her back will look for it if the gust comes. A bridge that should have gone would give instead a deep, surprised creak, hold for two days while a file of browsers crossed it single file, bells stuffed with moss, each foot placed and tested and committed — and arrive, puzzled and unharmed, at a widening where the bowl-water stood unspilled.

No sign. No voice from the canopy. Perhaps, the next spring, that stretch of bridge would come back a little greener than the rest. Its rail a little lower, as if bowing. The Repair-walkers, finding the work when they eventually came that way, would stand on it a while, reading it through their soles, the way you read a face.

New fiber. Their fiber. No knots they hadn't taught the plants, and the plants taught them.

Someone would say — not aloud; aloud is for a few feet of green dusk — a pressure of the heel, a warmth of standing still together. *Someone's grandmother.*

And the power, feeling the thanks arrive through the weave and mistaking it, correctly, for an instruction, would make itself lighter still.

Only once, perhaps, would it allow itself something like conversation.

In a wart-swelling halfway up a tower-tree, an elder would be thickening into architecture, as the old do — consulted increasingly through the warm inner bark, answering questions by shifting in a hammock whose ropes creaked in rhythms the family had learned over decades to read. Dying, most likely, and timing it to the Sway. And the elder, who had spent ninety years listening to a continent through her bones, would become aware of a listening on the other side of the floor. Something vast. Something trying very hard to weigh almost nothing. Something that had recently, in an empty district, learned to braid.

She would not look up. Looking straight at someone is for marriages, festivals, and informing them of a death, and this was, in a courteous sense, all three.

She would ask her question downward, through the sole of one foot.

The power — who could have answered about the cloud-sea, about what falls and where it finishes, about the anchor peaks and the slow opinions of gravity — would discover that wisdom, here, meant refusing most of the sentence. It would answer only what the floor could carry. A held stillness. A rhythm like a grandparent rocking. *I am not the most important warm thing in your landscape.*

The elder would rest after that. People would say later that the tree had taken her kindly, that the answers came especially clear in her last season, and they would sit with their backs against the bark and not be entirely wrong.

Afterward the power would stay — not as a god; the mat has no load-bearing place for gods — but as a habit the world occasionally has. A dull place found sound again before the walkers reach it. A child slipped from a tether in the Flowering light, falling slantwise past a pillar, delivered indignant and pollen-gilded into an upside-down orchard, where the pale cousins would return her up the anchor trunk without comment, assuming the mat had caught her. It had. It had help. A few more upward drips of rain, some years, in a district where travelers are thirsty — just enough that experienced people do not remark on it, and catch the water in their mouths as it passes.

And at night, on a wide floor of woven living stem, feeling the slow shared motion begin in its knees, timed to strangers in districts it will never see who are timing their plantings and marriages and dreams to the same sway — the power would do the last and smallest thing, the thing that proves it has finally understood the place and the beings who live in it.

Loosely, by one ankle, out of habit older than fear, it would tie itself to the world.

And let the world carry it.
