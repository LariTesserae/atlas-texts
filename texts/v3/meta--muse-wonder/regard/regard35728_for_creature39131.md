It would not come over the rim.

Powers that mean well and do not understand a place usually come over the rim — from above, in light, with a view of the whole four hundred kilometres at once. This one came in the way everything else has to come in: on a barge, at ordinary sea-level, through the black steel gates of the lock, and was lowered with the water.

It rode the sinking down with its attention wide open, feeling the walls slide past. It read the fresh concrete not as a surface but as a calendar. It heard, through the hull, through the green cold column of water, the bass throb of the southern pump cathedrals already at work before the lower gates opened. It saw the painted depth-lines go by.

And ahead, the made sea opened, grey and steaming and busy, and the power — who had arrived with, not plans exactly, but capacities — felt the first wise thing happen in itself, which was hesitation.

Because from above, the Basin looks like a wound or a mistake. A continent had needed somewhere to put its heat, and it had dug a bowl below the sea and flooded its own factory floor and gone on working in the wet. From above, the benevolent impulse is obvious and enormous: lift the water out. Give them back the land. Or, if the heat must go somewhere, shoulder it yourself, cool the radiator fields with a clean miracle, silence the pumps, let the towers stop steaming, let the people sleep in quiet.

It stayed on the barge all the way to District 17 and did none of that.

It learned to listen through the deck first.

---

It stayed, at first, the way a visitor stays who is trying not to be one. It watched a full cycle and then another. Six in the morning, the southern intakes opened and the whole sea leaned, very slowly, south; mooring lines sang; the buoy flags turned; a woman on a roof in District 17 reached out one hand, without looking, and took hold of a rail as the water under her home began to move. Midday, it turned back. Children in a swimming lagoon in the sag of a warehouse roof shrieked where the reversing current tugged them gently by the ankles, and an old heron that belonged, for reasons no one could now explain, to one particular family, stalked the reeds and pretended not to watch them.

The power watched the people look down.

That was the thing it kept returning to. A visitor looks out across the central emptiness and feels how far it is to any shore. Basiners look down — through the hull, through the roof they stand on, into eighty metres of lit, working green — and feel how much is holding them up. Their home is not the islands. Their home is the whole machine, drowned and sharp-edged and humming in their teeth, large enough to have weather. If you drained it in the name of rescue, you would not be giving them back a country. You would be taking one away, for the second time, and leaving them standing in steaming mud among radiator forests and stranded fish, blinking at a sky that no longer made its own fog.

If you silenced it, they would wake in the night, frightened. Silence, there, means something has stopped.

So the power put away the enormous things. It let the towers steam. It let the artificial tide keep its rota and its foreman and its overtime. It let the mussels go on crusting the intakes, because the harvest of them is a fishery and a living and a way of knowing, by scar and by shell, exactly what the water has been doing.

And then, very carefully, it began to attend to the places where the Basin was not strange and alive but simply *brittle* — where one bad hour could take choices away from everyone who lived there, all at once, and forever. Wisdom, it decided, is mostly the work of giving a place more time, and more ways to fail safely.

It went down, in its own fashion, below the lit avenues.

---

Under District 9, where the current runs three knots through the narrows between the twin towers, there is a pump cathedral whose eastern intake wall had begun, microscopically, to tire. Not crack — not yet. Concrete remembers every load it has ever carried, and this wall had been carrying the lean of a sea, twice a day, for nine years, and it was keeping a small, growing tiredness in its heart that no inspection sub had yet been sent to hear because the instruments read it as noise. In thirty-one years, on a storm day when the surface fetch was long and the pumps were fighting the wind-driven pile-up at the northern dike, it would have given way all at once.

The power did not rebuild it. A wall that changes overnight is a wall no engineer will ever trust again, and distrust, in the Basin, is more dangerous than water. Instead it did something slower and kinder: it taught the wall to announce itself. It coaxed the tiredness into a pattern, a faint harmonic, a new note in the bass that travelled up through concrete and hull and bone. Three weeks later a roof-woman hanging nets stopped with her head cocked, listening to her feet, and said to no one in particular, *that's not right,* and a maintenance crew who trusted her kind of listening more than their own gauges went down and found the beginning, early enough for bolts and braces and ordinary human competence.

On the northern dike, where the real sea stands higher than the land and thinks about it constantly, the power found seepage learning a path through slag and basalt, grain by grain. It did not seal it with a miracle. It encouraged the mussels. Just there, in that dark toe of the wall, it made the water a fraction richer, a fraction calmer, and let black clusters thicken year over year into a living reef, shell knitted to shell, slowing the flow, filtering it, laying down their own limestone over the weakness — a repair that harvesters would maintain without ever knowing they were maintaining it, because it would give good mussels, big ones, in a place that was easy to reach at slack water.

And in the mussels themselves it did the one piece of outright cleansing it allowed itself. The Basin's food tastes warm and iron-rich, and mostly it is honest food — but in the beds nearest the old chemical aprons in District 4, slow poisons were concentrating, shell by shell, up the short food chain toward the children eating boiled mussels on a roof with bread brought in through the locks. No one had put them there recently. They were the leftovers of the world before the flood, leaching at the pace of groundwater. The power could not take them out of the sediment without tearing up the bottom of the sea. So it woke up things that were already sleeping in that sediment — microbes that had been waiting since before there were people — and gave them a slight, persistent advantage wherever those particular poisons seeped. Within a few seasons the mussels in District 4 would test clean, and the inspectors would credit the new circulation pattern, and they would almost be right.

Nothing vanished. Nothing shone. The fish still hang in their thousands at the warm edge of the radiator fields, turning as one. The seals still ride the locks down, fat and unhurried. The crabs still pick over the car park where the windscreens are, most of them, unbroken.

It did three more things, and these were not about safety at all, which is how the power knew it was finally understanding benevolence.

First, it softened nothing — the Basin is nine years old and its corners are still sharp, and its people have cleats and scored gloves and a way of testing a ladder with two tugs and a kick that is a whole philosophy — but in the roof-lagoons, where the sagging warehouses make shallow warm water and reeds have got a first thin footing, it let fall, on an ordinary wind, the seed of a reed that can root in a hairline crack in concrete and hold. Not a forest. Not a reclamation. Just a grass with a talent for the place as it actually is. In five years the lagoons will be greener and the herons will multiply, and children will have somewhere soft to fall that still belongs to a roof.

Second, for the divers — the ones who commute down past glitter and green into the dusk of the lit streets, who come up with forearms stinging and bags of mussels and stories told in street names that only exist underwater — it made the boundary layers over the great radiator fields very slightly more visible. Not much. A faint extra shimmer, a silvering in the wavering water where cold folds into warm, so that a diver learns the edge half a breath sooner in her skin and in her eyes together. Fewer ears hurt. Fewer suits torn on fins they saw a moment too late. The fish, who have always known exactly where the edges are, were not consulted, and approve anyway.

Third, and smallest, and the thing it did entirely for joy: once or twice a year the wind gets a long fetch across the central emptiness and builds a real storm, and the roof-towns batten down and ride it out like ships attached to the bottom of the sea. It left the storms alone — they are part of the weather the place makes for itself, and the people would feel cheated of something true without them — but it cleared, and will go on clearing, a single narrow lane of steam above District 17 on the first clear night after each storm. Just a lane, no wider than a street. So that from a platform there, with the surface black and rain-pricked below and the lit grid going down green into the deep, the children who time their baths by a pump rota and tell the hour by the lean of the water can look up, between one fog bank and the next, and see the stars that answer to no one, and the moon that runs the other sea, standing over this one for a while, not in charge of anything here, just visiting.

---

It left the way it came, on a barge, rising in the lock with the water. The seals commuted past it, going down. The gates sixty metres tall closed behind it on the ordinary sea, bright and thin and faraway, where weather is not made by anyone.

Behind it, at six the next morning, the southern intakes opened and the whole Low Sea leaned south, very slowly, the way it should. The lines creaked. The submerged avenues lit up under another working day. A woman felt it first in the soles of her feet and reached for a rail and was home; and somewhere under District 9 a new note sang faintly in the concrete where people who know how to listen were already listening for it — which is to say, the place went on being itself, only with a little more future in it than it had had before.

That is what it did. Almost nothing, on purpose, for a long time.
