It would not come down the stair with light.

That is the first thing wisdom would learn, and quickly, even for a power to whom light is no trouble at all. Because this is not a darkness that needs filling. It is a place that has made a working agreement with darkness, with heat, with pressure held just below failure — and a being who has made the same agreement in his own body, at a pace of an inch a decade. To open the hatch wide and call down in a kind voice would be the same act, here, as flooding, as cold, as a blow. Rescue would arrive in the shape of destruction wearing its kindest face.

So it would wait.

Not for an hour. For a cycle. For several. It would learn the slow breathing of the room before it presumed to touch anything in it — when the pipes tick and swell and the low passages close by a shirt-button's width, when the pilot flames gutter, when the pump clears its throat and thinks better of it and when, once in a while, it does not. It would learn which warmth is the path and which is a warning. What is polished and what is merely rust. What is failing, and what has only reached the form it wants to be.

And in learning, it would come to the harder understanding: the man is not trapped in the machine.

He was, perhaps, once. Or he descended for a shift and the shift never quite ended, or the world above misplaced his name the way rust has since filled the letters cut into the boiler plate. That history, whatever it was, has long since been composted into something else. Now the valve is bright because his hand brightens it. The fish turns in the sump because his shadow feeds it. The fern does not cook because, in a cool hour, he trims it back from the flange. And the room, in return, holds a hollow on the middle catwalk in the exact shape of his sleeping body, keeps his cup under its one clean drip, keeps the air at the third catwalk at a heat his thinned blood and slowed bellows have learned to use. Separate them now and you do not get a man restored to the daylight and a room standing empty behind him. You get two unfinished things dying of exposure.

A foolish benevolence would fix his eyes.

A wise one would notice that his eyes have not failed — they have retired from an economy that no longer pays down here, and his skin does the seeing now. It would not straighten his spine, which is curved honestly to the vault. It would not give him back his lifeline and heartline; the warm line has been holding that hand for sixty years, and the smoothness is not a wound. It is a signature.

So, from inside the room, it would do almost nothing.

Almost.

There is a flange beyond the third catwalk, up where even he can only reach in the coolest hour, beginning — only beginning — to speak at a pitch his palm, through the green enamel of the standpipe, would not hear for another year. That one it might ease, a quarter-turn's worth, spread across a month of nights, at the moment his own hand is on the central valve, so the small change enters the system disguised as his own work. There is a pilot flame whose glass nipple has a crack running in lime, not his fault and not yet his problem. That one night, while he cups another flame and breathes it back to blue, something might breathe with him, just enough, from no direction. Not a miracle. A steadiness. The sort of help that does not announce itself because announcement would cost him his tenancy of the dark, and his tenancy is the dignity he has left that no one gave him.

If it communicated at all, it would not use words. Words misuse the air down here.

It would lay warmth, once, on the far side of a sweating standpipe while his palm is flat on the near side, feeling for pressure. A pause. An answering presence in the metal, neither hotter nor cooler than it should be — only *there*, the way another tenant is there. And then gone before it could become a demand. He might still his breath at it. He might not. He might, that night, read the gauges aloud to it in his pipe-voice of clicks and lips, numbers that correspond to nothing above, kept current by being spoken. That would be conversation enough. That would be consent, and company, and neither of them pretending it must lead anywhere.

Everything else it does, it would do from above, where its largeness is an advantage and its interference reads merely as the ordinary luck of old infrastructure.

A demolition order would find itself misfiled under a street that no longer exists. A surveyor, standing in the alley with his instruments, would feel an inexplicable certainty that the hatch is a sealed cistern, decommissioned, uninteresting, and write it down in a hand that looks like his own on a tired day. The gas line marked abandoned on every city plan would keep its faithful trickle to three blue nipples, year after year, paid for out of an account no auditor can quite locate and no auditor can quite bring herself to close. The cold-water inlet would never be repaired — but also, and this is the finer work, never shut off. The leak that has outlasted the building would be allowed to go on outlasting it. Rain would keep finding its slow way down through the seams days after a storm, tasting of pavement and distance.

Above them, life would be gently steered the way he steers pressure: a quarter-turn at a time. A building preserved not as a monument — monuments attract eyes — but as something too dull to demolish and too sound to gut. Warehouses. Archives. A laundry that runs at night and contributes its own honest steam to the general warmth of the block, and whose night manager, without ever knowing why, develops a superstition about the alley stair and keeps it clear of refuse and of people.

It would not make them eternal.

That would be the final cruelty dressed as love: the man held forever at the edge of his last winter, the room held forever in its long patient middle, the fish turning forever in black water that neither fills nor dries. Everything down here has earned its balance precisely by being allowed to change at the speed of lime. The power would honor the rate. When his knuckles begin, some year, to find the low routes harder; when the swelling hours come a little sooner for his chest than for the pipes; then the upstream world would ease almost imperceptibly — the purge, when it commits, arriving a breath less white, a breath less hot, and still nine minutes, always nine minutes, because some things are load-bearing rituals and you do not take them from an old tenant.

And when he dies — wedged, most likely, in the hollow between the two tanks that is exactly him, one finger resting in his sleep against the beltless flywheel — it would not raise him, and it would not rush down.

It would let the room find out slowly, the way it finds out everything. The valve a fraction less bright each week. The cup filling and overfilling under its drip. The fish turning once at a shadow that does not come, and then learning, as all life down there has learned, to live on what falls in. It would keep the hatch sealed and the trickle running a while longer than is strictly necessary, because grief, even a machine's, should not be hurried — and because the ferns are still there, and someone, even if it is only a power with its palm laid flat and listening, should keep a hand in contact with the warm line until the heat itself decides to give up, tick by tick, in the dark.

Then, at the end, one thing only, and for the record no one will keep: it would go down itself at last, without light, crabwise, shoulder first, chin tucked, the way he taught it without knowing. It would find the name cut into the boiler plate by the wired-shut firedoor, unreadable to eyes, and read it by touch, slowly, the letters filled with rust that blooms and flakes and blooms.

And it would not restore the name to the world above. Names belong to the hatch side of things, and he put his down a long time ago. It would simply know it — once, fully, in the dark, in his own place — and then lay its palm where his palm wore the yellow bands almost to the iron, and keep contact, and find its way back up before the room finished cooling.
