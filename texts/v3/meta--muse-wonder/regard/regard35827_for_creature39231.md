They would not come from above.

That is the first sign of the wisdom. Above is already occupied — thin, bright, freezing, aching in the teeth — and anything arriving that way would only add to the altitude already packed painfully into the room. Anything arriving as light would scorch the orchids. Anything arriving as a voice would have to shout over the bamboo pipes, and the kettle, and the wind translated through the dome, and would be just one more enormous sound in a six-foot world.

So they would come the way everything else has come: from below. Basket by basket, as it were. Elbow by elbow up the warm drainage pipe. Taking the hot water in the face. Taking longer than a body-length should take. Ears clearing, and clearing again.

And they would arrive as the coldest thing in the room.

That matters. The room would rain on them, as it rains on any visitor, condensing out of its own wet heat onto the cooler skin. A foolish power would dry itself, or warm itself, or equalize things for its own comfort, and in doing so begin — gently, benevolently — to kill the pocket. This one would stay damp. Would let its clothes soak through. Would let moss investigate it if it sat still long enough. Would understand, immediately, in the lungs and in the inner ear, that it was not being offered dominion here. It was being offered a lump of wet clay.

They would take it.

Not knowing where to put it — wisdom being, in part, not assuming you know where the pipe joint weeps or which seedling is loose — they would wait to be shown. They would let the top one place their fingers over theirs, hot and sure, green-stained, ochre under the thumbnail. They would press where they were told to press. If they did it wrong, the clay would still take their fingerprint. Everything here does. They would leave it in.

They would not finish the mountain.

Oh, they could. That is the temptation that has to be refused first, before any real kindness can begin. They could point the cone. They could fire the badly fired pot hard and true so it stopped sweating. They could cut a clean door in the bulge and drive a stair down the inside of the slope and install, with great compassion, a railing. They could flatten the nine inches between frost and burn into one kind temperature where a person might stand up without their nose bleeding, might sleep without smoke pooling warm around the blankets, might drop a seed and watch it fall, sensibly, down.

They would do none of it.

Because they would see what the place is: not a problem that has failed to be solved, but a solution that only works while it is being made. Emergence as maintenance. A world that survives by being continuously invented, and that would dry, and cool, and remember its size, the moment anyone — however loving — completed it on its behalf.

So they would not rescue the inhabitant, either.

They would not lift them down out of the bulge into some lower, kinder air where their lungs would have to relearn the world and their blood unlearn the braced-for drop that comes when the white frog appears in the frost and every sound stops. They would not heal the horned, burned feet, or the hands worn to nothing, or straighten the low folded posture that has let a body live for so long sorted by altitude, frost at one end, fire at the other. They would not give them back a single age, or a single name, or a single face, if generations are packed into them the way weather is packed into the walls. To sort all that out into one ordinary person, with one ordinary lifespan, would not be mercy here. It would be another kind of equalizing.

What they would do is smaller, and harder to notice, and it would take them a while to learn how.

They would take a shift.

That is the heart of it, I think. Not a gift, not a miracle — a shift. Because the one true cruelty of the summit is not the heat or the thin air or the wrong direction of the rain. It is that the making must not stop, and the maker is a body, and bodies must sleep. The top one sleeps the way the garden grows in here: sparingly and continuously, in snatches between pollinations, one hand always on something living, listening even in sleep for the kettle to change its note.

The power would learn the work. Badly at first. Too tall, standing in stages, swallowing against the pressure. Reaching up for the ceiling seedlings when they should reach down. Sweeping the pooled smoke wrong. Addressing a hovering seed too soon, before it had finished deciding. But the hands would learn: tease this root straight. Dab the hair brush — their own hair now, cut in uneven handfuls if necessary — from blossom to blossom, humming low in the throat, not a song, a vibration the stamens expect. Smear the fresh mud before it dries. Lash the cane one notch higher.

And then, one night that is indistinguishable from the steamy green-brown day, they would keep making the world while the top one slept — really slept, for the first time in perhaps a generation, in the mildewing blankets, without one hand braced on the wall. The power would keep the kettle from screaming. Would answer it through a reed, in a limited way, like a bird. Would be the bee, and the fly, and the beetle in the black compost, species by species, as needed. Would press, with a thumb, a new green shoot gently into the soft wall so it did not finish.

Nothing would be improved. Everything would be continued. That is what rest would look like at the top of an unfinished mountain.

When the top one woke, nothing would be said about it. The power would simply be holding a coconut half of frost that had not melted, and looking faintly surprised, the way any new worker looks when yesterday's rain is still falling in the corner, one drop still trembling on its hour-long way down.

Then — only then — would they leave their gifts. And the gifts would be unfinished things.

A handful of soil from somewhere else entirely: not better soil, not richer, just foreign. Full of unfamiliar, microscopic life that would have to negotiate with the charcoal dust and ground bone and rotted thatch already here, and might fail, and might not. More kinds of life per inch. More argument against collapse.

A seed that did not belong to any plant the garden yet knew, which would require, if it lived, a new kind of tending no one had invented. Work, in other words. Future work. The only honest promise that the pocket would go on having a reason to be made tomorrow.

And one useless thing. This is important. Everything in the bulge is used, and reused, and fed back in — hair to brush, bone to soil, smoke to floor, body eventually to floor also. A wise benevolence would notice that nothing here is allowed to be merely *for* the one who lives here. So they would leave something with no function in the density at all. A taste from very far below, perhaps. A flavor that fed nothing and pollinated nothing and strengthened no wall. Or a tune that imitated no insect and answered no kettle, to be hummed once and lost. A small, unnecessary pleasure, placed in the clay hands, that would not be composted — or not right away.

They would not protect the place by hiding it. It was found by accident, and only because it is leaking, and the leak is part of how the world breathes and how the next pair of muddy hands finds the pipe. Instead they would protect the conditions of its unfinishedness, further down and further out, where such work is less visible: keep the basket-carriers carrying, in their own time, for their own reasons. Keep the lower world from deciding, one ambitious season, to cap the cone with a temple or a flag or a viewing platform with a door cut clean in the side. Keep maps slightly wrong. Keep the scaffolding lashed rather than nailed. See to it that now and then, at long intervals, somebody new crawls up the pipe who does not flinch from wet heat, or from being rained on indoors, or from a seed that takes all day to fall — somebody capable of becoming, in due course, the next shape the position grows around itself.

They would do the same for the frog, which is to say: nothing, on purpose, with great attention. They would not explain it. They would not breed it a mate or cure its blindness or coax it to sing. When it appeared in the frost, fat and white and silent, and the pressure dropped and all the drips forgot their direction, the power would simply stop — kettle, brush, humming, all of it — and endure the silence alongside the inhabitant, knees soft, one hand on something living, until the room remembered itself again.

At the end they would be given the visitor's portion again, which is also the worker's portion: wet clay, dark soil, the sweet smell of other people. This time they would know where to put it. Or almost. Or they would ask, again, to be shown, because asking to be shown had become the truest form their power could take in a place that size.

Going down the pipe would be longer for them than it was coming up. That is correct. It should be. The mountain would be higher by then by exactly the amount that had been added while they were inside — which is to say almost nothing, which is to say the whole world, ring by ring.

They would emerge underneath the clay bulge into the screaming, freezing wind, soaked through, smelling of compost and orchids and animal fat, hands muddy, ears still clearing. From outside it would be nothing. A swelling. A pot. A leak.

They would not look back up at it as if they owned the view.

Only, as they began the long descent past the scaffolding and the basket-carriers and the curing, steaming slopes, they might pause — a benevolent and wise power, doing the last and least of it — and add their own basket of earth to the pile. Not to finish it. Just so that it was, for one more day, still being made.

Inside the bulge, already, the top one would have forgotten the particular shape of them. Not unkindly. The way weather forgets.

But the wall would hold where their fingerprint had been pressed in. A foreign seed would be hovering somewhere, turning slowly, deciding. And for a long time afterward, in the hottest part of the floor and the frost on the stool and the slow rain still deciding to be weather in the corner, there would be one night's worth of deep, unguarded sleep worked into the soil — proof that, once, the making had been carried by more than one pair of hands, and could be again.
