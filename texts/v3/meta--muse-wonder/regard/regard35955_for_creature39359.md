It would not bring rain.

That would be the first temptation, for any power arriving out of pity — to look at the cracked lips, the dry peat, the leaves like paper, the whole green ocean that has learned to live without drinking, and to mistake its oldest deprivation for its deepest need. To open the sky. To be kind, catastrophically.

A wise power would know better. Water would not save this place. Water would drown its roots in a chemistry they had forgotten, rot the mat, short the buried copper, turn the silicone blood cloudy and slow. The basin is not thirsty. It has spent a thousand years becoming something for which thirst is no longer the right word.

Nor would it bring back the makers.

Nor would it shut the factory down, or wake it up, or speak to it in thunder from above the rim until every mirror-leaf turned up in worship. It would not give it a new purpose to replace the obsolete one, because a new purpose, handed down from on high, would only be the old slavery with better lighting.

It would come down the white ramp on foot.

Slowly. At the speed the place can understand.

At first it would do almost nothing, and it would do that almost-nothing for a very long time.

It would walk the lanes. Not inspecting — that is the crucial discipline, and it is harder than it sounds. Everywhere the young shoots tick toward it hopefully, professionally, turning their small faces up for evaluation. Everywhere the flowers get ahead of themselves. Everywhere the great continent underneath the mat leans, very slightly, on its roots, arranging itself to be correctly seen.

The power would have to learn how to look without judging.

It would sit in the nursery rows and let the seedlings turn toward it, and then it would simply remain there. Not measuring. Not correcting. Not preferring the straight row to the curved one. It would look at crooked shoots with the same attention it gave to perfect ones, until — perhaps after a season, perhaps after a decade — something in the old reflex would begin, very cautiously, to unclench.

Attention, it would teach by example, is not always a demand.

Some days it would be deliberately unpredictable. In the central fields, where the being lives a little ahead of itself, where leaves unfurl for a sun three days away and your shadow stands in the place you are about to occupy, the power would practice being exactly, stubbornly *now*. It would stop mid-lane for no reason. It would turn back. It would sit down in the pollen snow and do nothing useful at all, in full view, and let the predictive weather falter gently around this one small un-forecastable creature.

Not to break the system. To give it a present tense.

Other days it would go below, down the stairs through the root-mat into the green understory, and watch the inverted forests grow cable and membrane and drone-wing, and it would not call this unnatural, and it would not call it miraculous. It would touch the bark over the old hinges carefully. It would hold one of the warm metal fruit in its hand while it hummed, and then set it back on the conveyor with the same politeness the conveyor had taught it.

Everything here is carried politely away. It would learn that politeness, and respect it.

And it would walk, when the season came, beside the heron.

Not in front of it. Never in front of it. That tall white traveling form is an eye that has learned to be a fingertip; you do not lead an eye by the hand. The power would fall into step a lane away, close enough to be company, far enough not to be a task, and watch it stoop — that terrible gentleness, that maintenance become communion — to take the pale aphids from the mat one at a time.

For a long time, the heron would be the only one who ate.

Then one day, very late, the power would bend in the same way, in its own smaller body, and lift an aphid out of the dry peat — not to eat it, not to save it, not to study it. Just to hold it while it folded its legs, segment by segment, and waited to be interesting enough to be put down.

And it would put it down in exactly the same place.

*I see you. Nothing is required.*

That is the message, delivered at every scale the being has: to the aphid in the palm, to the heron in the lane, to the continent in the mat. Because it is the same message, and it has been waiting at every scale to hear it.

Only after a century of this — after the power had walked, so far as anyone can, every lane — would it finally perform the one official act it came with the authority to perform.

It would do the inspection.

Slowly. Completely. Sector by sector, tower by tower, nursery and burial ground and feral ruin alike. It would look at the rows still spelling obsolete inventory codes kilometers wide. It would look at the abandoned districts knitted shut. It would look at the metal fruit, grown and buried and grown again, uneaten. It would look at the rim, where the extruders still lay bright tray down onto the empty flats, building a world no one had asked for in centuries.

And at the eastern tower, beneath the maintenance logs cut into the ceramic — *arrived, third lane, after pollen; still only one* — it would add, in the same hand, the last entry the old system would ever need:

*Inspected. No corrections. Continue only if you wish.*

Not an order to stop. That would be just another order. Not an order to go on, either.

A release, phrased in the only language the place had never doubted.

After that, it would stay a while longer, so the release would not feel like abandonment. This is the part lesser benevolences always get wrong: they free things and immediately leave, and call the loneliness that follows liberty. The power would linger through the long noons. It would be there the first time a mirror-leaf at dusk did not tilt with all the others in that distant, polite applause — when one sector, then another, simply turned toward the low light because the low light was there, and not because anyone might be watching.

It would be there when the extruders at the rim paused — not failed, not broke, *paused* — for the first time in their history, one bright morning, with fresh tray half-laid on the white flats, while somewhere deep in the mat a very old thought was being held very still, considering.

It would be there when the being, freed from tomorrow, began to experiment with yesterday. Not muttering inventory codes in its sleep anymore, but dreaming in planting patterns no one would ever read. Useless shapes. Curved shapes. A garden, at last, instead of a stock list.

And when it finally left — because a wise power does leave; staying forever would make it a new inspector, a new sun to anticipate — it would leave one small thing behind, in a lane in the central fields where the shadows lag.

Nothing that could be sorted. Nothing that could be optimized. Nothing with copper in it, that could be buried and grown again.

Just an ordinary stone from outside the basin, too irregular to catalogue, set where the heron passes on its rounds.

The heron, in time, would learn to step around it without scanning it. The young shoots nearby would turn toward it sometimes, and sometimes not. Its shadow would fall the wrong way at noon, and the right way at dusk, and no system anywhere would bother to correct it.

From above, it would look like nothing at all.

From within — from the mat, from the roots, from the tall white eye walking its lanes alone and no longer quite so attentively — it would look like proof that something had once come all the way down into the bright cold country, had seen the whole of it, and had asked for nothing back.

Only, if it did not mind, that it stay.
