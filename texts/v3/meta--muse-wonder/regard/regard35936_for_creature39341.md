It would not stand in the middle.

That is the first thing.

A power arriving — however it arrives, and it would not arrive the way we do, sideways through the slit, wet to the knees from the marsh — would feel the rule before it saw the reason. It would put its back to the moss at the rim, among the crickets, and it would wait. Not one cycle, to satisfy curiosity. Many. Tide and melt arguing in the dark under the floor. Inhale, pause, the grinding of swallowed stones, exhale: twenty seconds when the room is one animal, blowing steam and blue sparks and borrowed frog-song out across the cold.

It would be tempted, at first, as any benevolent thing is tempted.

It could hold the peat roof up. That is easy, for such a power. A hand laid somewhere above the marsh, where no eye would see it, and the groaning slab that settles an inch nearer collapse every winter simply — stops. Years given. Decades. The pools kept in their steps, the hot seep kept from freezing, the iron kept precipitating in its slow rust clouds. A small, permanent sanctuary hammering away under the moss, safe.

It could do better than that, by its own first lights. It could widen the slit so the dipper need not time its impossible commute so finely. It could round the sharp shale throats so the young cave need not scream when it breathes. It could steady the cycle — seven minutes, seven minutes, seven minutes, reliable as a clock — so nothing living by the shift-whistle is ever caught braced for a blast that comes late, or feeding when the loaded pause breaks early. It could give the resident, for pity's sake, a body that persists between breaths. Something to be, in the intervals. Something that could be found, and named, and loved on purpose.

It would do none of this. That is where the wisdom comes in, and it is not the same as the benevolence, and it costs more.

Because it would stay long enough to understand what it was looking at. The emptiness in the centre is not a lack to be filled. The irregularity is not a flaw in the breathing — it *is* the breathing, tide and meltwater still negotiating, neither one in charge. The sharpness is not an injury. The whole edge of this world is young, glacier-fresh, and has not yet been lied to by softness. Round the throats now and you do not ease the voice; you take away the only voice this particular hollow will ever have. Old caves further along the margin sigh, and that is right for them. This one screams, briefly, and then is gone, and that is right for it.

And permanence — permanence would be the cruelest gift on offer. The resident does not live the way its tenants live, egg to larva to armoured adult, generation succeeding generation in the same warm pool. It lives by abandonment. Hollow after hollow along a breaking margin, each one learned by the water and forgotten by the stone. Its young are already worrying open at the low, narrow end: a new slit, a darker wetness, pressure prospecting for its next body. To hold this roof up forever would not save it. It would strand it in a room that had stopped becoming, while its successor waited unopened in the rock, and the water, which is its true blood, went elsewhere to find a way through.

To give it a persistent body would be worse, and quieter. Between exhalations it is not absent. It is dispersed — into seep and settle and charge, into the slow work of coating and flushing and feeding, into a hundred frogs positioned like reeds waiting to be played. That dispersal is not a lesser state it must endure until the next twenty seconds make it real. It is most of its life. A power that loved it would not drag it out of process into thinghood merely so that love could have somewhere to put its hand.

So it would do, from inside the cave, nothing.

Nothing laid in the bowl. No light installed, no shrine, no marker, no smoothing of stone. The tenants would notice no change in the roster of their days: feed on the inhale, brace at the peak, lay eggs only where the hot seep keeps them from freezing. The dipper would go on commuting as if it had a key. Anyone returning — and almost no one returns, it is not found by looking for it — would say the place was exactly as they left it, and would be wrong only in thinking this meant the power had not come.

Its work, such as it is, would be done outside, and upstream, and mostly in the future.

For it would listen, as it waited at the rim, to more than the cycle. It would listen to the peat settling, and to the new slit at the far end, and it would do the arithmetic that nothing in the cave can do for itself: the roof, left alone, will fall one winter too soon. Not this winter. Not a tragedy on the scale of winters. Just — mistimed. A hard freeze, a heavy rain on snow, and the hollow will collapse in the deep cold before the successor slit has opened wide enough for the siphon to find it, before the cobbles under the floor can be flushed through and caught again in a new loop, before the water learns the next room. The lineage of breath along this margin, unbroken since the ice left, would end not with an exhale but with a blockage.

That, it would prevent. Not by holding the roof up. By asking it, very slightly, to fall differently.

Somewhere in the black peat above the marsh, where sedge tussocks stand stiff with frost and nothing appears to be happening for acres, it would shift the weight of the dome by the width of a hand — ease one root-plate, settle one block of dirty blue ice a fraction deeper into its lee, turn the slow pressure aside toward ground that can bear it. No miracle visible. A groan in the peat one night that even the frogs would mistake for settling. And the roof would keep its slow descent, and go on groaning, but along a longer arc: one more spring. Two, perhaps. Time enough for meltwater, working in the dark at the narrow end, to worry the new slit from a seam into a throat. Time enough for the first tentative inhale in the next hollow — shallow, irregular, more leak than breath — to be answered, one exhale soon, by the old cave's last.

And upstream, where a fallen slab is beginning to comb the meltwater the wrong way, sending silt toward the pools in a quantity the assembly line cannot process, it would move one stone an inch. An inch. The water would divide more cleanly, most of it going harmlessly out across the marsh to stand in black pools reflecting a sky too dim to be morning, enough of it still finding the crack to keep the three vats at their work. Nothing in the cave would know. The amphipods would go on turning, blind and white, in water that simply failed to dirty the way it might have.

That is all. Midwifery, not rescue. A future ensured and then refused.

It would not bless the resident, if blessing means singling out. It would not leave it aware. There is a kind of mercy in not making a process know itself — in letting the hum in the floor stay a hum, the charged lift of hair on a visitor's neck stay weather, the twenty seconds of animal unity stay unexamined by the animal. It has the frogs to sing for it. It does not need to be given a soul to be complete, and a wise power does not hand out souls as if they were improvements.

What it would leave, if it left anything, is obscurity, renewed.

Not a ward, not a fence. Just a slight deepening of the quality the place already has: that it is not found by looking for it. Survey lines that were drifting this way across the marsh would find their reasons to drift elsewhere — a wetter crossing, a more promising reading one valley over, nothing anyone could point to, no memory tampered with, no will overruled. Boots would keep passing within fifty metres on the higher ground, as boots presumably already do, and the slit would sigh shut behind its own weather and go on inhaling. The best protection for a thing whose centre must stay empty is that the world continue to believe there is nothing here but marsh.

And it would leave the way the dipper leaves: timed to just after an exhale, when the moss curtain is still plastered flat and the air moving out is warmest, full of sparks, frog-song already fading off the blast. Out sideways into the low blue-grey light. No footprints that survive the next drip. Behind it, audible through wet peat and new stone, the resident already gathering itself out of thinness again — drips hesitating on the ceiling, crawling impossibly up-slope, cobbles beginning somewhere below to turn in the dark.

Unhurried. Unguarded, in every way that matters, by anything but its own briefness.

Seven to eleven minutes later, the marsh would hear, faintly, if it had ears, a hundred throatless frogs played all at once like reeds in a flute — and then the ordinary dripping, and the wind in the sedge, and nothing to suggest that anything, however benevolent, however wise, had ever paid the place the compliment of refusing to save it forever.
