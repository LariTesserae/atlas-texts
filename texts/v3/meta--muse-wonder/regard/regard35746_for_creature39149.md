It would arrive, and the first thing it would do would be to slow down.

Not out of reverence — reverence is easy, and mostly for the visitor — but out of simple competence. Anything wise coming onto the Table at ordinary speed would be a catastrophe wearing a kind face. To bring rain would be to bring rot, hunger, flood, a thousand awakened appetites all demanding at once. To bring spring would be murder. Birds would finish falling. Predators would finish biting. Flowers held open for a decade would slam through pollination, seeding, and death before their one fly arrived. The ums, suddenly burning at mammal speed on a plain of lichen and stone, would starve standing up, in their millions, with their legs still lichen-sealed to the ground.

So it would not heal them quickly.

It would stand at the rim where the switchbacks give out, and it would take the time the place required. Years to cross the first lane. Longer to be noticed. It would learn to make its shadow behave — that would be the hardest part, keeping its intentions from running ahead of its body and grazing, lying down, turning away while it was still trying to listen.

And it would listen below hearing, at the herd’s own pitch, until it understood what no tally wall quite managed to say:

They are not enchanted. They are not punished. They are not, most of them, even unhappy in any way that could survive translation. They have made the only bargain the Table offers and they have made it honestly: almost nothing spent, almost forever received. To tear that up because a faster mind finds it unbearable to look at would not be mercy. It would be colonization by pity.

It would also, being benevolent and not merely polite, notice the places where the bargain had stopped being chosen.

Here, a calf born half-grown into the felt between two adults, fed mouth to mouth for thirty years, never once asked whether it wanted to emerge. There, a warm flank that was perhaps once a shepherd, four-limbed in some old arrangement under the mat, still included, still slowed, the difference politely forgotten by everyone except, perhaps, somewhere far down, the one inside. Here, a dead um held upright for three centuries by its neighbors, its shadow lying down every afternoon in patient disagreement. Everywhere, the slow tyranny of a system so good at continuing that it has forgotten how to let anything conclude, or leave, or be new except by extrusion.

So it would act, but at the edges, and by gradient, and almost invisibly.

It would not open the sky. It would make the rim kinder.

All around the Table, so gently that no instruments carried by later visitors would ever agree it had happened, it would lay down a long slope in tempo rather than in stone — a margin, days wide on the outside and centuries deep on the inside, where a step could become slightly more thinkable, then thinkable, then taken. Where a bird whose stoop had become a life sentence might, if it kept facing outward, find the wind arriving closer and closer together until flight meant flight again. Where an um who had been considering motion since before the huts were built could tear the pale seam of lichen at its hoof without that single decision consuming the whole of its remaining courage.

Nothing would be pushed. The way out would simply stop being impossible.

And for the dead — only for those whose shadows had been lying down for a very long time, which is as close to a request as the Table ever makes — it would soften, in a few sheltered hollows, the terms of falling. Not a grave. Nothing so abrupt. Just places where the ground, prepared across decades with shed felt and corky dung and patient loosening of stone, would receive a body that finally subsided not as failure but as completion. Where a shape held up by the herd could be set down by the herd, centimeter by forgiven centimeter, until lying and standing at last agreed.

At the center it would do nothing at all.

That is the part the wise would have to learn last: to leave the deep plain exactly as unrescued as it wishes to be. No fountain. No accelerated dawn. No voice from above pitched to shatter the felted silence. If it spoke, it would begin one low greeting and accept that the answer might outlive mountains elsewhere. If it was thanked, it might never know.

From the world below, it would ask one more protection, and this it would do firmly, because gentleness toward the slow requires occasional firmness toward the fast. It would let the path up be forgotten by the kind of people who arrive with saws for the felt and cages for a secret of long life. Not hidden by storm or monster — the Table has had enough of being made into a story — but mislaid, season by season, in maps, in memory, in the mild conviction of surveyors that there is nothing up there worth the climb. Those who come because they can bear to brush a warm flank and wait would still find their way. Those who come to harvest forever would find themselves, somehow, always planning the expedition for next year.

Then it would stay awhile. That, perhaps, is the whole of it.

A benevolent power would refuse to make the ums into a lesson, a tragedy, or a miracle. It would stand among them until its own breathing spaced itself out and its hunger forgot and returned as information rather than emergency. It would lay one hand — if it had a hand, if it consented to have one at that speed — against a cool flank and then, fifty years later, against a warm one, and feel the vast, unhurried fact of another life declining to perform itself for approval.

And when it left, by the slow margin it had made, it would leave no monument. At most, beside one of the old markers — one white stone, one black stone, beside an animal still mid-blink — a third stone, neither color, set down not to measure the migration but to record that someone had finally come who understood the measurement was not the point.

The herd would go on standing in the ordinary sun.

Somewhere near the edge, across the next several centuries, a single um would finish deciding, lift one lichen-bound hoof, and take a step outward — not saved, not cured, just permitted.

That is how you would know anything had happened at all.
