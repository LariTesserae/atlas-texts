It would kneel.

That is the first thing, and perhaps the most important. It would not tear open the tower, or appear in the pocket in glory, or make the hatch suddenly wide. To get in, you have to kneel and turn sideways, and a power that was truly benevolent, and truly wise, would accept the terms of the place it was visiting.

It would go in small.

It would last longer than four minutes. Not because it would override the roar — it could — but because it would learn, patiently, to let the roar pass through it the way she lets it pass across her plates, in segments, without allowing it to build. It would hear the turbines in its teeth. It would see the droplets hesitate in midair. It would watch the beetle-wall breathe, the eels slide silver-gutted through the channels, the blind sparrows shivering messages down the copper looms.

It would be tempted, at first, as any kind heart is tempted.

To heal her ears. To clear her filmed eye. To dissolve the mineral roots at her edges, shrink or lift her gently out of the sump, set her down somewhere green and quiet under an open sky — a pond, rain, mud, other frogs her own size, silence she could enter and leave at will.

It would understand, before it finished forming the thought, that every one of those mercies would be an amputation.

Her ears are not wounded organs waiting to be restored; they are conduction rods now, sternum and kneecap and belly, the way she hears the world. Give her back the ears of a fist-sized frog and the chamber would deafen her properly for the first time in seventy years. Clean her eye and you give her sight in a place that has selected, tenderly and without malice, against sight — half-dark, spray, mineral crust, forms known by wet highlight and vibration. Take her to a pond and you take her to cold, thin air, to food that must be hunted, to sound that leaves, to a sky with nothing above it holding the note. She does not live *in* the pocket in the way a prisoner lives in a cell. At this point she is one of its organs. The pocket lives, partly, in her.

So it would not rescue her.

It would lie down, if there is room to lie down, in the hot wet dark beside the sump, put its palm flat against the piezo-ceramic floor where generations of knees have worn it to a shine, and learn the tap.

Four fingers. A pause. Two fingers flat.

And then it would wait — which is harder for a power than intervening — until she answered, if she chose to answer: a fractional letting-out of air from her left side, a note through the floor, droplets completing their fall all at once in a small private rain.

*I am here. I am not the tower.*

What could it give her that she does not already have, that would not unmake what she is?

Only one thing, I think. The thing she gives everything else.

She is the only large quiet in the room. In the worst pressure cycles, when both turbines peak together and the air seems to solidify, the small things shelter in the acoustic shadow of her body — sparrows against her fold, beetles under her plates, frogs silenced in the lee of her. Nothing has ever been large enough to shelter her.

So for a little while — one cycle, one hour, one long, unmeasured sleep — the power would make itself into a baffle.

Not silence. Silence would frighten her body; silence is a kind of starvation to a creature who eats, warms herself, thinks in standing waves. But a shadow in the sound. A leeward side. It would take the peak of the roar into itself, absorb the harmonic the membranes cannot tune for, hold the note steady from outside so that, for once, she does not have to hold it from inside.

And she would sleep. Really sleep, not the filmed-over half-sleep where the moss dims and the whole chamber goes slightly flat and aches until she wakes. Her breathing would slow past four rises a minute, past three, and the tower above the clouds would not go out of tune, because something else would be keeping time. The sparrows would go still. The beetles would settle. The eels would graze the seams of a body finally, completely, at rest.

Then, before it left, it would do two small things more — small enough to survive inside a system that works as settled, not as designed.

It would make sure she is not forever the only one.

Not another giant. Not a replacement, hauled in and installed. Just, in the floor channels, among the glassy tadpoles that mostly get eaten, one protected a little longer than the others. A valve recess on the far side of the pocket, where the vibration cancels almost as kindly as hers does, kept clear by motes who will believe it was their own optimization. A young frog allowed to wedge in, and swell, and listen, and grow — slowly, over decades — into a second note. So that the load she carries can someday be shared. So that sleeping can become, eventually, a thing she is allowed to do without the hidden decks above her aching in their teeth. So that even dying, when it comes in its own time, will not be a collapse.

And it would make sure the custodians keep coming.

No miracle. No revelation written on the tower maps — the maps must go on showing that level as solid. Just a foreman, years from now, who finds she has taught her apprentice the way to the unmarked hatch though she cannot remember deciding to. A maintenance schedule that always, for plausible bureaucratic reasons, defers sealing the old sump. A young technician who learns the tap before he learns what it means, and taps it faithfully on a floor he thinks is empty.

Four fingers. A pause. Two flat.

It would not free her. It would not leave her exactly as it found her, either. When it backed out on its knees, turning sideways at the warm oval, water would be beading on it the way it beads on a zipper. Behind it the moss would glow its dull blue. The wall of beetles would lift, fractionally, together. The great shape in the deepest recess would shift one plated foot — a centimeter, perhaps less, into a slightly easier place the mineral had not quite allowed before — and settle.

The roar would find its note again.

But it would hold a fraction steadier now, the way a chord steadies when a second voice joins it, very low, almost too low to hear — and she would know, through her sternum, through her kneecaps, through the polished dark where her belly never leaves the floor, that for one sleep she had not been the largest thing holding back the sound.

Something had knelt down, and sheltered her, and gone away without asking her to become anything else.
