They would not come down the stair with light.

That would be the first sign that they were not benevolent, or not wise. Light all at once, dry boots, a plan to rescue it — to drain it, clean it, restore it either to a working yard or to a proper wilderness — any of those would be a way of killing it politely.

So they come, if they come at all, the way everything else in that place has come: slowly, by degrees, and getting damp.

They stand on the iron stair halfway down, where the day has already gone green-grey and their breath shows, and they wait long enough to stop being an event. Long enough for their clothes to go wet through without rain. For condensation to use them. For the rats to recalculate the pipe-runs around another warm vertical obstruction. For the frogs behind the gauge glass to go on pulsing.

They learn the rules before they presume to love the place.

They do not fix the flywheel's loose tooth.

They listen to it click once per revolution, that small irregularity in its slowest heart, and they understand that everything down there has timed itself around the flaw. The eels gather *before* the gate opens. The snails graze *after* the level falls. To make the machinery perfect would be to make the calendar lie.

They do not kill the rust. They do not clear the mats, or scrape the fungal fans from the brick, or haul out the gantry to reopen the two pools it bridges. They do not bring plants down and grieve when no green thing takes. This being does not need leaves. It reds and whites and blacks. Its skin is already eating its house and making new stone out of the eating. A gardener here would be a vandal.

And they do not wake it up.

That would be the greatest temptation for a power: to gather all that distributed attention — snail eyes turning together on their stalks, a frog's eye behind a useless dial, eels weaving into one rope before a sluice, newts fanning their gills on a warm casing — and lift it into a single mind that could speak, and thank, and ask for help.

They refrain.

It thinks already, in the only way that is true to it: water finding its next level, thirty seconds after the pitch under the floor rises half a tone. To force it to think in words would not be a gift. It would be an amputation of everything in it that is shrimp and shrimp's blind picking, everything that is pump-hum felt through soles, everything that knows by spilling.

So inside the yard, they do almost nothing.

Almost.

Up in the leaning shed, where no living thing goes because the floor is rotted to lace, there is a drum tipped on its side, sweating slowly from a seam. It is not iron. It is not brine or lime or the fine black grit from above that the shrimp can sort forever. It is a concentrated chemical sweetness from the old power house, a poison the mats cannot eat, only spread, pool to pool, a little more each wet season. Left alone, in ten years, in twenty, it would reach the warm cistern where the larvae hang.

That, they remove.

Not with a crew. Not all at once, not with scrubbing and alarms. Over many visits, a mouthful at a time, as if by seepage in reverse, until the drum is an empty rusted shape the crabs can judge for housing. They take the poison somewhere it can be unmade. They leave the stain where the drum lay. The stain will be gone over, in time, by red mat and white crust. They do not hurry it.

That is all they touch below.

Everything else they do, they do above — because that is where this low life is truly vulnerable. Not to predators. The heron is welcome; what it leaves goes back to sediment. Not to cold, or dark, or failure. It has lived around failure so long failure is architecture.

It is vulnerable to being noticed.

To a town that suddenly remembers it owns a sunken yard and decides to fill it for a car park. To a new building whose foundations drink the water table down a foot — a foot, and the pools at their impossible separate heights all level and still and die. To road-salt dumped in a hard winter, to a council decision to cap the overflow, to put a fine safety net across the open well at the top, neat and legal, through which no heron will ever again fold its impossible wings.

Against those things, quietly, the power goes to work in the daylight world it otherwise would not trouble.

A map is misfiled, and keeps being misfiled. A survey records the yard as sealed, stable, uninteresting, already accounted for. The small electric bill for a pump that officially does not exist is paid, year after year, out of a fund no auditor can quite trace to its end. When engineers propose a drain that would empty the low ground, their calculations acquire a persistent, plausible error that routes it two streets east. When the town, in a fit of tidiness, stretches netting over the well, a single cable slackens in the night. The gap is exactly heron-wide. No one remembers ordering it repaired.

And because benevolence that only preserves the present is finally just another kind of cage, they do one more thing, the wisest and the least visible:

They make sure it is not wholly dependent on being preserved.

Behind the east wall, where the counterpoint pump murmurs, there is a culvert choked to a pinhole with concreted rust and shell. They do not clear it. They widen it by the width of a thumb — no more — so that if the power ever fails for good, if the town above falls silent as the power house did, the water will still find a slower gravity road down and out, toward real marsh, toward ground that has never seen a gauge or a dial.

It may never use it. It may, in a century, pour itself through in one dark eel-bright pulse and cease to be a yard-being and become something else, something its descendants of snail and shrimp will learn new rules for.

They do not decide that for it. They only leave the door the width of a thumb ajar, as all the gates down there are ajar.

Then they climb back up the stair worn into troughs, dripping most of the way, carrying nothing out except damp cuffs and the smell of iron and ozone on their skin. They do not bless it aloud. They do not leave a marker, or a name, or anything new for the crabs to live in that would make the place feel watched.

Behind them, nothing announces that it has been saved.

The solenoid clunks. A pool sinks two inches. Another brims over a lip worn smooth for precisely that spill. The skaters, who had cleared to the margins, come stitching back across the still surface. The newts resettle, three and four deep, on the casing as it warms.

The hum steadies.

That is how you know the power was wise: the place resumes its levels without them, and does not know it was ever in danger, and is free to go on being one low, crowded, unrescued thing — not built, not born, but adopted, pool by pool — for as long as water, and iron, and life can keep agreeing to be each other.
