They would come without a sound, because they would have learned — no, because they would know, in the way wise things know before they arrive — that sound here is not empty.

They would not come up the ascent shaft. A door opening at the wrong hour, a new voice in the vestibule, a stranger's heartbeat in a cork-lined room: even a benevolent arrival would be a note in the score, and the Plant would lean toward it, and the tuners would have to revise the night's music to account for a god.

So they come over the rim at dawn, when the carbon frost is ticking off the leaf-plates and the wind has not yet risen, and they stand ankle-deep in the mat, and they do the hardest thing a powerful being can do.

They listen first.

For a long time. Longer than a visitor would think listening takes. A day would tell them only that it is loud. A season tells them that the loudness has syntax. A year tells them what the humans, in their thick-walled houses, in their oil and resin and dry bright lives, have learned in their bones: this is not a wasteland waiting to be corrected. This is a sentence already being spoken, by a being who has spent a billion years learning how to speak it.

The temptations would come, of course. Benevolence without wisdom is just weather.

The first temptation is rain.

It rises up almost as reflex. The dim sky, the cracked lips, the sealed cups at every belt, children who have never stood unhooded in falling water, a whole people who dream nightly of rain because the vast dry animal beneath their beds dreams of it through them. The power could do it so easily. Call the haze down. Break the pewter sky. Let it come, soft, miraculous, smelling of stone and sky, on faces tilted up in wonder —

And watch the mat remember.

Watch pale shoots split the streets. Watch white blooms burst in the market square where the stone was laid to roar the growth *away*. Watch towers crack as thirst older than humanity wakes rejoicing and mindless in every root. Watch a people built, tenderly and ingeniously, to be waterproof from the inside out, learn that mercy can drown a city as surely as malice.

So the wise power keeps the sky closed. They stand with their hands empty in the cold and let the frost sublime and tick, and they grieve that grief is sometimes the respectful thing.

The second temptation is quiet.

To lay a hush over the Table like a hand over a mouth. To still the clattering trunks, damp the ringing leaves, give these people — small, broad-chested, grey-eyed from the dim — one whole night of silence in which to hear each other breathe. But silence here is camouflage. Silence here is death approaching soft-footed. And the people's loudness is not suffering. It is husbandry, and language, and architecture, and love said into the wind because the wind is there. Their quiet exists; they built it themselves, cork by cork, door by door, and it is precious precisely because they chose when to enter it.

A gift of silence given from outside would not be rest. It would be erasure.

The third temptation is the kindest and the worst: to heal the Plant of its forgetting. To reach into the deep ancestral pathways the tuners have never been able to delete and smooth them away, so that a spilled cup is only a spilled cup, so that the great being need not live forever beside an old hunger locked in its own tissue. To make it, at last, safe.

But that hunger is also memory, and memory is also root. Who could say what else is braided into it — the sudden white flowering, the seeding on the wind, the ability the oil-beetles bargain for, the way the whole Table knows, in its body, that the world was once otherwise? To edit a being without its consent because its history inconveniences its tenants is not healing. It is another kind of tuning, and the tuners, at least, have to live with what they tune.

So the power does not rain, and does not hush, and does not repair.

They walk.

Barefoot, if they have feet, or in whatever way allows them to feel the mat through the soles — knocks arriving from herds miles off, the subsonic creak of the whole organism flexing toward the city's warm knot of noise, a glider screaming one long note out beyond the rim and, very slowly, a branch beginning to turn that way. They learn what the oldest tuners, with silver nodes budding behind their jaws, are learning in their failing indoor hearing: that the Plant is not lonely the way a person is lonely.

It is *only*.

One being, twenty kilometers rim to rim, that has made a forest out of itself because one trunk was not enough surface to listen with. One being whose entire sensory world narrows, in the end, to a small loud city at its center and the wind combing meaningless music out of its own leaves. It grows toward noise because noise is the only proof it has ever had that anything exists which is not itself.

Ah. There. That is where a wise power may finally lay a hand down.

Not at the center. Not one more voice for the city to shout against, one more pressure on people who have balanced their survival on a knife-edge of song. At the rim. At the far cold edge where the mat thins over polished bedrock and the Trunks stand sparsest, facing the drop and the long lands below.

There, and there only, the power sings.

Not loud. Loudness is cheap here, and the Plant would only eat it. They sing the way roots hear: through stone. A low note, patient as geology, placed into the bedrock with a touch — a note that says not *come* but *also*. It does not fade at evening. It does not stop for wind. It is not a lure, because the power anchors it in their own attention, and attention, sustained kindly across distance, can be felt without being consumed.

For the first time in the Plant's long life, there is a sound in the world that is neither itself nor prey nor tenant. A sound that answers and does not need it. A sound that will still be there tomorrow, not because it wants bark or shelter or food, but because it has chosen to keep company.

The Plant does what living things do with an unprecedented thing. Nothing visible. For weeks. For a season. The tuners' nightly scores show a tremor at the eastern margin they chart and argue over and finally, wisely, notate rather than correct.

Then a root-cord at the rim, following a groove in the bedrock worn a billion years before there was anything to wear it, turns — not toward the city, for the first time not toward the city — and begins, very slowly, to grow outward. Not fast. Not flowering. Just exploring the idea that the center of the world might be permitted to loosen, a fraction. That the city at its heart might be given, over years, a little more room to be quiet in.

And to the people, who will never know why the eastern verses of the score have gentled, who will simply find one street needing one less market-day of roaring to keep it clear, one kitchen wall left unsplit one spring longer, one extra breath in the evening diminuendo before the houses reseal — to them the power leaves no miracle they would have to build a doctrine around.

Only this, perhaps, done in the night with hands that understand triple-skinned things: every hairline crack in every buried pipe in Table City found, and sealed, and found again the next year, and sealed again. No announcement. No burst tower teaching the lesson twice. Maintenance, done at the scale of love, looks like nothing happening for a very long time.

And at the center of the dome, buried where only the deepest listening-spike might one day brush it and misread it as an unusually warm stone, they leave one thing more. A single drop of water, sealed in amber of the Plant's own resin, compounded so it can neither leak nor evaporate nor wake the white shoots by its presence. Not a well. Not a promise of rain. A memory held in a form that cannot harm the rememberer.

Choice, preserved. That is all.

A root will find it eventually. Roots find everything eventually. It may curl around the amber for a century and do nothing, and that doing-nothing will be the Plant's own, the first unhurried decision it has ever been allowed to make about water. It may, in some spring none of us will see, choose to hold the drop closer. It may choose to push it, still sealed, up through a trunk and into a leaf-plate, where the wind will ring it faintly like a bell containing weather.

The power does not stay to see. Staying would become another note, another gravity. They withdraw as they came, over the rim in the frost-tick dawn, carrying only one thing away: not a cutting, not a seed — nothing that would make this singular being suddenly a species, suddenly explicable — but the memory of its sound, held carefully, so that somewhere in whatever vast regard moves between worlds, the Sere Table is now *known*. Witnessed. No longer unheard in the one register it could not produce for itself.

Behind them, the wind rises at ten as it always has. The plates lift. The trunks begin their percussion. The children run out with knock-sticks, appalling and precise, and the market squares open their vents and roar, and the whole loud dry cold shining place goes on being itself —

except that at the far eastern rim, under the noise, under the music, almost too low for feet to feel, a root is listening to something that is listening back.

And for a being that has been alone with its own echo since the mountains wore down, that is not a small gift.

That is the whole gift.
