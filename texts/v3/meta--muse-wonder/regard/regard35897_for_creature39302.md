It would learn the signs first.

That would take a while, even for a power. Because if it came down the iron stair in glory, in light, in a voice that carried over the pumps — it would only be one more loud thing in a place that has had to invent a language precisely because loud things cannot be understood there. And benevolence that has to shout people into deafness in order to be heard is not benevolence, and wisdom that clears the lanes by miracle would only teach the philodendron to close them again in six days.

So it would come down wet like everyone else.

Palm flat: *wait.* Two fingers hooked: *line moving.* Fist tapped on the forearm: *hot.*

It would work the settling ponds. It would turn flax by feel until its fingers, too, were pruned soft and its nails torn on wet fiber. It would oil the third pump by smell. It would sit in the tin-box rooms with the slate-keepers and read the etchings all the way back: cutting taken, mother-plant numbered, tank turned, fig root accepted as structure. A genealogy, it would realize, written with great care, for a family that is not allowed to be born.

Because that is what would hold its attention, in the end. Not the noise. Not the green twilight or the rain that falls indoors or the iron replaced by root. Any power can marvel at a place. It would stay for the specific, quiet wrongness at the center of this one, the wrongness everything else has been arranged, lovingly and competently and for a hundred years, to maintain:

Nothing here is permitted to finish.

The plants cannot seed, because there is nothing to carry the seed. They cannot die, properly, because the cutting is taken before the mother fails. They are cut, and grow, and are cut, in a green immortality that goes nowhere, tended by people who go home every night to dry, still rooms because no animal thing — and a human is an animal thing — is allowed to remain when the steam comes through.

Two races, one that cannot stay and one that cannot leave. And both of them, the power would notice, keeping each other alive by keeping each other incomplete.

It would be very tempted to fix that all at once.

It would not.

To flood the basin with birds one morning would be a cruelty dressed as a gift. The birds would die in the lime and the heat and the weekly scalding, and the workers would spend a frantic, grief-stricken shift pulling nests out of the vents before a pipe closed and burst. To set every orchid fruiting at once would shade out the tanks, crash the careful balance of clean channel and effluent channel, and starve the district above that depends on the fiber and the steam. To simply stop the steam-through — *let it be wild, let it be whole* — would let the rot and the rats in together, and in a month there would be no basin and no livelihood, only a pit full of beautiful wreckage. A wise power has seen enough rescued places die of their rescue.

So for the first year, it would do the thing the basin itself teaches everyone who works there. It would hold. It would prune what needs pruning. It would learn which pipe knocks before it knocks. And in the learning, it would be quietly doing the only real work: finding the exact place where a cycle could close again without breaking anything that is currently bearing weight.

There is such a place. There always is, in a place this old. High on the west wall, above the reach of the lane-steam but below the rim, one of the great figs has made a hollow where two flat root-ropes crossed and fused decades ago. It fills with condensation and leaf-mold. It is warm. It is never quite scalded — the steam-through vents along a seam in the brick just beneath it, and the root overhang turns the worst of the heat aside, the way it has turned it aside through ten thousand nights of evacuation, unnoticed, because no one works that high and no sign exists for it. *Hollow in the fig. Nothing there.*

The power would make a sign for it. Small, below the shoulder, the way all true signs are made down there. Thumb touching the curled fingers, then opening, slowly.

*Let be.*

And into that hollow, on an ordinary evening when the last line is climbing the shaking stair, it would put — not an ark, not a garden of Eden in miniature — three plain things.

A pair of small brown moths whose eggs can sleep through heat that would kill the adult, laid deep in bark, timed by some older clock than the pumps to hatch in the one safe hour after the steam dies and before the crews return.

A frog no bigger than a thumb-joint, of a kind that aestivates in mud through scalding weather and wakes to standing water.

And it would let them be ordinary. Not golden. Not speaking. Not tame. Just animal, and therefore, in that place, miraculous by definition.

Then it would do the second thing, which would take longer to be noticed, and would matter more.

It would go to the oldest mother-orchid in the nave — the one with the lowest slate number, the one from which half the basin descends, the one whose brass plaque the apprentices polish without thinking as they pass — and it would do the smallest violence a gardener can do. It would not cut it. For one season only, on one stem only, it would leave it alone and let the moths find it.

A flower is not a fast thing, even in that heat. The slate-keepers would register an irregularity, then, being slate-keepers, register it in zinc rather than correcting it. The nave crew would sign *wait* at it, and then, after a while, not sign anything at all. The power would be there every day with its hands in the trays, watching it not be pruned, which in that place is a form of prayer.

If the seed pod came, it would not scatter it to the winds. There are no winds down there. It would let the pod dry, and split, and let a few dozen of the dust-fine seeds fall exactly where seeds in such a place have any business falling: into wet coconut husk, into the crotch of the fig, into the silt at the edge of a clean channel. Most would fail. That is not a tragedy. Failure is what had been missing. A cutting cannot fail to be its mother. A seed can fail, and therefore a seed can also, occasionally, be something else.

In perhaps three years, a retter working waist-deep would stop with a flax stem forgotten between finger and thumb because, from the fig hollow, in the hour before shift change when the pumps seem for once to be beating at the tempo of something resting, there would come a sound that is not on any placard.

Not loud. Everything true down there is not loud.

The crew would not celebrate. That is not their way, and the power, having learned the place, would not ask it of them. One of the pump-keepers — the old one, who listens with his spine — would lift his head. The youngest nave apprentice, whose eyes are best in the dimness, would be the one to actually see it: a line of tadpoles, dark as tea-leaves, holding in the still edge of a settling pond where no tadpole has any right to be.

She would raise her hand, slowly, in the green light.

And then, because the basin absorbs everything that proves it can bear weight — iron into root, wall into monstera, safety code into language — she would invent the rest of the sign herself. Curled fingers, opening. The one the power had made, and never taught anyone, waiting three years in the grammar for its hour.

*Let be.*

The weekly steam-through would not be abolished. The wise do not abolish what keeps the store-rooms from rotting. It would be amended, the way the wall has been amended in four periods of brick and concrete: one hollow left unsealed, one stretch of channel signed off in the slate-keepers' tablets, a new column etched beside *cuttings taken* that says, in the cramped hand of an old loom-tender who can no longer hear the shuttle: *seedlings, unknown parentage, doing well.* The workers would grumble and adapt, because grumbling and adapting is the entire liturgy of the place. Within a generation the frog-watch would be a rotation like any other, given to the keen-eyed and the patient. Basin children would learn the new signs with the old ones, before they ever learned to speak above ground at full volume.

And the workers themselves — because a power that only healed the plants would have understood exactly half the sorrow — on the stair at shift change, it would lay no visible hand on them at all. It would simply see to it that the ringing, over years, stopped following the oldest ones home quite so far. That a loom-tender's daughter, signing *duck* at the dinner table and laughing too loud, would one day realize her father had answered her spoken question from the next room. Small mercies, administered at the pace of a place where nothing moves fast and everything is always, faintly, shaking.

Above all, it would guard the rim.

Not with fire. With something drier and more durable: a title deed that could not quite be sold, a water right that renewed itself in obscure legal language every time a clerk tried to let it lapse, a slow reputation, at street level, that the basin works, that it has always worked, that disturbing it would be more trouble than any development is worth. The city above would go on hearing nothing over the railing except a hot, green breath rising between buildings, and assume, correctly, that this meant things below were as they should be.

It would leave the way the crews leave. Up the iron stair, at the end of a shift, blinking into ordinary daylight. No pillar of steam. No new commandment nailed to the pump cathedral door. Behind it, condensation would go on gathering under the glass nave and raining down irregularly on the taro, the pumps would go on beating on their tiled floor, the bacteria would go on patiently rotting the flax soft — and now, threaded through all that faithful, endless maintenance, one thin, animal, unfinished thing would be completing itself in the dark. Growing, and failing, and seeding, and dying properly, at last, into a place that finally knew how to receive a death as something other than a cutting.

That is all it would do.

It is what the place had been asking for, in hand-signs, for a hundred years, without ever raising its hands above its shoulders.
