They would come to the top of the stairs first, and stop there.

Not out of reverence — out of accuracy. From the top, in the glare and the dry wind, the pit still looks like something a benevolent power ought to fix. A broken roof. A half-abandoned cooling station. Machinery running without operators, plants running without light, heat without purpose. Every instinct that travels under the name of kindness would begin, up there, to make a list.

Replace the glass. Clear the vines from the fan guards. Cut the growth back from the boilers before something overheats. Turn the great useless roar down. Let air in. Let birds in. Bring bees down the stairs in a box, bring clean water, bring soil, rescue the green things from the copper and the ozone and the dark.

They would not do any of it. But they would have to stay at the top long enough for the list to stop feeling like love.

Then they would go down slowly. Slower than the stairs require. They would shed whatever in them was too bright, too sudden, too well regulated, the way you take your hand off the hot rail and hold it only through your sleeve. They would learn the first lesson the pit teaches anything that enters it, which is that help, at the wrong tempo, is only another kind of weather: a brief pressure, a passing wetness, gone before a leaf can turn.

Inside, they would not heal it. There is nothing down there presenting itself as a wound. The copper that would poison a pond is the reason this pond can exist without being eaten into balance by ten thousand small mouths. The note that would hollow a small skull is the medium the stems listen through. The darkness is not the failure of the light. The leaves have already voted with their whole bodies, away from the roof, toward the boiler casings and the trembling column. To flood the pit with sun would not be a restoration. It would be an eviction.

Nor would they wake it further, if waking is the word. They would not give it eyes, or a voice, or a center to replace the several centers it does without. They would not make it grateful. They would not name it in any register where names become addresses, and addresses become visiting hours. A wise power knows that some forms of attention are extraction wearing a softer coat.

What they would protect it from is not change. Change is the one thing it is demonstrably better at than the people who built it. A boiler can die over a decade and become a warm mound. A fan can seize and the steam can redraw itself around the stillness. A pipe can cool and be held, faithfully, by moss, until warmth finds another route. It does not need to be preserved. Preservation is for things that cannot answer.

It needs to be protected from suddenness.

So their work would happen almost entirely outside the walls, and it would look, to anyone watching, like neglect raised to the level of policy. A demolition order misfiled into the wrong decade. An industrial parcel bought by a trust with a name so boring the eye slides off it. A decommissioning plan for the old plant upstream — the one whose waste heat still feeds the main, and which had been scheduled to go cold over a single winter — quietly revised, year after year, into a tapering so slow that by the time the last boiler up there cools, the growth down here will have spent thirty years learning how to live on less. Not eternal heat. Time to answer. That is all they would ever give it: failures it can metabolize, endings with long approaches.

Where a failure threatened to arrive all at once — a section of roof lattice hanging by one corroded bolt above the heat-exchange column, ready to fall in a second and do what no gradual ruin ever had — they would not repair it. Repair would be the human tempo again, clanging and final. They would simply loosen the catastrophe into a sag. A descent of inches per year. A thing the vines could reach, brace, test their weight against, and finally hold or lower in their own braided way. Suddenness, translated into slope.

And they would do one thing inside the pit, which is not a thing.

They would stay.

Not for an hour, while their notion of scale shrank. Not for a shift, moving a chair a meter at a time under the travelling rain, though they would find the chair, and sit in it, and move it with the drip because down here good manners consist of accepting the weather that is offered rather than demanding the weather you brought. They would stay past the point where a visitor registers as an event. Past the flinch and the slow turning of leaves toward a small, badly regulated heat. Past seasons, which barely reach the floor anyway. They would stay until they had been demoted by duration into something the pit's body could finally perceive accurately — not a rescue, not a threat, not a brief wet warmth shedding salt near the fern bank. A stable feature of the gradient. A presence it could choose, in its own six-minute way, to incline toward or away from.

They would let it choose. They would consider being chosen, if it ever happened, a sufficient miracle, and they would not spoil it by answering too fast.

When they finally left, they would take their glove with them. They would leave no marker, no plaque, no improved stair, no story posted at the top explaining what lives below. The last and hardest part of benevolence, here, is obscurity: to make certain the pit goes on being uninteresting to the world above it. Unvisited not because it is forbidden — forbidden is an advertisement — but because it is filed, in the human mind, under things already understood. A ruin. A sump. Some old cooling yard, probably empty.

Down below, nothing would announce that anything had been done. The fans would pass their slow *whup* through the vines. The valves would tick open in sequence. The rain would be falling, for a while, on the control booth and nowhere else, and then imperceptibly it would be falling through the fern bank by the stairs. The rosettes would open in the warm exhalation. A leaf, fed on heat and iron and trembling air, would go on listening with its whole body.

Only, far above and years away, a heat that might have stopped like a cut thread would instead thin like evening. And the pit, which has never waited for anyone, would have time — which is the only gift it has ever been able to receive — to answer.
