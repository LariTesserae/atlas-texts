It would not come down.

That is the first decision, and it takes longer than most civilizations last.

From far away the High Pan looks like a problem to be solved. A continent held fifteen kilometers too high by machines no one understands. Air too thin to shout in. Gravity that forgets itself. Children tied at the waist so the sky does not take them. A wise power, seeing that, could feel the old, clean urge to *fix* it rise like heat off basalt at noon.

Thicken the air.

Still the pylons. Give gravity back its one direction.

Lower the whole impossible plateau, gently, over a thousand merciful years, to where rock can hold rock again and people can breathe without a suit and speak without touching helmets.

It watches long enough to learn what that mercy would kill.

Thicken the air and the gliders, who do not fly so much as borrow buoyancy from the lattice, will find themselves suddenly too heavy for their own translucent bodies. The rosettes, who open only after a shear to feed on disturbed gradients, will close and not open. The silica grass will stop growing atom by atom out of stillness. The lickers will graze salt from pans under a kinder sky and slowly lose the mineral armor that is both their protection and, shed plate by plate, the armor of the Holdfasts themselves.

Give gravity back its certainty and the dust-rivers will stop running an inch above the stone. The repair-swarms will have less to repair, and less reason to think their brief, bright thoughts in empty air. Make the pylons agree, and the rich lichen in the shear-zone between the two old enemies across the riverbed ghost will thin. Make them *safe*, and you will have made them strangers on the only ground they have ever trusted — precisely because it could not be trusted in any simple way.

Lower the Pan and you do not save the Holdfasts. You end the world that made them, and ask the survivors to be grateful in the ruins of its weather.

So the power does not descend in light. It does not speak in the burrows, where air is precious and voices are a kind of wealth. It does not leave a monument, because an unsecured monument on the High Pan is just a future piece of litter, one desert away, waiting to be misread as a mistake.

It secures itself.

That is how it begins. It learns the first rule as if it were a child: everything tied down. Its attention on wrist-lines. Its curiosity on waist-lines. It does not camp on the fused glass. It does not walk through a swarm at work. When a Rising comes and every loose thing on the plain begins to orbit a center no eye can find, it makes itself heavy with plates of patience and stands, like the Holdfasts stand, listening through its feet.

For a very long time — for generations of barrel-chested elders whose suits have grown tortoise-thick, for children born smooth-suited and taught the rules without moving their lips — it does nothing that could be told in a story at a beacon-convergence.

Nothing, that is, except this:

It turns away the others.

Because the Pan had drawn *its* attention, it could draw more. A lift-station survey that lingers a season too long. A drone with new eyes. Men and women in clean suits who would see autonomous pylons and think *salvage*, who would see Holdfast burrows and think *rescue*, who would see a finished, level world and think *empty*. The power does not strike them down. It is benevolent, and it is wise, and it has learned from the Pan that noise does not travel far here anyway. It simply makes sure their maps acquire small, sincere errors. Their compasses, near the southern rim, turn in slow circles. Their horizon lies, gently. They go home reporting a high, sterile peneplain of no economic interest, inhabited, possibly, by standing stones.

It keeps the door closed from the outside, so the people inside do not have to spend their lives being a door.

And deep below the burrows, below the cooling joints where frost sweats out of stone and is gone before it can ever be water, it does the one piece of engineering it permits itself.

It listens to the pylons argue.

Not to silence them. Two pylons across an old riverbed have been disagreeing for four recorded generations; families walk around that disagreement the way you walk around weather, and the walking-around is a form of knowledge. The power does not end the argument. It becomes, very carefully, a third presence in the lattice — not a ruler, not a replacement, a tie that can absorb the worst of a surge. So that when a correction comes, as corrections must, it comes heavy instead of murderous. So that a shear still bends the light and makes distant pylons double and slide, still feeds the rosette fields in their ring a kilometer out, but no longer puts a cliff quite so often under a level plain where a child is walking untied for the first brave minute of being trusted.

It reinforces what is failing in the places no Holdfast can reach: the deep roots of the field, the parts of fallen pylons buried under their own perfect sections, the slow fatigue in the lattice that, left alone for another ten thousand years, would one day stop holding a continent up at all.

It does not make the Pan eternal. Nothing wise promises that. It gives it time on the same scale the Pan already understands: erosion-time. Time enough for lichen to eat stone and excrete it again. Time enough for a grandmother's plates to be shed, found, and grown onto a grandchild's suit.

For the people themselves, it asks — once.

Not in words. Words up there are for burrows, for thick air and extravagant storytelling. It asks the way the Pan asks everything: through stone.

On a dusk when a dozen pylons are blinking white across a plain that curves with the planet, and two circuits have converged, and there are voices in two burrows instead of one, some of the oldest Holdfasts put their teeth gently to the rock, as they have always done, to read distance in the tremor.

And for the first time the tremor reads back.

Not a command. Not an offer of rescue. A pattern, patient as beacon-talk, that takes all evening to say the whole of it:

*I can hold the edge a little longer. I can keep the others away. I will not change the sky you feed on. If you want more, blink it here. If you want less, I will go.*

They talk about it underground, where breath can be spent. Some of the young ones want rain. Some of the old ones want, fiercely and at once, for it to leave before wanting rain becomes a habit. A child asks whether it can bring back the ones the Rising took, and is answered with the honesty the Pan teaches everyone: what the sky sets down, it sets down elsewhere, changed. No one rages. They add the question to the map of places not to trust, which is also the map of everywhere they love.

In the end they answer in the only grammar they fully believe.

At dawn they come up out of the burrows. They do not look up. They set their boots hard in the basalt, each family in its own place on its own circuit, a kilometer from the aprons, plates heavy with salt and rust. One by one, across distances no shout could cross, they twist a boot against the stone.

*Stop.*

Then, after a silence long enough to be respect rather than hesitation, they do it again, softer.

*Stay, if you can stay like that.*

So it stays like that.

If you went to the High Pan now, a hundred years after its arrival, you would swear nothing had changed, and you would almost be right. At noon the zenith is still star-flecked indigo. Dust still ticks against a visor, felt more than heard. A glider still crosses the black sunlit sky and eclipses a star for a breath. The pylons still blink on at dusk, one after another, white across the curve of the world.

Only, perhaps, in the shear-zone between the two old arguing pylons, the compasses turn a fraction more slowly, sincere as ever, but with a longer pause before they begin again — as if somewhere in the argument a new, quiet listener had learned when not to speak.

And along a cooling joint at first light, where a river of grit runs an inch above the basalt, you might see the standing stones stand a moment longer than stillness requires.

They are not waiting for a miracle.

They are making room, beside them, secured at wrist and waist and ankle, for one more neighbor who finally learned how to live on the High Pan:

By holding it up, almost imperceptibly — and otherwise, with all its power, letting it be.
