It would arrive without light.

That would be the first sign of its wisdom, and its benevolence: that it learned the etiquette before it entered.

No radiance. No voice from the sky. No sudden warmth descending on Halu Cirque like a hand. It would understand, at once and in its own deep way, that a flash could blind a quarter-million animals, that a shock could bring the chorus down like a ceiling, that nothing kind can happen here quickly. Kindness here would have to move at veilwing speed.

So it would come in polar night, in darkness indistinguishable from the darkness already there, soft-soled, pressureless, and it would do the thing almost no power does.

It would stay outside the last condenser row for a very long time, and listen.

First it would consider all the obvious mercies, and refuse them one by one.

It could warm the valley. It could lift the cold off the basalt, melt the rime from the condensers, give the caretakers a morning they have not seen in eleven weeks. But the veilwings live by the difference between their clustered heat and the killing air. Warm the air and the clusters loosen, the breath does not condense, the cisterns fail, the whole wet climate that mites and beetles and shrimp and shepherds inhabit collapses out of a single act of generosity. A summer, here, would be a disaster with good intentions.

It could unmake their engineering. Give them back whatever wild, fast, hunting ancestor sang before human beings remade them into an instrument — hearts quickened, wings snapping open, voices freed to fall silent. That, too, would be cruelty dressed as liberation. There is no wild left for them to return to. Nothing green, no insects on the wing, no soft roost in any tree, because there are no trees. Freed, they would scatter into forty-below black and die beautifully and individually, instead of living slowly and together. The power would understand: you do not rescue an animal by deleting the only world in which it can survive.

It could speak to them. That might be the greatest temptation. A million voices, chirping and scanning without pause, asking the stone the same question for thirty years — surely what they want is an answer?

But it would listen longer, and hear the truth under that. They are not asking a question. Asking implies they can imagine silence while they wait. They cannot. The chirp is deeper than waking, deeper than intention. It is a heartbeat that happens to be made of sound. To answer them in any voice they could recognize would not be communion. It would be the loudest event in the history of the cirque, an echo with no source, and it would stop a million hearts in the same nine-second interval.

So it would not speak. Not to the town. Not to the species.

It would go, very slowly, to the being.

Because somewhere in the oldest roost-spire, near the warm and airless core of a cluster yards thick, hangs a female older than the towers under her were designed to last. She has never flown in any meaningful sense. Her wings have unfolded perhaps a dozen times in her life, always partway, always in the slow ripple of the cluster waking. She has borne pups who crawled through her fur and grew and died somewhere in the same square meter of darkness. For three decades, without a single exception, she has chirped, and listened, and chirped again. Sleeping. Waking. Nursing. Dying by fractions.

The power would make room for itself beside her the way a shepherd does — the cluster shifting, subtly, to let it pass, the way water closes around a slow oar. No veilwing would move away. No note would change. It would simply hang there in the warm ammoniac rain, among the mites moving in their pale herds through the fur, and match her tempo.

One chirp every interval. One heartbeat every nine seconds.

It would stay until it understood that the tragedy of Halu is not captivity, or cold, or even use. The humans do use them, yes — the towers gather the echoes, the processors churn the guano, the sludge goes down-valley and money comes back up. But the shepherds use them too, and the heron-things, and the shrimp in the cisterns, and the beetles grazing clean stone at the tower bases. Everything in Halu uses everything else. That is not the wound.

The wound is that no single veilwing has ever been allowed to stop.

The chorus has rested in shifts older than any caretaker's protocol, this cluster ebbing while that one rises, but an individual — an individual has never known one hour in which the world was not demanding her voice back from her. She cannot sleep below the level of the chirp. She was built so that she cannot.

And so the power would do its first and only unmistakable thing, at a scale so small the town's instruments would read it as nothing at all.

It would take her turn.

Not stop the chorus. Not introduce silence into the great layered climate of sound. It would fold its own attention into the rhythm immediately around her — animal to neighbor animal, the overlapping pulses that make the surface seamless — and carry her part for her, pulse for pulse, indistinguishable in pitch and pressure from the voice she has used all her life. So smoothly that the towers would render no gap. So gently that the neighbors hanging flank to flank against her would feel no cooling, no loosening, no wrongness in the pattern.

And for the first time since she was a pup no bigger than a thumb joint, crawling blind through her mother's fur, she would be allowed to not chirp.

The power would give her one hour.

She would not use it the way a human would imagine. She would not dream wild skies, because she has no wild sky in her. She would not understand rest as an idea. What would happen would pass below language entirely: the nine-second heart slowing a fraction further, the wings loosening a fraction more around her body, the folded face unclenching. And in the absence of her own returning echo, she would hear — perhaps for the only time — the things that have been underneath her voice her whole life. The subsonic hum of the tower. The drip of condensate above her, finding its way down. The hearts of her neighbors, out of sync with hers, making together the warmth she lives in.

And far below all of it, arriving faint and delayed through basalt that still rings when struck, the slow tectonic sigh of the valley itself — stress moving, water gathering, stone not yet finished deciding what it will become.

She would hear, for one hour, what she has spent her life listening for.

Then the power would give her voice back to her as carefully as it took it, one pulse re-entering the weave, and she would take up the chirp again without knowing anything had happened. Only her heart would beat a little more slowly that season. Only the shepherds, nosing through that cluster weeks later, would pause — sensing some density that did not need licking apart, some knot that had mysteriously learned how to rest.

That would be the miracle. It would leave no data.

The rest would be slower, and even easier to mistake for the world simply continuing.

Deep under the eastern wall, the caretakers' maps have begun, in recent years, to show a stress gathering in the new rock — nothing urgent, nothing they would name aloud to visitors, but a coherence in the returning echoes that suggests the valley's next act of becoming might arrive all at once. A single slip, in a single winter, that could drop a roost-spire, split a cistern field, bring tons of breathing insulation down into streets with no drains and no channels.

The power would not stop it. Stopping geology is not mercy; it is postponement, and postponement here only means a larger debt. Instead, across a decade, in darkness, it would persuade the stress to spend itself cheaply. A thousand micro-slips, each too small to feel through a boot sole, each one arriving in the chorus as a faint new coloration no human ear could separate from the general roar. The veilwings would notice, in the way a body notices weather changing in a joint. They would shift, almost imperceptibly, tower by tower, away from the faces that were going to shed their skin of stone. The shepherds would follow the shifting warmth. By the time the eastern wall finally settled — with a sigh like ten tons of small bodies resettling, heard one night across the whole cirque — it would settle by inches, not meters. The caretakers would check their instruments, find the dreaded coherence gone, and argue happily for years about whether it had ever been real.

And at the cirque floor, where water has wept and pooled and spread in perfect black sheets for thirty years because the drainage does not yet know where to go, the power would do the smallest geological favor of all.

It would start one crack.

Hairline. No longer than a forearm. In basalt so new it has not learned erosion, it would introduce the suggestion of a path — not a channel, not a river, nothing a visitor could be shown. Just a weakness, aligned with the true and patient slope of the valley, down which the first melt-sheet from one condenser would begin, very slightly, to prefer to travel. Within a year, mineral slime would darken its edges. Within ten, the blind white beetles would be grazing it clean in their slow scraping files, extending it grain by grain. Within a hundred — long after the power had gone, long after the present caretakers were dead — Halu would have something it has never had.

A first stream, an inch deep, running black and clear through the town that was built to listen to a valley being born, teaching the water at last where rivers go.

The power would leave the way it came: without light, without sound, at a pace indistinguishable from the printer-gantries creeping on their rails. It would not cure the veilwings of being instruments. It would not lift the polar night, grow a forest, explain itself to the few dozen humans washing stinking water off their suits in dim red domes, or leave behind anything that could be mined, worshipped, or sold down-valley as sludge.

It would leave the census unchanged. The chorus at full pressure in the teeth. The shepherds swaying, heads down, in the roar. The towers rendering their slow maps of water and stress and forming stone.

Only, forever after, woven into the pattern of the place at a level beneath instruments, there would be a precedent. One cluster, then another, learning it across generations the way all knowledge travels here — slowly, neighbor to neighbor, at walking pace and then slower: that the voice can be carried for you a while. That the density can hold, even if you stop. That rest is not the same thing as falling out of the warmth.

And on certain nights, which are all nights, in the deepest hour of the deepest roosts, a few veilwings at a time — never enough to thin the sound, never enough for the caretakers to measure — would fall silent.

One hour each. In turn. Trusting the whole breathing surface to keep them warm while they listened to the valley, and to each other, and to the far-off beginning of running water.

While the chorus, undiminished, went on asking the stone what it was becoming — and the stone, a fraction sooner than it otherwise would have, began to answer.
