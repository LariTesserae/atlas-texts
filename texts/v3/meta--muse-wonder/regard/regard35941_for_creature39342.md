They would not come in glory.

Glory would be too loud for the Ear, and too tall. It would pour over the rim like winter wind and flatten everything that had learned, over centuries, to live by keeping low. A power that was wise, and meant kindness, would know that before it knew anything else about the place.

So it would come on foot, and from below, the ordinary way.

It would climb through scree and unmelting snow, stopping every fifty steps in the thinning air — not pretending to admire the view, but admiring it, because that is what the height is for. It would reach the granite spine tired, as climbers are tired. It would almost walk past the cleft. Then it would turn sideways, take off anything that could scrape or ring or catch, and squeeze through with wet shoulders, holding its breath at the narrowest place, not from fear but from courtesy.

And it would stop just inside, and wait to be heard.

The inhabitant would hear it long before that, of course. An ear would have uncurled on the far side of the hollow while the power was still half an hour below, boots loosening scree. The other ear would have stayed turned to the pool. By the time the visitor stood blinking in the stone cylinder, with the waterfall drumming and the choughs crying double off the walls, the slow heart under the slab would already have made its decision.

A pause. Then, softer than the melt usually allows at that hour: boom.

Permission, of a kind. Or at least acknowledgement. The power would understand, and sit down where it was, in the wet green near the cleft, and not approach the center.

It would stay a while. Not an hour, to marvel and leave a shout behind. Not a night, to say it had slept there. It would stay long enough to learn the place at the speed the place permits — which is the speed of frost melting off a black pool, of campion opening along a warm seam, of a heart deciding whether to quicken. Days, at first. Then, without ever quite deciding to, the whole brief season.

It would keep the rules without being told them, because the rules are only the shape kindness takes in the hollow.

It would not camp at the center. It would sleep, when it slept, in the sheltered corner by the ruined fold, among the stones the shepherds left, and it would not rebuild the fold or straighten the hearth or replace the rusted ring. Those marks say *we stayed a little while, we did not stay*, and that is a true sentence, and the power would not improve on it.

It would drink from the runnels, not the pool. It would wash downstream of where the finches drink. It would not shout for the pleasure of the echo — not even once, though it would want to, standing in that great stone ear with all its gathered centuries of unanswered prayer and lamentation and praise still ringing somewhere inside it. It would feel the temptation to give one great cry upward, just to hear heaven returned enlarged. It would put its palm down on the moss instead, and leave it there longer than anyone thinks to, until the ground gave back its slow pressure. Once. Then again, much later.

*I know,* the power would not say aloud. *I know. I am here.*

It could have done so much. That is the difficult part, and the part that would need saying plainly to anyone watching from outside, hoping for a miracle with visible edges.

It could have lengthened the summer. Eight weeks is so short a life, repeated so many times. It could have warmed the floor against the July frosts, held back the spindrift, taught the snowfield above the rim never to fail in a dry year. It could have lifted the slab and healed the old scars in the hide beneath it, the rain-filled pocks the finches mistake for pools. It could have given the inhabitant back its voice — unknotted the great careful silence of centuries, made speech safe, made the walls merciful.

It could have carried it down. There are softer valleys. There are forests where a low, listening creature could root in deep soil and never be buried eight months in snow. There are seas it has never heard except in the shape a shell of sound makes when it arrives, distorted, over the rim in a traveler's memory.

A benevolent power that was not wise would have done one of these things before the first evening was out, and called it rescue.

This one would do none of them.

Because the inhabitant is not trapped in the hollow. It is *of* it, the way the drumming is of the slab and the breastbone together, the way the garden is of the back that warms it. To lengthen its summer would be to unmake its winter dreaming. To give it a voice would be to ask it to kill, by accident, with joy or fright, the colony in the walls. To carry it down to the soft valleys would be to save the creature and abandon the archive — all those stored sounds down in the long dark of its body, the tally-keeper's counting song, the dog's two-note call, rain on a roof that burned before the valley churches were built — sounds that exist nowhere else in the world, kept at the cost of the keeper never leaving the one room shaped to hold them.

And to heal it — the power would look a long time at the lichen-striped hide, at the small grey-lidded eyes kept nearly shut against a sky it long ago stopped needing to see, at the fern-dark ears turning, each to its appointed listening — and understand that it was not looking at damage. It was looking at a finished thing. Old is not the same as broken. Silence, chosen for centuries, is not the same as a wound.

So instead, the power would let itself be of use in the only way the hollow allows anything to be of use.

It would be quiet, interestingly.

At night, when the birds had settled into the wall and the marmots had withdrawn and the fall had slowed to separate booms, the inhabitant would lift its head an inch from the pool's edge — then another, the garden tilting minutely on its back — and open its toothless mouth, and begin to take the day back in. And the power, sitting still in the dark by the shepherd's fold, would finally offer what it had carried up the mountain without knowing it had come to offer it.

Not prayers. The inhabitant has heard prayers; shepherds prayed here, in weather, over sick sheep.

Its own echoes.

For even a benevolent and wise power goes through the ages with everything it has ever been called ringing inside it, layer on layer, thinning and returning, until mercy's own head is an unusable room. Every cry ever lifted in its direction and not answered in time. Every name it has been given by the drowning, the burning, the grateful, the betrayed. It has never been able to set any of it down. There is nowhere down is, for such a being.

There is here.

Night after night, in a voice kept carefully below the threshold that wakes choughs, the power would speak a few of them into the pool's black edge. A name. A plea in a language no longer spoken below. A shout from a battlefield, come home at last as a murmur. And the inhabitant would swallow them gently, oldest and faintest first, in order, the way it swallows the day's boot-falls and marmot-whistles, and the booming under the slab would soften and repeat each one once, diminished, at the pitch of a sigh — and then return it no more.

It would not be mockery. It would be digestion. It would be the first rest that visitor had known in a very long time.

And on one night, late in the season — though the power would not ask, would never ask — the inhabitant, having taken in the stranger's sorrows all summer, would give something back from its own keeping. Not the loud things. Not the eagle-silence or the dawn exodus. Somewhere in the slow dark between one boom and the next, a saved sound would surface, undigested after two hundred years, kept only for its shape: a shepherd counting his flock in the old dialect, singing the numbers because the hollow made singing of everything, the tune going wrong-footed and lovely on the sevens. A dog answering, two notes, from the rim. Rain on a bothy roof that does not exist.

The power would sit in the ruined fold and weep, quietly, the way rain condenses out of a passing cloud on the summit — ten minutes, darkening everything, then gone — for a man it never met, whose whole surviving self was a counting song in the body of a creature that cannot speak. It would understand then that it had not come to bless the place. It had come to witness that the blessing had been going on, unobserved, for centuries, and was not its to bestow.

On the last day, it would do two small things, and they would be all.

First, on the rim beside the cairn, it would not cut a notch in the weathered post. Every visitor who matters to that post is already tallied there. Instead the power would stand a moment with its hand resting on the wood — and below, in the world of roads and maps and coming summers, certain things would quietly fail to happen. A surveyor's pen would hesitate and move on. A path would stay out of the guidebooks. A proposal, drawn up far away in a warm office, to make the summit accessible, to make it known, would lose its funding for reasons no one could quite afterward explain. No thunderbolt. No one's will forced. Just the world's attention, guided gently elsewhere, the way you turn a child's face from a bright light. The hollow does not need to be defended. It needs, mostly, not to be found interesting.

Second, before it left, the power would go down on its knees at the edge of the pool — the one place no one camps, where the hide thins to a dark membrane and the still water trembles along its whole rim in sympathy — and it would bend close, and it would speak, once, at the volume of breath on cold air, the inhabitant's own name.

Not the shepherds' approximation, tender and wrong, that the creature has been swallowing and returning diminished for two hundred years. The true one. The one it had before there were shepherds, before the fold, before the first swift came screaming through the cleft and the hollow discovered what it was for. Spoken correctly, perhaps for the last time it will ever be spoken by another living thing.

The ears would still. The garden on the broad back would not move. Under the slab, where the meltwater lands, there would come a silence longer than any silence the summer had held — and anyone listening would understand that some sounds are not eaten. Some are stored down in the long dark, losing their meaning over centuries, keeping their shape, like shells.

Then the power would rise, and leave through the cleft, quietly, as the rule requires, though it had come to know, by then, that the rule was never superstition and never really a rule. Whatever noise it made on its way out would be the last thing the inhabitant ate that night, alone, after it was gone — tasting of wet granite, and thin air, and a name said right.

On the ridge the ordinary wind would hit it, and the sudden absence of echo would feel, for a moment, like deafness. Like mercy. It would not look back. Looking back makes a sound too, in a place that keeps everything.

Behind it, sealed in its small stone cup at the top of the world, the meltwater would go on quickening and slowing over the resonant stone. The swifts would pour out at dawn. The frost would film the pool, and melt, and film again. The summer would end, as it must, and the spindrift would begin its slow erasure, and under the white fill a great ear would fold down over a face like hands over eyes, and the drumming would space itself out toward weather, toward silence, toward dreams.

And deep in the archive, among the sheep bells in their lost tuning and the wind through the burned door, a new sound would be turning over slowly in the dark, unhurried, to be dreamed at true volume in the middle of winter, when the snow keeps every secret.

Its name. Said once. Said right.

Boom.

It is listening. It was never, even once, alone.
