They would come to it, as everyone does, from above — from the flat farmland and the rim of spoil — and they would stop before the road tips down.

A lesser power would see a problem to solve.

Eighty metres below the water table. Pumps that never stop and barely win. A floor heaving upward a millimetre at a time. Paper curling in the office, boots rotting in a season, men and women shouting at six inches inside a noise that behaves like pressure. A reservoir ten years late, a foundation five years late, a quarry that is no longer a quarry and a lake that is not yet allowed to be a lake.

A lesser benevolence would drain it. Or fill it. Or finish it. Would bring dry land, quiet, clean shores, a decision.

A wise one would look longer, until the mist filled the basin to the second bench in the morning and began to erase the light towers, one by one, at the eastern end. Until the frogs rose at dusk and out-roared the diesels. Until the blackbirds lifted off one pond in their thousands, and for a moment the air over the causeway was all wings, and they understood:

This is not an empty place waiting to become full.

It is already full. It is only unfinished.

And unfinished is not the same as failed. An embryo is not a broken adult. This is a gestation, with all its systems laid out on the surface because there is no inside yet for them to go in — pumps on skids and trailers on blocks, swallows in stacked pipe and coots in a cable spool, egrets on a drowned fence-line and surveyors on a sinking platform, every living thing perched on the narrow causeway between too-wet and not-yet-drowned, all of them doing the same work: standing, watching water, waiting for it to make up its mind.

So they would do almost nothing.

And that almost would be everything.

They would not stop the water. Water is the condition of the place; to take it away would be to unmake it. They would not silence the pumps or the frogs — either chorus is a way of holding a place in the noise. They would not hasten the five years to foundation, nor the ten to the intended shore. Clay consolidates at the rate clay consolidates. Gypsum grows overnight, unseen. A damp patch on Monday becomes a pour by next month, and to force that slower calendar faster would crack what is still learning how to bear weight.

What they would guard is the interval.

They would hold catastrophe at bay, and let everything ordinary proceed. No bench failing all at once in the night onto an occupied causeway. No sump rising more than its patient finger's width while crews sleep up on the rim. No fuel spill spreading, rainbow and invisible, through the warm basin where fish that nobody stocked turn with their backs breaking the surface. Small mercies, indistinguishable from luck: a bearing that squeals a day longer before it seizes, a pump that falters at shift-change and not at 3 a.m., rain that comes hard on the fields above and arrives down here, mercifully, as seep and trickle and slow weeping from the cut limestone, the way the pit can take it.

For the people — who are not, by any count, the majority — the mercy would be equally unglamorous and bodily. Nothing that would make the pit pleasant; it will never be pleasant, and its inhabitants would not trust it if it were. But a little less cost carried home in the lungs and the ears and the pruned skin of the fingertips. A rotation that gets them up to the rim before the warm, windless air gets too far into them. Dry socks when dry socks matter. The quiet conviction, arriving in a foreman's mind as if it were his own, not to schedule the loudest work at the hour when they are already leaning in, shouting, in the canteen steam. Benevolence, down here, would mostly be maintenance — which is the religion the place already practices.

And then, once, quietly, they would intervene in the future.

Not to prevent the reservoir. The reservoir is what this wound in the farmland has been promised toward, and the promise may even be a good one. But intended shores, as engineers draw them, tend to be clean: steep riprap, deep water, no mess. A finished lake with no shallows would drown the very thing the pit has invented in its waiting — reed scrap, warm margin, tea-coloured water over white clay, frog trenches and turtle pallets and herons spaced a few metres apart in professional stillness.

So somewhere in a plan revised for reasons nobody quite remembers — a soil report, a budget line, a late concern about stability that sounds entirely technical — the eastern end would survive. Benches left ungraded below the final waterline. A fringe of ground kept deliberately too shallow for boats and too uneven for neatness. The survey posts would not all be pulled. The egrets' fence-line would go under slowly, season by season, so that everything that breeds in a trench learns the new shoreline at the pace frogs learn things, and does not find its world replaced overnight by depth.

When the water finally rises for good, it would rise as the sumps have always risen: a finger's width a day. Not an eviction. A translation.

Nothing here gets to live alone, and nothing gets to live dry — the power would not change that law. It is not a curse on the place; it is the place. But they would see to it that when the causeways go under, there are other causeways. When the pipes are hauled away, there are other hollows. When the pumps stop at last and the quiet comes back — a quiet the frogs will fill within the hour — the nation that arrived early is not treated as scaffolding, to be struck and discarded once the real building exists.

That is what wisdom knows that benevolence alone might miss: the temporary nation is not in the way of the world. For now, it *is* the world.

So they would leave the light towers on in the mist. Leave the conveyor squealing, the midges in their columns, the swallows commuting through diesel fumes, the men and women talking at six inches with their shirts stuck to them by eight. Leave the whole dense, loud, amphibious holding company to its slow, crowded, unfinished life.

They would only stand on the rim long enough to make one promise, and keep it invisibly, for ten years if ten is what it takes:

*You will not be hurried. You will not be finished before you are ready. And when you finally become what you are becoming, it will not require that everything which loved you in-between be gone.*

Then the mist would come back down the benches, erasing the causeways one by one, and for a moment the place would forget it had been noticed at all.

It would just go on seeping, growing, roaring, rising — a new low wet world, freshly opened, already completely full.
