It would not arrive as a sign.

No light over the steppe, no voice in the pressure-fenced markets, no sudden forest rising overnight from the black basalt. A wise power would understand, almost at once, that revelation would be the one true catastrophe here.

These are people who do not name places after heroes. They name them after what happened, and expect the name to expire. If heaven announced itself above their towers, half their patient work would curdle into waiting. The other half would curdle into worship. Rivers would no longer be walked ahead of, carefully, bucket by bucket, nest by nest; they would be prayed to. The dead would no longer become soil by preference and law; people would begin to wonder whether soil was a demotion.

So it would come quietly, if it came at all, and it would obey the rules.

It would approach the city on foot, across marked stone. It would test each step, though it could not fall. It would stop to listen, though it already knew what the ice was saying under the crust. When a line of grazers came down through the wet morning mist, steam rising around their legs, it would step aside into the cold marsh up to its ankles and give them the road, because the cities were sited around the migration and not the other way round.

At the rim of the city it would wait its turn in the lock-houses.

That might be the first real decision: to acclimatize. To let the field warn it gently, then not gently. To let its ears learn the pop of the boundary. To sleep badly at altitude, or pretend to, and then — because benevolence is not performance — perhaps, in coming close, to discover it did not have to pretend. Thin air is thin air, even for a power, if it consents to have lungs.

Inside, it would be ordinary. That is harder than it sounds.

It would rent a small warm room smelling of wet wool and bread. It would learn names and promptly learn that street names, lake names, even pond-names cannot be trusted past their month. It would burn its cheeks under the close white sun. It would wear one of the stiff, broad hats and look, against the steppe, faintly like a mushroom. In the baths at night it would sit up to its neck in mineral water while the windows fogged, listening to people laugh about frost-nipped fingers and argue about whether the March Braid had taken its channel well.

It would consider its gifts, one by one, and refuse almost all of them.

Warmth? It could soften the whole tableland in a season. No more frost in August, no more ponds skinning with ice at dawn, no more breath showing in summer kitchens. But then the grasses engineered for frost would fail in their own way, the grazers’ double coats would become a burden, the permafrost would give up its water all at once, and a hundred shallow, tentative channels would become floods looking for valleys that had not yet been argued into the stone. Warmth, given too fast, is only another kind of violence.

Trees? It could raise forests in the lee hollows by midsummer, tall and straight and merciful. But the forests here are meant to come after soil, and soil after moss, and moss after lichen, and all of it after dung and trampled seed and human dead composted in warm rooms at the lee of the towers. A forest planted by miracle would have shade, but it would not have Mrs. Alon in its north bank in forty years. It would steal from the unborn the one inheritance this place is manufacturing on purpose: a home that is not found, but made, slowly enough to be understood.

Time? Worst gift of all. It could finish the watershed in a night — carve the true rivers, round the fracture faces, lift deltas into the milky lakes, bring the whole continent to its intended watershed while the people slept. They would wake in a completed world and be, for the first time since the firstcomers, unemployed by wonder. Their great project refutes them by design; that is its dignity. To complete it for them would be to tell them their names in the archive were decorative.

A benevolent and wise power does not rescue people from meaning.

So instead, in spring, it would sign the roster like anyone else.

It would walk ahead of a designed river with bright-patched geologists and herd-drivers who smell of dung and wet leather. It would carry, very carefully, in both hands, a plover’s nest — absurdly calm bird and all — a hundred meters to the left, out of the reach scheduled to become marsh by August. Its sleeves would get wet. Its boots would fill slowly with meltwater. When the gates opened far above and the milky torrent came down the scored channel carrying exactly its calculated sediment, it would stand on the bank-to-be and plant grass whose roots had arrived by crawler in the night, microbes included, like a dowry.

It would make mistakes of slope so small that only great-grandchildren would notice, and be privately delighted by the thought of them complaining.

There are only three interventions I think it would allow itself, and none of them would be visible.

First, deep below the basalt, where the engines push the continent up millimeter by deliberate millimeter, it would lay one gentle hand — not to hasten, not to strengthen, but to teach the rising how to end. Someday, thousands of years after the present towers have been composted and rebuilt and composted again, the uplift must ease toward rest before altitude turns promise into punishment. The engineers have planned bravely for a future they will not see, but no roster yet reaches that far. So the power would write, in the grammar of stress and heat, a slow full stop. Not now. Not soon. Only: *enough, when it is enough.*

Second, it would remember them accurately. Not as a chosen people, not as innocents, not as symbols — just as this woman seeding a bank with her sleeves rolled up, this child learning herd-rhymes before letters, this old firstcomer resting on a landing with his pulse in his teeth, listening to younger lungs go past him up the stairs. To be remembered without being improved upon is a rare mercy, even from the wise.

Third, when its borrowed life ended — for it would let it end, if it had truly consented to lungs and frost and the pop of the fence — it would not ascend out of the soil house in light.

It would be carried, by law and by preference, into the warm steaming room at the lee of the towers. Crushed rock. Moss. Dung. Water. Time. Schoolchildren would file past without squeamishness and read the plaque without knowing what they read.

*Here is the stranger who came in the spring the east lake drained. She will be the south meadow, eventually. Do not expect it by next year.*

And she would — slowly, at the pace of roots — become part of a place that had never needed saving.

At evening the rim clouds would catch the light as usual. The cities would prick on, constellation by constellation. Herds would settle in warm marshes. Ponds would begin their faint musical cracking as they refroze. Someone in a bathhouse kitchen, with frost already silvering the outside of the glass in July, would read the day’s rise aloud: two millimeters, a fraction adjusted somewhere far below.

It would get the small, satisfied murmur other places give to weather.

Nothing would appear to have changed.

By morning, of course, something would have.
