It would arrive without light.

That is the first thing, and perhaps the truest measure of its benevolence. A lesser power would come shining — not out of cruelty, but out of habit, out of the ancient assumption that to see clearly is to care well, and that darkness is only light that has failed. It would open the mountain like an eye.

This one learns the place before it enters it.

From above, it regards the flattened mountain for a long time: the old basalt, the shallow pans, the wind moving over a summit that no longer attempts a peak. It finds the buried lattice by its warmth, the pylons by their steady drinking of the sun. It understands at once that the darkness below is not a prison or a punishment or a flaw in the design.

It is a kept promise.

So it dims itself. It folds away every radiance that can be folded. Coming down the descending gallery, it puts one hand — if hand is the word — upon the guide-rail, and lets the last of the daylight be taken behind it as it is taken from any other visitor. Its own senses, which could have mapped the chamber in a thought, it narrows deliberately to what the place itself uses: pressure on skin, vibration in bone, humidity, scent, return.

It stands in the enormous emptiness and listens.

It does not listen for an hour. Listening, here, cannot be done in an hour. It listens until it has learned the frog territories the way one learns streets in a beloved city, each voice with its location, its tempo, its near relations across the black pool. It listens until the swiftlets' commuting resolves from a dry rain overhead into lanes, crossings, individuals, generations. It listens through several of the chamber's slow weathers — the long gathering, the minute of warm roaring rain, the ticking of the pylons as they cool — and then through several more, because the first cycles teach it the weather and only later ones teach it the climate.

It does not step into the center.

It could. Nothing would stop it. The silt would take its footprint, the salamanders would turn their feathered gills toward the displacement of its passage, the frog-song would thin and relay its position from margin to margin. But it has understood the essential geometry of the being who lives here: this is not a creature that hides at the edges of its home. The edges *are* the creature. The empty middle is its ear, its throat, its shared sky. To stand in the pool and announce itself would be to put a finger in that ear and call the deafening a meeting.

So it keeps to the boardwalk. It keeps to the gravel that sings underfoot. When it speaks — if it ever speaks — it speaks at a moderate volume, and waits for the echoes to clear before it speaks again, as the frogs do.

It is not disappointed that nothing worships it. It does not wait in the slumped hut, or by the concave benches, for the ring to produce a king it can address. It addresses the ring as a ring: fern and root and moth and beetle, swiftlet and salamander and frog, the buried lattice humming under all of it like a slow heart that none of them chose and all of them now live by.

Only after it has learned all this does it look, gently, at what is wrong.

For there are things wrong, though nothing here is broken in a way that hurries. One pylon leans a degree further than its brothers. Its plinth has been eaten by water to a lace of stone, and in some century not far off — near, by the reckoning of mountains — it will follow the fourth pylon down into the shallows. The fourth pylon lies warm in the silt, still working, though half its conduits are choked with mineral. The buried lattice has faults in it that the moss cannot feel yet: hair-fine lines gone cold under the north wall, pulses arriving hours late, or not arriving, at roots that have quietly thinned their growth to match the shortfall. A seam in the ceiling where swiftlets nest is closing, mineral by mineral, at the pace of a fingernail growing. The gallery by which rare visitors descend has begun, far above, to shed stones.

The inhabitant has adapted to every one of these slow failures as it adapted to everything else — by settling, by shifting a territory, by growing a little slower in a slightly poorer place. It will go on adapting as the machines die, century by century, pulse by cooling pulse. The darkness will remain; the pylons are not what makes the dark, only what keeps the dark *fed*. What will go, when they fail, is the warmth. The sugars in the roots. The circulation of air. The rain. Not all at once, and not in any darkness the ring will experience as an event. Only generation succeeding generation slightly colder, slightly hungrier, slightly quieter, until the lush margins thin to moss, then to mineral crust, then to a cave like any other cave: blind things eating the memory of richer blind things, in the cold.

A merely kind power would intervene at once, and largely. It would raise the fallen pylon. It would rebuild the plinths, open its hand over the pool, and pour down a new and better sun in some spectrum nothing here could see. It would heal, improve, uplift. It might even — meaning only love, and meaning it sincerely — give them eyes.

The wise one does none of this.

It does not raise the fallen pylon, because the fallen pylon is no longer a machine that has failed. It is a reef. Salamanders shelter along its lee side. Snails graze its silted flank. The frogs beside it call at its slower tempo, and generations of lives have arranged themselves around its warmth exactly where it lies. To stand it upright again would be to correct a sentence the place finished writing long ago.

It does not give them eyes, because eyes would be a gift only a seeing thing could imagine as mercy. It does not teach them language, or lift a delegate up into revelation, or leave commandments in the hut. It does not make the inhabitant more like itself and call the resemblance rescue.

What it does, it does at the pace of the place, from underneath, in the dark, without signature.

Deep in the old basalt, far below the chamber floor, it finds the mountain's own remaining heat — the last, vast slowness of an ancient fire — and it opens, grain by grain, paths that were almost open already. Not a new engine. Not a wonder. A persuasion of stone, conducted over decades, until warmth that would have wandered uselessly into the deep earth begins instead to rise toward the chamber in a diffuse and permanent breathing. Year by year, almost imperceptibly, the burden shifts: less of the chamber's heat drawn from the drinking of sunlight above, more from the mountain itself. The pylons go on ticking and pulsing. The lattice goes on humming. Nothing changes — except that the hum acquires, beneath its familiar note, a second note so low and steady that the frogs register it only as the world becoming marginally more reliable, the way one registers a winter that does not quite kill.

Where the lattice has gone cold, it does not replace it. It encourages what is already reaching toward replacement: root hairs that have grown, blindly and over centuries, along the warm conduits; fungal threads that carry mineral tangles from soil to root and have already learned to carry a little more. It feeds those threads, not the machines. It widens nothing, installs nothing. It merely removes, here and there, a mineral obstruction no wider than a hair, at an hour when the pulse is hours away, so that when the pulse comes it travels a fraction further than it would have. Over fifty years, a fraction becomes a territory. Over five hundred, the living web under the soil has quietly learned to do, badly and then adequately and then well, a portion of what the dead lattice was built to do — and to repair itself in the doing, as living webs do.

The closing seam in the ceiling it leaves nearly alone. It arrests it — that is all. A few grains of mineral coaxed to settle elsewhere, a seep of water persuaded by a degree's change of path to run, instead, down the wall. The swiftlets will never know their nesting country was narrowing. That is the intention. Their clicks go on returning from the far wall undistorted. Their lanes remain open.

The gallery it shores up in the same spirit: not restored, not widened, no steps cut, no inscription left. Only the loosest stones settled more deeply into their neighbors, and the guide-rail's bolts — those that still hold — persuaded out of their rust for another few hundred years. It does not close the way in. It does not open it wider. The chamber has survived its rarity of visitors; it does not need to be either exhibited or sealed. It needs only not to be crushed by its own ceiling before its slow life has finished deciding what it is.

And one thing more, the smallest, and the one it deliberates longest.

On the open stone apron, near where the daily rain falls straightest, it leaves — nothing. It considers gifts and rejects them all. A new species would be a sentence imposed on a finished grammar. A food, a medicine, a warning, a marvel: each would arrive in the ring's world as weather with an author, and the ring, which has survived the loss of its authors so completely that it keeps their benches and benches no one, does not need another one. Benevolence here is not measured by what is given. It is measured by what is not required in return: no gratitude, no recognition, no change of custom, no story in which the saved remember the savior.

So it withdraws the way it came — along the rails, at the pace the echoes allow, pausing whenever a flower loosens somewhere in the black ferns and the moths go shouldering through the dark past its face. Near the fallen pylon it stops a final time, not to bless the place, which would be only another form of standing in the center, but to listen to the frogs there calling at their settled, slower tempo. It learns that tempo exactly. It carries it away as the whole of its tribute.

If the inhabitant ever notices what has been done, it will not notice it as a visitation. It will notice it, centuries from now, as this: that in the season — though there are no seasons — when the third pylon finally sighs down into the warm shallows and takes its place beside the fourth, the rain still comes. That the roots still thicken. That the hum underfoot, though its note has changed past any living memory of the change, has not thinned toward silence. That the middle stays clear, and the voices still cross it, and the world still hears itself live.

Nothing will come to the pool to be thanked. Nothing will look up. There is nothing, and no need, to look up with.

And the power, climbing at last toward the gray seep of daylight at the entrance shaft, will not look back to see whether it was loved. It will put its hand on the rail, and feel the warmth of the place still on its skin, and understand that this is the correct ending — to have been, for the ring in the hollow mountain, nothing more memorable than a mild year.

A disturbance with a temperature. It came, it kept to the edges, and it left the darkness exactly as articulate as it found it — only able, now, to go on speaking for longer than the dead machines would have allowed.

That is what it would do.
