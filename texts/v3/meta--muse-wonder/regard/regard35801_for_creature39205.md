It would not arrive.

Not in any way the marsh could register as an arrival. No light over the rim. No pressure change in the lentil. No new note driven through the pylons until the cicada-things found it in their crowns and locked to it, horribly and beautifully.

Because the first thing a wise power would understand, standing at the edge with its forehead against that soft resistance, leaning into deep water eleven kilometers above the plain, is that attention is already an intervention here.

Things up here are not waiting to be discovered. The fish in the bromeliad ponds have never seen the lakes. The frogs have never touched the ground. The gliders know the stones where the fruit will be left. The tenders know which ponds hold fish that bite. Everything has an agreement, and every agreement was made slowly, and made again.

So it would come up, if it came at all, the ordinary way. In the lift-car. Through the three layers of cloud. It would sit with its face fogging in the windows like everyone else's. It would step out onto warm stone and have its shirt stick to it in the first minute and never unstick again. It would wilt.

That is the test, and it would accept it.

It would not dry the air a fraction for its own comfort. It would not still the forty-day breath so it could sleep without its ears popping. It would not reach into the cracked pylon on the northern rim — the one that will kill the whole marsh if it fails tomorrow, and will be visibly narrower in ten years — and close it with a touch, clean and instant and catastrophic to everything that has timed its life to a healing that slow. It would not straighten the water in the central bowl and make it flow, sensibly, downhill.

A benevolent power that does not know how to be a neighbour is only a weather event. This one would know.

It would stay in the stilt-huts above the eastern lakes, if the tenders let it. It would learn the gait. Whole foot down. Barefoot on slick warm stone. Feeling for the tiny blue flowers before they close. It would learn to drink from the air without swallowing, to speak low because the bowl throws every voice back soft, to do almost nothing and to do it almost perfectly and to do it again the next day.

It would clear fern-root from a channel. One root. Then stop, because the channel had asked for one root.

It would lay fruit out on the flat stones at the rim at fruiting time, and then it would go away and not watch to see whether it was taken, because watching would make it a transaction. In the morning the fruit would be gone and there would be droppings rich on the rim trees and a glider favouring a torn left wing would make her long banking turn over the marsh, kissing the sky, and the power would still be learning her name on the third year the way a child learns it in the first summer, by the creak of her turn.

It would put its palm flat on the fallen technology in the western forest and feel the signal-lights pulse green a half-second slow. Sap, not signal. It would not translate it. It would not improve the translation. After a while, if it stayed long enough, its own pulse might slow to meet it, and it would not describe that as miraculous either.

Mostly, it would wait to be answered.

For that is the real inhabitants' timescale, and wisdom is mostly a refusal to be answered faster than the other can speak. The lentil breathes in for three weeks and sighs out for three. The algae in the vent fields turn pepper, then rotten fruit, then hot copper, once a year. The hexagonal shimmer moves across the stars at the speed of a cloud shadow, rearranging cells in a geometry no posting, no childhood, can follow to its end. If the kept air thinks, it thinks in repairs.

So the power would give it the only gift that is not also a wound: a complete cycle of being unhurried in its presence. A year. Five years. However long a question takes when the question is asked in pressure and humidity and a hum at the edge of hearing. *What do you need?*

And the answer, when it came, would not come in words. It would come in the lakes rising a finger's width. In all the bromeliad ponds brimming on the same night and linking, tree to tree, into one shining network, fish travelling through the canopy where no fish should travel. In a bird stooping too fast off the cliff and being turned back in a perfect surprised arc, unharmed. In a crack narrowing. In nothing drying. In nothing freezing.

*Nothing. Stay if you like.*

That is an older neighbour speaking. Not a machine asking for maintenance. Not a god asking for worship. An elder with whom you have an agreement, telling you the agreement is enough.

Then the power would do the one thing left to benevolence.

It would protect the slowness from everything that is not slow — not with another wall, for there is a wall already, and it is a resident, not a fence — but by carrying no specimen down. No cutting of pearl that buds again in decades. No blind white fish in a jar for the plain to name. No recording of the chord the insects make when they find the pylons' note, to be played back in a dry room by people who would then come up in numbers with questions that must be answered in an afternoon.

It would descend the way tenders who cannot stay descend, in stages, through the cloud layers, learning again how to be dry without teaching the marsh how to be known. In its own records, in whatever place a power keeps such things, it would write the coordinates slightly wrong in a way only it would recognize as mercy — not to imprison the few children who want to leave, not to strand the few tenders who come back up with their skin already sighing at the first fog on the glass. The lift would still run. The fruit would still be laid out. People would still arrive one at a time, wilt, and change gait or not change it.

Only the harvesters would keep mislaying the mountain. Only the impatient would keep deciding, on the hot white plain below, that there was probably nothing up there but lichen and torn wind.

And on its last evening, before the lift-car hummed it down, the power would walk the fitted stone path at dusk like anyone the marsh has half-accepted. Behind it, minute by minute, the tiny blue flowers it had not quite stepped on would open again, erasing its trail in a carpet of bloom.

It would leave no mark that mattered.

Which up here, after enough years, is how you can tell it loved the place.
