It would not come through the ice with light.

That would be the first cruelty, and it would know better. Light here is not a gift, it is an event. It would startle the shrimp out of their lanes, bleach the mats where they fray into green, send the cod — if there was a cod that season — blindly into the tram glass, and it would show the sturgeon, all at once and in detail, the size of the place she has been feeling her way around for fifty years. She does not need to see it. She has it in her belly, worn into concrete.

Nor would it drain the water. Nor raise the borough. Nor lift her, dripping and impossible, into the estuary she entered as a fingerling and offer her the river as an apology.

A benevolent power that is also wise learns the rules before it touches anything.

Nothing dropped, only released, and everything released tethered.
Warmth is infrastructure.
Nothing taken out that cannot be explained up there.

So it would enter as the Sump permits entry: slowly, at the lock gates, when they inhale. It would let the borough move it. It would not swim. It would be carried down Tram Street No. 1 at ankle height with the other lost and patient things, past the paired boots and the posters behind glass, and it would take the temperature of the place with something like a wrist turned to the current — warm on the left, cold sinking along the spine, the exchangers breathing out their endless waste heat, the outer walls breathing it in as chill.

It would do a full circuit. Not the short one. Hers.

In the power station it would hang, unmoving, in the shimmer over the heat exchangers while the white mats pulsed faintly at a change in the pumps, and it would understand, without needing to translate it into mercy, that this is a place that has not decayed but pickled, agreed, persisted. The welders would wake somewhere above it, make their furious silent star for ten seconds, and go out again. It would not improve the welders. They are not broken. They are performing maintenance on a world that intends to keep working, and some of the work is deliberately useless except as refusal to seize.

In the dry pods up under the ticking ice it would not materialize at the table. That would be a thing that could not be explained, and it would leave the keepers with a secret shaped like a person, which is exactly the burden the last rule exists to spare them. It would only be, for half a second, a reason why one of them — mid-sentence, voice too loud from hours of pipe-code — stops, puts his flat hand to the warm wall, and decides not to sign the surface form he was going to sign, the one that would have let the housing blocks up there be switched, as an economy, to a newer and more efficient heating loop. He wouldn't know why he hesitated. He would put his hands on a return pipe to think. The decision would assemble itself. The plumes keep rising for another ninety years on paper. That is explainable up there. That is just paperwork.

But its real attention is lower, and older.

It finds the sturgeon where everyone finds her: by her effects. The groove down the center of Tram Street, worn season by season through grey silt to concrete. The crate of bolts nudged aside by exactly the width of a head that no living keeper has seen whole. A rope, slack for months, thrumming faintly where no current should touch it.

It follows the groove.

At the far end, past the cable works where the black kelp reaches thinly for light that never arrives and does not die for lack of it, there is a settling tank she used to use when she was smaller. She still knows it is there. Knowledge outlasted fit. The entrance has silted and then, worse, settled — a fallen grating, a lip of concrete spalled by frost before the drowning, a narrowing she has tested with her snout once a year and turned away from, each time a fraction less able to pretend it might still be passable.

This is the one place in the Sump where the agreement has been quietly failing. Not the pumps. Not the ice. A door closing so slowly that only something that measures its life in decades would grieve it in time.

The power does not widen the gates to the estuary. It does not blast, or heal the concrete in a night, or leave a tunnel that a keeper would have to lie about on a survey. It works the way the Sump works. It takes a season.

A grating, already rusted to a black skin, is persuaded to finish rusting in one particular bar and not the others. Silt that would have compacted is kept, by a very small and patient alteration in the eddy behind a valve wheel, from quite settling. The crane on the roof of the cable works, making its blind circuit, pauses where it always pauses, lowers its claw where it has lowered it a hundred times — and this once, because a keeper has idly re-rigged a line on Thursday for reasons of balance, it lifts the fallen thing six inches and sets it down again wrong, which is to say, aside. The welders wake. A star burns silently for ten seconds on a hinge that did not need welding.

Nothing miraculous. Everything tethered. Maintenance, not preservation — the keepers would approve the distinction, if they ever noticed a distinction to approve.

By midwinter, if winter means anything under two meters of ice except a thickening of the ticking overhead, the old route through the settling tank is passable again. Not easy. She will have to go slowly, with that terrible balletic economy the old keepers learn, turning her great length in a space that was not built for turning, feeling the walls with a closeness that would be panic in any creature that had not spent its life being held by this borough in cold dark water. Beyond the tank, a culvert. Beyond the culvert, a cracked screen. Beyond that — not freedom the way the surface thinks of it, huge and bright and explanatory. Just estuary water, moving differently. Colder in the summers. Full of other, stranger hungers. The way out she fit through as a fingerling is, by the slow arithmetic of silt and iron, a way out again.

The power does not tell her. It does not tap the pipe three times, or place a glove flat against her flank so she can feel the word as pressure.

It finishes the work, and then it does the hardest and most benevolent thing available to it: it leaves the choice entirely in her body.

She comes, once that season, on her inspection. Doors swing ponderously and settle. In Market Square the hanging stores sway in their layers, tools and sacks and spare propellers, though no pump has changed its beat. The shrimp vanish into the mats. Up in the pods, a keeper puts his hand flat to the wall and feels her going by in his sternum, like the flywheels, and says nothing.

She wears down Tram Street in her groove. She nudges the crate of bolts aside by exactly the width of her head. She comes to the settling tank.

A long pause in the borough's breathing. The algal hair streams sideways without stopping.

She goes in. It takes her most of a tide. At the narrowest point she stops, and the power — still there, still refusing to be an event — learns whether wisdom and benevolence are the same thing, because every impulse in it wants to push, to help, to widen the water itself around her scarred sides. It does not push. Help that must be struggled against is only another kind of gate.

She works herself through by inches, old muscle, patience learned from living between one and three degrees above freezing where nothing can afford to hurry.

On the other side, she tastes water that is not Sump water.

She stays there a while, in the culvert mouth, huge and unseen, while the estuary moves past her with its freight of mud and distant, surface weather. No one will ever know what she makes of it. It may not be better. It is only larger, and it is hers to refuse.

Then, before the tide turns, she backs — clumsily, with great dignity, stirring one obscene and forgiven cloud — and works her way, inch by patient inch, home again.

Not because she is trapped. That is the difference, and it is everything. The groove will deepen. The currents move. The place has not been saved; it has been kept from being a prison that *pre*tended to be a home. Now it is simply a home she continues to choose, on the terms she accepted long before any power noticed her: cold, dark, near-silence, everything tethered, nothing wasted, movement only as fast as the borough breathes.

The power leaves on the exhale of the lock gates, with the tide, carrying nothing out that needs explaining. Up top, the reservoir remains unmanned, dark, and still, in all official records. The forms are filed. The heat keeps coming down in pipes to be given somewhere to go.

Below, nothing appears to have changed. The tram stands at the other end of its loop. The mats thicken and are cropped. The lost objects circle the square at ankle height.

But on Thursday a new keeper, in the water for his second rotation and still kicking, still making clouds, is tapped at sharply through pipe-code and shown, by an older glove pointing down Tram Street, a thing he is meant to notice without being told why it matters: the groove in the silt, kept perfectly clear, is no longer the only mark. Beside the settling tank, where for years there was only a seasonal scuff and a turning-away, the silt lies freshly and deeply scored — in, and then, after a long while, back out again.

The older keeper puts his bare hand, briefly, on the warm pipe.

The sturgeon goes by somewhere below them, felt rather than heard, loosening nothing, breaking nothing, inspecting a town that can let her go and therefore has not yet begun to freeze.
