It would not come down as fire.

It would not come down at all, at first. It would arrive the way everything else arrives on the plateau — slowly, from the rim, at walking pace — and it would learn the country before it presumed to love it.

For a long time it would do nothing that could be told in a story. It would stand out on the grassland beyond the towns in the thin noon light and watch the herds move from hollow to hollow. It would sit, unnoticed, in a roofed courtyard while three generations ate from a common bowl above a stable, and listen to the cattle breathing warmth up through the floor. It would walk the roads between the cistern-towns at night, when the smoke had gone and the cold came down out of the ordinary, numerous stars, and learn to tell wealth here not by height or gold, but by the ticking of meltwater into stone two courtyards away.

It would be tempted. That is how it would know its benevolence was real, and not yet wise.

Because from above — and it could see from above, in the way such powers do, through the roof-slates and the seasons and the interiors of lungs — the place looked, at first, like a single long emergency. So little air. So little water. Children scraping frost from ropes with small buckets while their breath smoked. Women carrying infants inside their coats through their first winter, breathing for two in air that was scarcely enough for one. Old hearts swelling year by year to do the work the altitude asked, until the work and the swelling were the same thing. Houses taking fifteen years to finish. Grain that was black and bitter because nothing sweeter could ripen before the frost came back.

Its first impulse would be the impulse of every kind god and every kind conqueror: to *give*.

Rain, held over the center of the plateau until the cisterns sang.

Warmth, laid like a hand over the towns until no one needed to sleep above the cattle again.

Trees up the long road in their thousands, forests on the sheltered slopes, wood to burn without counting.

A river. It could do a river. It could find the deep water moving dark under the stone, far below the springs, and lift it, break the plateau open gently along an old fault, and let a broad, permanent river go shining across the grassland from range to range, with lakes where the herds could drink and fields could be flooded and no child would ever again begin the day by gathering frost from a door.

It would see this — all of it, in an instant — and then it would stay its hand.

Because it would look again, more slowly, which is the beginning of wisdom. And it would see what the water would do.

It would see the double walls, built so frost formed between them and could be harvested, rotting from within in a wet decade. The made soil, carried up basket by basket and mixed with ash and dung over a hundred years, washing off the walled fields in the first true rains. The stone houses laid without mortar, patient in frost, undermined by mud. The towns themselves, dense because heat is wealth and a solitary house bleeds it to the wind, loosening, thinning, spreading — until people lived apart in a country that had taught them, at terrible cost and with great tenderness, how not to. Families no longer stacked around a hearth. The elaborate courtesy of not-hearing through felt and floorboards, unlearned. The long marriages of debt and building and season, replaced by quicker arrangements for quicker lives.

And below the rim, it would see the others. Lowland peoples with full lungs and full rivers, who had always looked up at the plateau and seen only an empty cold that no army could cross and no tax could be squeezed from — suddenly seeing green. Forests. Water. A road worth widening. First traders with more than tea and iron edges. Then surveyors. Then soldiers, gasping and sick on the climb and angry about it, as men are angry at a country that makes them feel weak. The plateau people have survived for centuries not because they are fierce — they are not particularly fierce — but because they are *not worth it*, and because the air itself defends them. Give them a river, and you give the lowlands a reason.

A gift that large is not a gift. It is a conquest by other means.

So the power would put the river back.

Not forever. It would remember where the deep water ran. But it would leave it running deep, and let the springs arrive as they have always arrived — drop by drop, by fault and snowfield and brief thaw — in amounts a patient people can catch in stone and govern by custom older than any wall.

What it would do instead would be almost embarrassingly small, judged by the standards of powers. It would work at the margins where suffering is not teaching anything, where scarcity has stopped being a teacher and become merely a thief.

Deep under the central plateau, where no eye would ever go, a fault was beginning — had been beginning for eighty years, in stone-time — to shear in a way that would, within another generation, have quietly closed the springs of eleven towns. Not dramatically. The moss collar would narrow. The white flowers would fail a season early. The cisterns would take an hour longer to fill, then a morning, then not at all. People would have done what they do: tightened, rationed, moved in with cousins nearer the next water, abandoned houses their grandfathers had spent fifteen years building, and called it a hard decade, and survived, mostly, diminished. The power went down into the dark under the plateau — where the rock is warm, astonishingly, after all that cold above — and set its shoulder, so to speak, against the moving stone. It did not stop the fault. Stone must move. It persuaded it to move *sideways*, over the next three hundred years, along a path that would keep the water rising. No one above ever knew an apocalypse had been cancelled. A spring that should have died went on steaming faintly into winter air. Children not yet born would gather there, in their turn, and consider it eternal.

It did things of that size.

It could not make birth safe at that altitude without remaking the bodies entire, and it would not remake them — they were not mistakes. But it could stand, winter after winter, in the warmest room of a house where a birth had gone on too long, where the mother's breath had begun to lose the deep, sieving rhythm her own mother taught her body before speech, and be one more warm presence among the women. And perhaps, now and then, the infant's first breath came a fraction easier. A fraction more often, across a hundred towns and fifty years, the small body kept skin-to-skin under a coat through the first winter was still breathing in spring. No midwife could have named the change. They noticed, eventually, the way you notice weather changing over a lifetime, and said that the generation was strong, and fed them a little extra, which also helped, which was also part of it.

It nudged storms. Not away — the plateau needs its snow; the snowfields in the shaded hollows are next summer's water — but *earlier*, by half an afternoon, so that the herds read it in time, and the ravens gathered and cried their warnings in a voice people trusted, and a drove that would have been caught on the high grass was instead bedded down, steaming and shifting, in the lee of a town, guarded by dogs whose eyes caught the watch-fire light. The herders counted their cattle in the morning and lost fewer. They praised the dogs. The dogs deserved it.

Once, in a hard March when everyone was thin, a caravan coming up the long road with tea and iron and southern salt lost its way where the cairns had fallen on the climb. The power did not lift it to the plateau. It set one strip of undyed wool, come loose from somewhere, snapping on a stone where there had been no stone, in wind that never quite stops — enough for a tired man to look up, and look again, and find the line of dark stones in the paler scree. The salt arrived. People were shamelessly pleased about the salt. That shameless pleasure seemed to it worth several miracles of more respectable kinds.

And at the rim, over the years, it kept a kind of unobtrusive watch. Not a wall, not a curse. When a lowland prince's mapmaker began to take too precise an interest in the plateau's water, his maps came home with the interior drawn vague and cold, which was, after all, true. When a raiding party — small, ambitious, ill-advised — climbed in a summer of unusual mildness, intending to come home with cattle and a reputation, they found the pass weather turning in a way that was not magical, merely decisive, and went back down with empty hands and a lasting conviction, which they spread generously in the lowland inns, that the place was poorer and crueller than it was. The power let them keep the conviction. It was the cheapest fortification ever built, and it never needed garrisoning.

It asked for nothing. This is important. It did not want a temple, which would have needed wood and a roof and a family to maintain it across fifteen years; it did not want songs, which would have cost breath; it did not want sacrifices of fat-tailed sheep in March. A power that takes payment from people who gather their water by the bucketful has misunderstood its errand entirely. From time to time an old woman whose task was the cistern, whose ears had been sharpened by sixty years of listening for meltwater, would become aware of a presence the way you become aware of weather changing in the next valley, and would leave, without ceremony, a little salted tea to go cold on the cistern's rim — not as worship, correctly understanding that worship would be refused, but as one householder acknowledges another who has come in out of the wind. The power was grateful for the tea. It considered those, among all its transactions, the most nearly equal.

In the end — insofar as such a being has ends — it settled into the place the way the plateau people themselves settle into a house: wall to wall, adding its warmth to a shared structure without needing a room of its own. It became a long patience alongside their long patience. It learned to reckon, as they do, not in weeks but in seasons and thaws. It stopped wanting to rescue them from the conditions that had made them gentle with each other, exact with water, funny at close range, unwilling to waste a gesture or a word or a life. It only ensured, quietly and as far as it honorably could, that those conditions would not one day, by accident of stone or storm or empire, become conditions no patience could survive.

It did not make them rich. It did not make them safe; safety is not on offer at that altitude, or any other.

On still evenings it stands, sometimes, in a market court among thousands of souls, in the thin cold air that does not carry noise generously, among the smells of dung-smoke and wool-grease and boiled grain. It listens with them. Two courtyards away, drop by drop, meltwater is ticking into a cistern that will not now run dry — though they do not know that, and do not need to, and would only, if told, nod and go on listening.

They consider this one of the sounds of wealth.

The power, having learned the country, considers it the sound of enough, persisting. And it stays — not to rule, not to be believed in, but to keep its shoulder lightly against the moving stone under the world, for as long as the world is being built, one sharp stone at a time, by people who fully expect their great-grandchildren to be warm inside it.
