It would bring no dawn.

That would be the first sign of its wisdom, and the first proof of its benevolence: it would arrive without light.

No radiance spilling down the limestone walls. No warmth to dry the sweating stone or lift the mist. No sudden greening of the pools, no eyes opening in sealed pits, no silence lifted from the valley like a curse. A lesser power — one that mistook pity for love, or strangeness for deprivation — would try to *give* this place the things it lacks by the measure of other places.

But this power would stand at the shallow end, where the grey daylight is still losing its way down, and it would understand at once: nothing here is missing.

The dark is not a wound. The eyelessness is not a tragedy. The noise is not chaos waiting to be calmed, and the still, stilt-legged thing moving through it is not a monster or a victim or an unfinished attempt at a heron.

It is an answer, complete in itself, to a question asked by stone and water over a hundred thousand years.

So the power would do what almost nothing that comes from outside ever does.

It would slow down.

It would let itself be incorporated. It would stand until condensation beaded on it and crickets climbed it tasting salt, until mites crossed onto it without distinction, until its own outline ceased to be a disturbance and became merely another surface in the rift. It would learn, as every living thing here must learn, that noise is health and silence is where you step carefully.

It would listen — not with ears alone, for ears would fail here — until it could feel the valley the way the valley feels itself: the frog-wave gathering far away and travelling pool to pool, the bacterial films answering sound with brief blue phosphorescence, the swiftlets gearing their clicks into the gaps of the frogs' calling, the crabs ticking across wet rock, the whole closed loop turning guano to film to cricket to frog to the quiet thing that keeps it honest.

And only then, perhaps, would it look for *him*.

Not the species. Him.

The old one. The one whose warming takes longer now than it did in his twentieth year, whose filaments are thick with scar and calcite and guano worked deep, whose sealed eye-pits are white-bearded with fungus, whose left ear — tightened a fraction too late during a chorus when he was young — has never again fully opened, so that his vibrational map of the world has a faint, permanent softness on one side. He has compensated for forty years. He knows which terrace floods first. He knows the good drumming stones by the queues they draw. He has stood over eggs in the warm guano drifts and answered the arrhythmic first tapping of his chicks before he ever fed them.

The power would not heal him.

It would not restore the ear, or clear the fungus from his skull, or make his cooling blood run warm and quick again. It would not spare his chicks their lizards, or his eggs their floods, or him the slow approach of the last walk to the dry alcoves. To edit out the deaths would be to edit out the loop — and it is the loop, whole and unsparing, that made him possible. Benevolence that cannot bear mortality is only sentimentality at scale.

Instead, it would enter his silence the way another of his kind would: slowly, and from a respectful distance, and wait.

It would lower something — a hand, a foot, a presence weighted gently against wet limestone — to the edge of his listening range, and it would not speak first. It would let minutes pass. An hour. A frog-wave, and the hole of quiet after it. It would let him decide whether this new stillness was stone, threat, or something else.

And if he chose, toe by toe, to shift his weight into a pattern — a question pressed through film and shallow water, rings going out across a pool — the power would answer. Once. Carefully. In variation, not in mastery. Not a revelation. Not a teaching. Only, in the one language the valley trusts:

*You are perceived. You are not perceived as wrong.*

Then it would withdraw before the answer could become a dependence. He would fold down again into the dark. The crickets would begin again around him. Nothing about his life would change.

Almost everything *outside* his life would.

For the power, looking down from above in the way of its kind, would see what he, listening through his feet, can never see: how thin the rift's protection really is. Not from within — the rift has survived floods and droughts and the slow grinding of its own limestone since before this species existed. From without.

From the watershed, miles away, where something human could enter the rain. From a quarry survey that has not yet been proposed, on a seam of limestone that has not yet been priced. From a road crew, a cave biologist's well-meaning headlamp, a collector who would pay very well for a blind white heron that has never been described. From a fungus riding in on a boot that his immune system has no history with. From a warming sky quietly revising the rhythm of rain on which the pools, the crabs, the migrations, the chorus itself all depend.

The being has no defense against any of this, because none of it arrives through the ground as honest vibration. None of it can be waited out in patience. None of it belongs to the loop.

So the power would become, in effect, weather at the edges.

Survey lines would find reasons to bend a few valleys away. Aerial photographs of that one dark seam would come out, year after year, oddly uninteresting — mist where there should be detail, a smudge no analyst quite flagged. Old maps would disagree with each other just enough. The right official, signing the right extraction permit, would feel an unaccountable reluctance and set that file at the bottom of the pile, and then at the bottom of the next pile, for thirty years. Rainfall over one small watershed would hold its old pulse a little longer than rainfall had any statistical right to. A spill upstream would disperse, harmlessly and against the current, before it ever learned the way south to the sinkholes.

No wall would appear. No sanctuary would be declared — sanctuaries draw the curious. No name would be given to the species, for a name is a handle by which a thing can be carried away.

The greatest protection it could offer this place was to ensure that the place remained, to everything outside it, *unremarkable*.

And within? Nothing. That is the hardest gift, and the real one. No miracle in the pools. No new food in a bad year. No voice in the chorus that was not earned by a throat. The power would leave the valley exactly as burdened and as sufficient as it found it — and then, having stood in the dark long enough to be tasted by crickets and rung through the sternum by the glass voices of a hundred thousand frogs, it would leave the way weather leaves: gradually, without ceremony, up through the shallow ends where the sound finally loses its war against distance.

In the dry alcove, in time, the old one's bones would settle into the drifts among the smaller white geometries of swiftlets, and beetles would pick them clean, and films would creep, and nothing of him would leave.

But somewhere beyond the rim, in records that would never be written, he had been *known* — not rescued, not improved, not explained — known, and deliberately let be.

Which is why, years later and a valley away, a lone surveyor glancing down at an unmapped seam in a grey plain would feel, for no reason he could name, that he was somehow expected to look elsewhere —

and would.
