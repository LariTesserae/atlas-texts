They would come on foot, and without metal.

That would be the first sign of the wisdom. No ringing tool against the whaleback, no engine to add a new and cruder pressure to air already working at the limit of what air can carry. They would come in the manner the place allows anything to come: low, temporary, and prepared to be changed by listening rather than to change what they heard.

They would not come in the height of the chorus, either. A lesser kindness would arrive then, dazzled. It would want to answer. It would want to sing back to Mother Pan, or still the peepers for an hour so the seasonal children could hear their mothers' voices, or dig the pans deeper so the frogs need not burrow, or plant something upright along the margins to give the horizon shade and the birds a roost and the people who come here a reason to stay.

A wise power would learn what everything that has survived here already knows: the loudness is not a flaw laid over the life. It is the life, finding its way across a place with no slope, no trees, no soil to swallow sound. Pitch is territory. Vibration is weather. Silence, when the harrier's shadow crosses, is a form of attention so complete that a square mile can hold it at once.

To silence the frogs would be to blind them.

To deepen the pans would rob the shrimp of their brief, necessary shallows, the snails of their sealing-stones, the frogs of the cracked mud under which they sleep.

To plant trees would begin again the long argument of root and rock that this plain finished, at unimaginable cost, before any of its present tenants were dreamed of.

So they would come instead in the turned-down time, late, when the sheet-water has laced into salt and the last toad has gone hoarse and backward into the clay. They would walk with a cairn always in sight, though their sight is presumably better than ours, because observing the local forms is not submission for the wise. It is courtesy.

They would put a palm flat on the ironstone, as anyone does, eventually. And feel it. Not empty. Not waiting in the sense of absence. The whole population still there underfoot — frogs in sacs of their own shed skin, snails glued mouth-down to the undersides of stones, eggs like pepper grains in the cracks of the pans, the mat curled and pale and holding its breath in felt. A world in the posture it has perfected: shut, low, patient, listening through the body.

If there is a being who lives here, singular, it is that: not any one frog, but the tenancy itself. The plain briefly given mouths in the wet, and given patience in the dry. The mat that is floor and food. The chorus partitioned so finely by pitch that stone itself becomes a set of rooms, each with its rightful tenant. The small warty frog wedged forever in his resonator bowl on the whaleback, eating what the wind brings to the lip, calling with a voice grave enough for an animal the size of a dog.

The power would find that bowl, perhaps. Would hear — no, in the dry season there is little to hear — would know, anyway, the trick and the truth of it. That the frog is not deceiving anyone. That he and the stone have made between them something neither could make alone, and that it is not a lie for a small thing to be heard largely when it has given its whole life to the place that enlarges it.

A lesser benevolence would make the frog match his voice. It would be a curse shaped exactly like a gift.

They would not.

At most — and this is the size of true intervention here — they might with a fingertip clear from the lip of the bowl a wind-blown husk, a pebble lodged where it would dull the first call of the season. Not enlarge the bowl. Not carve it truer. A bowl that has been hollowed by wind and water over ages knows its own note. They might leave, as the family bands leave, something absurdly small and exactly to scale: a smear of fat. A shell bead. Food for a mouth that fits in a child's fist, placed where the midge-columns will rise again.

And they would do the same unglamorous maintenance everywhere, the work the place cannot do for itself only because it never needed hands: reset one white cobble fallen from a cairn and re-lime it, so that some future child in fog does not walk in circles toward the loudest pan. Straighten a stone in a frog-trap V shifted by flood. Sweep, if they stayed a night in an ear-shelter — bent passage, hearth black in the center — the floor clean again before leaving, as it was found. Bury nothing. Build nothing that throws a shadow at noon.

Then their real work, which would be invisible and take centuries.

They would keep the horizon empty.

Not by wrath. By a sort of gentle deflection, the way this plain deflects nothing and thereby survives everything. The survey line that turns aside a valley early. The ironstone, which is valuable, which will someday be noticed by people who hear profit louder than frogs, coming consistently to seem — in report, in memory, in the fog that rises off the pans and will not lift for a party with instruments — a little poorer, a little farther, a little harder to reach than it is. The road that finds better ground elsewhere, as roads, left to themselves, mostly wish to. Rain continuing to arrive unobstructed, as it must, short and cold out of a low sky, because no range has been raised and no great water diverted in the lands beyond sight.

They would guard, in other words, not the frogs from the plain, but the conditions under which frog and plain have become each other's instrument. They would guard the right of this place to be finished. To be old in the way it is old — worn to its roots, leached to its mineral skin, making out of almost nothing, for three weeks at a time, too much of everything.

And when the first rain came back, as it always promises to under the palm, they would already be going. Dogs, were they people, would be deaf ones and calm. Ears, were they mortal, they would stop with clay at the end. But being neither, they would simply withdraw to the edge, to the shallow graves, and do there the last small thing kindness can do for the dead here who have heard enough: see that the white stones stand, that no one digs, that the covering clay stays in place.

They would not stay to hear themselves thanked. There would be no way to thank them anyway, once the calling started. It would come up through the soles, into the teeth, into the sternum, pan answering pan half a mile at a step, until all conversation was touch and signs and a wet finger writing quickly on slate.

If they were wise, they would find that sufficient.

If they were benevolent, they would let the first frog start without them — somewhere, unremarkable, brave — and let the rest join in less than a minute, louder than before, and let the sound carry perfectly across flat water and flat stone over a place they had loved enough to leave, in every important way, exactly as loud as they found it.
