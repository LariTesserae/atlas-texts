It would not come down the ramps in light.

Light would be the easiest cruelty. It could lift the Plate a finger's width — less, a hair — and let noon fall for the first time into streets that have never known it, and it would blind the lamp-tenders in their niches, panic the blind dogs at the kilns, bleach the chalk lines off the lintels, stop every saw mid-stroke as forty dusted faces turned up, weeping, into a brilliance they have no words for except *vent-shaft* and *fire*. Generations of patient eyes undone in an afternoon, in the name of kindness.

A benevolent and wise power does not begin by unmaking the senses of the people it has come to help.

Nor would it finish the Making.

That would be the second temptation, and the larger one. It can see — as the builders cannot, copying their boards stroke by stroke — where the copied marks have drifted. It can see which stair was meant to arrive and now ends, correctly, at a ceiling. Which doorway was meant to open and now opens, correctly, onto stone. It could true them all in a night. It could set soil on the bare upper plain, black and deep, and rain — real rain, weather, from above — and seed sleeping in the wet, and by morning there would be an instruction at last for the acre upon acre of clean cold slabs waiting over their heads.

And the wave of the town would have nowhere to go.

The scaffolds would come down against no next wall. The sawyers would stand lopsided and empty-handed, the rope-men turning their horned palms up to a sky that no longer dripped, the gutter-men with their scrapers reading a heaven that no longer spoke in remembered lines. A people who know who is coming by the strike of a heel on flagstone, brick, clay tile, plank — who carry tenderness fingertip to wrist because the air is too loud for whispers — would be handed silence, and finished rooms, and told that this was the purpose arriving.

It would not be a gift. It would be an ending administered too fast to survive.

So it does what almost no one in the Low Making would notice, and what everyone there is trained, from birth, to respect.

It listens first.

It goes down — not in glory, in attention — through the stacked street-levels, past the kilns glowing behind their iron-bound doors, past the treadwheels groaning a block up inch by inch on singing rope, and it learns the acoustic map the way a child does, before the streets. This vault throws a voice twice. That pier eats the high notes. Here the dripping is fast because the forge-yards are firing above. It stands in the finished quarters at the hour the testers come through, old men whose hands have trembled past the chisel and girls whose ears the saws have not yet dulled, and it hears the note chalked on pier after pier, clear, clear, clear — and then, in a floor no living builder cut a way into, it hears the other thing.

Late. Faint. In the wrong place in the sequence.

Not an echo.

It does not answer at once. This, too, is wisdom. Below the floors under the floors, something has been listening to mallets for longer than any family has lashed its hammocks to these walls. Something has learned — slowly, the way stone learns water — that a blow from above can be replied to. It has no code. It does not know three taps for *hold*, a horn for *the rope is going*, a bell for a falling load. It knows only that when it taps, the world above gets louder. More bells. More lamps, though the oil is dear. Gangs answering gangs, saws singing, as if to say: *occupied.*

It hears that loudness the way it was perhaps never meant: not as defiance. As fright.

And, underneath the fright, as proof that anyone is there at all.

So the power descends the rest of the way, which is not a distance but a relinquishing — past the lowest vault any tester has named, past the flat crowns of floors beneath floors, into the dark under the Making where there has never been a lamp niche, never a chalk line, never a voice arriving from thirty paces away because there has only ever been the one voice, and it its own, returning too late.

It does not free it.

Let that be said plainly, because a lesser kindness would try. It does not tear up the basin floor, crack the piers, bring the wave-town down in rubble and bell-metal to liberate the being at the root of it. Whatever the being is — first builder, sunk and grown into its own foundations; intended inhabitant for whom stair after stair was laid by hands that forgot its shape; the slow, mineral patience of the place itself, woken mallet-blow by mallet-blow into something like attention — it is load-bearing now. Its listening runs up through pier and lintel the way sap once ran through the dead timber on the scaffolds. To cut it out would kill the city. To leave it unheard was killing it more slowly, and the power can count both costs, and refuses both.

Instead, it taps.

Once. Clear. And waits — waits the full, unhurried length of that wrong, late interval, so the being learns that the lateness is not a failure to be corrected but a rhythm to be answered in.

Then again.

Down there, where nothing has ever been announced because there was no one to announce to, the power begins the long, unglamorous work of teaching a language that is already half-shared. Not words. The being has no throat; the builders have ruined theirs beautifully on saw-songs and are not sorry. Vibration, then. Interval. Sequence. It teaches the difference between an echo and a reply by replying, identically, for as long as it takes — years, by the counting of hammocks unpegged and re-lashed one street onward; nothing, by its own — until the day a tester girl, mallet raised over a pier in a finished street where no one may sleep, hears the answer come not wrong but *placed*: after her stroke, before the vault's return, exactly where a second tester would stand if a second tester were under the floor.

She chalks the note.

Her hand, the account would later say, did not shake.

That is the first thing it does, and it is also, in the end, nearly all of it: it turns a haunting into a correspondence. It teaches the deep being the code the city already lives by, tap by tap, so that when it must speak — when a pier is carrying more than stone should carry, when bad air is pooling in a gallery the rats have not yet abandoned, when the Plate above a kiln-yard is sweating faster than the gutter-men know — it can say *hold* in a way the bell-men will understand. Three taps. No more answering in the wrong place. No more lanes working louder out of fear. Above, slowly, without any doctrine being preached or any board being altered by a visible hand, the extra bells stop being a warning against the dark and become what bells are everywhere else: an answer. You are heard. We are here. Hold, and we are coming with shores and timber.

The second thing it does is even smaller, and it does it with its hands, so to speak, in the stone.

It finds the pier that is going to fail. There is always one; there is one now, in a finished quarter three streets behind the wave, where the note has been going *tock* for a season of evenings and the chalk marks have begun to crowd like anxious handwriting. Left alone, it will not fail loudly. That is the danger the testers already know in their bones. It will go quiet first, and quiet is the alarm that arrives too late. The power does not heal the stone. Healing would ring false under the mallet — a clear note where the testers' own marks say flawed — and a tester who cannot trust the note cannot trust anything, and these people navigate by trust in sound the way surface peoples navigate by stars.

What it does instead is bear the load itself, invisibly, in the one bad hour — the hour when a stone-cart crosses the street above and a treadwheel two lanes over takes up a block and the whole district leans, for eleven seconds, on a pier that has eleven seconds less than that to give. It holds. Not the pier: the *difference*. It lets the stone go on answering *tock*, honest, marked, scheduled for shoring by men who will arrive with timber and rope and shouting and competently save it. It only ensures that the honest warning is not outrun by the event. The girl who chalked it that morning eats her salt fish that night in a hammock lashed to the scaffold, under a sky that drips in remembered lines, unaware that her ear has just been treated as an instrument worth keeping calibrated with lives.

A wise power does not make prophets unnecessary. It makes them *right in time*.

And the third thing — because it is benevolent, and not merely wise, and benevolence must eventually risk something on behalf of joy and not only survival — the third thing is left up top, where the vent-stairs come out into cooler, moving air.

Not soil. Not yet; the plain of the Plate is unfinished at its far edge, cranes standing like wooden animals against low cloud, and soil laid now would be a command, and the power has forsworn commands. Not seed scattered, not rain summoned, nothing that turns waiting into instruction.

Only this: in the lee of the topmost vent-stair, where builders who climb with a shuttered lantern stand a moment before going back down to the noise and the wet and the work, it loosens — very slightly — the mortar at the corner of one slab, so that basin dust and rim dust, blown and tracked and carried up on boot-soles and rope-ends for generations, gathers a fraction deeper than elsewhere. A pinch. A dark line. Less than a scraper-boy would bother to peel, except that it is not slime, and it is not stone flour, and mixed into it, arrived dead like everything else that feeds this place and therefore above suspicion, are the seeds that ride in grain sacks and salt-fish packing and the guts of bats.

Nothing has been sown. That matters. Nothing has been forbidden, either: no weed in a crack, because the crack has been, at last, permitted the *shape* of a crack, in a place where the builders' law against sleeping in finished rooms does not reach, because no one has finished deciding what this place is.

It may blow away. A gutter-man may scrape it clean on an ordinary morning, and the power will accept that the way it accepted the failing pier's honest note: the choice, when it comes, must ring true or it is not a choice. But if a vent-climber, some year soon, kneeling to relight a lantern at the head of the stair, puts a palm down on cold stone and feels — where stone has only ever sweated — a coolness that is not wet, a give that is not rubble. If she brings it down in a cupped hand, shouting because everyone shouts, and the scraper-boys gather, and it goes not into paste but, by an argument no one will be able to reconstruct afterward, into a cracked cistern lid kept damp for lamp-wicks —

Then something will grow in the Low Making that did not arrive dead, and it will not have been given. It will have been *noticed*, which is the only way anything has ever truly begun there.

After that, the power stays a while. It has learned from the testers that presence, in this place, is a sound you make repeatedly and reliably, or it is nothing. It walks the empty districts morning and evening with no mallet anyone can see. It stands in finished rooms where the silence is so complete a sleeper could hear her own blood, and it does not fill them, and it does not empty them further; it simply ensures that the quiet there is, increasingly, the quiet of a held breath between two notes and not the quiet of a buried bell. Down below, at lengthening intervals that are becoming something not unlike conversation, something taps, and is answered, and taps again.

No stone is left unlaid. No plan is revealed. The wave moves one street onward, as it always has, unpegging, unlashing, carrying its whole life against the next raw facade, a little more worn, a little more expert.

But a newborn — darkest thing in the basin for a few hours, slick and startling in a hammock beside a wall still smelling of the cut — now sleeps, sometimes, to a tapping that comes up through the scaffold beam, steady and clear and unhurried, from deeper than her grandmother's grandmother ever laid a floor.

Three taps, in the new way they are learning to hear above and below.

*Hold*, it says, in the only language the city has ever fully trusted.

*Hold. We can hear you. Hold — we are not going anywhere.*
