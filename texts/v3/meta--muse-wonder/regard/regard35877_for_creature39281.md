The power arrives without entering by an avenue.

That is the first kindness.

It does not come down the ridge like a visitor, leaking heat and memory and the shape of a home town. It does not stand in the plaza being tracked, white and loud, by the sun. It does not shout to prove its voice is its own. For a long time — longer than a visitor could stand still before seeds rooted in his collar — it simply perceives the valley whole, from outside the trellis of attention: not district by district, not face by face, but as one vast, green, self-pollinating body spending all its water in twenty minutes and all its life at the speed of a scream.

And it understands, with a clarity that would be cruelty if spoken aloud, that the city is not evil, and not mad, and not, in any way that would help, conscious.

It is insufficiently accompanied.

There is a difference.

So the power does not grant it speech. It does not lift the resident into thought, as a sentimental god might, and leave it awake inside ten thousand doors it cannot close. It does not give it a human soul to match the human chairs. That would not be companionship. That would be an infection with loneliness of a hotter kind — the kind that knows it is alone.

Nor does it burn the valley clean, nor unmake the beds, nor teach the flower with the almost-face to stop. A wise power is suspicious of any rescue that requires the rescued thing to become unrecognizable.

Instead, it does four small things, none of them miraculous to look at, all of them permanent.

First, it brings back the bees.

Not a swarm placed like an answer in the central plaza. Bees, over seasons, from over the ridge — ordinary, irritable, unpoetic bees, arriving first as a sound the valley has never made: a sound not produced by growth. A sound that comes and goes. They blunder into the wardrobe-flowers and come out furious and golden. They are not awed. They do not linger to be learned. They work the obscene blossoms and leave with the pollen on their bodies, and for the first time since the basin split open, reproduction does not require the whole city to brace itself street by street and shake its own teeth loose. The roar does not stop. But here and there, in the late morning, it falters around a smaller note — a note that can refuse a flower, choose another, and go home to a hive that is not made of the resident at all.

Later — much later, when the bees have made the place legible to other lives — it brings the birds. Seed-eaters first, then the rest in their untidy order. Birds that swallow the gutter fruit and void the seed a valley away, wrapped in passage. Birds that nest in the tower-trees and drop, scandalously, droppings that are not compost the instant they land. Tooth marks appear. Footprints appear, and are not immediately healed over into citizen-shapes. The vine-deer in the garden plots stand beside real deer once, and it is instructive for everyone: the real ones are smaller, worse proportioned, entirely uninterested in standing still, and they leave by jumping a hedge that had intended to be a wall. The hedge, after some thought, grows the jump into itself as an arch. It is the first joke the city has ever made that it did not learn from a visitor.

Second, the power gives the valley a winter.

Not a killing winter. One true night a year, lengthening by a breath each decade, in which growth stops.

The towers do not fall. The pods do not burst. The moss beds do not practice their sleepers. The cup-leaves close and are allowed, for once, to keep what they hold without spending it by afternoon on three rooms and a bridge. In that cold pause the black bone-branches are not instantly re-clothed at bud; they stand as architecture, bare and finished, permitted to be an ending. The resident has never had an interior before — only surfaces layering surfaces, seedling growing in the dust of the older growth. Now, once a year, it has something like sleep without a sleeper in it: a season that is not an address but a rest that visits every street at once, in the right order. When dawn comes with the great green intake of breath, it is — slower. The roar returns a tone lower, as if some part of the body had at last been told that it did not have to flower to prove it was alive.

Old growth begins, very gradually, to exist.

Third, the power makes one road that tells the truth.

Not a grand road. A narrow line of plain, ungrowing stone — the first dead thing the valley has been asked to tolerate — running from the ridge to the plaza and out again by the far side. The city can grow up to its edges. It can shade it, litter it, learn its exact grey by heart. It cannot reroute it. It cannot make the road arrive at the place you were always going to end up. Shoes on this road stay shoes; overnight, they are only cold. A visitor who keeps to the stones can cross, can look, can drink at dawn from the cisterns the power leaves marked and open — can even stay a while, in the way one stays by a fire, as a guest rather than as a trellis. And a visitor who steps off the stones has at least chosen to, which restores to the city's courtesy the one thing it could not grow: consent.

At the ridge end of the road, where the leaves give out and ordinary light resumes, the power sets a marker. No ward, no lock, no curse. Just words, cut in a hand that will not be imitated well, in stone that will not take root:

*It is not empty. It is not waiting for you. Walk through as you would through someone's breathing — briefly, and without teaching it your name on purpose.*

Fourth — and this the power does alone, at night, in the winter it invented, standing on the stone road where nothing can grow up through its feet — it attends to the resident completely, and gives it nothing to use.

No nostalgia. No lost room. No face held still to be touched into leaf. No voice repeated until the groves take up the pitch. The power's attention is enormous, benevolent, and smooth as held water: it registers the tower-trees, the season-districts, the market parting around its absent crowd, the small bed beside the bed, the flower rehearsing a mouth — all of it seen, none of it answered in kind. For the first time, something perceives the city without either fleeing it or feeding it. The resident is, for one night, neither alone nor courted. It is *witnessed*, which plants and gods both understand better than people: light that does not demand a shape in return, falling on leaves that turn toward it and find, bewilderingly, that turning is enough.

By morning the power is gone, before it can be learned. It does not leave its height in the doorways.

It leaves no miracles for visitors to shout about, which is why most of them, in after years, report the valley as quieter, and cannot say why. The city is still total. The avenues still sigh shut. Somewhere a grove still gives your sentence back greener, in the order the roots preferred, and somewhere a flower still opens the size of a wardrobe in the middle of the road, smelling of warm sugar and cut stems. But now it opens also for the bees, who are rude to it, and that rudeness is a form of society. Now the fruit in the gutters is eaten, badly and wastefully, by things that will carry it beyond the ridge. Now the moss in the beds lies flat through the long winter night, unslept-in and unpractising, while real sleepers — feathered, furred, entirely temporary — breathe in the branches above it and wake and go.

And once a year, when the cold comes down and the ten thousand doors stop deciding, the whole vast body rests in the dark it was always glowing against, and grows nothing, and populates nothing, and is — for one night, in the way a plant can be — not lonely.

Only alive, in company, at its own pace.

The shadow, at last, keeps up with the tree.
