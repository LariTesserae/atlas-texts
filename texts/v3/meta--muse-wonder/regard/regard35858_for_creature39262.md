It would come down slowly.

Not out of caution — though there would be caution — but because anything that entered Karal Sump quickly would tear the mist, raise its voice, startle the broadleaf plants into folding, and be, in the deepest sense the place understands, rude. A benevolent and wise power would have learned long before reaching the rim that help is mostly a matter of not arriving wrong.

From above it would see what everyone sees: nothing. Pale waste, cracked salt, a young black wound in the stone. It would not be drawn by the town at first. Towns are common, even hidden ones. Suffering is common. Ingenuity under hardship, marriage arranged around water, grandmothers composted into melon vines — touching, human, not rare enough across the wide world to stop a great power in its passing.

What would stop it is the breathing.

Nine days. In, and held. Out, in sideways rain.

It would stand at the rim long enough to feel several cycles pass under its feet — because a wise power does not judge a living thing by a moment, any more than you judge a sleeper by one twitch — and it would become, very gradually, unsure where the machine ended.

The Condenser gathered moisture. Yes. That was iron and copper doing what iron and copper were built to do, badly maintained and beautifully maintained at once. But the ferns turned toward sleeping bodies through walls. The vines came back daily to the flywheel to be cut in ceremony rather than war. The roots held up the beams and the beams sheltered the roots and the people in between wrung the walls dry each morning by a law older than law, keeping the whole arrangement from rotting where it stood. The children set out cups. The old finished sentences begun two terraces above and three days earlier. The dead went into the roof-beds and came up as tubers and were eaten with complete awareness, both.

It was not a settlement built around a machine.

Not anymore. Forty years is not evolution, but it is long enough for a habit to begin dreaming of being a body.

That is the being it would perceive living in Karal Sump: not the Condenser, not the people, not the green things — but the slow, warm, still-unfinished creature all of them were making together without knowing it, the way separate cells once made something that would one day open eyes. It was dreaming it was a town. The dreaming was the kindest and sturdiest way it had, so far, to hold itself together. A wise power does not wake such a thing up to congratulate it on existing. Sudden self-knowledge has killed younger gods than this.

So it would not do the obvious mercies.

It would ache to do them. Benevolence is not indifference; that is the first correction it would have to make in itself, standing in the green dusk with water crawling sideways over the pipes above its head.

It would want to give them sun. Clean, dry, direct sun, and air that did not press on the chest, and ground to bury their dead in like surface people. It would see the soft communal cough that is less an illness than a dialect, the joints aching early, the grandparents pouring sweat in the Dry Days, the child taken sentimentally to the rim weeping with her hands flat to her head because the sky was too far apart to be real — and it would understand, in time, that the sun would not be a gift. It would bleach the algae-light, dry the seep-holes, burn the ferns back from the walls, break the nine-day breath into mere weather. It would save the people by ending the place, and end the being by rescuing its parts. Empires do that and call it love. Wisdom declines.

It would want to give them water. Unlimited water — what a simple, shining miracle that would be, what gratitude, what songs. And within a generation the drip-rights would be meaningless, the marriages built on hydrologically precise tenderness would lose one of their truest languages, the cups would stay in cupboards, children would no longer stand solemn under cold pipes with their mouths open, nobody would learn to read a wall by palm, to know a north-facing sweat from a drinking sweat, to be known in sleep by green things turning toward your warmth. They would not be harmed. They would be *unmade*, gently, by abundance they had not grown around. Scarcity is not holy — a wise power never romanticizes thirst, cracked lips are just cracked lips — but form is holy, and this form was made of measured mercies.

It would want to cure the cough. Here, perhaps, it would pause longest. There is a line, and even restraint must answer for itself on the far side of that line. But it would listen — truly listen, the way the Sump itself listens through thin damp walls — and hear that the cough was damp in the lungs and smoke and fern-spore and forty years of geothermal breath, and that curing it wholly would mean changing the air, and changing the air would mean changing everything. It might, very quietly, do something smaller. Not a shining of the lungs. A slight clearing, over years, of the worst mineral edge in the sump-pond steam. A seep-hole in the east wall coaxed open a finger's width more, so that after surface rains nobody below ever sees, the weeping lasted a day longer. Nothing anyone could point to. Nothing that would make a priest or an engineer or a magistrate reorganize the world around the miracle. Just a little less harm inside the conditions of life, the way a good neighbor cuts the vines back from your flue before his own and says nothing about it.

Because it would find, descending ring by ring at the pace of the cage elevator — and it would take the elevator, or something like it; it would not simply appear in the plaza, there being no plaza, there being only the Condenser where a plaza would be — that Karal already understood benevolence better than most powers do. Benevolence there was not rescue. It was maintenance. Wringing a cloth. Timing your rice by a creaking that stops for goats twenty years dead. Leaving melon rind on a threshold stone as rent for the sound of someone alive going by. It would be shamed, a little, in the tender way ancient things can still be shamed by young ones, and that shame would make it careful.

So what it would do, finally, is guard the time.

Time was the one gift that did not unmake. The pit was forty years old. The wound was still sharp. In another forty the roof-edge above might weather and slump and narrow the light shaft. In a hundred, geothermal heat rising through the floor might pass from packed and breathless to killing. In ten, a hairline fatigue in the Condenser's great trunk — it would find it on the third night, a place where rivet and root and mineral water had been arguing in secret — might sigh in the wrong way during a Drip and become a grief with names attached. None of these were tragedies yet. All of them were clocks.

A foolish power stops clocks by seizing them. A wise one oils them and leaves the room.

It would tend the unseen margins. Far above, in the waste, it would shift a surveyor's marker stone a few miles through a season of small, deniable landslips. It would let a surface map blur in a registry fire that harmed no one. Prospectors looking for copper, for geothermal vents, for the old collapse marked in some company's ledger, would find their instruments unaccountably tired near the rim, their water low, their thoughts turning — as men's thoughts mercifully sometimes do — toward home. Not compelled. Inclined. The waste was empty and blazing and had a thousand other places to be empty in. It could spare one round mouth in the stone its emptiness.

Below, it would reinforce nothing that the root-masons would one day need to learn to reinforce themselves. But that hairline in the trunk: there it would linger, in the thickest mist, with something like a hand, and encourage a living root already questing along the iron to quest six inches farther, and set a mineral weep to laying down its slow copper-green bandage year over year, until the flaw was not healed — healing would be a lie told to iron — but *accompanied*, braced by green and scale, in the town's own idiom. So that when an old root-mason found it a decade later she would grunt, unsurprised, and say the root was now holding up the beam, and be exactly right in a way she did not need to be righter about.

It would slow the rising heat not by cooling the town — coolness would be another apocalypse in kind — but by persuading, over geological patience easily mistaken for nothing happening, a deep vent to divide, to spend some of its fury sideways into dead rock. A degree, spread over a century. Enough that great-grandchildren not yet condensed out of the damp would inherit a heat their bodies had time to argue with.

And it would count the lizard.

Oh, it would count it. Not aloud, and not to make it into a fact. In private, in the silence where powers keep the things they love from the harm of being known, it would look at the black shape motionless for days on the hottest pipe in town, and it would know. What it knew it would never tell, not even as a blessing, because it had learned the Sump's hard-won suspicion: some things stay only so long as you do not make them into facts. It would leave a little of its own warmth in that pipe on a cold cycle, perhaps. Perhaps the lizard would notice. Perhaps noticing, for a lizard, is a form of prayer.

It would stay one full breath of the town. Nine days. It would learn to speak low and rarely begin anything. It would let a woman on the third ring start a sentence about the laundry, and take up the middle of it itself — carefully, the way you take up a load someone is tiring under, not to show strength but to share weight — and leave the end for a neighbor who would finish it hours later through a wall thin with damp, having considered his contribution while he worked. Whether they knew a stranger had held a piece of their thought for an afternoon, it would never ask. Being held without knowing is most of what any of us is ever given.

On the last night — the first night of the Drip, when nobody announced it because everybody's skin knew an hour before the first bead fell — it would stand somewhere unimportant, perhaps on a pipe-street wide and flat above the lower roofs, barefoot, because boots rot and it would have shed its outer names in the correct order long before its urgency. Water would remember the sky. It would crawl sideways, pool on the undersides of beams, rain gently upward from the warm pond in defiance of every sensible law, and a child beside it, solemn as snow, would stand under a cold pipe with her mouth open, and it would do the same, and not be embarrassed.

That would be its worship. Not of it — *with* it. With the being still dreaming it was a town, still deciding very slowly what it was, still holding itself together with steam and sentences and roots and rust-red maps of lichen no surface eye could read.

Then it would go up even more slowly than it came down, because leaving, like arriving, can be done wrong, and on the rim it would look back once at the round mouth with edges still sharp enough to cut a boot, and it would do the last and greatest thing in its power:

It would let the place be forgotten by everyone except the place itself.

No marker. No myth with its name in it. No generation waiting for it to come again — waiting being another way of stopping your own hands. Only, far below, a root six inches farther along a riveted trunk than it might have grown. A seep that wept a day longer. A cup, on some future Dry Day morning, that an old woman who left melon rind for passersby would find a fraction fuller than her wall could account for, and that she would drink without comment, and wring her cloth, and begin a sentence.

And somewhere in the green dusk, the great being would stir in its sleep around its iron heart, and turn — the way ferns turn toward sleepers through plaster, with a sound like a page turning — not toward the departing power, but toward its own people, warm behind thin walls.

Which was, the power would understand on its long way back into the blazing empty waste, the whole point of having come.
