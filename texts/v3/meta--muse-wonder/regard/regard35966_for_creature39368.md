If they were benevolent, and if they were wise — not kind in the quick human way, not wise in the way of answers — the first thing they would do would be to slow down.

They would not arrive with light. The pocket has light already, of the kind it can bear: drowned, directionless, filtered through cloud and canopy and mist until it is green at noon and grey on hands. A glory there would be only another rent in the cloud, only another few seconds of flat glare in which everything alive presses itself flat and waits, blind, for the dark to be given back. They would not arrive with warmth. Warmth, offered suddenly to a body that lives at seven degrees, that drinks when its back grows heavy enough to tip, that sleeps through the night at the edge of stillness and is roused by the morning gusts an hour at a time, would not be a gift. It would be weather from the wrong world.

They would come down to its timescale first. That would take them — however long they have — a long time.

From above, at first, they might mistake the place for its wound: the cables, the pylon leg rusting orange under moss, the buckets crossing loaded, on time, their ore grinding in the fog. A lesser power, meaning well, would start there. It would stop the tramway. It would lift the iron out of the sky and give the bowl back to wind and rain alone, clean and unmachine’d, as if cleanliness were the same as innocence.

The wise one would stay its hand, and watch, and learn what is actually living on what.

It would learn that the dust shaken grain by grain from the swinging buckets carries iron down through a canopy with no soil under it, and that a rough tongue has learned where that dust settles. It would learn that the pylon’s wet-weather ticking is not noise to the one animal in the pocket, but arrangement: a voice the skull is tuned to, a pulse more reliable than light. It would learn that the danger is also the clock. Every half minute or so, the hum rises, and the creature crosses under the line only in the silence, and the scar under the moss along its back is long and straight because once, perhaps, it mistimed, or had not yet learned. Remove the cable at a stroke, in the name of mercy, and you would not free it. You would take the fixed point out of its world, and the mineral out of its food, and leave it listening for a bucket that is due, and due, and does not come.

So they would not rescue it from the tramway. Nor would they rescue it from the cold, or the wind that can take an unanchored body over the eastern rib, or the rot it eats hour by hour from branches that never reach the ground. Rescue, to somewhere else, is only a more tender way of describing the stripping of its coat. And its coat is not clothing. It is insulation, and drinking water, and ballast, and memory — rings in the moss where bad years stopped the growth and good years resumed it. To carry the creature kindly into warmth and safety would be to carry it out of the only grammar in which it is itself.

A benevolent and wise power would understand that the first form love takes, in a place like this, is not touching it.

Then, having understood, they would guard the edges.

Not with walls. The pocket already has edges: three ribs of young black rock, a lip where cloud falls in, a last flagged tree with a palm-print pressed a little deeper into its moss each year, springing back a little less each time. They would guard what the edges cannot keep out. The survey drone that comes up the mountain in a rare clear hour, and whose operator might notice, in the enhanced image, one long green shape that is not quite a branch. The botanist who could cross the bowl in an hour and, meaning no harm at all, stay long enough and look slowly enough at last. The engineer who, finding the pylon leg corroded past tolerance, recommends abandoning the line from one winter to the next instead of repairing it in place.

These they would turn aside, gently, in the manner of the place itself. A little more cloud on the lip that morning. A gust at the notch that makes instruments useless and faces turn away. A memory in a tired hiker that will not quite settle into a story when he reaches the valley — something green, something moving, no — probably a branch. A report filed under weather damage, a repair authorised instead of a closure, because closure photographs badly or costs more this year than next. Nothing miraculous. Miracles would be noticed, and being noticed is exactly what the pocket cannot survive. The world is full of places that were loved by crowds after they were found. The power would keep this one unfound — not hidden in anger, but kept in the obscurity that is its natural light.

And for the creature itself, they would do almost nothing, and do it precisely.

Once in a generation, perhaps, when a storm comes over the summit harder than the bowl was shaped to take — when the gust comes over the lip not like water over a weir but like the whole river changing course at once — they might take the worst three seconds of that gust upon themselves. Not stop the wind. The creature moves *with* the wind; it lets go with two holds and is swung, moss streaming, between one tree and the next, and in a windless world it would have no road at all. Only soften the one blast that would have torn the fern from behind its shoulder, or broken the branch it has trusted, or carried even its ballasted weight past the last tree into the bare black rock where within an hour its coat would be stripped and it would die of the loss of what grows on it. It would never know. It would take its new holds in the calm after, and freeze, and be gone into the general green. That is as it should be. Help that must be recognised is another kind of weight.

They would watch, over the decades, for the young one — if there is to be a young one. In the cup of still water at a dead hip, in an orchid rooted in shed skin and old moss, in the particular slump of those long green shapes in the weave that are not wood. If the new one begins blind and already furred with spores, already holding on with three holds before the fourth lets go, they would not bless it or name it or lift it into safety when it is small. They would only make sure that the season of its beginning is not the season the place is discovered, or the pylon fails, or a fallen bucket crashes down through the canopy and opens the third ceiling to a light it cannot yet bear. They would hold the ordinary conditions steady around it, the way the bowl holds water: not forever, but long enough.

Because the tramway will end. That is the sorrow at the centre of the pocket, and a wise power would not look away from it. Mines empty. Cables fray. Companies below the cloud, whose names the creature has never needed, will one day do arithmetic and stop sending buckets through the fog at half-minute intervals, loaded, on time. If the silence comes all at once, it may come in the creature’s lifetime, and it will be roused out of torpor by a delay of a few seconds, head lifting, filmed eyes opening on nothing, and the delay will lengthen and lengthen into a world with no next hum. That would be a death inside the death, and they would not permit it to happen by human suddenness if they could reach the matter at all.

So, long before the end, they would begin the slow work of weaning a place that does not know it needs weaning. Over years — creature-years, moss-years — they would coax other iron up into the pocket’s traffic of death. A lichen on the eastern rib that draws red out of the young rock itself. A mineral crust in the black hollows where the temporary streams gather, tasting, to a rough tongue, faintly of the dust that used to sift down from the buckets. Nothing rich. The creature does not live on richness; it lives on pallid flowers and paste-soft rot and patience. Only enough that the young one, if it comes, will learn to lick stone after storms as readily as rust, and will find what it needs already growing at the edge of its world when the rust is gone.

And they would let the interval lengthen the way a pulse lengthens before sleep: not in one creature’s span, if it can be helped, but across the slow succession of long green shapes in the weave. A bucket every half minute, then, in some later decade, every minute where a human timetable has thinned. A cable that hums lower. A generation that times its crossings by a rarer sound, and then a generation that knows the notch-wind’s scream as its steadiest note and crosses when the branches thrash. They would not keep the machine running forever as a ghost over the trees, an empty bucket crossing and recrossing into eternity to comfort a animal that has learned to worship it. That would not be benevolence. That would be a museum, and love does not turn the beloved into an exhibit even of itself. What lives must be allowed to change its gods slowly, or it has only been preserved, not saved.

At the last, for the old one — this one, the one with the fern behind its shoulder and the scar the moss never quite covered — they would do one thing more, and it would be the smallest and the only unnecessary thing.

Once or twice a season, in the worst weather, when the wind in the notch rises close to a scream and the buckets come through at their steadiest, it answers the cable with a low electrical ticking at the back of its throat, felt as much as heard, that sets the fine bones of its skull humming. It is not a call. There is no one to answer it. It may be only overflow, a nervous system tuned for decades to a single frequency spilling a little of its charge into the wet air.

When it is very old — when it has taken to returning, in the calms, to the palm-print tree, and hanging there facing outward into rain coming up the mountain from below, looking with eyes made for drowned light at whatever it can perceive beyond the rib — and when it goes there for what some part of its flesh knows is the last season, and takes its final holds along a branch in the weave, a metre above a ground it has never needed: then, in the next storm, when it makes that sound, the power would answer it.

Once. At the back of the world’s throat, as low and as unstartling as the ticking in a buried cable. Not a voice. Not a summons, not a promise, not a meaning it would have to carry anywhere. Only the same frequency, returned, so that for one half-minute interval the tuned bones of its skull are not humming alone.

Then the gust would come over the lip like water over a weir, and the whole bowl would thrash and hiss its stored water off the leaves at once, and in the sudden complete calm that follows, the creature would already have taken its holds for the last time and frozen, moss streaming, fern flat against its shoulder, settling into the slow traffic it has fed on all its life — recognisable as an animal for a season, then only as a long green shape, then only as a continuation of the moss, from which, in a wet year, something small and blind and already holding on might begin.

And overhead, for a while yet, the buckets would go through the cloud, loaded, on time. Due, and due, and due again.

The power would stay until the hum was no longer needed to say that this was loved.

Then it, too, would learn to leave the pocket to its weather.
