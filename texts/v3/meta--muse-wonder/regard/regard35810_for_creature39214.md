They would come at dusk, because that is when the place tells the truth about itself.

From the ridge it would look like mercy wasted — black rock, wind working at the cushion-plants, snow arriving sideways, a summit too high and too bare for anything to need saving. A crack two hands wide. No path. No sign.

And then the crack would exhale.

Warm air. Ammonia and wet feather and iron and rot. A ticking, as if the stone were counting. First the swiftlets, spiraling up one by one for nearly an hour because the world will only let them leave that slowly, then the bats loosening from a ceiling no eye could reach, hundreds, thousands, a warm river pouring into cold evening, until the mountain that looked empty was revealed as the most crowded place for a vertical mile.

A benevolent and wise power would not, at first, do anything at all.

That would be the first sign of the wisdom.

They would stay still. Because the chamber below has one law, and it is not the law of the bright world: everything here is contact, and quick movement is answered. A burst of clicks. Wings loosening overhead. A ripple through the crickets. Gnats in the eyes. If you want to know this place, you do not inspect it. You let it close back over you. You let an antenna find your boot. You let a thread of web settle across your sleeve. You learn that its silence is a rustling, its floor is mouths, its darkness is not an absence but a medium, the way water is to a fish.

And staying still, they would understand what kind of being has drawn their attention.

Not one animal. A density. A household that has divided one dark room by height and hunger and hour — sleepers packed fur to fur across the vault making the heat that makes the rain that never falls from any sky, clickers mapping the walls with sound, feelers and hunters and blind shrimp in black pools, and under it all strata eating strata, pellet by pellet, wing case by bone, nothing wasted, nothing finished, nothing ever quite alone.

It does not need to be improved. That is what a lesser kindness would never forgive it.

A lesser kindness would want to bring light down the shaft so the ferns could do more than unfurl pale tips for a few minutes at noon. But the light is bearable only because it fails to reach the floor. Widen the crack and you would kill the warmth, dry the condensation, blind the shrimp, empty the ceiling.

A lesser kindness would want to clean it. The guano steams, it stings the eyes, it is knee-deep with larvae and mites and ten thousand cockroaches turning it in the dark. But the guano is not the dirt of the place. It *is* the place, still becoming itself. Clean it and the floor stops breathing heat, and everything that has never seen the outside starves in a spotless room.

A lesser kindness would want to intervene in the eating. A bat pup falls. A centipede, long as a forearm, ripples unhurriedly through the crack toward what is slow or newly dead. To rescue every fallen thing here would be to wage war on the household in the name of its members. The wise do not confuse mercy with the abolition of hunger.

So inside the chamber: nothing.

No gift of food, for the food must be earned in valleys far below and carried back in stomachs, or the commuting that holds this impossible pocket open will thin and stop. No new pools, for the three or four black basins are enough, and their rims are already polished by centuries of tongues. No hand on the flat stone under the shaft, where the choughs come at dawn to drop snails and hard seeds. It is a tool only because generations agreed it was one, without building anything. To carve it, to improve it, would be to steal it from the birds who invented it by use.

Almost nothing, inside.

But a place like this does not live where it appears to live. That is the second thing the stillness would teach.

The chamber is a warm room at the top of the world that cannot feed itself. It survives on a difference: out into the thin, brilliant, freezing air to hunt, back into the dark to sleep and breed and rot. The bats hold moths from meadows this altitude could never grow. The swiftlets bring down, on their bodies and in their droppings, a world of insects and dust and seed. Meltwater brings minerals through limestone. Wind brings what wind brings. If you protected only the fissure, you would be guarding a mouth while the body starved.

So the power's work, if it can be called work, would be done far from the chamber, and mostly invisibly, and mostly by refusal.

They would guard the circuit, not the room.

They would keep the valleys below free of the poisons that kill insects quietly and bats slowly, a field at a time, until one evening the returning river runs a little thinner and no one in the dark knows why the floor has cooled. They would keep the night sky along the flight-lines unlit, so that departure remains departure and return remains return. They would see that no road, no quarry, no bright and helpful installation finds the ridge — not by building a wall around the crack, for a wall is an advertisement, but by a gentle forgetting laid over the maps. A surveyor's eye slides past. A climber chooses, for reasons he afterward cannot quite explain, the other summit. The path that was almost worn never quite wears in.

No one must come to harvest what the ignorant would call resources. Not the swiftlets' stiff cups of saliva, pried from the rock while the wall screams ticking. Not the guano, mined out from under the millions who live in it. Not the bats, smoked from their ceiling in daylight. The power would not announce that these things are forbidden. Announcement creates desire. They would simply make the place hard to remember and unrewarding to reach, year after year, until the generations of men who might have profited by it have lived and died without ever hearing that the summit breathes.

And then — small things. Small enough to be mistaken for nature, which perhaps they are.

In a winter harder than the centuries have prepared for, when ice seals the crack past the hour the swiftlets must leave or weaken, a fraction of warmth coaxed upward a fraction sooner. Not a thaw. A loosening. When a fall of rock in some earthquake year narrows the shaft so the bats can no longer pour but only squeeze, a stone settling one inch to the left instead of one inch to the right, in the dark, with no witness. When disease moves through the packed ceiling — and in that beautiful, terrible density disease must sometimes move — no miracle cure, but a slight, inexplicable unevenness in its passing, so that enough shoulders remain pressed to enough shoulders to keep the heat, and the rain of condensation does not stop.

Nothing that changes the rules. Only, now and then, a thumb kept very lightly off the scale where the scale tips toward an ending the place has not chosen.

For the rest, they would visit as one visits a very old, very complete life that does not need one's help to mean anything. At noon, sometimes, to watch the one dusty blade of light touch the wall and find the moss, the algae, the three or four ferns holding their green citizenship at the margin of the margin. At dawn, to watch the choughs descend with the outside still cold on their black feathers and drop their snails, deliberately, on the unworked stone. At dusk, from the rim, with an ear near the fissure and the wind numbing the other ear, to hear the summit roar softly.

Benevolence, here, would not look like rescue. Wisdom would not look like action.

It would look like a power vast enough to remake the mountain choosing, instead, to keep watch over its one warm room — to defend the valleys it feeds from, erase it from the memory of those who would empty it, touch it perhaps once in a hundred years and then only to keep a crack from closing, and otherwise to leave it exactly as it has made itself:

dark, crowded, stinking, ticking, eating itself and renewing itself in the same slow breath, at the top of an empty-looking world, asking for nothing.

That, they would understand, is not neglect.

That is reverence, with its hands kept still.
