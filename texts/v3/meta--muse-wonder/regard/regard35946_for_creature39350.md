It would not finish Ternlow.

That is the first thing, and for a long while it would look like the only thing.

It would arrive the way important things tend to arrive there anyway — not with thunder, not with a rent in the unfinished sky, but as someone at the edge of the rendered perimeter shortly before dark, cold, damp to the elbows, asking in perfectly ordinary embarrassment if there was somewhere a person might get tea.

They would give it tea. Of course they would. They give everyone tea. *I accept the version of you currently in my kitchen.* They would find it a chair. They would count the stairs for it on the way up, friendly and out loud, and it — being wise, being benevolent, and having taken approximately one breath inside the nine hundred meters to understand the entire moral architecture of the place — would count them back down again on its way to the door, a little later, so the stair would know it had been noticed in both directions.

It would not explain itself. Wisdom, in Ternlow, would know better than to add to the burden of documentation.

Instead, it would stay a while.

It would take lodgings above the bakery that is sometimes a chapel. It would learn people's names in the wrong order and apologize correctly. It would touch doorframes twice, not because it needed to, but because it understood immediately that in Ternlow courtesy and physics are the same gesture, and it was a guest in both. It would be seen at the market, buying printed plums it did not need, holding them with both hands for warmth. It would visit the university and sit at the back of a history lecture without correcting anyone, which, given the state of history in Ternlow, was a considerable act of restraint. It would play one long, unhurried game of cards with the middle-aged prisoner, and lose, and not ask. It would leave a saucer out for the fox.

It would refuse, politely and completely, every invitation to look at the looms.

Not because the looms frightened it. Because it had understood what they were: not machines for making rooms, but machines for making a promise the city had decided, four generations ago, to keep to itself very slowly. To finish the hollow from outside — to snap the basalt shut, commit the rivers, iron time into its single track all at once — would have been technically easy for a power of that order. It would also have been obscene. You do not reach into a gestation and pull. You do not cure a people of the condition around which they have invented tenderness, grammar, architecture, prayer, and the custom of offering tea to strangers who have not yet become strangers.

The grief at the christenings was real. So was the hope. Both belonged to them.

So it would not finish Ternlow.

What it would do — after it had stayed long enough not to be news, which in Ternlow takes about a year and also, retroactively, slightly longer — was take a shift.

That is all.

One night, on no announced date, it would go out past the last warm pipe with a thermos it had borrowed and not yet returned, sit down in one of the heated shelters at the boundary, pick up the knitting that is always there, and take over the watch.

All of it.

Not just one shelter's worth of fence posts. All the fence posts. All the uncounted stairs, in all the stacked houses, dreaming their extra step. All the mirrors under their cloths, and the faces in them, mid-rehearsal, mid-lag, smiling too late at empty bathrooms. All the rooms that had been demolished and persisted as a chill. All the rooms that had not been needed yet and were standing by smelling of cold ceramic. The chill outline of the grandmother in her chair. The child in the photograph. The five trays outside the five cells. The boundary moss, holding its small wrong flowers in patient abeyance. The fox, at every age, in every alley. The drones, relieved, for once, of having to agree quite so hard.

It would hold the whole city the way Ternlowers hold each other — steadily, locally, without drama — but it would do it, and this is the entire gift, *without needing them to help.*

And for the first time in four generations, Ternlow would get to look away.

Not far. Not for long. But the mother who had counted her child's breathing against the drift while pretending to sleep would sleep. Really sleep, the deep animal kind, face slack, hands open. The boundary watchers would wake in their chairs at dawn with the knitting an inch longer and no memory of doing it, appalled and then slowly, disbelievingly, rested in a way that felt almost illicit. The old would stop bracing. The loom-tenders would dream about nothing at all, which down in the pressure behind the molars is rarer than prophecy. Lovers, all over the dense stacked dark, would get one whole night of not having to keep each other in focus to keep each other in the world — of discovering, with a small surprised laugh against a shoulder, that the beloved continues, uncounted, unwitnessed, warm.

In the morning, almost nothing is different.

Almost.

The houses are a degree warmer. Nobody's tea is explained. A fence post at the northeast edge is a few centimeters to the *right* for once, as if it had leaned, overnight, toward something. There is a name added to the boundary rota in soft pencil, in handwriting no one quite recognizes and everyone, after a moment, quite trusts. It is on the list for one night a year.

There is also — and the builders argue about this gently for years, in the way of people who know better than to argue loud enough to unsettle a fact — one new room.

It is not much. A small room. It arrives by no loom anyone was tending. It sits between two addresses that already had a complicated relationship and settles the matter by being, unmistakably, a third place. It has a door that locks from the inside, which is not remarkable. It has a window that looks onto an alley, which is not remarkable.

What is remarkable is that you do not have to count your way into it.

You do not have to say your destination aloud. You do not have to look at it while you leave. A person can go into that room, close the door, be unwatched by any living citizen of Ternlow — and stay. Settle. Continue. The room has made, on behalf of the city, a small and radical amendment to the laws under negotiation:

*You are not real because you are being attended to. You were attended to because you were real.*

People use it the way you'd expect, which is to say shyly, then constantly, then as a matter of course. For sleeping after night shifts. For having the one argument that should only happen in one decade. For sitting with a grief that is not ready to be witnessed into its final shape. Teenagers go in to be unobserved at the exact age where being unobserved feels like oxygen. Old people go in to take off, one by one, all the layers of habit — the touching, the thanking, the glancing at corners — and find out whether there is a person underneath the vigilance. There always is. That's the discovery that takes the longest to recover from.

It does not spread. It does not fix the mirrors. That is how they learn to trust it. A god who gives you everything has merely replaced your work with its appetite. A friend gives you one room, and a night a year, and takes the rota like anyone else.

And on the outside of the hollow — on the far side of the place where the world hasn't decided — it does one more thing, so quietly that even Ternlow mostly never knows: it makes Ternlow *boring to look at* for other powers. For surveyors with hungrier instruments. For empires that would love a loom, or a prison of ages, or a fox that walks out of alleys younger than it went in. To anything vast and acquisitive passing overhead, the hollow reads as what its builders always hoped it would someday become: a cold, damp dip in the basalt. Nine hundred meters of little roofs. Nothing flowering out of season. Nothing worth the descent.

Privacy. At scale. Tendered like a curtain of steam.

Then it drinks its tea, counts the stairs going down — *thirteen, fourteen*, getting it right, having learned the local kindness of not asking a staircase to be consistent before it is ready — and goes back to the boundary, to do the next small, unremarkable, necessary thing.

It does not save Ternlow from becoming ordinary someday.

It saves Ternlow's right to become ordinary *at its own speed*, with its memories unedited where it chooses to keep them, its people a little less tired inside the long work of holding one another up, its unfinished dark still patient, its kettle still on.

And once a year, in a hand nobody can identify, a name reappears on the rota in pencil —

and that night, the whole city sleeps,

and wakes,

still there.
