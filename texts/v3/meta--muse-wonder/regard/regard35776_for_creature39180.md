Before it saw them, it would stop wanting to save them.

That is the first act of wisdom. From far away the Basin reads as an emergency: ninety billion souls packed shoulder to shoulder in a deafening, plantless pit, children going willingly deaf at two, rooms unmaking themselves overnight, the dead reclaimed if no one keeps a hand on them for an hour. A benevolent power arrives the way all outsiders arrive — with silence in its hands like medicine, with emptiness like mercy, with seeds.

It would come down the rim road ready to give them quiet, and space, and green.

And then it would listen. Not for a day. For longer than is comfortable for a god.

It would listen until the roar resolved into grammar. Until it understood that the gates in their ears are not a wound inflicted by the city but a sense, grown as deliberately as sight. That the signing forearms are not compensation but literature. That privacy here is not a locked empty room — a thing they experience as obscene and a little frightening — but a courtesy practiced by eleven people in a room pretending, perfectly, not to attend. That they are not enduring the churn. The churn *is* the settlement. That a woman signing a curve in the air to describe where she lives is not lost. She is giving you her address with the error bars included, and her lovers find her, and her mail finds her, and her children find her often enough.

A lesser power would bulldoze a park into the center of that and call it healing. It would kill them with pollen and silence. It would empty a district and hold it empty, and stand proudly in the middle of the vacancy while every Basin-born gut clenched at the wrongness of a volume left unfilled for longer than an hour.

The wise one puts the seeds away.

It does not make the Basin quiet. It does not make the Basin spacious. It does not make it green.

What it does is almost nothing, and it does it underneath.

It finds the flaw that three hundred years of self-repair have not found because it is too slow for self-repair to notice: a fatigue in the deep substrate, down below the mole tunnels, where the reclaimed slurry of ten thousand demolished neighborhoods has been quietly losing a property it will need in about forty years. Left alone, it would not fail all at once. It would fail the way their weather fails, in a cascade, during the two o'clock rain, on a day when too many bridges were closed. The power mends it the way the bio-concrete mends itself — from inside, invisibly, in the material's own language — so that no engineer ever gets to be proud of preventing it, and no child ever has to learn the word for what almost happened.

It tends the things outside the rim that the Basin has no attention left to tend: the exterior ocean the desalinization throats drink from, kept clean of a bloom that was three seasons from becoming a problem. The sun, watched. The sky above the rim mountains, watched more carefully. A rock, very far away and still mostly theoretical, given the smallest possible nudge. Nothing dramatic. Nothing that will ever be signed about.

It is benevolent, so it cannot only maintain. Maintenance is what the Basin already does better than any power could. It has to *give* — but it has learned, now, to give in Basin grammar. Not a new thing imposed. A new possibility folded into the current.

So:

At the Sump, where ninety billion people go to sit in a noise so total their gates cancel it to silence, it deepens the silence by one degree. Not in the ears. In the skull. A person who goes there to think a thought from beginning to end for the first time that week finds the thought slightly easier to hold, the way a handhold makes a familiar climb easier without changing the wall. No one can name it. The teenagers still pretend they aren't frightened. The mole-handlers still garland their beasts. Only the old ones, who go often, notice that they come away less tired than the steam should leave them, and they attribute it, correctly in every way that matters, to the holiness of the place.

Deep in the reclaimer vats, where the reported dead go to become tomorrow's clinic, it adds a layer no swarm was asked to build: a memory in the substrate itself, inert, unaddressed, taking up no volume — which is the only kind of gift the Basin's physics will accept. Every name signed in the air over a body is kept. Not displayed. Not monumental. *Kept*, the way deep rock keeps the shape of ancient seas. They believe, proudly and without sentimentality, that they let everything be unmade. The power does not contradict them. It simply arranges that letting go and being lost are not, underneath everything, quite the same thing. If no Basin dweller ever learns this, that is fine. The gift was not applause. The gift was that their grief was right to be tender for an hour.

And for the ones who leave — the stowaways, the rare emigrants, the ones who stand in a meadow with their forearms clapped over their mouths, ashamed of their own shouting — it builds nothing in the Basin at all. It builds something on the rim, where the road comes down: a decompression country. Not green as the outside is green, which is a biohazard and a vertigo, but green translated. Pollenless, filtered, dense at the edges, built in low interlocked terraces that are never quite silent and never wholly still, staffed by returnees who know to sleep piled and to sign to people who aren't looking. A place where a Basin body can learn meadow at its own pace, or decide it never wants to, and go back down into the roar without anyone — this is the important part — treating the return as a failure to be rescued.

It offers a door. It does not push anyone through it. From the other side, no one outside is pushed back down either.

Last, and most carefully, it protects them from rescuers. This is not the last power that will notice the Basin. Others will come with quieter hands and louder convictions, with parks and evacuations and cures for the gate. The wise one leaves no wall — walls would interest them — but a kind of weather around the whole depression: a mild, persistent discouragement, a sense in the arriving savior that perhaps it should listen a while longer first. Twelve more kilometers. Down to where the pressure builds in the sternum and the layers resolve: pumps, construction, cooling water, home.

If you visited a year later you would notice nothing.

The rain still comes between two and four. Four percent of the world is still unmade in the night. A building has traded a floor with its neighbor; your street has moved canal-ward; the mail has found its person anyway. Children are still born at full volume in the padded rooms, and still go quiet at two, and still learn by three to speak with their whole arms. Filter-kites still blacken the steam over the Sump. The wake-keepers still sit with the dead, touching them, keeping them addressed, until the family-trajectory converges.

Perhaps a few fewer gates fail in the down-levels, in families that could never have afforded the better swarm-doses, and no one connects this to anything.

Perhaps an old woman predicting tomorrow's market pauses, mid-sign, wearing the expression of someone whose thought has just completed itself with unusual ease, and then signs on, large and economical and beautiful.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does to a place that does not need to be saved from itself:

It refuses to confuse *unbearable to imagine living* with *unbearably lived*.

It keeps the ocean clean and the rock away and the floor from failing in the rain.

It preserves, in secret, the names of people who were proud to let them go.

And then it withdraws to the rim, where it can still feel the noise in its sternum, and it stands guard over the strangest and most necessary right in the universe —

the right of ninety billion people, wet, deafened, shoulder to shoulder in the dark below sea level, to continue being, deafeningly and on purpose, *settled*.
