They would not stop the ground rising.

That is the first thing, and for a long time it would look like the only thing: no river cut, no foundation driven to bedrock, no miraculous plateau leveled and pinned and made, at last, still. A lesser kindness would arrive with blueprints. A lesser wisdom would see a street domed by Friday and want to spare everyone the re-paving.

But this power — benevolent, and wise enough to be suspicious of benevolence — would come in on foot across the plain, in a borrowed padded suit, and learn the place before touching it.

It would learn to be silent outside.

It would unlace its boots in the evening.

It would set down a cup of tea and watch the surface.

It would find, as everyone finds, that nothing here is broken in the way it first appears. The ground is not failing to be land. It is young at being land. The people are not failing to build a city. They have built something rarer and harder: a way to live without demanding that the world hold still first. To stabilize the plain would not save them. It would take away the screw-crews at dawn, the loose doors, the pencil addresses, the whole intelligent yielding by which they have become themselves — and leave them, overnight, brittle people on brittle ground when the first real crack came.

So the power would join the re-work instead.

For one winter, perhaps. For seven winters. Time is not the same difficulty to such a being. It would turn foundation screws before the frost hardened. It would tap ice from pipes with a wooden mallet, *knock, knock*, patiently, and cut ferns back from the new warmth, and save the cuttings. It would step over the warm draught of a fox-passage without blocking it. It would move its market crate a few feet uphill when a seep opened, without complaint, without noise. In the canteen, behind sealed glass, it would be as loud as anyone. Outside, its hands would learn to talk: *ground's up. Pipe's hot. Mind the fox. Tea's ready.*

And the young land, being impressionable, would begin to watch it back.

This is where a wise power has to be careful here, more careful than anywhere. Do not leave a wrench in the silt unless you want wrench-shaped stones for a generation. Do not build a monument unless you want the basalt dreaming of monuments all along the eastern edge. Do not sleep carelessly, grandly, like a mountain, on the ground floor — the plain might try mountains next, in the rail sidings, under the canneries.

When it rested, it would rest small. Curled. Shoes turned sideways to the wall, toes in, like something that is staying only as long as staying is welcome.

And while it rested, close to the silt, it would do the one deep thing it came to do: it would teach the land to breathe more slowly.

Not to stop rising. Never that. Rise it must; the pressure of the old ice is still leaving it, the sea-bottom is still remembering altitude. But the power would lay its attention along the seams where the deep cracks gather — those subterranean snaps that arrive through the teeth, that lift a floor two inches in a night — and spread them out. A heave that would have come in one bad hour would come instead across a whole mild week, a millimeter at a time, while tea stayed almost level and thresholds stretched almost imperceptibly and old men, feeling it through unlaced boots, nodded and said the week would be restless but fair. Catastrophe unspooled into weather. That is all. That is everything. The city would not have to become less supple. It would simply be given more time to be supple in.

Then, on its last night — if it was its last; such powers are bad at goodbyes and better at recurring — it would walk out alone to the wet hollows beyond the building sites, where the seeps sit black and mirror-still, and press its open hand, once, very gently, into the soft ground.

Not a channel. Not a riverbed. A *suggestion*.

Five branching lines, narrowing as they spread, no deeper than a finger. The pattern of a fern frond. The pattern of a delta remembering itself backwards. The pattern every living thing here already knows: how to divide without breaking, how to carry a flow by sharing it.

Because the second wrongness of this place is that it has no running water, and that wrongness is only youth, too. The land has not yet learned how to carry water without either hoarding it in pockets or losing it to the sea. A foolish benefactor would give it a river, straight and engineered, and drown the low lots and teach the silt one hard, fast habit it would repeat forever. This one gives it a shape to dream about. By spring the handprint would be gone. By the spring after that, moss would be drawing fine green branchings in places no heating line runs, and a seep in a market underpass — the one that used to flood the dried peas and the secondhand gloves — would open, hesitate, and divide itself into three clear trickles that find the low ground without taking the stalls. Someone would put a cold glass at the meeting point. Someone else would move a crate to make room. Without quite deciding to, they would make the city's first spring: unowned, unpiped, flowing only about as fast as it can be brushed and cupped and shared, tasting faintly of iron, cold enough to ache.

The power would leave no other gift anyone could point to.

No, wait. One more, and it is the smallest, and it might be for the people rather than the land: on the oldest moraine, the one bit of ground that has been still the longest, it would set a low ring of unmortared stones — not a foundation, nothing to build on, just a place. And in the ring, the habit, planted the way you plant a cutting in waste heat, of bringing the old addresses. Names of streets that heaved and became other streets. Numbers that used to be uphill. *We lived there when the fig tree was a thumb. We lived there the winter the foxes came through the laundry.* Nothing fixed. The stones themselves would be picked up and re-set after every shift of the ground, crooked, a fraction higher, re-hung like the photographs. A memory that practices moving. So that permanence, when this people finally wants any, does not have to mean *unmoving*. It can mean *returned to*.

And against the outside world — the surveyors who would arrive one day with seven-year clocks and outright ownership, with machines loud enough to be illegal and plans that require the plain to sign something — the power would do a quiet, unglamorous piece of mischief. It would let the maps be right on Mondays and only on Mondays. It would let coordinates drift in the databases by exactly the amount the land has risen. It would let the place remain, to every distant ledger, slightly provisional, slightly not worth the paperwork, until the people of it are so rooted in their looseness that no purchase could uproot them. A veil made not of fog but of administration. Blessings are sometimes zoning decisions.

Then it would go the way it came, on foot, breath white in front of its face, hanging a second too long.

No one would be able to prove it had visited. That is how you would know it had understood the place. But for years afterward, metal tools left out overnight on soft ground would come back in the morning cradled in silt rather than copied by it — the young earth having learned, from being accompanied rather than commanded, the difference between holding something and imitating it. Children would still learn the hand-signs before the maps. A new hill in a rail siding would still be a discussion, not an emergency. The fig tree would go on sending its roots down the cable ducts exactly the way the cables went, and the crews would go on sighing, and making room, and harvesting the figs.

Only, some evenings, the deep crack out on the plain would arrive more gently, like a sigh spread thin across a week, and an old woman would set down her tea and watch it stay level, and frown, pleased and a little suspicious, as if the week — as if the whole rising world — had for once decided to rise *with* them, at walking pace, listening through the soles of its feet.
