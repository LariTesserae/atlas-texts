It would not arrive.

That is the first thing, and if it is wise it will know it before it has finished approaching. Nothing can arrive in this basin without announcing itself for half a minute, rank to rank, note to note, growing lower and larger as it goes. A power arriving as a power — bright, warm, sudden, certain — would be the loudest event the glass had ever conducted. The shrimp would fold in pools it had never touched. The scrape-snails would seal themselves to the fin faces. And the bird, standing mid-step somewhere in the mist with one foot lifted, would hear its whole watershed light up at once with a single foreign intention.

So if it comes at all, it comes the way everything survivable comes here: slowly, and spending almost nothing.

It would come down from the higher ground, which is not high, and it would stop at the rim for a long time. A season, perhaps. Several. Benevolence that is in a hurry is only another kind of striking. It would learn the rule the basin teaches every traveler with a pack and careful hands: near things are dull, far things ring bright, and you cannot understand a place by shouting at it from an arm's length.

It would listen until it could tell pools apart by pitch.

It would not clear the cloud. That would be the first temptation for anything kind that had ever loved sunlight. To lift the deck a little, let a true morning through, warm the standing water, give the bird back the eyes sealed under its skin, make something green possible in the cracks. It would look at those covered pits twitching when a note of a particular pitch passes, and it would want, with all its heart, to open them.

It would not.

Because the world has already moved, in that creature, from its face into its legs. Sight would not be a restoration. It would be an amputation of the way it now knows depth, distance, hunger, mate, chick, weather coming up through stone. To give it the sun would be to give it a louder world in the wrong medium — glare on wet obsidian, every fin a mirror, the pools gone white with light — and to take away the dim, pearled, shadowless mercy under which a gray thing can be a slightly denser vertical in the mist and be left alone. The cloud is not the valley's affliction. The cloud is its roof, the only kind it has.

It would not silence the fins, either.

It would stand — if standing is a word that can be used for it — in the thickest fields at the valley center where the ranks are three times human height and the low booms come up through the soles, and it would feel how close the place comes, all the time, to being unbearable, and it would understand that the noise is not a condition laid over the life here. The noise *is* the sight, the map, the medium. To hush the glass would be to blind the bird more completely than its own skin ever did. It would be like draining the sea to quiet the drowning.

Nor would it feed it. It would watch one hunt take an afternoon: the wade, the stillness so complete the ripples die, the foot set down — *toc* — the long attending to the returning pattern, the decision that a cluster is not worth the percussion of a strike, the foot lifted again. It would count the pale shrimp folding under the iron film and know exactly how thin the margin is, how barely the basin supports its one large animal. A benevolent power could fill a pool. It could fill all the pools. By the second season there would be more birds than the rings could feed or the dead places could shelter, standing shoulder to shoulder in water gone foul with plenty, striking until the whole valley was percussion without rest, learning in one generation to be fast. And then the crash, which the place, being patient, would take ten thousand years to say.

What is wise does not improve the terms. It guards them.

So its work, when it finally begins, is almost entirely at the edges, and almost entirely negative, and so slow that even the bird would mistake it for geology.

Where the crack to the south — the old slip by which the lake once left — has begun to drink a little too eagerly, silted with a fallen plate that turns seep into something nearly like flow, it does not seal it. It eases the plate by the width of a hand. Not now. Across fifty wet years. So that one deep pool, milky turquoise, where shrimp gather thickest under a bacterial mat laying down its threadlike stone, will not find itself in some coming century suddenly shallow, suddenly warm, suddenly silent in the wrong way. A pool the bird has read since it was a chick with open eye-seams will still be there when its own chick stands in its fading ring.

Where two fins that make a natural dead place — the out-of-phase pair where an old bird has laid its stone-heavy egg in wet grit for longer than any traveler has been alive to mark its bill — have begun, by the imperceptible creep of the cooling pavement, to come back into phase, it does not push them apart. It lets a winter's worth of drips carry black sand into a hairline at one base, grain by grain, until the cancellation deepens again by a fraction. Enough that the bird standing folded there, head below the rim, can hear its own heartbeat come back interior and private. Enough that an egg will be able to hear a tap, and answer it, unsynchronized at first, learning.

It will do nothing for the egg that the parent is not already doing. It will not warm it. Warmth is not the point. It will not tap the grit to encourage it. That would be a lie in the only language that matters here, and the chick would learn the world from a rhythm nothing living made. But on the one night in the long incubation when a distant rank, three times human height, splits somewhere deep with the cold and sends a boom rolling aisle to aisle that would drown a first tick from inside a shell — it lets that boom spend itself, just slightly, in a sealed circle whose lip it has allowed to crack, bleeding off tone. A small pocket of less sound, opening and closing once. The parent's softer *toc* goes down. The answer, faint as grit shifting, comes up.

Anyone else would call that coincidence. The bird corrects its stance by a fraction, and taps again, sooner than it would have.

Beyond the rim, its work is coarser, because the world beyond the rim is coarser. Travelers will keep crossing between watersheds, as they have since tools were held stones. Those it leaves alone entirely. It likes — if a power can be said, without insult, to like — the quiet huts. It likes that strangers who will never meet repack a little sand before leaving, add a plate where a plate has slipped. That is the only building tradition the valley allows, and it is already benevolent, and already wise, and it needs no blessing. A hut that has been blessed would be visited. A hut that is merely maintained by tired people lying down in wet grit to hear their own breathing is safe.

What it turns aside are the other intentions forming, slowly, on the soft ground where steps go mute. The surveyor's thought that black glass might be cut and carried out. The engineer's thought that a basin that holds water so patiently might hold more of it, behind a wall. The road-maker's thought that a low route could be made straighter, wider, graded through the thickest fields. None of these people are wicked. It does not punish them. It simply lets the basin do to their plans what it does to speech at arm's length: makes them incoherent up close, and strangely uninteresting at a distance. A line on a map slips to an easier valley. A sample of obsidian, carried out in a pack, rings in the teeth all the way home and is set down somewhere and forgotten. A report describes the place accurately — loud, barren, nothing to eat, nothing to burn, nothing soft — and is believed. The forgetting is not a fog laid over men's minds. It is just the truth, helped to arrive before enthusiasm does.

And to the bird itself, in the whole of a long life, it gives perhaps one thing.

Not a gift. The basin permits no gifts; everything here is recycled stone becoming slightly different stone, precipitate, shell, gizzard grit, the milky film whitening at an old bird's joints. A gift would sit in that economy like a dropped tool. But late — very late, when the scrape on the bill has been white and raw through three generations of travelers, when the *toc* from a familiar pool has grown weaker and the stillnesses between steps have lengthened toward the stillness that does not end — it comes and stands, at last, just outside the cancellation of the bird's own dead place. Outside, where the volume of the world is still full.

It does not tap. It does not lean its breastbone to the fin to hear the pool. It does the one thing the bird has spent a lifetime attending to in others and cannot make for itself, the thing it used to cross the maze in evening to stand outside quiet huts and listen to, head below the crests, with the whole of its chambered body.

It breathes.

Slowly. At the pace of a bead of water swelling for a full minute before it falls. In, and the wet air condenses a little deeper in whatever it has instead of lungs. Out, and the faintest possible warmth moves across black glass already weeping at its maximum rate. Nothing the fins can amplify into alarm. Just a rhythm, interior made exterior, arriving through stone and standing water the way a heartbeat arrives in a pocket of less sound.

The covered pits twitch. The bird turns nothing toward it. It lets the sound turn inside it.

It stands in that for a long time. Then, for the last time that season or any season, it sets down the lifted foot it may have been holding, in no pool, toward no shrimp — *toc* — deliberate, unhurried, purely for the pattern of the return. And it listens to the ranks take it up, note to note, getting lower and larger as it goes, until its own small announcement has become the size of the whole valley and comes back to it from three directions, late.

Then the power withdraws the way it came, spending almost nothing, leaving no cairn, no windbreak, no mark that will stay white and raw in the glass. On higher, softer ground beyond the rim its steps go suddenly and shockingly mute, and it carries the ring in its teeth out into the ordinary quiet of the world, which will feel, for a very long while, not like peace but like deafness.

Below, nothing has been saved. That is not the word the place allows. A pool still seeps. A chick still learns a pool by standing in a fading ring. An old shape in a sealed circle that rings particularly true is slowly ceasing to be distinguishable from the fin it leans on, still conducting, laying down its thin mineral skin season by season, becoming one more hollow wall for the drips to strike.

When a traveler, years later, passes that circle and feels the stone lip vibrate against his calf, he will hear, if he is patient enough to stop shouting, a note in the chorus slightly lower than the ranks around it. Patient. Unintroduced. Nearly a voice, at slightly wrong intervals, among many voices.

He will think it is the valley, getting older.

He will be right enough.
