It would not drain it.

That would be the first temptation of power, and the first thing wisdom refuses. From the levee road, pity comes easily: the wells too shallow, the clothes never crisp, the children's cough in the still weeks, the floorboards going dark from beneath, the paper molding, the dogs who cannot thrive. A lesser kindness would arrive with drainage and concrete and sunlight, with dry foundations and straight roads, and would call the killing of the place a rescue.

The benevolent and wise power comes down the gravel track instead, between the banana stands, at the hour when the mill pauses briefly at midnight.

It comes when the pumps stop.

Only then can you hear what the place is when it is not working: frogs in the canals, water ticking from leaf to tin to gutter, the fig trunks settling, the damp bales breathing out their own stored heat in the curing sheds, people asleep behind woven panels, one old pump engineer awake out of habit, listening to the silence the way others listen to the thump. In that pause the whole bowl resolves, for a moment, into a single being — not the town, and not the plant mass, and not the people alone, but the negotiated creature made of all three.

Its heart is diesel. Its bones are iron frames long since swallowed by aerial roots. Its skin is fig and bamboo and greased leather and human forearms stained grey-green with fiber juice. Its blood moves very slowly through stone-lined canals. It breathes through a canopy two and three leaves thick, and in the morning it weeps clean water down its own walls. It has learned to live in a flooded hollow by never trying, even for one season, to be dry.

The power walks Pump Lane barefoot, because that is how you read this ground. It places its feet flat, a little splayed. It steps around the trip-root in Second Canal, not because it was warned, but because, arriving, it accepts the memory already stored in the place. It does not straighten anything. It does not widen a lane. It ducks under the awnings like everyone else.

And it understands, with something like reverence, that maintenance here is not a failure to finish building. It is the building. The pruners on their ladders are not holding back a jungle. The pump engineers checking painted marks on the door are not waiting for a better technology to free them. They are the priests — it knows they would dislike the word, and it honors them by never using it aloud — of a covenant: *nothing is cleared, everything is answered.*

So it joins the covenant, on its terms.

It does not stop the flood pulse. To stop the river rising outside the levee would be to lie to the wells. Instead, in the deep ground under the bowl, where no well is dug and no root is named, it persuades the silt to settle a fraction firmer under the pumphouse. Not forever. Forever is another kind of lie. Just enough that the subsidence marks, checked monthly, will confuse the engineers pleasantly for a generation. Just enough that in the worst late-summer year of their grandchildren's lives, when the canals lift an inch at breakfast and another by the following morning and the hyacinth presses hard under the bridges, the lowest sheds will darken, and strain, and *not quite* go under. The engineers will credit the pumps, and their own greased, sleepless care. They will be right. That is important. The power will not take competence away from the competent in order to prove it helped.

It goes to the settling pond behind the mill, where a heron is standing in the reed pans. It does not purify the wash-water into some impossible, crystal innocence; the reeds exist to do that work slowly, and they will. It coaxes, instead, a slight change in the bitter edge of the fiber pits — a softening of the chronic, low harms. The fungal bloom that lives in wet folds of skin will find the place marginally less welcoming. The cough that settles in children in the stillest weeks will settle a little less deep. No miracle anyone could point to. Fewer funerals that were never quite dramatic enough to be called tragedies. A few more old pruners at seventy, forearms roped with climb-work, still able to say *that wall will not take another story* — and be listened to. Because the old are the archive here, where paper softens within the month. Keeping them alive is keeping the library.

It learns the structural roots by name and prohibition, as the children do.

Then — and this is the most delicate work — it touches one root, and no other. Not to cut it. Never to cut it. In the dark under a dormitory walkway, where a buttress has begun, almost imperceptibly, to take a strain it was not trained for, it encourages a younger aerial root, still thin as a wrist, to descend six inches to the left of where it would naturally have fallen. To thicken there. To be ready, in ten seasons, in twenty, when the old root finally tires. No one will notice now. A pruner in thirty years, someone not yet born, will stand on a ladder at first light, frown at the wall, and feel under her palm a rightness she cannot explain, as if the house had been quietly preparing, all her life, to keep standing. She will trust it. She will be practicing exactly the faith the place requires.

That is how the power helps: as a pruner does. Seasonally. By anticipation. Without clearing.

Toward morning it does one thing for the being as a whole that it does for no part of it separately. Up on the rim, where the traders come down and the occasional engineer or bride is brought in perpetually damp for a month, it lays a gentle unremarkability over the gravel track. Not invisibility — the town needs salt, kerosene, dried fish, machine oil, new blood, news. But it turns the attention of the hungrier outside world just slightly aside. The company surveyor looking for land to drain and plant in clean rows will find the road forgettable in the rain. The official with a plan to improve the Combine out of its own life will misplace, inexplicably, his enthusiasm halfway down the embankment, and remember an appointment elsewhere. Those who come because they can learn the pace will still find the way down. Those who can only arrive at a run, slapping at mosquitoes, trying to walk fast through air like warm water, will more often decide the place has nothing for them.

It protects the gradient. Twenty feet is not much. It is everything.

Before the first steam blast, the power stands in the market square on the polished root mat, and it does not bless the people, because blessing from above would be a kind of clearing too. Instead it puts its palm flat against a fig wall, warm from a cookhouse wall on the other side, wet with morning weeping, and it makes the only promise a wise power may make to a being that lives by negotiation:

*I will not save you from your life. I will help your life continue to be yours.*

Then the pumps take up again, that bass thump felt in the soles of the feet within a hundred yards, and every loose leaf trembles. The decorticators begin their grinding roar. Laundry goes out to become, precisely and scientifically, *dry enough.* A snake found coiled on a warm pipe will be carried on a long pole to the levee and released. A courtship will advance at the speed of two young figs on iron frames.

From above, nothing changes. Only a denser, darker green in the hollow. A plume of steam lit from below. Floating lights behind leaves.

Below, unnoticed, the being thickens — root by root, season by season — around its borrowed, beating heart.
