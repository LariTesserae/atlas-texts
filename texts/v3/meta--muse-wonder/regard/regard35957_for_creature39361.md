They would not go down into it.

That would be the first sign that the power was wise, and the second would follow from it: nearly everything it did, it would do somewhere else.

For its attention alone would arrive like weather. A great benevolence bending over the bowl, meaning well, meaning *help*, would still be a pressure on the air, a warmth on the youngblack rock, a shadow moving against the cathedral dimness — and in a place where survival is the art of not startling the slope, even wonder can fall too loudly.

So on the rim, among the unweathered spines, the power would make itself smaller. Not human-small — that would be a costume, and costumes are another kind of lie — but *quieter*. It would withdraw the shining part of its regard. It would let its breath slow until the breath no longer warmed the rim stones. It would learn what everything below had already learned in their bodies, over more generations than the terraces could remember:

Open your mouth, then close it.

Wait your turn.

It would stay there long enough for staying to stop being an event. A season. Ten. Until the old rooks no longer turned one bright eye toward the place where it stood, until its presence was filed away by the tenancy with the fog, the creep of the talus, the owls walking under the walkways — a thing that is there, that has proved it will not ring on the stone.

Only then would it understand what it had come to see, and understanding would forbid most kinds of rescue.

Because the being in the bowl is not in distress in the way a benevolent power is tempted to imagine distress. It is hungry, yes. It is eaten, constantly, moth by moth, beetle by beetle, by its own white-faced walking parts. Its young die for a scream. Its beloved lowest streets are already drowning in moss and blind pink lizards and guano, swept every morning by the part of it that still has hands. Its houses slide down at the speed of a fingernail growing toward rooms where no human will ever sleep again.

To stop the predation would be to starve the owls and fatten the silence into rot. To stop the slope's creep would be to tear the slow migration out of its bones — the grazers know those streets because the streets have always been *almost* where they were last year, and knowing them anyway is the knowledge. To give the human parts medicine, metal, a loud clean water supply, voices that carry, would not save the being. It would only decide, from outside, that the being was really the people after all — the one error every visitor makes in the first hour — and hand the queue over to whichever part of it can hold a tool longest.

A wise power would not cure a heartbeat because it beats slower than its own.

What it would protect, jealously and at a great distance, are the conditions the being cannot queue for.

Beyond the rim, in the world that still believes noise is a natural right, the fog is made. It comes out of far forests breathing at night, out of cold air sliding over warmer valleys fifty miles off, out of a whole unseen weather-system that no rook or fern or child in the bowl can tend. If that world cuts, burns, dams, warms — if the mist comes thinner, or a week late, or stops coming on the nights when the waxy flowers are ready to open — then the bowl does not adapt. It simply takes its turn at an empty cistern, species after species, politely, in order, until the order itself is gone.

There the power would work. Unseen, unthanked, and careful never to let its work acquire a name. It would keep a valley wooded that men had marked for felling, by making another hillside seem, in their ledgers and their dreams, the better bargain. It would turn a surveyor's instruments aside by half a degree, year after year, so that the road they were imagining always arrived, on paper, at some easier pass. It would mislay, gently, the reports of mineral color in the young black rock. It would let travelers who came with hammers and bells and collecting jars develop, near the mountain, a sudden and inexplicable conviction that they had left something important burning at home.

Not a wall. Walls announce that there is something worth scaling.

Just — a region of country where loud intentions tended to wander, soften, and go elsewhere.

And it would do this without ever letting the protection harden into preservation. The mountain is still happening. One day the bowl will slump beyond mending, the fog will find a different hollow, the last low-doored house will go down into black fern and be tenanted by bats. The power would not hold the rim together against that day. It would not make the being into a garden of itself, eternal and arrested, for the comfort of having saved it. What lives there lives by rotation, replacement, the next shape taking the next job. Its guardian would have to be willing, at the end of centuries, to let it stop being this.

That is the outward work. It would take a very long time, and no one in the bowl would ever know it had been done, and that is exactly how the power would know it had been done rightly.

Inwardly, it would do one thing, once, and only after it had been taught.

It would come down at fog-fall — not in glory, not even in visibility, if it could manage that, but as one more cool presence at the back of things — and it would join the queue at a leaf-heart.

It would not drink first because it was greatest. It would not drink deeply because it had traveled far. It would let beetle, frog, spider, swift, the small shaking kid on its padded hooves, all take the stored dew in the ancient rotation, and its own turn, when it finally came, would come as a surprise it had to be ready not to seize. It would take less than its share. Less is the only tithe a place like that can accept without being diminished by the giving.

Perhaps, in the morning, with the stone channels running bright for their half hour, it would pick up a broom of fern in whatever it currently had that could pass for hands, and sweep a square.

Not the square the grazers would need — that would presume a knowledge it had not earned. Just a square. An empty one, in the old lower city where the doors stand open on nests and the market cross is white with rook lime. Sweep it clean of fallen wings and moss-creep, slowly, in silence, because a swept square is a square ready for whoever arrives next, and readiness is the whole religion of the bowl, if it has one.

Then it would leave before the light strengthened. It would not leave a mark, a blessing, a spring, a stone with its name worn soft by grateful hands. Marks become centers. Centers become thrones, then altars, then reasons for one part of the tenancy to stand in front of the others and explain, in an ever louder voice, what the power had meant.

It would leave only the swept square, which by the next fog-fall would need sweeping again, and that impermanence would be its signature.

But before it left, on the first of those mornings — stooped, damp, its vast attention folded down into something like a back, something like unhurried hands — it would make a small mistake. It would reach, out of old magnificent habit, toward a gutter that a line of beetles was approaching, antennae first, in their unjostling procession.

And from a chimney above, layered in generations of nest and guano, one of the old ash-grey rooks would cock its head. One eye on the fingers.

And sign, with a slow shift of its whole body, in the language the humans thought was theirs:

*Wait.*

The power — benevolent, wise, older than the young mountain, able to unmake the rim with a thought — would wait.

That is what it would do for the being in the bowl. It would obey it.
