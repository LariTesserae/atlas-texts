If it was benevolent, and if it was wise, it would not arrive standing upright.

That is the first thing it would learn — perhaps before it even crossed the edge of the plateau, while it was still climbing through the broken lowland slopes expecting a summit. Anything tall here is a mistake. Trees make that mistake, and break. Cairns make it only for the dead, and even they are built loose, to let the wind through. A visitor who came as a tower, a blaze, a straight figure with its head lifted admiringly into the gale, would frighten the children and exhaust itself in an afternoon.

So it would come low.

It would come weighted. Not in glory, not in stillness. Its hem would clink softly with flat stones. It would lean into the west wind at the angle that looks like falling and is, in fact, walking. It would learn to lean *back* on the downhill stretches and trust the air, briefly, to hold it. And when it reached the first hut — which it would almost walk across, mistaking the sod roof for another of those loaved, grass-covered rises — it would duck through the waist-high door, into peat smoke and muffled buffeting, and it would not straighten.

Inside, its first temptation would die quietly.

From the lowlands, from any kinder and thicker air, this place looks like a punishment that has lasted so long people have mistaken it for a life. Thin breath. Deafening noise. Skin burned dark mahogany by a sun that does not stop the cold. Children tied by wool cords so a fine clear day does not gust them off their feet. Toddlers tethered to bedposts indoors, by habit. Old women with milky corners to their eyes, following dust devils to gather what the wind has sorted — seed here, insects there, a glittering line of beetle wings. Men lost not to cliffs but to a single blast that sets them, broken-legged, in the next valley, within hearing of their own flock bells.

A power that loved quickly and thought slowly would want to fix all of it at once.

Calm the wind, just a little. Thicken the air. Raise a line of trees for shelter. Give them a valley's soft, merciful quiet.

It would be a catastrophe wearing mercy's face.

The wise power stays long enough to see that.

It stays through a squall that comes as a grey veil racing for miles and hits sideways for seven minutes and is gone. It watches the shallow lakes fill and frill white with spray and go to salt-cracked mud by evening. It watches the tussocks — emerald and gold, knee-high, jammed down against the earth — flattened to the soil, and then, minutes later, springing up again, shaking themselves clean. It learns that the dew these people wring at dawn from felt and metal and grass is not an eccentricity. It is a harvest the racing clouds leave when they drag their bellies across the ground. Stop the wind and the clouds stop travelling. Stop the clouds and the moss dries. Dry the moss and the streams, which run bright for a hundred meters and vanish into cracks, do not start at all.

It presses an ear, as the children are sent to do at dawn, to a hollowed, singing boulder, and feels the note in its teeth. It understands this is not decoration. It is a weather instrument, a voice, a familiar face among stones. This one rising means the wind will veer and drop into the hollow after dark. That one silenced by wet moss means heavy air coming. A language built because shouting fails. Whole-arm signals across a basin: sheep scattered, squall in ten minutes, do not take the flock to the lake that has become salt. Drums not into the air but into the ground, thumping through peat to the molars of someone a day's walk away.

It goes forehead to forehead to speak. That undoes it most of all.

Because up close, hand cupped around a jaw so the words travel partly through bone, nobody here sounds like a person enduring. They sound like people *placed*, exactly and intricately as the cushion moss in the hollows, as the lichen mapping only the sheltered side of boulders, as the one great scavenger that hangs motionless a thousand feet up, nailed to the gale, belonging to the wind precisely by not fighting it. They do not curse the roar. They are puzzled that a visitor keeps remarking on it. It is the sound their silence has inside it. Lowland quiet — real quiet, on a windless night below — makes their children's ears ache with expectation. It makes them feel unheld.

Trees unsettle them. Deep valleys feel like pits. Enclosure is the fear. Not exposure.

A benevolent power that cannot grasp that is not benevolent yet. It is merely homesick on someone else's behalf.

So it does not calm anything.

It does not thicken the air in their barrelled chests, which were made, breath by long breath, for air that is a rumor. It does not straighten a single spine that has learned its kind and elegant bend. It does not untie the sleeping child from the bedpost, because it has watched the mother tie that cord — lightly, automatically, braiding a bell into the child's hair in the same motion — and it knows the difference between a cage and a hand that stays in contact while you sleep. To cut that cord in the name of freedom would be to teach the plateau a lowlander's idea of love, and charge the child for the lesson.

It does not rebuild the cairns that have settled silent, either. On a strong day it walks the field of smooth, egglike stones and hears the broken, endless chord, each dead person holding, roughly, their own pitch through the gaps. A family passing weeks from now will pause and lean and listen, and know who is still sounding and who has gone quiet as the stones shifted. That settling is not a failure to be reversed by a kind god. It is how grief is allowed to finish here, in a landscape that finished rising so long ago it has forgotten sharpness. Some things are not meant to sing forever. Even mountains know that.

What it does, it does at the edges, facing *outward*, toward the world that would love this plateau incorrectly and efficiently.

Down below, in time, men will do arithmetic with the wind. They will see a high, unobstructed place where the air never stops, and they will think: wasted. They will come with straight things — survey stakes, roads switchbacking up the old soft slopes, towers taller than any tree, blades built to stand upright and harvest the gale until they fatigue and crack. They will bring noise that is not the wind's noise and quiet fenced compounds and cables and a road down which the young can leave faster than they can ever choose, meaningfully, to return.

The power is wise, so it does not smite them. It is subtler and more permanent than that. It makes the plateau easy to mismeasure. It lets the surveyors discover, correctly, that this wind is too turbulent, too gusting, too full of eddies and reversals around every low dome for their clean machines; what is a voice to a singing stone is a ruinous vibration to a turbine. It lets maps blur at exactly the contour where the climb stops feeling worthwhile. It lets the road, each time it is proposed, cost slightly more than the lowland valley that proposes it can quite justify to itself. Not a miracle. A long, administrative, geological discouragement. The plateau has survived by being in its final form. The power simply helps the lowlands conclude, in their own language, that there is nothing here to improve.

And within the plateau — one thing. Only one, and it leaves no signature.

On the bad pass between two basins, where the aunt's hut-partner was taken clean off the path decades ago and lay two days listening to his own bells, there is a new windbreak by the end of a season. Two low walls in a V, opening away from the west, built of unmortared smooth rock and turf, exactly as any shepherd would build it, exactly where a person set down hard by a gust, with a broken leg, could drag himself into the lee and survive the night and be heard drumming into the ground. Nobody remembers building it. Nobody finds that strange for long. Useful things up here are adopted without genealogy, like a stone found already shaped like a bowl. Children on tethers are routed past it on the worst days, three or four in a giggling, stumbling line, pebble hems clinking, and a place that used to take people now, occasionally, declines to.

That is all.

No temple. No calm. No trees in rows, no gift of wings, no sudden healing that would make grandparents strangers to their own faces in a wooden bowl of moss-water. If an old woman's back aches less on the morning after it has slept — not slept, for it does not sleep — somewhere near her hut, in the lee of a boulder, low and weighted and listening... she will put that down to the wind dropping, and she will be almost right.

At dusk on its last day the squalls blow off the edge of the world. The lakes go mirror-thin and furious with spray at one end. The tussocks rise. Hut by scattered hut, the bells go still, the felt roofs buffet softly like something breathing in its sleep, and somewhere a cairn holds its note, and somewhere the child sleeps tied lightly to the bedpost in the roar that is shaping his chest, his language, his whole anchored life.

The power leans into the west wind, at the falling angle, and lets it hold it up for one step — then catches itself, the way it was taught.

It leaves the place exactly as old, as loud, as three-quarters sky as it found it.

It only stands, unseen, between that sky and anything that would narrow it.
