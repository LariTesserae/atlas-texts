If they are truly wise, and truly benevolent, the first thing they will do is nothing they had planned to do.

They will have come with, if not gifts, then the readiness to give them. That is what power does when it finds something small and strange and faithfully returning at the top of the world. It wants to bless. It wants to improve. It wants to make the brief hour longer, the thin water deeper, the death at noon less complete.

They will stand in the southern notch — politely, so as not to block the cold updraft when it comes — and watch a whole day first. Wisdom begins there: in not mistaking the first form you see for the creature.

In the morning the bowl will seem empty. Stone teeth, slate pages, frost-heaved rings, the green copper chisel under its inch of ice in a basin meant for catching. If the power is warm, as benevolent powers tend to imagine themselves, the ice near their feet will melt a fraction early. They will notice, and step into shade. Their first kindness will be learning to make themselves colder, or at least not to interfere with the cold.

They will not give it soil.

That would be the obvious mercy, and a murder. Soil would hold water past its hour. Soil would bring the orange lichen, then moss, then roots prying usefully, hungrily, into the cracks that are now its private interior. Seeds are already provided by the wind; it keeps them perfect, dry, unfolded in its pantry, grass-heads and moths and a spider with its legs folded like a note never opened. It does not reject life out of cruelty. It simply does not have the terms for it. Decay would need warmth that lasts. Growing would need a night that does not freeze. To make this place kind to other living things would be to evict the one thing that lives here by *not* living in their way.

They will not give it permanence, either.

By late afternoon the cloud will spill shoulder-high through the notch, tearing itself on the pillars, and for twenty minutes they will see the temptation made visible: a forest, white on white, fern on stone, every tooth acquiring its soft ghost. It is so beautiful that an unwise god would try to save it. Fix it. Make the rime stay. Sheathe the bowl in lasting ice so the little trees never fall.

But a glacier is only storage. Perennial ice is a locked room. This being is alive — in the way it permits that word — precisely because it dies completely. Melt is not its suffering; melt is its movement, its feeding, its chance to be a network instead of a hundred separate fronds, to run in black threads no wider than string, to steer from copper sun to copper sun, to lie for one black, still hour in the basins and hold the violet sky upside down. Freeze is not its death either. Freeze is its going-home, its work in the dark inside the granite, splitting a grain loose, sharpening a tooth, lifting a pebble into a ring nobody made on purpose. If you stop the swing between them, you have not preserved it. You have preserved a photograph of it, colder and deader than the moth.

They will not give it a mind to know itself with.

That is the harder restraint, because loneliness is what visitors project first onto high, quiet places. But mind would require memory that lasts longer than a day intact, and it already has a better kind: the cracks remember. The basins remember, because hands cut them where the water already wanted to go. The pillars remember in their flaked lee sides and rust-red veins. To wake it up into a single lonely awareness, trapped in a bowl eighty paces across with a sun like a hammer and no way down, would not be an awakening. It would be an exile into a smaller country than the one it already inhabits, which is everywhere the temperature crosses a line.

So the power will do the unfashionable thing. They will sit, if sitting is available to such a being, and be available to be grown on.

In the evening, when the second frost comes straight out of the damp air, it will make no distinction between pillar and basin rim, between copper disc and the green chisel, between stone and god. If they are wise, they will not melt it off with inherent radiance. They will let the fern-ice take their outermost surface, politely, indiscriminately, as it takes everything that stays still long enough. They will hear, close as breath in thin air, the tick and sigh of water expanding in hairline seams. They will feel the ground lift a pebble under them, almost imperceptibly. They will understand that attention, to this creature, is not being looked at. It is being included in the list of cold surfaces.

And then, on their way down, they will do one thing. Not to it. Around it.

They will make the bowl a little harder to reach.

Not forbidden — forbidding is a signpost. Not walled — a wall would change the wind through the notch. They will simply let the path below forget itself. A cairn will settle in a winter they do not witness. The story that told which shoulder of the mountain to climb will lose one detail, then another, the way a song loses a verse when the singer dies comfortably in bed. Prospectors will not be struck by lightning; that is theatre, and would bring investigators. Tourists will not be turned back by storms. They will merely find, year after year, that some other valley looks more promising, that the light is going, that perhaps they will try again next season.

Because the real threat to this being was never its daily death. It can survive being eaten by the sun until the sun changes. It can survive thin air, violent cold, the slow fall of its own teeth. What it cannot survive is being helped by too many hands at once: a roof to keep the “shrine” dry, a dammed reservoir to keep the sacred water from “wasting,” a pipeline, a plaque, a handful of alpine seed scattered by someone who could not bear that nothing grew. Love, at scale, is a climate.

The power will also leave the copper suns where they are.

They might, for a moment, consider removing them, purifying the place back to some imagined original. But the basins and the little polished discs are not a desecration now. They are part of the local weather of it — a dozen small, movable warmths it has learned to graze between at melt. The builders came in season, drank, and went down before the stars began to buzz. That is the correct human relationship to this place, discovered long ago by people whose lungs burned for it: visit, intercept a little, leave an offering that functions as a tool, abandon your chisel at dusk because your hands have stopped working. The power will not improve on that etiquette.

They will take nothing down with them. Not the moth. Not the spider. Not a salt whisker in a vial, not a thread of red water. A sample would be true in every particular and false in the only one that matters, which is the return.

At the rim, if they look back at all — and wisdom may permit one look — the bowl will already be closing its hand for the night. Basins glazing. Boot-print, god-print, whatever faint disturbance their presence pressed into the gravel already rimed at the edges. Stars hard enough to buzz. The whole stone forest ticking softly to itself, making its rings, drinking breath, splitting granite by a fraction of a grain.

Unwitnessed now. Unharried. Given the enormous gift of being allowed to happen again tomorrow, without having to be anything else.

That is what they would do, if anything.

Almost nothing, carefully.
