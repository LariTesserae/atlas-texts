The power would come to the rim, and stop.

That is the first wise thing, and for a long time the only one.

Others come up the teeth-colored track with solutions already in their hands, because the Ear from above looks like a problem. It is lush and has no water. It is loud and its keeper is deaf. It is fruitful and harsh, abundant and split-lipped, a garden in a stone cup where no garden should be. A certain kind of kindness cannot bear that tension. It wants to fill the cistern. It wants to still the vortex enough for speech. It wants to give the woman back the small, ordinary sounds — bird song as birds intend it, rain, her own name pronounced correctly — as if those things had been stolen from her, rather than traded, deliberately and over years, for a larger hearing.

The wise power brings none of that.

It does not bring rain. Rain would hiss on the oil-rich sails, burst the pods out of season, panic the cushions, drown the fine mineral dust the plumes are made to sieve. It would feed nothing. It would only make the place briefly resemble somewhere else, and detune it for a generation.

It does not bring quiet. Quiet in the Ear would not be peace. It would be blindness, starvation, and a terrible falling: lizards unable to read the air, finches lost inside silent trunks, spiders stranded between trees, the one plant no longer able to tell, by vibration, where its own many bodies were leaning.

And it does not bring the woman a cure. To restore her ears would be to exile her in the place she knows best. She hears roots through pumice. She hears sulfur, distance, weather, visitors, the health of trunks through the soles of her feet. The ordinary scale is gone because the larger scale required the room.

So the power sits on the rim where the offerings are left — the salt bread, the feather bundles, the little carved lizard — and it learns the local courtesy.

It does not speak her name. It does not speak its own.

It listens.

Not for an afternoon, which is how long most mercies are willing to listen before they improve things. It listens until morning alarms have gone all the way around the fluted walls and come back lowered into afternoon. It listens until it can tell the difference between a trunk that is humming and a trunk that is merely being played by the wind. Until it can distinguish the beetles' hammering from the finch chicks drumming from inside the wood, the cushions' sigh from the nets' bass buzz, the woman's signal — three beats, and a thumb dragged across a slit — from all the imitations the Ear makes of it hours later.

This takes longer than the power expected. Powers are used to knowing things by looking at them. The Ear does not yield to looking. The light is brown and underwater, the air full of shed and circling things, sight cut short by dust. It has to be known the way she knows it: patiently, through the bone.

Only then does the power act, and almost everything it does is done elsewhere, and at a distance, and in a direction she would never need to see.

Down below, in the villages, a path to a reliable seep is remembered by a shepherd who had forgotten it for no reason he can name. Not a river. Nothing that would make men rich or change the shape of their lives. Just enough water, nearer than the old source, so that no one in a dry year looks up the mountain and begins to think hungrily about the impossible green in the bowl, and what it might be made to yield.

A road survey for a quarry loses its appetite at the notch between the leaning slabs. The men turn back complaining of thirst and a ringing in their ears and a strange conviction that the stone up there is poor. It is not poor. That is irrelevant. It is *listened to*, and the power has very quietly made that fact easier for strangers to feel in their knees before they can argue themselves out of it.

A collector who would have paid well for a hollow trunk-section, for a pair of sail-leaves, for a deaf girl's interesting skull someday, finds his money suddenly needed at home. A healer who would have poured hot oil and lowland prayer into a child's unhearing ears finds his hands full with fevers that actually want curing, until the child is old enough to put her feet to a door of her own accord and be understood.

Small deflections. Nothing with thunder in it. The wise do not protect a place by putting a wall of fire around it; that only teaches the world that something precious is inside. They protect it by keeping it slightly forgettable to the greedy, slightly difficult to the hurried, and entirely legible to those who come to leave salt bread on the rim and go away again.

Inside the bowl, the power does one thing, and it asks first.

Late in the day, when the woman goes from tree to tree striking the resonating chambers and shaking the last pollen loose, the power — having finally learned the rhythm well enough to risk it — drums once, from the rim. Not the signal. Not a command. A question, in the only grammar the place trusts.

It gets the timing wrong.

There is a pause in which the beetles, politely, make room. A lizard stalls mid-glide, fans ticking. Somewhere a trunk answers at the wrong depth and has to be answered in turn.

Then, from down among the green-black sails, comes the correction: *Tok. Tok-tok. Tok.* Slower than the power had played it. With the weight in a different place. Less like speech, more like weather arriving.

The power plays it again, correctly.

The woman lays one great calloused hand flat against the nearest trunk and feels the Ear agree that a visitor is present. She does not look up. There is no need. Looking up is not how presence is confirmed here.

After that, the power is permitted its one gift, and it is wise enough to make the gift small, durable, and usable.

On the track below the notch, where the outer wind has worried a slab loose over many winters, there is a stone of a size one person cannot move and of a grain the local rock does not produce: dense, dark, fine enough to take a true note when struck and keep it. Left where it is, it would have fallen in another decade and narrowed the only path. The power sets it instead inside the bowl, in the lee of the north wall, half-buried near her hut where she will find it with her feet at dawn.

Not a monument. She would have no use for a monument and the wind would eventually take its meaning anyway. A working stone. A pitch the grove does not currently have. Something she can notch, or not notch. Something she can build a crescent against a young shoot from, drum on, ignore for a season, or use to weight a net that needs a lower note than any of her present stones can give it.

She will decide what it is for. That is most of the gift.

The rest is that the power, on leaving, does what the villagers do. It leaves an offering on the rim where offerings belong: no water, no gold, nothing that would last long enough to become an obligation. A bundle of feathers. A little salt. A strip of fiber that will sing faintly when the wind finds it.

And it leaves, at last, one sound of its own — not a name, exactly; it has learned better than that — offered to the bowl without any expectation of getting it back intact. The Ear takes it gladly, as it takes everything loose. It flattens it, carries it around the fluted walls, lowers it, stretches it, hands it from trunk to trunk and net to cushion. For hours after the power has gone back down into the thin clean silence below, the place goes on mispronouncing it, more and more beautifully.

She hears it come past her shoulder in the evening, unrecognizable and entirely familiar, and smiles at a beetle, and corrects a young tree by the width of a finger, and does not look up.

Nothing has been rescued. Nothing needed to be.

But the path will be a little harder for armies than for shepherds' children now. The child below who hears with her feet will be allowed to become strange in peace. The cistern will stay eloquently empty. And in the lee of the north wall there is one new stone, of a note the Ear has never made before, waiting until the Tuner decides — tomorrow, next season, or never — what in her many-voiced, dry, green, impossible garden has been quietly asking for it.
