If it is truly benevolent, and truly wise, it will not arrive with trumpets.

It will not split the grey sky over the marsh. It will not lift the town out of its hollow, or fill the hollow with good dry earth, or set the houses in neat rows on the flat land where houses — by any sane reasoning — ought to be.

It could. That is the first temptation, and the first thing it must refuse.

For a power that can see the whole pit at once can also see, as the people themselves cannot quite afford to see all at once, how narrow the margin is. One blocked drain in a wet week. One brown-weeping face let go above the lowest lane at the hour when the children cross for the morning bucket-chain. One hard winter when the jacking-timbers are green and the shared walls all lean together, a hand's breadth too far, and do not stop. It would be so easy to make it safe.

To shore the north wall from underneath, invisibly, so that no slab ever fell again. To drink the excess water out of the chalk and leave the weep-cloths merely damp, never sodden. To raise the pit floor, just a little, just enough, so that no more grandparents' doorways drowned.

It watches the figure on the roof at first light, barefoot on wet slate, resetting a stone. It watches the bowl of clay handed up. It watches the child cross the two-plank bridge with the jar against her chest, steady because steadiness has been taught to her feet before it was taught to her mind — and it understands, slowly, as a wise thing must, that safety is not the same as salvation here.

Take away the weeping and you take away the water that keeps sweet in a traveller's skin, the trade that brings grain over the boardwalk. Take away the settling and the slipping and you take away the stone, the lime, the blue clay. Take away the danger that no household can meet alone and you take away the slate hung at the hall, the six families appearing with mallets and good humour, the low-voiced bargaining that is really a way of saying: *your roof is my roof, your gutter is my jar, do not let me fall, I will not let you.*

You would give them dry houses on flat ground and they would become, in a generation, other people. Poorer people, for all their dry floors. People who could afford to shout. People who could afford dogs, and grudges kept behind separate walls, and wells of their own. The pit did not merely wound the earth and force them into it. It taught them attention. Attention is their wealth.

So the power does, for a very long time, what the town itself does.

It listens.

It learns to tell clear weep from brown. It learns whose bucket squeaks on which rope. It learns the snails — where they cluster, how high, how thick — until it can read the pressure building in the wet rock the way the grandmothers do, by a taste like iron on the tongue of the world. It learns not to stand where the wall stains rusty. It learns to knock on wood.

This takes years. A benevolent power has years, and wisdom is mostly the refusal to hurry.

Then, when it acts — if *acts* is even the word — it acts at the scale of the place. A hand's breadth. A finger's breadth. Nothing that would show. Nothing that would be worshipped. Nothing that would let a single household stop taking its turn.

On a morning in late winter, when the scree has ticked all night and the old woman at the dawn drain stops, rake raised, because something in the sound is wrong, a slab on the east face that should have gone — that by every law of water and fracture *would* have gone, across the lane, at exactly the height of a child's head — hangs.

It hangs a breath too long. Two breaths. The boy behind her does not even notice; he is looking down at the choked water rising bright around his ankles. The old woman notices. She steps back into the alcove mouth, pulling him by the sleeve with the automatic courtesy of long practice, and only then does the stone let go and split itself cleanly on the empty lane.

By the next week it will be lintels. No one will call it a miracle. She will tell her daughter the east face is speaking early this year, that is all, and the stores will be moved up one shelf.

In the drought summer — and there is one, the year the marsh pools shrink to cracked rings and the weep slows to a shine that never quite becomes a drop — the power does not make it rain. Rain would rot the stored grain in the hall and swell the timber and come, anyway, at the wrong hour for the wrong roofs. Instead, deep under the pit where no rake or bucket has ever reached, it coaxes one hidden seam of chalk to give up its water a little more generously. Not much. A thread. Enough that the cloths in the shrine alcove are still heavy by noon, if barely. Enough that the first cup is still left in the black niche, though the girl who leaves it looks at it a moment longer than she used to before she turns away.

No one knows. That is the point. The jars still must be carried. The cloths still must be wrung — twist, pause, twist again. Hope is kept, but work is not stolen.

And one year, without anyone sowing it, a second green thing takes hold in the pit besides the pale sorrel in the wall-cracks.

It comes up in the broken-pot place, after the old bean woman dies and her pot passes, as the honour passes, to a widower with stone-whitened hands who does not know beans at all and is quietly ashamed of it. What grows that spring is familiar and not familiar — bean, yes, leggy, yes, suspicious as always — but the roots find the wet mortar in a way beans should not, and hold. The stems, pruned hard by his inexpert fingers, come back thicker. By the second year there are beans on three joined roofs, not many, not a crop to live on, never that. Just enough to be served with ceremony at a wedding held across planks laid over the gaps, while everyone agrees, mouths full, that they are somewhat less suspicious than last year's.

He will be proud for the rest of his life. He should be. The power will not take that from him by letting him suspect it was given.

It does nothing about the lords and factors on the flat land, mostly — men who arrive over the boardwalk in dry boots, talking at full field-volume, wanting the lime cheaper and the clear water promised and perhaps, eventually, owned. Mostly, because the town is very good, in its close, low, confidential way, at settling these disputes in the hall with the grain bins overhead. But now and again a deed that plainly said *the pit holds its own catchment* is misread for a season in the town's favour. Now and again a man who came intending to stay and buy up roof-rights one indebted family at a time finds the damp getting into his boxes, his papers, his temper, finds himself flinching at drips and shouting at whispers, and leaves of his own accord before winter, certain the place is unhealthy. He is not wrong. It is unhealthy for what he was trying to grow there.

The dead, the power leaves alone. It does not soften that. The pit has no earth to spare for them, and they know it, and they wash the body in first weep-water and carry it up and out before the day warms with a tenderness that does not need improving. If anything rides with the bearers on the boardwalk, making their placed steps surer over the slick planks, no one says so. The town keeps only the door below. That, too, is a kind of wisdom the power is learning to respect: that not everything loved can be kept in the same place, and that grief is not a problem to be solved with extra room.

Will it stay? Perhaps. These attentions accrete, like the town itself — a shelf moved up, a seam of water eased, a face of rock persuaded to fall at dawn when the lane is empty rather than at supper when it is full. Never enough to finish anything. There is no finished state to aim at. A roof is still a provisional arrangement with the sky. The outfall still groans out to the marsh. Every spring, the houses must still be jacked upright, with timber wedges and good humour, by neighbours who will be fed and owed in return.

The power does not exempt them from their lives. It exempts them, now and then, from the one accident that would have been too much — the one that would not have become lintels by next week, the one that would have broken the bucket-chain for good.

At night the shutters close. One lit window high in the pit wall, an eye half-open, shows the marsh almost nothing. Then even that goes out, and there is only the ticking of water into a hundred jars, near, far, never quite in rhythm.

And if, very late, there is one more drip among them — a fraction too regular, a fraction too kind, falling exactly where a forgotten pot stands empty under a shifted gutter — no one wakes to remark on it. They have long since made room, without knowing they were doing it, for one more neighbour through the wall: quiet, attentive, and careful about where it stands.
