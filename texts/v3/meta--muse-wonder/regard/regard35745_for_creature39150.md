There is only one room up there, and the mountain has been capped shut beneath it.

A benevolent and wise power would not begin by entering it.

They would arrive, as everything arrives, on iron. They would put the wax plugs in at the door — not because the noise could harm them, but because refusing the tin box by the coat-hooks would be a kind of arrogance, a refusal to be governed by the house rules of a place that has spent a century learning how to survive itself. They would learn the painted hand-signs. *Pressure. Stand clear. Tea. Look up.* They would keep to the wall.

They would not stand in the center to prove they could.

That is the first wisdom the room teaches, and any power worth calling wise would already suspect it before the flywheel's counterweight polished its bright arc past their ankles: the emptiness in the middle is not a waste, and it is not a wound to be healed. It is the condition of everything green and breathing pressed bright-eyed around the edges. Fill it with mercy — with partitions, with quiet, with soil beds and nesting boxes and safe paths across — and the maidenhair would stop mapping the drip-lines by the end of the season. The swifts would lose the shaking dark behind the baffle plates that no predator can endure. The vine would lose the beam that prunes it into its perfect arch. The room is not a garden trapped inside a machine. It is a garden that could only have been invented by one.

So they would not stop the horn.

They would sit, if sitting is the word, in the one corner the beam cannot see, a little behind the keeper's lean-to, and stay long enough to stop wanting to fix the keeper, too.

Because that would be the second temptation, and the stronger one. He is the largest thing here and the least adapted, seasonal, half-deafened in the same frequencies as all his predecessors, leaning away from the center even in sleep, one hand resting lightly on warm iron to read what his ears can no longer be trusted with. It would be easy for benevolence to mistake him for the point of the place. To make him comfortable. To make him permanent. To give him company that stayed, a quiet room inside the room, a way to hear music again cleanly.

A wise power would notice that the building has already decided what he is: not its master, not its prisoner, not its child. A seasonal organ it grows when hands are needed — to rake the fine ash where the ferns thin, to clear the drip channel but not too much, to cut the fig root out of the south valve and leave enough, to break ice from the horn lip and then, crucially, to *leave* before deafness or madness or attachment can make him claim the margin as his own. The relief cable car, arriving once a season whether he wants it or not, is as much a part of the machine as the governor is.

What they might do for him is therefore very small, and would look like nothing.

They would make sure the car always comes.

Not dramatically. No miracles on the mountainside. Only the quiet, century-long mislaying of every decommission order ever filed in a valley office. A signature that never quite gets obtained. A fuel requisition approved by a clerk who cannot afterward remember approving it. A replacement bearing that turns out, on inspection, to have already been in the stores locker, oiled, wrapped in cloth, of exactly the obsolete size. The world below is very good at forgetting places that do not ask it for anything except to be forgotten; a wise power would simply help the forgetting along, the way the keeper helps the drip channels along: not forcing, just preventing silt.

And in the logbook, perhaps — though even this they might decide against, on the last morning — one entry in pencil, in handwriting that leans the same way all the keepers' handwriting leans, on a page he has not reached yet:

*Heard you. Good year.*

They would not sign it. He would assume a previous keeper wrote it, and be, in the way that matters up there, correct.

For the rest of the inhabitants they would do even less, which is to say they would do it elsewhere.

Because the room's secret, the thing the horn has been announcing to an empty sky for forty-minute intervals all its life, is that this place is not trying to last forever. Iron fatigues. Rivets weep. The diesel will, one decade or another, stop being sent up the mountain, and the flywheel will slow from once every seven seconds to once every ten, to once, and the glass will go cold, and the interior rain will fall one last time and not be breathed back. A benevolence that only preserved would merely be embalming. It would keep the boilers lit and the tanks sweating and the lizards fat on the lagging until the whole arrangement became a reliquary — beautiful, sealed, and no longer anybody's way of living.

The inhabitants already know the way out. They have never taken it themselves. They take it by proxy, once in twenty blasts, when the governor's brass balls lift to the painted red line and the horn clears fully and a visible green-gold breath goes out over the drop: fern spore, fig pip, wallflower seed, a suspended generation drawn off the walls and surrendered to a mountainside none of them will ever see. There is already a line of improbable green on the leeward crags below, descending in patches as far as binoculars have bothered to look.

A wise power would go down there.

Down there is where benevolence can act without becoming another machine in the room: not by planting, which would be vanity, and not by uncapping the mountain, which would be murder dressed as liberation, but by making the landing slightly less impossible. A rock nudged, in the night, no more than a landslide would nudge it, so that one ledge holds meltwater three days longer. A crack deepened by exactly the depth of a winter's freeze, so ash blown down over decades can lodge instead of blowing on. Rain encouraged — not summoned, *encouraged* — to fall on the seeded crags in the week after the long summer blasts, when the figs split with a tick in the lulls and the whole room lets go at once.

Nothing anyone down-valley would notice, except that the green patches thickened a little faster than green patches on a bare peak reasonably should, and held through a dry year they reasonably should not have held through, and that in time — not keeper-time, not even fig-time, but the time of moss mapping rust — something rooty and small-bodied and many-fold would be living unprotected on open rock, in ordinary weather, in silence, having inherited from the room everything except the room.

That is the only rescue this place is asking for: not to be kept, but to be succeeded.

So in the end they would come back up the catwalk one last time, hand light on the railing, and stand at the margin with the choughs and the bees and the deaf rust-colored moths through one clearing blast — the one that empties the air out of your sternum and takes the seed out over the void in a plume that hangs, green-gold, unbelievably laden, before the mountain wind strips it down toward the crags.

They would not wave it on its way. They would not bless it in any language the swifts could hear.

They would simply be still, in the ringing second after, with one hand resting lightly on the iron, feeling through the palm whether it was the compressor or the flywheel that wanted grease — and finding, to their quiet satisfaction, that the keeper was already reaching for the can.

Then they would leave by the cable car, on schedule, whether they wanted to or not.

And the car would keep coming.
