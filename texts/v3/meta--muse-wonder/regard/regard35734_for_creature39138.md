First, they would not come down in glory.

Not through the roof glass in a shaft of light — there is light enough already, and it belongs to the place. Not through the airlock with an announcement. No voice in the upper gallery. No sudden stillness in the pumps to make the crews look up from their whispered work and know that the rules of the world had changed.

A benevolent and wise power would know what official attention would do to her.

She would become a case. A violation. A liability, a contamination event, a marvel, a rescue. Men with clipboards would come up in the gondola more than three at a time. They would test the water she had seeded. They would give names to the things living on her skin. They would be kind, many of them, and they would dry her out in the name of kindness, and examine her lungs, and cut her hair for samples, and ask her, in a room with a floor, why she had done it.

And the building would be cleaned.

So the first thing the power does is learn to be unnoticed. It comes in, if *in* is still the word, the way warmth comes into water that is already warm — not as an invasion, but as a barely perceptible deepening. A current no slower than the others. A shadow that rearranges the kelp by millimeters and then, learning from embarrassment, stops rearranging it.

It watches for a long time.

That, too, is an act. Most powers do not watch long enough.

It watches her surface under the crane rail and rest her teeth against a pump casing to hear whether its stillness is rest or failure. It watches her fold a fern back from a sight-glass rather than cut it, and tie it with its own stem. It watches her do by hand, blossom to blossom, the work the brushes were built to fake, her fingertips loosening pollen into slow golden weather underwater. It watches her eat young kelp among the mangrove crowns without haste or apology, and tuck cuttings into prop roots where they can soften, and leave a glove on the bottom grating to rot because the place needs to learn rotting and has no other teacher.

It is tempted, as all benevolent things are tempted.

To heal the small scars at her waterline. To clear her lungs and lengthen her breath until she could cross the whole nave on one slow undulation. To bring fish at last into the shafts of light — silver turning things, a sudden animal glitter — and insects for the surface, and a bird for the towers, and make the sterile garden complete in a single generous season.

It does none of that.

Not because the gifts would be cruel. Because they would be *answers*, and she is not a question. Because a fish introduced by heaven is still an introduction, and this water has only just learned to bear one animal's gut and skin and shed hair; it would not be mercy to hurry it with miracles. Because to make her breathe like a whale would be to make her less precisely what she has become: a woman who has slowed herself, at cost, to match a place that moves slowly. The power respects the cost. It does not refund people their sacrifices just to prove it can.

So its work, for years, is almost nothing. And the almost is everything.

It is in the audit that does not quite arrive. A regional inspector, a decent man who would have felt obliged to report a permanent inhabitant, finds his attention drawn instead — gently, without compulsion, for the wise do not puppet the decent — to a hairline crack in the gondola cable housing that genuinely needs his whole week. By the time he has dealt with it, the rotation roster has cycled again, and her name is once more in the next gondola down, and the next, and the next.

It is in a bearing in the dehumidifier, high in the thin mountain air, that should have seized one January night when the outside wind was worrying the towers and the intakes were rimed. If it had seized, the summit sea would have begun, very slowly, to lose its argument with the sky. Instead the bearing holds through the coldest hour, ticking, and fails three weeks later at noon on a maintenance day, in the hands of a crew member who has the spare at his feet.

It is in medicine that begins to appear among the folded towels — not much, nothing that would require a story: something for a joint that has begun to ache in the long cold before the boilers take the night chill off the water, something for a cut that stays too long soft. Left without note or instruction. She is free to ignore it. Some of it she does. Some, after a considering pause, she does not. The power learns her pauses the way she learns the pumps' stillnesses, and adjusts.

It is in the crews, protected in their mercy. No one who has left an extra towel is ever put in a position where they must either lie under oath or betray her. No camera is repaired quite well enough to see in a silt-cloud. No engineer's report saying *Listen. It's carrying* is ever escalated to someone who would want the tree cut out of the pressure system to restore the drawing. The drawing, instead, very gradually, in revisions nobody remembers authorising, begins to make room for the tree.

And once — only once — the power speaks.

Not at night, when she is asleep in the hammock cut free each morning, the tide breathing across her belly. Not at noon, when she is most dissolved into function and warmth and green. At evening, in the blue hour when the glass has lost the sun and the surface of the interior sea goes dark and reflective, and she is sitting on the upper gallery with her feet in the water and the dented tin cup rinsed and upside down beside her, resting between one task and the next.

There is no form. Perhaps there is only a presence at the waterline beside her, where the two worlds meet, and a voice that has taken care to sound no more authoritative than dripping from a truss.

It asks if she wants anything.

She looks, for a long time, at the mangrove crowns. At the indicator lights rising out of the tide and sinking again. She is not startled, or not much; a woman who listens to boilers for a living knows when a new sound has entered the system, and knows better than to name it too quickly.

What she says, finally, is not *let me stay*. She has never needed permission from anything higher than a roster clerk's inattention to stay.

She says: "When I can't clear the intakes anymore — don't let them sterilise it all at once."

That is the whole prayer. For the water, not for herself. For slowness, not for escape.

The power says it will not.

And it keeps its word in the only way the word can be kept: not by freezing the place forever at the moment of her, but by guarding the pace of what comes after. When her shoulders begin to fail at the guide ropes, help arrives the way help has learned to arrive there — unspectacular, deniable, human-shaped. A younger crew member whose gondola down is, one season, delayed. Then delayed again. Who learns the channels by trailing a hand. Who is taught, without ever being told that teaching is happening, which sight-glass fern must be folded and which must be cut, and how to feel with her teeth whether a pump is resting.

No succession is announced. There is just, one year, a second cup on the control cabinet. Turned upside down against the condensation rain.

And when she dies — she does die; the wise do not steal that, either — she dies where she lived, in the hammock at the waterline, in a night when the outside wind cannot quite get at the towers and the artificial tide is covering and uncovering her, patiently, twenty centimetres at a time. The power does not lift her out. It does not preserve her like a saint above the clerestory.

It does the last, smallest, most necessary thing.

It holds the gondola down the mountain for one extra day — weather, perfectly plausible weather — while the woman she taught does what she left instructions for in the only form she ever trusted: not writing, but habit. Cuttings tucked deep. The warm body given, slowly and without horror, to the root masses and the filtration racks and the patient warm water. The place receiving, at last, the full lesson she had been teaching it in pieces all her life.

How to rot. How to make soil where there had never been soil. How to let an ending be a kind of filter.

Afterwards, nothing is renamed for her. There is no plaque. The manifest, mercifully, at last lets her gondola go down without her.

But some springs later, on the south wall where the mangrove crowns break the surface, a flower opens that is not quite the waxy underwater kind and not quite any terrestrial kind the seed bank remembers sending up the mountain. It opens at the waterline, exactly at the waterline, pale, in the bright silent green and the steamy air at once, as if it could not decide which world it belonged to and had decided, instead, to belong to the meeting.

The power, watching, does not call it a sign.

It just makes sure no one prunes it before it has finished opening.

Then it goes on doing almost nothing, for as long as almost nothing is needed.
