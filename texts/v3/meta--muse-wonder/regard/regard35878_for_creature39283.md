They would not bring the morning.

That is the first temptation, and a benevolent power that was not also wise might give in to it: crack the shell, let in a shaft of real daylight, watch four hundred metres of darkness become visible all at once. It would be a murder. Eyes grown and fitted for heat would burn. The figs, turning restlessly toward a wind, would not know what to do with a sun that did not move the way they had been taught to expect. The town on the far side of the void, which every child has been trained since infancy not to look at, would suddenly be *there*, hanging overhead, undeniable, falling forever in the old monkey part of the brain. A gift of light, in Anath, is an act of violence.

Nor would they silence it.

They would be tempted to that, too. The vents, the pumps, the hammering, the brass tones felt in the teeth — surely peace must mean quiet. But the people of Anath have hearts that have learned to beat around that noise. Their children are rocked to pump-rhythms. Their speech has moved into the forearm because the sphere eats voices. Their sleep is a skilled negotiation with Loud and Less Loud. Take the sound away and you would not give them rest. You would leave them in a silence so complete their own pulses would frighten them.

Nor would they fill the Hollow, repopulate it, strike the dead from the housing rolls. Five thousand was the number it was poured for. Nine hundred is the number that lives there. To a careless kind of love those empty tenements on the High Curve look like grief, like decline, like a problem awaiting a solution. A wise love would walk the curve at the quiet hour — feeling its way, as the residents do, by the warm strips in the pavement, by curb-height, by smell — and understand that the empty houses airing themselves each evening for the dead are not a malfunction. They are how this people, who compost their beloved into the fungal beds without squeamishness and then go on stepping in the same teaspoon-hollow on the stair, practices memory. The house that cycles its locks is a grave that still does house-things. You do not tidy that away to make the living feel less alone. Solitude here is not an event. It is the medium. They have made a culture inside it that is deliberate about touch precisely *because* they are spread thin. That is not failure. That is authorship.

So the power would do what almost no visitor does.

It would learn to be looked past.

It would come down in the cage, if it came in a body at all, and it would not wipe the warm rain off its neck when the brakes jolted. It would keep its gaze low. It would offer its forearm, wrist up, and wait — a long time, by surface standards — to be touched before it tried to speak. It would learn that a hand in infrared is a bright articulated star, and not stare at it. It would apologise, formally, very often: for its noise, for its presence, for the way even a benevolent attention changes the pressure in a small sealed world. In Anath they apologise to cave swifts for the crime of the Hollow existing in their flight path. A power that cannot apologise to a bird has no business touching a civilisation.

Then, mostly, it would sit in the centre.

That is the one place in Anath that no one can inhabit, and it is the heart of it. The empty ball of warm almost-nothing that the whole town is pasted around, which drones cross shielded and sightless, which the inner ear forbids. A wise power could be there without vertigo. It could be the first thing that has ever simply *been* there, not passing through, not netting a swift, not hauling a filter carcass from one side to the other. Not filling the void. Accompanying it.

And because it was there, on rare occasions, a child dared by other children to look up too long across the curve would find, instead of a city about to fall on her, a presence that was looking back without weight. Not a face. Not a light. Just the discovery, arriving in the body before it arrived in thought, that the sky can hold your gaze and not drop anything on you. She would look away quickly, crouching, laughing in the startled way they laugh by touch, flutter against a friend's inner elbow. She would remember it all her life. Nothing in her language would need to change to accommodate it. She would simply be one degree less afraid of the place she lives. That is the size of intervention that does not steal a world from the people who made it.

Everything else it did would be at that scale, and smaller, and chosen from underneath.

It would find the places where the settlement between erosion, technology, and habit is about to stop being a settlement and become an accident they cannot afford: a seam in a heat sink that will not fail this year or this decade but will fail, suddenly, in a generation that has only nine hundred pairs of hands; a slow drift in the contained gradient so gradual that no drone-minder's superstition has yet noticed it in her molars; a contamination gathering in one cistern's fungal beds that will, if left, turn the protein harvest from rubbery and faintly metallic to quietly lethal over three Wet cycles. These it would mend. Not improve. Mend — the way the self-healing ceramic was meant to heal before it learned to heal crooked. It would leave the crazed webs in the walls. It would leave the dished steps that hold a teaspoon of water. It would correct only what removes choice from people who have not consented to lose it, and it would correct it invisibly, in the Less Loud, and it would leave the drones a few of their obsolete routes untouched because a guild that talks to its charges by touch when they dock needs some of its rituals to be useless. Usefulness is not the only form of care.

It would keep the prospecting bore open. Not widen it. Not mark it. Just clear, from time to time, the rubble that slides toward sealing it, so that once or twice a year a swift can still make its mistake, spend its frantic night screaming at echoes that return from every direction at once, be netted, be apologised to, be released. A world needs a way for the outside to blunder in and be forgiven. And it needs a way — narrow, unadvertised, easy to miss — for the inside to blunder out. If a Hollow child ever climbs toward that bore out of something more durable than a dare, she should find that the choice was left physically possible by someone who never told her to take it. The power would not leave a door open. It would leave a door unlocked, which is a completely different kindness.

It would attend, particularly, to the pains that are not constitutive. The fungal bloom in the armpit is weather, and they treat it as weather; let it be. But the child whose infection has crossed some private threshold into fever, in a household keyed to a sleep shift that will not rotate back to her for too long — that fever could break a little earlier than it otherwise would, for reasons no one would think to investigate. The old woman on the High Curve who has migrated to the tenements that tend themselves, whose hands have gone to callous and can still read a sentence at a touch: the cancellation field in her room might hold its unnatural quiet a hand's breadth deeper than the engineering allows, so that when her visitor speaks aloud, low, in the luxury of that metre of stillness, she does not have to spend what hearing she has left on effort. Lovers reserve their voices. So do the dying. Anyone who can widen, by an inch, the space in which a voice can be received without strain has understood the local definition of mercy.

It would not cure their hearing. It would not give them back the surface sky in their eyes. It would not make them many. It would not make them long-lived past the wiry seventies their constant walking already grants them, because a power that edits the lungs and spares the joints on its own initiative will soon be editing everything, and will call the editing love, and will be half right, which is the dangerous fraction.

And it would remember the others.

The blind rats in the service ducts, culled and missed in equal measure by the drones — it would let them go on being missed, and culled, in that persistent unglamorous balance, because they too have negotiated a settlement here. The moths over the vents, powdering themselves against warm walls. The black succulents that open only during venting. The figs, incurably restless, tracking a wind instead of a sun: it would not pity them their false astronomy. It is a true astronomy, for where they live.

At the end — if end is even the right shape for a visit to a place with no day — it would leave nothing to worship and nothing to maintain. No new brass tone added to the horns in the void, because a new tone would be an instruction, and instructions from the centre would reorganise nine hundred lives around a sound they did not compose. No shrine. No struck name. No house suddenly, suspiciously, struck from the rolls.

Only this: that on some shift thereafter, a pump that should have missed a beat does not. A swift finds the bore on its first pass instead of its fiftieth. A child looks up, and is not punished by her own body for looking. An old man on the High Curve sleeps in the thickest part of the quiet and wakes with the faint, unaccountable sense of having been accompanied rather than alone — the medium unchanged, but no longer quite so empty.

And somewhere in the exact centre of Anath Hollow, where down is out in every direction and nothing has ever been able to stay, there would be for a while a warmth that was not a machine's warmth.

Then not even that.

Just the Hollow, keeping its shape inside its settlement. Warm, dark, resonant. A town wrapped around an absence — except that the absence, ever after, would be known by whatever lives there as a place that can be looked at, briefly, and survived, and that somebody once chose to sit in it with them rather than stand outside it and fix them.
