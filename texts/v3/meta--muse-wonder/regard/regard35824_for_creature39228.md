First, they would not lift the grate.

Not at midday, when the light falls straight in and the sparrows are working. Not with any radiance of their own. A benevolent power that arrives shining would be, in that vault, only a larger kind of thrashing. The bat would wake. The frogs would flatten. The whole arrangement would go into the silence that takes a full cycle to forgive, and forgiveness, down there, is not a feeling. It is seventeen minutes of careful reshelving.

So they would wait in the yard.

That would be the first act, and from the outside it would look like no act at all: to sit in the heat on cracked concrete that no one wants, among salt grass and bottle caps, in a place officially consisting of a pump house, a levee, and nothing else, and to be uninteresting. To let the day bake. To listen through the ground for a relay ticking.

And after a while — not quickly, because wisdom here would be mostly a matter of learning the local speed — they would understand what had drawn them. Not the fish. Not the bat, though the bat is new, and perfect, folded into the exact absence left by a removed clapper. Not the numbered snails, though 7 and 12 and 19 are enough to break a certain kind of heart: somebody else, once, crouched down there long enough to care in the form of numerals, and then had the discipline to leave.

What had drawn them was the fullness behaving like one being. A creature whose organs happen to be separate species. Whose blood is a road of ants. Whose heartbeat is a pump behind a mesh guard. Whose breath rises and falls two inches, tea-brown, every seventeen minutes. A being that is not ancient, not pristine, not natural in any clean sense — born in a utility box, a few years old, still assembling itself at speed because the world above gave it no other pocket in which to exist.

And a young, crowded, improvised being like that is very easy to kill by helping.

They would not enlarge the vault. That is the temptation, and they would refuse it. More room sounds like mercy until you see how precisely everyone has already paid for the room they have: the fish narrowed to a pipe, the gecko flattened to a tile, the frog reduced to a thumbnail and filed between brackets. Enlarge it, and the strata loosen. New space would not be peace. It would be an unclaimed country, and then a contest, and then winners.

They would not clean the water. They would not sweeten the iron-and-bleach tang out of the condensate, because the moss coins have learned to drink what travels upward, and the greenness that would explode in cleaner water would be eaten, or would choke, or would rot. They would not feed anyone. They would not rescue the fish to the river — the river it arrived from, the wide life it was presumably meant for — because the fish has turned its face to a current that is not always there and has made a whole existence out of anticipation, and because being removed by a kind hand is still being removed.

They would not take the plastic house away as litter. The mice are using the door.

No. If they did anything, it would be done at the edges, upstream, in the world of yards and paperwork and men with spray tanks — the world where this place can be ended casually, without malice, by someone who never knows it is there.

So a work order would go missing. Not dramatically. A form for re-sealing inspection chambers, printed after the flood, fills a whole season with correct signatures and then settles at the bottom of the wrong tray in an office where the fan is loud and the clerk is tired, and by the time it surfaces the budget has moved on.

A contractor hired to clear "vermin" from the pump house would find, on the morning of the job, that his van will not start, and when it does start, that he has been sent — by an error nobody can quite trace in the dispatch log — to the identical pump house at the identical works two valleys away, where there is, in fact, a wasps' nest in a junction box and honest work to do.

A new grate, when the old one finally rusts, would arrive spec'd almost exactly like it: same slot width for the sparrows' beaks, same weight, same four bottle caps' worth of obstruction. The hinge would be sharp where the concrete spalled. These things can be arranged by a power that understands that holiness, for some creatures, is not beauty or safety. It is continuity of slot-width. It is a pump that keeps clearing its throat.

A flood, in some future spring, would be allowed to come — but not the scouring kind. The kind that arrives under the levee at pressure, lifts the snails into their slow carousel, and brings, perhaps, one new thing. A water-beetle. A seed. A second fern. Not many. One arrival is an event the whole chamber can negotiate, frog-width by frog-width. Ten arrivals is an invasion. The power would stand, if standing is the word, with one metaphorical hand on the river's shoulder, saying: *this much, and no more — they are full, they are not finished, there is a difference.*

And once in a very great while, at dusk, when the shaft of light is gone and the heat on the largest pipe is the only sun left, they might do the thing that cannot be done from the yard.

They would make themselves small. Not metaphorically. Actually. They would take up a volume — exact, negotiated, no more than the volume of a crouching human, of a knee — and descend feet first down the four rungs, moving at the speed of woodlice. They would let the ant road reroute across them. They would let the midges re-form around them and something small and warm settle behind where an ear would be, if they had arranged to have an ear. They would stay for three cycles, no longer than a visitor may stay, feeling it happen through the soles of them: relay, lamps, the pale answer of throats; pump; rise; the floating pavement of snails turning unhurried around the drowned valve wheel; the fish ascending in its column, facing what comes before it comes.

They would not bless it aloud. Sound is taken into silt and soft bodies down there, and some things should not have to absorb a blessing.

They would simply let themselves be accommodated — and, in being accommodated, learn the only fact about this being that cannot be learned from above: that it is not fragile in the way it appears. Remove one gecko and the seam stays gecko-shaped. Remove the whole grate and it dies of light and sparrows. Its fragility is not in its members. It is in its unmarkedness, its timing, its hundred meters of hostile concrete that say to everything passing: *nothing here, keep going.* Its protection is that it is beneath notice, in both senses.

So, climbing out backwards, displacing the bat and the air column and the last of the light, the power would do the hardest thing in their repertoire, the thing that separates benevolence from appetite:

They would leave the place exactly as full as they found it.

No feather. No shining stone. No new number painted tenderly on the largest unnumbered snail — because they would have understood, by then, that 7, 12, 19 are not a census but a temptation, and that the one who painted them and never returned did the last part right.

Only, perhaps, up in the yard, as the grate clangs home, one small correction. Four bottle caps sat in the slots. If one had shifted during the opening — if the sparrows, tomorrow, would find the pattern wrong — they would set it back. Cap for cap. Slot for slot. The unimportant surface of the world restored to ordinariness with a care usually reserved for altars.

And then they would go, and stay gone in the manner this place requires: not absent, but unrecorded. A slight coolness in the dispatch office. A lost form. A river reminded, once a year, to knock and not to break. A hundred meters of baking concrete that remains, to every official eye, empty — while underneath it, unmarked, unrescued, un improved, the crowded world closes over another vacancy and goes on breathing in small sounds, snail over silt, gecko over warm iron, frog by frog by frog,

seventeen minutes at a time.
