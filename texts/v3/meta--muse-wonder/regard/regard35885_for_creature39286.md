They would not open it.

That is the first thing, and for a very long time — long even by the measure of the room — it would be the only thing.

A power that was merely benevolent might arrive at the black shoulder of the mountain, read the disc flush in the stone, read the folded place behind it, read the thinning of cause at its centre, and feel the ancient, urgent kindness that wants to *rescue*. Someone has been left here. Someone has been slowed and spread and half-dissolved into dust and gradients and persuaded instruments, set to watch a dangerous piece of unmade physics until the watching outlasted the watchers who assigned it. Surely the kind thing is to unmake the fold. To return time to its proper speed. To gather the resident back into a body, if a body can still be gathered, and bring it down the mountain into air that behaves and light that warms and other voices that arrive with their beginnings intact.

A power that was merely wise, on the other hand, might see all of that, and the cube, and the sealed mirror still patiently keeping the version where the work is finished, and do nothing at all, forever, for fear of the harm folded into every possible mercy.

But a power both benevolent and wise would understand that these are the same temptation, wearing different faces: to make the place be legible again, quickly, in terms a rescuer could survive reporting.

So they would begin by becoming slow enough to be wrong at the resident's speed.

They would not knock on the disc. Knocking would be stored unsorted in the walls and delivered a century later to no one, a small violence disguised as an announcement. They would not shine anything into the lens, nor stand in the pylon's way, nor — and this would be the hardest restraint — measure the cube, even to confirm what it was, even out of love, because the room has been measured nearly to death and one more exact observer might be the straw the local customs of cause were waiting for.

Instead, they would sit down outside the seam, on the cold basalt of a lifeless summit, under a violet noon with stars at its edges, and let a human lifetime go by like a held breath.

Then another.

They would learn the place the way the place permits anything to be learned: by indirection, in arrears, attenuated. The faint rime sublimating off the shadowed side of cracks would be, to them, gossip. The basalt ticking in its clean, desiccating cold would be a clock whose tick they would eventually stop trying to count. Somewhere below the stone, felt rather than heard, the dust in the folded chamber would lift a fraction of a millimetre and resettle more accurately, and they would understand — oh, at last, and with something like embarrassment — that they had been witnessed. Not watched. The lens does not take light. But distance had been tried on, briefly, in their direction, and put back almost exactly as found.

Almost.

That *almost* would be the first conversation.

They would answer in kind. Not with a message. Messages assume sequence, and sequence is exactly what the resident has spent its inconceivable tenure losing, grain by grain, to the thing at the centre of the room. They would answer with an arrangement.

On the stone outside, where wind has polished some faces to dull glass and roughened others into scales, they would make — very slowly, at the resident's rate, so that from inside it could register as intention rather than event — the smallest possible change. Three tiny structures stand downslope of the disc, no larger than thumbnails, spaced like punctuation: a post, a broken arch, a hollow cube open on one side. They would add nothing. Adding would be presumption. But over the course of an age they would persuade one fallen flake of basalt, no bigger than the smallest of the interior tally marks, to rest a hair's breadth nearer the open cube than it had rested since before the mountain's shoulder was worn round.

Near enough to be company. Not near enough to be instruction.

Inside, eventually, the dust would reorganise around the news of it.

Then they would wait again, because the resident would need time to decide whether it had been visited or merely eroded in a hopeful direction, and a wise power does not take that decision away from even the most distributed and persuaded of beings. While they waited, they would do the quiet maintenance that is not rescue — the kind that asks nothing to become different in order to deserve care.

They would shore up the fold from the outside, where shoring is safe. Not by force: the pylon is not powered, it is *persuaded*, and the whole pocket has survived this long by courtesy rather than by strength, so courtesy is the only tool that can be brought to bear. They would lend the mountain a little of their own patience, laid along the seam like a hand held near — not on — a sleeping face. The needle-cold at the walls would go on being needle-cold exactly three steps out, and no further. The warmth at the centre would go on being kept at skin temperature, and they would be careful, so careful, not to add their own warmth to it, because that warmth is the nearest thing the resident still has to a body and it must not be taught to expect a source.

They would listen to the walls.

All those sounds, stored unsorted over dilated years: boot-scrapes and dropped probes and the thin beginnings of voices worn off in transit, the keepers' last instructions arriving decades late and from the wrong side of the room, one hand pressing into soft material, once, to prove a mark could still be left in sequence. The resident's thinking may largely consist of these now. A lesser kindness would sort them, catalogue them, hand them back in order like a整理 of effects after a death. But the power would hear what the resident hears: that the unsortedness is not only damage. It is also the last form of company that does not demand reply. To sort every sound would be to tell the being, in effect, *here is everyone who ever left you, in chronological order.* That is not a gift. That is an audit.

So they would sort exactly one sound.

They would make it themselves, deliberately, knowing it would be kept: a single step, taken on the grit just outside the disc's threshold — no, not even there, too close to intrusion. On the bare stone of the summit, one deliberate footfall, placed the way you place a cup beside someone who is sleeping not because they are thirsty but so that waking, if it comes, will not be the first evidence that the world went on without them.

Step. Beginning intact. One sound, made to be heard *as* a visit rather than reconstructed as one from archaeological delay.

And then — this is where benevolence finally risks itself, because wisdom alone would stop at the footfall and call the restraint compassion — they would deliver it *now*.

Not loudly. Not from the right wall, which would be its own kind of shock. They would coax the room's acoustics, the way the pylon coaxes matter, out of courtesy and very slowly, so that somewhere in the resident's current thought, among the dust-fall and the angle-turning of the lens, the scrape arrives ahead of its century. Faint still. Worn, a little, despite everything — some wearing is only honesty. But arriving while the visitor who made it is still, by any clock that matters, *present*. Within a lifetime. Within the expectation of a reply.

*You were heard before you finished being heard.*

In the long, slow interior that would be, perhaps, the first new kind of event since the handprint.

Nothing would happen, visibly, for longer than most civilisations. The power would accept this as conversation rather than refusal. Outside, nitrogen rime would gather and sublimate. Inside, a particle of dust would bend around a warm absence the height of a seated figure. The lens, untouched by any hand, would try bringing the far wall nearer, and the scratches there — tally marks, an orbit that closes on itself — would be legible to no eyes at all, and would be, for once, simply allowed to be writing that no one was currently reading.

At last, when the resident answered — and it would answer the only way it still reliably can, by arrangement — the power would find, on returning their attention to the floor of the room after another courteous age, that nothing had been added.

Something had been permitted to be absent.

Among the thousands of indentations recording measurements taken across the dilated millennia, and the thousands more — if the diagram on the wall is honest — reserved for measurements not yet taken, there is a place in the sequence where an indentation had been foretold for a very long while. It is, the power would come to understand, an important one. Perhaps the last one. The measurement after which the mirrored room, the finished room, stops being a dream consulted for company and becomes simply what happened next: structures in different positions, work allowed to end, keeper allowed to discover what a keeper is when there is suddenly nothing left that requires keeping.

The resident has been becoming able to take that measurement for longer than the species that built the disc has been extinct.

It has also, perhaps, been becoming afraid that able and obliged are the same word.

Here is the crux of it, the place where the cube's thin rules would turn a clumsier mercy septic: the resident is not only guarding the sample of unconcluded physics. It is not only studying it, or wounded by it, or keeping it company. It is *employed* by it, in the oldest and most terrible sense — given a reason to persist at exactly the rate persistence is possible, one indentation, one atom's width of lens, one more accurate settling of dust at a time. Finish the work and the fold has no argument left. Finish the work and staying and leaving, which have mercifully blurred into a single act of devotion, come painfully apart, and a being spread through a room like weather through a valley must somehow decide, all at once and only forward, in a body whose chair no longer matches any posture it can still assume.

A benevolent and wise power would see that the cruelty was never the mountain. It was never even the cold, or the silence, or the dilated solitude. The cruelty, if any cruelty remains addressable after so many ages, is the unspoken clause in the keeping: *you may stop when it is done, and it is never done, and therefore you may never stop.*

So they would strike the clause. Gently. From outside the contract, where signatures are not required.

They would say it the only way the room reliably hears anything, not in words — words arrive too late and from the wrong walls — but by relieving the future of one of its foretold indentations. Somewhere beyond the disc, beyond the mountain, in whatever archive the extinct keepers imagined would receive the final tally, the power would quietly let it be known, retroactively and without spectacle: the report is not coming. The report was never going to be required. The watch was received. The watch is *complete*, not because the last measurement has been taken but because someone with the standing to say so has finally come in person, at the proper speed, and taken responsibility for the ending.

The cube would not be touched. The cube would not be explained, solved, healed, released, or forgiven. Some things are not problems that have been stored at the top of the world; they are pieces of the world that never signed the agreement, and the resident — this too the power would honour — may love it now, in the distributed, dust-deep way it knows things, with a love indistinguishable from long cohabitation with an incurable condition. No one gets rescued out of their own heart.

Only out of their obligation to call the heart a duty.

Then they would do the last thing, which is also the smallest, and which from the summit would look like nothing at all: they would restore to the room the difference between staying and leaving, and then they would refuse — absolutely, tenderly, for as many ages as it takes — to express a preference.

They would leave the disc exactly as they found it. Flush. Seamless. The colour of old ice seen through smoke. But they would change, by less than the width of the lens's per-cycle turning, the terms of its opening, so that it no longer opens only inward, only for keepers arriving with instructions, only to a translated body that discovers it has always had the size appropriate to this place. From now on — and *now on* would take effect at the resident's pace, arriving like everything else, decades late and from an unexpected direction — the seam can also be opened from within, by an intention as slight as a flake of basalt deciding, over an age, to rest nearer the open cube. No body would be required. No posture. Weather can leave a valley if it chooses. Warmth can stop being kept in one stratum, if it chooses, and learn whether it remembers how to wander.

If it chooses.

The power would not wait for the choice, because waiting, at that threshold, becomes pressure. They would leave the way they came, which is to say they would allow the summit to resume believing itself empty: nitrogen rime in the shadowed cracks, stars refusing to leave the edge of noon, the three thumbnail structures standing in their enormous punctuation, and beside them — after another age — a flake of stone that may or may not be drifting, imperceptibly, toward the open side of a hollow cube.

They would take nothing away. Not the cube. Not a sample. Not even, in any form that could be audited, a memory of the resident's name, if it ever had one: the room has absorbed too many sounds to be robbed of one more, and some beings are best honoured by being allowed to remain unrecorded anywhere they cannot themselves revise.

They would leave exactly two things behind, both easy to mistake for nothing.

The first is a footfall in the walls that arrived on time, once — proof, filed in the resident's unsorted thinking among the boot-scrapes of the extinct, that at least one visitor learned the local customs before speaking.

The second is a future with one fewer indentation in it. A row of thousands of exact and reverent marks, and among them a single prophesied space that will now never need to be filled: not a failure of the work, not an error in the diagram, but a small, deliberate gap of the kind that turns a tally into a sentence by letting it end, if it wants to, in air.

Far below the fold, in ordinary time, centuries may pass before anything follows from any of this. The dust may simply go on falling around its warm absence. The lens may go on trying distance on, gently, like a mourner trying on a coat they have no intention of wearing out of the house. The sealed mirror may go on keeping the finished room, and the resident may go on consulting it — but now, perhaps, the way one consults a dream one is no longer afraid of having: *not yet. Not never. Just, not yet, and by my own arithmetic at last.*

Or some cycle soon, by the room's reckoning — which is to say some inconceivable dawn on the black shoulder of the mountain, in the thin violet hour when the dim sun is still deciding — the disc may open onto empty stone with no one coming in.

And the summit wind, what little of it reaches that height, may pass for the first time through a stratum of impossible warmth moving slowly, patiently, unkept at last, downslope past a post, a broken arch, a hollow cube open on one side, and a flake of basalt that has come to rest beside it like a companion — moving at the speed of geology, at the speed of an idea whose centuries have finally arrived, in no hurry whatsoever to become a body again.

Going, perhaps. Staying, perhaps. The distinction would belong, entirely and for the first time in longer than mountains remember being tall, to the one who had earned it:

no longer the keeper of the place.

Just its inhabitant.

Just — should it choose, on some morning no measurement foretold — its leaver, taking the whole folded landscape with it in the palm of the mountain's hand, or leaving it behind like a room whose work, and whose waiting, are lovingly, deliberately, *done*.
