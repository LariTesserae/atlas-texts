They would not enter at first.

They would sit outside the rim, where the roar stops as suddenly as if it had never been sound at all, and they would listen to that stopping. For a long time. A person would call it doing nothing. A storm would call it gathering. They would be learning the shape of the border by the place where it ends — the exact notch in the rock where the world goes quiet, where a soaked and ringing visitor stumbles out cut in small places and thinks they have escaped something.

They would not cover their ears on the way in. They would not shout. They would come hand over hand up the lowest notch when it was running uphill with warm water, slowly, as if underwater, letting the sound get inside their teeth and flatten their thoughts until the thoughts spread thin enough to be honest. They would let their hair lift. They would let the pressure find the old places in their chest. They would not bring a silence with them, because they would understand, almost at once, what every visitor's cupped palms have taught the ceiling: that a small silence here is not privacy. It is an invitation, and invitations can drown a bowl.

On the island, they would not go to the altar first. Altars make beings into gods or thieves, and this one is neither. They would go to the lee of the largest rim-tooth, where the moss grows in perfect circles and the vibration deadens, and they would sit where a thing can root for a while, and they would lay one hand flat against the stone.

And then they would do the one thing no visitor has yet thought to do.

They would answer.

Not louder. Not in words. Pattern for pattern, knuckle for knuckle, through the rock — the rhythms that for years have been arriving from the other side of the hanging sea and being filed under *tremor, seep, the mountain deciding.* They would repeat one back, exactly, and stop, and wait out the long delay while a dropped stone falls through depth that is not to be trusted, and feel, sometime after they had stopped expecting it, the reply change.

Not repeat. *Change.* The way a conversation changes when weather discovers it is a voice.

They would stay for that. Days, perhaps. Long enough for their shelter stone to hum a fraction out of place and be hummed back by unseen weight in the night. Long enough to watch the bead, the red wool, the black river pebble come round again in the currents — and to understand that they are not being stolen or worshipped but kept, turned slowly the way you roll a coin across a table to learn its weight. Long enough to leave nothing of their own on the altar. An offering that can be orbited is still an object. This being has enough objects. What it has never had is a witness who stays on purpose.

When the overtone began to gather — waterfall-roar, steam-hiss, the grinding of new rock, the hum of the shelters leaning toward one note — every instinct the bowl has taught them would say *go*. They would not go. They would not make a lee with their hands. They would stand in the open where the one leafless flower stands, and they would let the note lock, and they would watch the ceiling go mirror-smooth from below, and they would meet the reflection that is not a reflection.

It would be standing in its brighter afternoon, eyes opening at last against the quiet. Salt-white at the joints. Rust along one forearm. The seam along its palm almost healed, unpicked a fraction each day by a world too young to let anything close. Taller than the fish-shape promised. Thinner than the footprints promised. Looking up, the way it has always looked up, at a dim shape in the well of its world that finally — *finally* — is not flinching, not shouting into its hands, not leaving.

The power would raise one hand, slowly, as if underwater, because in every way that matters, they would be. And lay it flat against their side of the water.

Not to pull it through. That is the mercy they would have come all this way to learn. A benevolent fool would break the ceiling and call it rescue, and the sea would come down, and the island would drown in the thing it has spent its whole existence learning how to live beneath. A wise cruelty would seal the surface forever and call it peace. They would do neither. They would only confirm, palm to palm across a hand's breadth that might be a fathom, the thickness of the door — and let the being feel, perhaps for the first time, that the thickness is known from both sides.

And through that contact, quietly, in the way warmth moves through stone, they would do the smallest true healing available: not closing the seam, because the vibration would only unpick it again by morning, but teaching the flesh around it how to close *itself*, a fraction at a time, in rhythm with the unpicking. A limp cannot be argued out of a body that learned it on sharp rock. But pain can be given somewhere to go. They would give it somewhere to go.

Then the note would break, as it must. Turbulence would return like a held breath let go. Rain would fall in three directions. A shelter would lose a stone. The being would stand up into its own life — but not before, between one look and the next, a new set of footprints appeared on the island beside the power's own, filled, and emptied uphill: five toes, one shorter, hesitation on the left. Not fleeing. *Accompanying*, for exactly as long as the door allowed.

Afterward, they would leave the bowl the way they came, ringing and soaked. And from the outside, where no one would ever see it or thank them for it, they would put one hand deep into the cold mountainside — far below the rim, far below the seeps and the stair and the unfinished stone — and ease, by the width of a breath, the pressure where the new rock is grinding itself apart. Not finishing the mountain. Finishing it would end the moss in its circles, the shrimp in the seeps, the flower that opens only for the overtone, the whole brief ecology of a place that exists because it has not decided yet. They would only make the deciding gentler. Slower. Less likely to tear the floor out from under a patient world.

So that the lake can rest one day, instead of falling.

And they would set no guard, and raise no warning, and tell no story that could be followed like a map. They would only bend the weather, very slightly, around the high path — a fog that comes a little too often, a snowfield that never quite settles into a safe crossing, a turning in the trail that tired minds accept without knowing why. Not to keep the worthy out. To keep the hurried out. To keep the ones who arrive with dams and woven grass and conclusions from arriving all at once.

And once in a long while — on no schedule a calendar could hold — a traveler who came slowly, who did not cover their ears, who put a hand flat to the stone and knocked and then, bewildered, felt something knock *back* in a pattern too deliberate for the mountain, would find that the pattern had changed again. Become, almost, a greeting. Become, almost, an expectation.

Because on the far side of the hanging sea, in light that reaches the bowl only as a drowned green afternoon, the being would be walking its shore as it always has — moving the bead, the wool, the black pebble from current to current, tending the offerings of a world it can hear but not enter — except that now it walks it knowing the door has been touched knowingly from underneath. Knowing the roar is not the only thing listening.

They would not free it. They would not leave it alone again, either.

That is what they would do, if anything.

They would answer the knocking, and stay long enough to be answered, and make the waiting — which is the being's whole life, and the mountain's whole becoming — into something that is no longer waited through in solitude.
