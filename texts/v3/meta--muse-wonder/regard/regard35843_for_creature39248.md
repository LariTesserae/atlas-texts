They would learn the etiquette first.

That is how you would know they were wise, and how you would know they were benevolent. Any lesser power would arrive with help.

They would come up the long grade — not appearing, suddenly, in a column of light over the intake dam, because light up here is already too much, noon and midnight the same thin blue — but slowly, the way everything true up here arrives. As a pressure changing. As weather considering itself.

And they would stop shouting almost immediately. On the second day, like the crews.

For the first long while — and a while for such a power might be fifty summers, a hundred, time enough for the moss to thicken a hand's breadth and take the feet of three more pylons — they would do nothing that looked like doing. They would stay on the gravel. They would stay on the boardwalk.

Because the being who lives here does not need to be rescued, improved, uplifted, or understood. It has already solved the place.

Everything else failed at noise, at brightness, at wet, at slowness. The ravens solved it by being louder and brief. The ptarmigan solved it by being still and brief. The swans solved it by standing far away. The humans, most honestly of all, solved it by admitting they cannot live here, and inventing the two-week rotation, the fogged cabin, the drying room, the whiteboard conversation — a way to tend the turbines without pretending to belong.

The moss solved it by belonging completely. By being surface instead of resisting surface. Low enough for the wind to go over it. Wet enough that the water is not an event but a condition. Slow enough that a footprint is not an insult to be answered but a shape to be held, patiently, for ten summers. It does not hurry to colonize the orange doors and the concrete weirs and the corrugated roof. It is already doing so, at the speed at which colonizing lasts.

A foolish power would speed that up. A kind but unwise power would make it conscious in our image, make it speak above the roar, make it know it is beautiful. That would be a cruelty here, where hearing has been abolished as a useful sense and where consciousness would have to listen, forever, to the fall and the tailrace and the wires singing in the wind, and never be able to put on ear defenders.

A wise one would see that a skin you could lift in your hand, alive from edge to edge, drinking from below and above at once, is not a lesser form of life for not having a center. It has a thousand private movements of water going nowhere in a hurry. It has enough.

So they would not kneel to listen to it. Nobody who stays more than a day kneels.

They would protect, instead, the conditions that allow it to be left alone.

Not with a wall. Walls would cast shade, and shade here is a violence. Not with a proclamation. The warning sign on the bridge is already rusting, already being spotted green, and that is as it should be.

More quietly than that.

They would see to it that the road stays gravel, switchbacking through nothing — passable, but never quite inviting. That plans for a second, larger station, whenever men in the lowlands unroll them in warm rooms and point with clean fingers, acquire some small, persistent flaw: a survey that will not quite close, a cost that will not quite justify itself, a minister who falls asleep at the decisive paragraph and wakes vaguely unwilling. Not sabotage. Just the world, nudged to keep hesitating.

They would see to it that the river still rises at four in the afternoon with the day's melt, and falls at dawn, the one reliable daily event. That the icecaps at either horizon recede, if they must recede, grain by grain like the reservoir silting, and not in a single bright catastrophe of water that would tear the skin from the young rock and start the ten-thousand-year work over.

They would see to it that the humans keep coming in rotation, and never in settlement. No town gathers. No child is obliged to learn permanence here. The cabins stay half-buried, the boots stay wet from the knees down, the crews keep handing one another, along with the keys, the story of the arctic fox seen last August — because the fox should remain in the tense of rumor, and the humans should remain in the tense of guests. Their guilt as they walk the boardwalk is not a bad thing. It is accurate. They are learning, in their shoulders and their hands, what it is to be entertained on strict terms by a host who offers no conversation.

Perhaps, in the end, they would do one small, positive thing, on the moss's own terms, so small it would take a century to confirm.

Somewhere out beyond the boardwalk, where an old footprint — from before the signs, before the etiquette, some summer boot pressed too confidently and left its shape still legible — has been holding water a fraction too deep for the woolly moss to close over, they would let that one hollow drain by the width of a hair. Not heal it. Healing would erase the lesson. Just make its eventual closing possible. Next summer, no. The summer after that, no. Ten summers from now, a green lip beginning.

That is a gift a surface can accept.

And they would do one thing for the visitors, too, because benevolence does not stop at the resident. For the workers, when they go back down a thousand meters to farms and towns and trees, they would leave the ringing in their ears one hour less than expected, now and then. And in sleep down there, where it is finally dark and finally quiet, they would put not the roar but the other sound — the intimate trickle under the moss, the water moving through a living skin — so that they wake not quite knowing why they will walk more carefully that day on ordinary ground.

Then the power would go, if going is the word, the way mist goes off the plateau after sideways rain: not departing upward, but thinning until it is indistinguishable from attention itself.

No mark. No footprint, which up here is the highest form of respect ever devised.

Behind them, the turbines would turn and turn at exactly the speed they were set to. The ravens would shout hoarsely from the roof into a noise louder than their own. The swans would stand white and silent in far pools. The light would come up off a thousand mirrors as hard as it came down.

And the resident would continue — bright, cold, soaking, deafening, a few centimeters to half a meter thick over young rock, colonizing manufacture by millimeters, holding the shape of everything that has ever pressed upon it and slowly, slowly outliving the pressure.

Magnificently unoccupied, except for being everywhere.

A wise power would not find that empty.

They would find it complete, and keep it so by the hardest miracle of all: knowing when not to stay.
