They would arrive intending to help.

That is how you can tell they are benevolent. It is how they learn, very slowly, standing in that thin air while their own breath falls straight down as frost, that benevolence is not yet wisdom.

At first, from far away, the plateau looks like a problem to be solved. So cold. So dim. So little light, so little food, so little speed. Everything there living at the very edge of what living should be able to do: lichen eating stone, moss taking a century to cross a footprint, great warm beasts feeding on mineral crust because there is nothing else, and turning themselves into furnaces because the world will not warm them.

The impulse is immediate, and kind, and catastrophic.

*Give them more sun.*
*Thicken the air.*
*Break the high-pressure dome and let weather in, let rain in.*
*Make the growing season longer. Make the melt-pans deeper. Teach them — give them — fire outside the body, tools outside the teeth, shelter, speech, storage, all the external things that would let them stop spending two hundred years just to walk and eat and not freeze.*

The power could do it. That is the danger.

So first, they do nothing, for a long time. Longer than feels like mercy. They learn how to be there without becoming weather.

They make themselves small, and then they make themselves slow, and then — hardest of all — they make themselves quiet in the ways this place is quiet. No radiance. No voice from the sky. No pressure in the teeth that does not belong. They pad their own footfall until the permafrost carries almost nothing. Because even attention, here, is a physical thing. A herd reads the ground for kilometres. A gaze with weight behind it would be felt, investigated, endured — and everything here endures too much already to be asked to endure being watched by a god.

And in that long, disciplined not-doing, the understanding changes.

The cold is not an enemy the beasts have failed to defeat. It is the condition that shaped the victory. Warm the plateau by three degrees and the melt runs instead of lingering; the pans drain, the algae blooms too early for the hatch, the fishers strike at nothing. Thicken the air and sound carries, breath carries, disease carries. Give them fire and you have given them a mouth that is always open, a chimney they cannot close, a reason to gather, to stay, to cut their sixty-year circuit short. Give them tools and within ten generations the grown teeth dull, the gut cultures thin, the inherited industry — smelter, circuitry, seeder-glands, bacteria passed calf-ward like a craft no single mind holds — begins to be forgotten by bodies that no longer need to remember.

They are not primitive versions of something else. They are not waiting.

Nor are they suffering in the way the power first imagined suffering. A calf taken by an ambusher at the thinning edge of the field is not a tragedy the herd is asking to have abolished; remove the ambushers and the calves multiply past the lichen, past the stone's slow ability to be scraped and seeded and made soil, and the herd eats its own future in a single spiral. A Furnace that stops after three centuries and subsides into rusting ribs is not asking for more life. It *is* more life, for a hundred years after its death — beetle, burrower, lichen, ore. To rescue everything would be to starve everything, more kindly and more completely.

There is no prayer here. That matters.

No song, no upward face, no artifact pointed at the sky, no young hidden in a nest and crying to be fed by heaven. Their technology is anatomy. Their culture is inheritance of microbes. Their communication is pressure through frozen ground and a brief brightening of dots along the throat at dusk. If there is a soul in this place — and the power, who has seen souls in stranger arrangements, believes there is — it is not located in one beast. It is in the circuit: Breaker, Furnace, Seeder, cleaner, burrower, glider, the wake of moss sixty years behind, the soil ten years ahead. A single animal is a word. The herd is a sentence so slow that only something very old can hear it finish.

You do not improve a sentence by shouting the rest of it for them.

So what do they do, this benevolent and wise power, having come with hands full of light?

They turn around. They work at the edges, where working will never be noticed.

Out beyond the rim, where the storms already circle and break, they quietly strengthen what the plateau built for itself. Not by much. Just enough to answer a world changing elsewhere: the slow warming creeping up from lower latitudes, the dark dust of distant burning settling molecule by molecule on pale frost, where one soot-darkened spring could drink the sun and start a melt this place has no rivers to survive. These things were not part of the argument the plateau had finished. They are new, and they come from outside, and they carry no lesson worth learning.

So the dust is lifted before it lands, too finely for any whisker to register.
The warming is bled off at altitude, into the already-broken storms at the rim.
A survey orbit, human or otherwise, passing overhead finds its instruments politely uncertain in exactly the way instruments are uncertain over uninteresting geology. Coordinates drift by a margin no one bothers to correct. The industrial sprawl resolves, on second and third scans, into ore deposits, into glacial erratics, into nothing worth the fuel to land on. A name in a catalogue becomes a classification, becomes an archived error.

Not a wall. Walls invite climbing. Just a vast, maintained uninterestingness — the plateau protected in the only camouflage it has ever used: being misread as empty.

And contamination, the one true accident: a boot-print from the few who did land, carrying in its tread an off-world microbe bright and fast and stupidly hungry, the kind of quick life that could get into a melt-pan and bloom wrong, get into a calf's first mouthful of its mother's seeding and burn through the gut-fire like gossip through a village — that, the power removes. Carefully. Cell by cell, without heat, without scar, until the print is only a print again, damp at the bottom, ready for the proper moss. This is not interference. This is taking back interference. This is sweeping the doorstep of a house you were never invited into.

They do not save the straying calf.
They do not lengthen a life.
They do not leave a mark, a myth, a sudden mutation for the better. A myth would be a weight on the ground-pattern for a thousand years. These people speak in pressure. The worst thing a god could do here is become something they have to keep saying.

Once — only once, and perhaps this is indulgence, and perhaps even wisdom is allowed one — as a herd passes in the long polar dusk, heads down, throat-lights brightening and dimming in their slow exchange, the power sets down, very far off and very gently, a single answering pressure. Not a word. There is no word. Just the permafrost equivalent of: *something was here, and heard the tempo, and matched it.*

Along the nearest basin rim, over three days, a few rows of blue-white dots brighten, puzzled, in the direction of empty stone.

Then dim.

Then the herd, in its own good time, moves on, and the power is careful to be already gone before curiosity could become following, before following could become story.

In the end they take one thing and leave one thing, and both are invisible.

They take the memory — whole, exact, beloved — of a fullness that human senses misclassify as absence: millions of tonnes of life conducting its business in static and heat and conducted pressure, at centimetres an hour, in near-darkness, manufacturing weather and soil and metal out of lichen and stone. They carry it away with them into whatever councils such powers keep, as evidence. Evidence that flourishing does not always grow louder, brighter, faster. Evidence against the next well-meaning intervention, somewhere else, on some other quiet world.

And they leave the plateau time. Not more life, not better life — just its own life, unhurried and unobserved, for as many millions of years as stone and circuit can hold. Time in which a Seeder plants for Furnaces not yet born. Time in which a footprint fills, freezes, thaws, and greens. Time in which nothing has to become anything else in order to have been enough.

Nothing is built. Nothing is said.

In a decade, in the shallow, steaming oval where the power once stood learning how to stand, something dark will be growing in the frost — very slowly, in the cold, indistinguishable from everything else that was already beginning there.

Which is to say: exactly as it should be.
