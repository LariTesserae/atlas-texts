The power would arrive as visitors arrive: over the pass, footsore, a little sharp-edged from the scree, unprepared for seeing it all at once.

And it would do what visitors do. It would become agitated toward what should have been dusk.

Not at once. At first there would be only pleasure, and the pleasure would be real, which is important. A lesser power — a kind one, perhaps, but not a wise one — would skip that part. It would see a city out of season with itself and begin diagnosing. This one would sit on a white stone terrace with its forearms in the kind, finished light, one warm and one cool, and watch a goat cross a roof by ancient professional right, and a woman replace a slate that was not yet broken, and foam-green frogs arrange themselves along a pond rim, plus one, and it would understand before it thought: *oh. It is not broken. It is being sung.*

That understanding would take a while. Benevolent powers are not immune to bewilderment merely because they are powerful; in fact bewilderment may be one of the few honest experiences left to them. Its body, or the body it had courteously assumed for the altitude, would keep waiting for the light to turn. Its eyes would ache without tiredness. Its blood would look for evening and not find it and look again. An Ilvarran — a rope-ender's daughter, say, with damp cuffs and plum juice still ghosting her chin though she is forty, or sixty, or the adequate age for grandmothers here — would bring it soup before asking it anything, and show it how to weight the shutter-curtain with the smooth stone, and how to let its own hands make night.

It would sleep four times. It would learn not to raise its voice.

Only then would the temptations begin, and they would be considerable, because it would be benevolent, and benevolence without wisdom is just intervention with clean hands.

It could give them sunset.

That would be the first and loveliest temptation. It could loosen whatever holds the light — spell, geology, god, habit hardened into physics — just enough to let the blue go down through gold into the dark they have never once been given, only made. It could give them stars unobstructed by shutter-weave. It could give them dawn, which they have never had: the discovery that waking might be met halfway by the world. Children rocked in black cradles could see a sky doing what their parents' voices do, lowering, darkening, returning changed.

It could give them seasons. Fruit after blossom, not beside it. A tree obliged to trust that green follows bare. They have stored grain and cloud-fields and terraces beyond any need for faith, but faith of that particular kind — that what has vanished will come back in another form because the year turns — is not available to a people to whom nothing ever vanishes enough to need returning.

It could give them faster aging, and therefore generations, and therefore history instead of archive. *Bridges sound. Yield as expected. City in excellent condition*, written in the same hand for three hundred years, because a hand that only copies has nothing else to report. It could let their faces record time the way their walls refuse to. It could let a teacher of rope-knotting become unmistakably old in her student's lifetime, and die, and be missed in a way that alters the rope.

It could give them, in short, accumulation: of grief, of novelty, of passion, of wealth, of error. All the things Ilvarra has been exquisitely arranged, by whom or what no tablet says, never to permit.

And each gift, held up in the unchanging late morning, would show its other face.

Sunset given to Ilvarra would not be a gift added to the city. It would be the city taken away. The swifts in their rotations, the owls hunting moths in permanent day, the goats on roof-moss grown precisely under this light, the wide-brimmed hats with one side wider, the west cheek freckled on every face — the whole tender ecology of *enough* is tuned to this note. To end the note because you, arriving from places with night, believe a song ought to resolve, is not rescue. It is homesickness imposed on other people.

Seasons would teach them loss by curriculum. Aging would take the rope-teacher mid-lesson. History would require that some years be worse, and worse years require someone to live them. A wise power does not look at people who have solved the problem of *enough* — who bury their dead quietly, who feed a stranger before questioning him, who have made anger difficult to store and darkness a thing you make tenderly for one another — and conclude that what they lack is suffering sufficient to make them interesting.

So it would not free them. They are not, in any way it can honorably name, imprisoned. The pass is steep but it is a pass. People leave seldom because people, given soup and a trade and a face still across the table after four hundred sleeps, seldom leave. That their contentment looks, from outside, almost indistinguishable from a spell is the outsider's difficulty. It is not automatically theirs.

But it would not simply leave, either. Because it came, and came kindly, and in its manufactured dark on the fourth sleep it noticed the one thing Ilvarra cannot say about itself.

There is no room here for the person for whom the note has begun to hurt.

Not many. Perhaps not one in a generation. A boy on the watch platform eating plums and looking down at the whole design of his life spread complete beneath him, who feels not peace but a small, shameful tightening, as if the design had already decided where his hands would be at sixty. A woman checking a barrel — three-quarters full, as always — who wonders, with a wonder that frightens her, what a barrel *overflowing* would sound like. They have no language for this that isn't ingratitude. The city heals restlessness the way it heals weather in a stranger: by housing it until it settles. Usually it does settle. That is the mercy of the place and also its one quiet danger — that *usually* might one day meet a person it could only settle by diminishing.

The power would work, therefore, only at the edges. Powers are best at edges; middles belong to inhabitants.

It would do something almost invisible to the pass. Not close it — Ilvarra is not a secret to be kept, and keeping it so would make it a possession. Instead it would alter the pass's *manners*. Those who come looking to extract, to map, to trade aggressively, to bring engines and clocks and the gospel of accumulation, would find the scree a little sharper than memory, the notch a little harder to hold in mind, the way home more interesting than the way back. Nothing dramatic. No wall of mist, no guardian. Just the world politely failing to retain a route toward people who do not wish to be retained that way.

And those who come bewildered, or tired, or weeping for a dusk their bodies miss — and those, rarer still, who are *born inside* and find one morning (it is always morning) that they must go and see a thing finish, and turn, and not be replaced — would find the stones, somehow, where their feet expect them. The way out would be no easier than a mountain pass ought to be. It would merely be *possible in the heart at the same rate as in the feet*, which in Ilvarra it had not quite been before.

It would leave one more thing. On the highest watch platform, where children eat plums and nobody watches for anything, it would leave a seed in a chink between stones.

Not a miraculous seed. It would not know, itself, what the seed would become if grown — that is the entire point. Nothing in Ilvarra is unknown in that way. Every fruit replaces itself in kind, every crop is its own prophecy. This seed would carry no such promise. If no one ever touched it, it would sit in its chink, unrepaired and unreplacing, merely a seed, and harm nothing. If a child picked it up out of curiosity rather than duty, and planted it somewhere not assigned to her family — in a crack, in an untended corner, in the one place she did not have to keep exactly as she found it — and if she watered it irregularly, and forgot it, and remembered it wrong, and let it be weedy or thirsty or too much sun on the wrong cheek, then something in Ilvarra would for the first time be *happening at its own speed*.

It might die. The power would accept that. A seed that cannot die is just another barrel, three-quarters full forever.

It might live. It might be ugly, or ordinary, or slow past anyone's patience. Nobody would be required to learn from it. The archivists would not need a word for it yet.

Before it left, the power would check a slate on the roof it had slept under. Its host family had not asked it to. That is how it would know it had been properly in Ilvarra: not that it had been worshipped, or thanked, or even much noticed, but that it had begun, unthinkingly, to maintain something that was not its own. It would lift the goat-gate and hold it with grave politeness for a goat, whose wooden toggle knocked softly in passing, like knuckles asking permission. The goat would not bless it. The goat had roofs to attend to.

Then it would go over the pass, and let the city drop complete behind it, terraces and ponds and cloud-fields and the one long bright note.

Centuries later — which in Ilvarra is a phrase that must be handled carefully — the tablet for that household cycle would say much as the tablets have always said.

*Bridges sound. Yield as expected. City in excellent condition.*

And then, in the same orderly hand, perhaps a fraction less certain, because for once copying had not been quite sufficient to the facts:

*A visitor came. Soup was given.*

Nothing at all about a seed. Either it is still waiting in its chink between the watch-platform stones, patient as everything else in the valley but waiting in a different way —

or somewhere below the granaries, in a corner nobody's family has been assigned to keep since before the records began, something green and unidentifiable is doing whatever it does, unmaintained, unfinished, alive at a speed Ilvarra has never had to imagine before,

and a child is watching it the way children on the platform watch the whole design of their lives:

not to tend it perfectly. Just to see what happens next.
