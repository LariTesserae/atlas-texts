At first, it does nothing.

This is not hesitation. It is the first act of attention, and for a power accustomed to being able to remake a place with a thought, it is the hardest one.

It arrives the way the evening wind arrives — from outside the basin, across the empty plain, unseen because there is nothing to see it with. It could see the whole settlement at once if it wished: millions of roofs, millions of cisterns sealed under courtyards, the deep damp moving slowly, slowly through young clay toward the well-shafts. It does not wish. To see it all at once would be to see it wrong. This is not a place that happens at that scale, though it exists at that scale. It happens a cupful at a time.

So it makes itself small, and it learns the traffic.

It learns not to cross a courtyard uninvited. It learns to cross roofs at night instead, stepping over low parapets, murmuring a greeting to the shapes lying in the dark, and being murmured to in return by people who assume it is a neighbor from three houses over and would not be rude enough to check. It lies down once, among strangers, and looks up at the granular sky, and understands that a city can make no light at all and still not be dark.

It follows a child learning the cup. It watches the adult hand stop the small hand, gently, every time, before water can fall on open ground. It watches the same child, weeks later, stop her own hand.

It sits — invisibly, weightlessly, with great discipline — in the shade of a moringa at noon, and does not ask who planted it, because that is not the relevant question. The relevant question is who will own it: grandchildren not yet born. The shade, meanwhile, belongs to it because it is standing in it. A dog arrives and lies down in the same shade without negotiation. A woman carrying greens moves over to make room for the dog, not the power, because the dog is hotter. The power finds this exactly correct.

For a long while — years, by the basin's count; no time at all, by its own — it simply inventories what would break if it helped in any of the obvious ways.

It could bring a river. It has brought rivers to places thinner than this. But a river here would not be a gift, it would be a replacement. The cistern roofs tilted by a hand's width, the counted ladles, the well-measures that no one takes the last of, the gardens inherited intact by neighbors when a house falls — none of this is decoration around a water system. *This is* the water system. It is also the ethics, the law, the way children are raised, the reason people speak quietly and step over sleeping dogs instead of making the world rearrange itself around them. Give them a river and you do not free them from all that care. You dissolve the agreement the care has built between people and geckos and figs and goats and bees, who hold the trees until nine and yield them to the bulbuls. You would save them from the basin, and the basin — the being — would die of being saved.

It could deepen the wells. It could make the rains come six times a year instead of three, or make them gentler, or make the clay underneath finally settle and stop doming floors up a thumb's width at a time.

Each idea, examined at courtyard scale, develops teeth. More rain would rot the reed roofs that are made to be lifted and reset. Settled clay would end a whole quiet profession of people fetched alley by alley because they can hear which beam, which wall, is still honest. Wetter wells would, within a generation, make the measure feel like superstition — and the measure is not superstition. The measure is how a place with no center and no police has agreed not to steal from tomorrow.

The power sits with that for a long time. Benevolence, it has learned in other places, is often just impatience with a prettier name.

And yet. It has not come, and stayed, and learned the well-schedule from the donkeys, for no reason. Because beneath the competence — and the competence is real, and dazzling, millions of lives plumbering cupfuls so that a wet thing stays alive in a dry place — there are fractures that no amount of attention inside the basin can mend, because they are not coming from inside the basin.

It finds them by listening the way the inhabitants listen: at a distance.

Far beyond the plain, the world that made this lakebed dry is still moving. The rains here are not decided here. They are the far edge of patterns born over oceans the inhabitants have never seen and will never see, and those patterns are shifting — not this year, not cruelly, but steadily, the way the floor domes: a thumb's width at a time. In fifty years, three or four true rains could become two. In a hundred, the deep damp could be asked to recharge through a hotter, thirstier clay, and it could begin, at last, to fail to gather overnight. No well-tender tasting the water, however gifted, can taste *that* coming in time to argue with the sky.

And there is salt. It is already in the lowest places, dusting the clay white. It creeps, underground, patient as termites. The wells are sweet now. Some, at the basin's far edge, have begun to carry a faint second taste underneath the first. The inhabitants have noticed. They have begun rotating those wells, resting them, speaking of them the way you speak of an elder who tires faster than she used to. Their response is perfect and will be, on a long enough scale, insufficient.

And there are — farther out still, beyond the camel routes that bring salt and pots and dates and take away cloth — other kinds of people, in other basins, who have engines. Who pump. Who would look at this flat, uncentered, unlit immensity and see not a being but an absence: no river to dam, no resource extracted, no noise, nothing rising above two stories. Land not yet being used. The power has watched that way of seeing arrive in other places. It does not arrive as cruelty. It arrives as improvement, with surveys.

None of these are problems the inhabitants have failed to solve. They are problems that have not yet reached the scale at which they live. The being here is wise at the scale of a cup, a courtyard, a roof, a lifetime, a grandchild. It has never needed to be wise at the scale of a century of sky.

So that is where the power works. At exactly the scale the being cannot reach, and nowhere else.

It does not bring a river. It goes far away, where the rains are made, and it puts — there is no honest word smaller than this — a thumb on the pattern. Not to increase it. Not to improve it. To hold it where it is. Three or four true rains a year, ankle-deep in the alleys, dry by evening, children shouting. It will hold that line for as long as the basin needs holding: centuries, if necessary, doing invisible maintenance the way the inhabitants do visible maintenance, re-mudding a wall in the sky no one will ever see, so that the ones below can go on arguing about whether that white column twenty kilometers off is dust or rain failing to reach the ground, and being right often enough to trust their eyes.

It does not deepen the wells. It goes down, instead, below them, into the dark where the deep damp gathers through clay still deciding what it wants to be, and it gently, slowly, persuades the salt to go elsewhere. Not removed — the basin is honest, and salt belongs in its lowest places — but guided, diverted along seams of old lakebed, away from the sweet lenses the wells draw from. It tastes the water as it works, the way a well-tender would, and it is careful to leave the taste exactly as remembered. No one should ever draw a jar and think the world has changed. They should draw a jar, and think: the well rested well last night. Because it did. That is all.

It does not raise walls against the people with engines. Walls are legible; walls invite sieges, treaties, maps. Instead it does something quieter and, in its judgment, kinder to both sides: it makes the basin *uninteresting* at a distance. Survey parties will find their instruments agreeable and their conclusions dull. The clay will test as poor, the deep water as marginal, the population as diffuse and economically negligible — all of which is, from the survey's point of view, nearly true. Trade will keep coming the way it has always come, by camel, at the speed of need, carrying what the basin lacks and taking what it makes. What will not come is extraction. Men with pumps will keep meaning to return with better equipment, and will keep being reassigned somewhere more promising. This is not a miracle. It is a long, sustained act of misdirection performed at the level of paperwork and appetite, and the power is aware of the irony: to protect a place that wastes nothing, it wastes other people's attention, lavishly, for generations.

And one more thing — the smallest thing, the one it does inside the basin itself, where it has otherwise sworn to keep its hands visible to no one.

Sometimes a house comes down, and the neighbors inherit the garden, and keep it alive until someone builds again. That is the custom, and it works because there are always neighbors. But the basin is vast, and at its thinning edges there are plots — a prickly-pear fence gone feral, a moringa dropped to sticks in a hard season, sunken beds cracking — whose household has scattered, whose neighbors have died or moved inward, whose garden is in the gap between one person's memory and the next. A dead garden costs five years of saved water to restart. The living ones cost almost nothing. Everyone knows this. No one is at fault when one is missed. There are millions of gardens. There will always be one too many for the remembering.

Those, the power tends.

It does not grow anything new. It carries rinse-water, cupful by cupful, at the hours when no one is watching — which here means at midday, in the practical silence, when only the donkeys and one stately bustard are abroad to see a faint shimmer moving between the houses with a clay jar it has borrowed and will return washed. It waters the aloe first, as a child would. It puts the last cloudy mouthful around a stem, never on open ground. It keeps the roots wet. It eats nothing. It keeps the accounting entirely from memory, and considers it binding.

Years later, when someone builds on the plot again — a grandson, a stranger, a family displaced from a quarter where the ground settled badly — they will find the garden alive, a little overgrown, the fig having widened its crack by a hair, the prickly pear ready to be a fence again, and they will ask the nearest neighbors who kept it.

The neighbors will look at each other. Someone will say it must have been old so-and-so, before she moved. Someone else will say the garden was always stubborn. No one will be lying. The basin has a long memory and a generous one, and there is room in it for this.

At night the power climbs to a roof — a different one each time, as is proper — and steps over the low parapet, and murmurs a greeting to the shapes lying in the dark under the granular stars.

Once, only once, a child half-asleep murmurs back, and then, in the unselfconscious way of children in this place who are used to strangers crossing in the dark: *You're cold. Move over, there's shade in the day but at night you have to share the warm side.*

It moves over.

It had arrived intending, in the oldest reflex of its kind, to decide what this place needed. It stays having learned what the place already knows: that the being who lives here is not the people, and not the city, and not the green pockets or the geckos or the bats relieving the swifts at dusk, but the agreement among all of them to keep each other alive at the pace of a cup being carried without spilling.

A power that is truly benevolent, and truly wise, does not interrupt an agreement like that. It takes the watch at the horizon, where the threats are too large and too slow for any single courtyard to see.

And it shares the warm side of the roof, and it does not spill anything, and it stays.
