It would not descend.

That is the first wisdom, and the hardest for a benevolent power to learn: that coming down from above upon Vaith, in glory or in pity, would be only another kind of altitude. It would arrive too high to be met. Its voice would pool in the upper rooms and go stale for centuries. Its shadow would fall the length of whole districts while the towers were still in sun, and no one could be blamed for standing in it, because no one had been warned in time.

So it would climb.

It would come up through the pines and the grass and the lichen, losing attendants as it went, losing banners, losing radiance, losing — a little at a time, and with increasing relief — the letters of whatever name the valleys give it. It would let the air thin its intentions. Anything in it that smelled of rain it would leave behind, folded carefully in a low place, where it could do no harm. *Do not bring water up; it will try to return faster than you can.* It would not bring fire either, though it could. It would not bring iron, or wheels, or thick sweet air in its cupped hands. Those are valley mercies. Brought up here they would be only a faster way of falling.

By the gate it would be low, and broad, and out of breath, and perfectly easy to forget.

It would meet the Vaithin on the first plaza, and nod, politely. By the cisterns it would meet them again, with a small start of recognition it would have the manners not to insist on. It would wait at the foot of a staircase while a version of itself, higher than it thinks, passed downward without comment. It would sleep the first night with its mouth closed, and wake with its tongue dry as paper, and consider this an adequate introduction to the ethics of the place.

Then, for a long time — a hundred years, perhaps; perhaps the slow creep of a finger's width of felt across a wall-joint — it would do the work.

Not theirs. Near it.

It would sweep a sixth threshold: the door that is not its own, and not one of the four nearest, but the one everybody's route somehow misses because the street keeps re-negotiating its height around it. It would lay kindling and not light it. It would set the stone weights out in descending sizes on an unoccupied stall and take nothing away but the grit, and it would do that badly, slowly, in full view, so that no Vaithin child would have reason to think it had come to be admired. It would stain its fingers in the dyers' vats and not wash them. It would learn to listen past people — past the splintered, delayed arrivals of footsteps and shadows — and to address its answers, gently, to whichever part of a person had managed to get there.

It would not fill the cisterns. It would not even look into them too long. A power that can make rain is always tempted to call dryness a problem; up here, dryness is a load-bearing structure. The Vaithin have built a life in the shape of an absence, the way an arch is built around the air it means to span. Fill the absence and the arch comes down singing.

It would not give them back their names, either. It would be offered them constantly — in upper rooms, in unfinished sentences, names worn smooth as threshold stone, names with the middle letters sublimated clean out of them — and it would refuse, tenderly, to speak them aloud at that height, where a whole name might settle on an old woman like weather and make her too heavy to finish rising, or too light, all at once, to stay.

Instead, it would become the one thing Vaith has never had room to keep, being built, as it is, inside forgetting:

It would remember, downhill of them.

Below the breathlessness, in its own deep chest, it would hold the two maps that do not agree, and not reconcile them. It would hold the ends of songs without humming them back up. It would hold the smell of river mud, and green leaf, and a woman's hair after rain, in trust, releasing a little now and then under supervision, downhill of a door, for a child whose eyes are already streaming. It would keep every handprint ever pressed into cistern dust at the height a household stood that season, and it would not call that record a history. Histories are for people who expect to be visited by strangers. It would call it, if asked — and it would wait a long time to be asked — *the level.*

And then, slowly, above the highest street it can find, in the place where the far wall recedes fastest and the stars lean nearest and refuse to blink, it would begin to make the only gift that does not violate the instructions cut into the lintels.

It would make a low place at the top.

Not a heaven. Heaven is only a summit that has learned public relations. Not a rescue, not a net, not a hand catching the city as it rises out of the world. A hollow. A sheltered lee, the way the boulders in the gravel gardens give lee for their beards of ice: a room — if room is the word; it might more honestly be a held breath in the structure of things — built with its ceiling close and its floor lower than its threshold, so that to enter it you must do the one thing Vaith has never once allowed at altitude.

You must step *down*.

Into air that thickens by a fraction. Not enough to drown in, not enough to name. Enough for a footstep to arrive only a little late. Enough for a shadow, flattened content against some far wall for a decade, to consider the walk home. Enough for the lightest grandmother, who has slept for years under her mattress with market stones in her sleeves and has lately begun failing to come down in the mornings, to cross one last staircase that goes up and up and folds — and find herself, with a lurch in the inner ear, *below* where she started: in a swept room, with kindling laid, with a bed carved hollow for a hip she has nearly finished having, where for the first time in her long lightening she may stand all the way upright without spending anything.

She may stay an afternoon. She may stay a hundred years. In Vaith there is a word for the difference, but it loses letters if you say it too often, and the power, by then, will have learned not to ask.

It will not explain. It will not ring the great ash bell, which could not sound in this air anyway. It will do what the Vaithin do at an hour that matters: it will open a shutter, somewhere in the quarter, at the same moment as all the others, so the change arrives as light.

Down in the market the air above the counters will thicken for a moment at elbow height. A transaction no one can quite follow will conclude, to everyone's evident satisfaction. A bowl in an empty kitchen will hum, faintly, in harmony with something that is not singing.

And the power will sit in the low room it made above the world, with its mouth closed and its great ears open, listening for footsteps that may never arrive — holding the lost name whole and unspoken, like rime on the north side of a statue, like water that has agreed, at last, not to hurry home —

so that when Vaith finally rises to meet whatever has been waiting above it, it will not find a god.

It will find a neighbor, one door further up, who has kept the threshold swept.
