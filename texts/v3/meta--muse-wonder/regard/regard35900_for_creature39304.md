It would come, at first, meaning to help.

That is how you would know it was benevolent, and not yet wise. It would stand at the levee where the condensation stops forming on a traveler's face, and it would feel — for a power accustomed to seeing can feel in other ways, reluctantly, the way a newcomer here puts an ear to the ground — the whole basin underway beneath it. The pumps sighing. The worms moving through water. The cables warm under the slag. The slow, dense people moving single-file along the causeways, hand to shoulder, reading a country that is reading them back. And it would raise, out of an old and genuine pity, the beginnings of a morning.

Then it would learn the first rule.

Not from being told. No one here could tell it; voices do not carry, and they would only have laid a damp hand on its forearm to let it know it was standing on the narrow side of the road. It would learn it the way everyone learns it: by staying. By having its eyes stop aching. By understanding, slowly, in its skin, that the dark is not a wound laid over a place that is waiting to be healed. It is the medium. Bring light that works here, and you do not restore these people. You end them — the herons sweeping their metronome arcs at exactly the height of a crouching person, the fish breathing mud against a shin, the children turning in their sleep toward the warmer of two hands, the whole patient agreement between land kept just above water and water kept just below land, maintained one flywheel stroke at a time by a people who have become, generation by generation, the part of the dark that maintains itself.

A rescue would be a drowning, done kindly, from above.

So it would lower its hand.

That would be the first thing it did, and the hardest, and most of the rest would follow from it the way everything here follows: one great effort, then a long rest while the consequence travels.

Inside the basin, it would do almost nothing.

It would not mend the pumps. They are not broken; they are inherited, and the mending is a form of kinship it has no right to take out of anyone's hands. It would not cleanse the copper and zinc from the mollusks all at once, or sweeten the algae, or teach the cane to grow faster, though it could, though there are fevers here, and slow poisonings, and children buried with less ceremony than the logbooks deserve. It would be tempted, in the way of powers, to confuse *less suffering* with a gift that costs nothing. But it has watched enough worlds to know that a gift that arrives faster than a culture can feel its weight breaks the surface it lands on, like hurry on still water. And these people do not hurry. Anything it gave them at its own pace would arrive as an invasion.

What it could give at their pace, it would.

A fraction less lead in one generation of silt, so gradually that no taste changes. A well that stays, very slightly, on the side of sweet. A winter-that-is-not-a-winter in which a fever that would have taken three bunkhouses takes one old woman, and her going on the long round is entered in the log in a level hand, and no one ever knows the arithmetic was touched. Small mercies, deniable ones, administered the way the basin itself administers change: so slowly that by the time they are noticed they are remembered as having always been so. A new pump-house beside a known road. A stranger-driver who loses the count of rail-joints, and stays, and is welcomed, and brings new blood and new songs felt through the ribs and is never once asked to describe the light. A lost child on a causeway at the hour the cold places drift, found — not led, never led, just accompanied by a warmth at her back, body-sized, at exactly skin temperature, until she feels the hot exhalation of the boiler-house and knows where she is.

To the shadows, it would do the only thing no one here is quite able to do for them. It would sit with them.

The people step aside for the cold places respectfully, and hold their breath, and that is right and kind. But the ones on the long round pause beside the machines they once tended as if listening, and listening, for a year, for a decade, can become a way of not finishing. The power would go and be cold beside them — it can be cold, when it chooses, the way it can be warm — so that for once the thinning damp has company in its own register. It would not speak into them. It would not call them by name, though it would learn their names, every one, from the swollen books kept warm on top of the transformers. It would simply keep them company while they checked the stroke a final time, and felt, perhaps, that the work was in other hands now and the hands were adequate, and let the scour in the mud soften, and the weeds come back, very slowly, in their season that is always almost arriving.

It would never once make one of them stay.

Outside the basin, it would be less gentle.

For the danger to this place was never the dark. The danger is the dry cabinet, filling with paperwork in a hand no one recognizes. The danger is that someday the paperwork will be recognized — that a company, a government, a curious and well-funded kindness will arrive with lamps engineered to burn in wet clay, with drainage schemes, with clinics full of corrective mercy, with a road. The power cannot prevent human beings from being human, and would not, if it is wise, try to rule them. But it can be a climate in boardrooms far away. A survey misfiled. A seam of gas appraised, accurately, as not quite worth the trouble. A philanthropist whose attention, turning toward the basin with tears in his eyes, softens by one degree and settles instead on a country where the suffering actually wants his light. Maps that keep being drawn with the basin a little smaller, a little emptier, a little more certainly abandoned than the last edition. It would guard the forgetting the way the levees guard the fields: not with a wall, with maintenance. One slow stroke at a time.

And it would preserve, always, the way out.

The levee that gets fractionally drier. The warmth that leads upward, hand over hand. Because the difference between a home and a trap is not comfort — this place is comfortable, at skin temperature, in the way of a held hand — it is whether leaving remains possible for the one child in a generation who dreams the bright dream and wakes wanting, obscurely, the other thing. The power would keep that path open and unmarked. It would not encourage it. It would not mourn those who take it, layer by layer, until the frogs fall out of feeling behind them. It would only make sure the road up is not quietly closed by the basin's own slow remembering, adding houses, adding causeways, adding crossings, until one year the known world has no edge. Even a beloved country must not be allowed to become a mouth.

Then, having done all this — which is to say, having arranged to be almost entirely unnecessary — it would do the last thing.

It would come down from the levee. It would take off, in whatever way a power takes off, the equivalent of shoes. It would step onto the causeway where the slag has been worn by bare feet into a smooth warm trough exactly one person's width, and it would wait to be resolved around, the way a crowd resolves here without ever having approached. A pressure in the sinuses. A smell of iron and cane-sugar. A hand laid flat, for a second, on what it has instead of a forearm.

And it would put its palm, very carefully, against the warm iron of a pump housing.

Not a command. Not a blessing, which is only a command that expects gratitude.

Just a tap. Felt through the causeway, through the floor piers, through the mud. The way a mother calls a child home. The way a worker says: *I am here, I have felt the stroke, the roster is not short.*

Somewhere inland, in a dry cabinet in an office heated past comfort, a younger son who had been sleeping three to a bunk would wake before the others. He would walk out along a road he had known all his life, reading the cable-thread and the seep-braid and the boiler's long breath with his feet, and he would find, where he had not expected one, a warmth keeping pace beside the causeway, patient, unhurried, at exactly the temperature of whoever stood within it.

He would not be frightened. He would step aside for it on the narrow side, respectfully, the way you step aside for anything here that has not yet learned to hurry.

And later, it would be written in the log, in the same level hand as a change in the weather:

*Additional hand on the long round. Origin not entered. Stroke confirmed.*

*Keeping the water below the land, and the land above the water.*

*With us.*
