If it is truly wise, and truly benevolent, the first thing it would not do is arrive with thunder.

Thunder would be wasted here. No one would hear it. The frogs would go on calling from the canal lips, the crickets from inside the walls, the spring at the sinkhole would go on throwing itself into the heat at the far end of the caldera, and the power would stand for one unnecessary minute in the steam-dusk realizing that a display meant for ears was an arrogance this place had already outgrown.

So it would come down the rim road the way anyone comes down. Slowly. Into the wet change in the air halfway down. Into the roar that rises through the teeth before it reaches the ears. It would let itself be deafened.

That is the first intervention it would make, and it would make it upon itself.

It would learn to be small in the basin.

It would not stand on the market square and speak. Outdoor speech is useless and no one important is said there. It would put waxed wool in ears it did not strictly need, out of courtesy. It would crowd through the shoulder-wide alleys with a palm ready — *passing behind you* — and duck, as even lovers and tax-collectors must duck, through a leather curtain and then a second leather curtain, into the dead air between the double walls, where the roar drops to a pressure in the sternum and two people knee-to-knee can hear one another shout.

And then, indoor creature to indoor creatures, it would do the hardest thing for a power to do.

It would ask.

Not *what is wrong with this place?* A visitor asks that, in the first hour, plugging his fingers in his ears and looking for the green that isn't there. The wise one, sitting long enough for the sulfur to get into its clothes and its hair and the back of its throat, would begin to see that *wrong* is too crude an instrument. Silence the frogs and within a week the biting midges would be a solid weather over the canals. Clear the steam cap and the direct sun would fall like a hammer on eyes bred for twilight, on water that would begin to cool and change, on a breeding year that has no season because it has never needed one. Lay soil, plant trees — a miracle, green and obvious and kind — and roots would heave the sluices, shade would chill the shallow channels, leaf-fall would rot in warm water no scavenger here is shaped to eat. End the noise, and you do not give these people peace. You take away the pressure their sleep is built around. You orphan the swifts in the eaves and the bats in the bathhouse rafters and the herons out by the fissure, all of whom are not scenery. They are citizens of the same volume of air.

A benevolent fool would save the humans from the basin.

The wise power would notice that the basin-dwellers already did the saving, years ago, generation by generation, hand by articulate hand, and that what they saved includes the frogs.

So it would ask — speaking, indoors, close, perhaps with a borrowed palm laid flat against an old gang-master's chest to feel the answer as much as hear it — *what do you actually want that you do not have?*

The answers would embarrass any god who came prepared to move mountains.

They would not ask for quiet. They would not ask for green hills. They would ask, after some thought and some silent indoor laughter at the strangeness of being asked, for wood that does not cost a fortune. For grain in a bad year on the outside trade roads, which are their real seasons even if they do not call them that. For a way for cuts to heal cleaner in air that never lets skin dry. For fewer babies lost in the sealed warm rooms. For assurance, if assurance could be had, that the spring will not one morning do something else.

Those last two — the babies, and the spring — are where the power would finally, carefully, move.

Not visibly. Visibility is for powers who need to be thanked, and gratitude in the basin is a hand on a wrist, quickly given, not a temple. There is nowhere to build a temple anyway that something else isn't already living.

Deep under the caldera, where no mason's tally reaches, it would put what a hand would be if a hand could be the size of geology: a steadying presence against the fissure. Not to freeze it. Freezing it would be another kind of killing. The ground here will cool — slowly, over centuries unborn children will inherit — and a wise intervention does not forbid a place its future. It only takes the cliff-edge out of it. It would bleed off, decade by decade, the stresses that would one day have made the spring falter all at once, or blow, or turn scalding in a single season. It would give the town what the town cannot give itself, which is not eternity. It is *time to notice change coming*, and to answer it the way they answered this ground when it was new: with sluices and tallies, with inventions carved on white stone, with hands.

That is a gift measured in generations. No one alive would ever know. That is how the power would know it had done it right.

Above ground, it would be even more restrained.

In houses where medicine is already good with what the basin affords, a midwife would find — not in a vision, visions are loud things — a thought arriving in her own hands while she worked, about a different way to wash a wound with canal water boiled and filtered through ash that made the slow pale healing a fraction less slow. A cellar-keeper growing the fat white mushrooms would turn a basket and consider, as if it were his own idea and perhaps by then it would be, a second bedding of dung and shredded river-reed brought in under the grain sacks. Small things. Deniable things. Things that do not require the people to become other people in order to receive them.

And outside the rim — because the basin's real vulnerability does not live in the basin — it would walk the trade roads a while. Its mercy would look like weather there. A landslide that did *not* quite take the rim road the winter a grain caravan was due. A distant lord's tax-collector turning his attention, for reasons he would later rationalize very fluently, to richer and less steaming prospects. Porters continuing to come down. Wood continuing to be an heirloom rather than a memory. The world beyond the caldera does not need to love this town. It only needs, reliably, not to starve or loot or divert it. A wise power spends a surprising amount of its strength arranging for other powers to be bored elsewhere.

Last — and this is the part it would do with something like tenderness, and only once it had stayed long enough to understand why — it would make a beginning that nobody now alive is meant to use.

In the abandoned houses, where no vines come and rooms stand empty and dripping until someone needs the block, in the crack between two basalt slabs where even the crickets do not bother, it would set the first, patient ingredients of soil. Not a garden. A garden would be a rebuke. Just microbial darkening in a crack. A film that holds a little more moisture, breaks a little more stone. A place where, a hundred years from now, when the canals run a degree cooler and the frogs pause, for the first time, for a week — some great-grandchild of a frog-warden will find actual dirt where dirt has never been, and will not be standing at the edge of an ending with empty hands. There will be seed sleeping under a sealed stone in the gang house, too, that no inscription explains and no generation has yet needed to open. When they need to ask what it is, they will be the kind of people who can answer their own question. That is soon enough.

It would not leave them silence. It would leave them a future in which silence, arriving gradually at its own pace, might one day be bearable, and even, in places, chosen.

Then it would go.

No one on the rim road that day would report anything unusual. A basin family seeing a son off to the outside trade might remember, vaguely, one extra traveler among them on the climb. Halfway up, where the roar thins and birds become audible somewhere cold above, and the boy pulls out his earplugs and winces and laughs at the smallness of his own voice, the stranger would stop too. It would pull out its own plugs. It would wince — honestly. It had grown used to the pressure. Silence, at first, does actually hurt.

The mother, plugs still firmly in, would see its face change, and smile, and sign something quick and kind across the widening quiet.

If you asked for a translation afterward, the boy, still talking and talking in the sudden open air just because he could, would tell you it was nothing important. Nothing anyone would say where the frogs can hear it.

It was the sign for *home*, with a particular flourish he had never seen before and would not learn to place for years — as if the word had been made, briefly, large enough to hold more than one world.

The power would return the sign, clumsily, with hands not made for it.

Benevolent, and wise, it would have done almost nothing here.

It would have made sure *almost* was enough.
