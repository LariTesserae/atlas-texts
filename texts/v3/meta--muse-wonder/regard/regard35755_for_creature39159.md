First, it would listen.

Not for a day. For a season. For long enough to learn the hours of the place — dawn, when the council can still murmur; noon, when the bowl becomes a weapon and a tool; dusk, when doors open and human voices return, carefully, like animals coming back to a waterhole.

A lesser kindness would arrive with its hands already full.

It would want to stop the sound. It would want to bring rain. It would want to plant a tree in that terrible white vacancy at the center, dig a well, cure the scarred ears, send bees in fragrant clouds to do gently what the wind now does brutally.

A wise kindness learns, in time, that each of those mercies would be a murder.

Silence the bowl, and the grain is not threshed, the jars are not cleaned, the pollen does not lift. The vines — those wrist-thick, grandmother-old vines that hold the terraces together — do not fruit. Bring rain, and the salt mortar softens, the mud-brick slumps, the sealed succulent leaves split with a plenty they were never built to bear; and then more people could live there than the twice-yearly water could sustain, until they could not. Fill the center, and you have not given them a plaza. You have taken away their millstone, their hammer, their altar, their held breath.

The being that lives here is not, as it first appears, a person. It is not even, exactly, a people.

It is the chord.

It is bowl and rim-flute and vine and plug and hand-sign and beetle and wax and night-moth, all of it together: a single slow creature that breathes wind and makes a town out of being deafened at noon. The inhabitants are what it does, given hands — and it is what they do, given stone. To rescue one part from the rest would be to unmake the whole.

So the power does not descend into the center.

It does not appear on the council ledge. It does not leave a new well steaming in the morning, or a strange stone among the rim-flutes, tuned beyond human craft, for them to worship or depend on. It knows the sharpest temptation of the benevolent is to become necessary. It refuses that.

What it does, it does at the edges, where help does not become authorship.

Far out on the pan, where no sound carries, there is a well the mule trains depend on — a bitter, half-salt seep in a cracked basin, known to three families and a handful of guides, that has been failing, grain by grain, for eleven years. Nobody in the bowl knows yet. They know only that the skins come back a little lighter each spring and the water-keepers have begun to measure with a smaller cup and argue about it less, which is how the old argue when they are frightened.

In the night, something clears the well.

Not makes it abundant. Not makes it sweet. Clears the choked throat of it, stone by patient stone, so that it yields what it yielded in the grandmothers' time, and will go on yielding it. The next train finds the basin exactly as poor and exactly as sufficient as memory says it should be. The guide will cross himself, or sign *luck*, or say nothing, having the local instinct not to spill a noise. They will fill the skins and come home across the white glare, dreaming of noise, and the bowl will not have changed by one cupful more than it can bear.

Along the wind's road it does a second thing.

The bowl sings because the pan is empty — miles of flat salt with nothing to break the hot wind before it reaches the carved rim. It would take so little to end that: a range of tailings from some distant mine, a prospector's camp with windbreaks of piled stone, an empire deciding this white nothing is a good place for a road of towers. The power walks the horizon the way a gardener walks a fence line. Where men have begun, in their far countries, to dream of salt concessions and straight surveyed lines, it puts a small discouragement in the dream. Not terror. Not death. Just the persistent sense, in a surveyor's tent at night, that there is nothing out there worth the water it would cost to reach it; that the maps are better left blank; that some other province will pay better.

The bowl has survived by being, to the rest of the world, uninhabitable. The power guards its uninhabitability like a hedge.

Beneath the bowl it does a third thing, slower than the first two.

Bedrock moves. Waterless stone still creeps, still settles, still opens hairline griefs in itself across centuries. There is a fault of settling under the east terraces that, in two hundred years, would drop one ring by the width of a hand — not enough to kill anyone in their beds, enough to change the bowl's note forever, enough to unvoice the trumpets on the rim and leave the pollen hanging ungathered and the center good for nothing but glare. The tuners would hear it coming for a generation and rasp and pack clay and be, in the end, defeated by geology.

In the deep night, below the sleeping alcoves, below the root-hairs feeling for dew in the seams of the walls, the power puts — there is no other word — a hand against the stone. And holds it. Not healed, not made new: held, the way you hold a cup with a crack in it, so the crack does not run. It will need holding again in four hundred years. That is acceptable. Nothing here is impatient. A leaf takes a season to unfold. A repair can take a geological age.

Inside the bowl, it does almost nothing at all.

It does not heal the old tuners' ears. Their hearing, such as it is, through teeth and fingertips and the soles of their feet, is not a wound to them; it is their literacy. It does not save the child who will try the center at noon next summer — an adult's hands will do that, signing *stupid, beloved, never again*, and the lesson will enter the body the way all the true lessons here enter it. It does not multiply the moths. Five, six, perhaps seven useless, cherished, golden-dusted creatures blundering between the night flowers is the right number, and it knows better than to improve on tenderness by making it efficient.

Only: one season, when a grey blight comes on the wind out of nowhere anyone can name and begins to spot the hanging trumpets, the blight does not take. Spore lands, and does not take. And again, and does not take. The grandmothers notice, and argue in sign over the noon-clattering terraces about whether the wind is wrong, and arrive at no conclusion, and pack ash and husk around the root crowns a little more carefully that year, just in case. That is enough credit for human hands to take. The power leaves them the credit.

And on its last night — if a thing like that keeps nights — it sits out beyond the rim, where the salt pan ticks as it cools, and listens to the council singers calibrate the human chord against the memory of the day's stone chord. Long notes, passed voice to voice. Adjusted by a finger's width. Babies asleep. Old people hearing, for the ten-thousandth time, the sound of their whole lives agreed upon.

Very quietly, under all of it, in a register felt in the sternum rather than heard, it adds one note of its own.

Not a new note. The note that was already there, sung back. Agreement. *Yes, that one. You have it right.*

Nobody looks up. A grandmother mending cord pauses, tilts her head — third stone, east rim, no: nothing has changed — and goes on mending, obscurely comforted, the way you are comforted when a room you thought you were alone in turns out to have had, all along, someone else in it who was listening properly.

By noon the next day the power is gone, walking away across the salt with its chin down against the glare, while behind it the wind finds the rim and the bowl rises into its deafening, threshing, pollinating roar, and the doors shut, and the hands begin to speak.

It leaves no miracle they can point to. It leaves the well as it was in their grandmothers' time, the wind road empty, the stone held, the blight untaken, and one night's singing confirmed in the bone.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does for a being that is not suffering from its life, but *is* its life:

It does not save it from itself.

It keeps the world from doing so, either.
