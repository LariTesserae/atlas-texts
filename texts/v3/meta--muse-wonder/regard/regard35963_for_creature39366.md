They would not come down at first.

That would be the first sign of them: the patience, and the refusal to enter as yet another thing to be organized.

From the rim — broken black stone, ankle-twisting, pale with dust — they would stand and watch, not for an hour, because an hour is a human length of attention, but for the length the place actually keeps. A season. A year turned thin by cold. Long enough for a wall to gain a course of stones still dusty from below ground. Long enough for a house at the eastern edge to sigh down into gravel and for a new enclosure, roofless and fresh-edged, to shoulder up nearer the centre. Long enough to see the rosettes fold and open, fold and open, and creep a hand's breadth along a wall, leaving the stone paler behind them. Long enough to mistake nothing.

Because from the rim, at first, every kindness presents itself as an obvious one.

To bring water. The basin in the plaza is so beautifully made, fitted tight as teeth, and so bone-pale with never having been wet, that pity rises almost before thought: surely it is thirsty, surely it is waiting. A wise power would set no rain over the bowl. They would watch a mouthful spilled from a flask vanish instantly between the grains, leaving no darkening, and understand: water here is not mercy. Water pulled down to where the cold still lives becomes machinery. Enough of it would not fill the basin. It would heave the town out of its own shape, lift walls where no wall should be, make the pattern run feverish and wrong. The town lives by not drinking. They would keep their hands, and their weather, to themselves.

To bring inhabitants. That would be the second pity, and the deeper one. Sixty, seventy houses. Lanes, thresholds, a plaza, a long communal wall against the wind. Surely such shelter is wasted without anyone to shelter. Surely the emptiness is a kind of loneliness. They would stand inside a roofless house where the stillness is not empty but held, where no nest or dropping or hearth has ever troubled the floor, and they would resist the old, benevolent urge to complete a thing by putting someone inside it. People would bring fire, and paths worn between door and well, and names for the lanes, and the wish to repair what is slumping and prevent what is rising. Within a generation they would be fighting the town in order to live in it. And the town, which has never needed tenants, would begin — stone by patient stone — to incorporate their cairns, their graves, their dropped straps and cherished markers, and erase them toward its own alignments. It would not be cruelty. It would be digestion. A wise power does not feed a living thing to a metaphor.

To wake it. That would be the subtlest temptation, and the one they would sit with longest, palm flat against a wall that is warm where no sun has struck. Under the hand: a thrum, not sound but behaviour, stones settling a millimetre as they cool. A whole body, two hundred paces across, sorting itself night after night into streets. How easy, for such a power, to lay a mind inside that process. To let it know its own lanes. To let it mean them.

They would not.

It does not know it is a town, and that unknowing is not a deficiency in it. It is the condition of its peace. To give it awareness would not crown the pattern; it would exile it. It would wake, if it woke, inside a shape made entirely of dwellings, and find no dwellers; inside a body made entirely of shelter, and discover it could not leave; it would learn the word *empty* and apply it to itself for the first time. Benevolence that requires its object to thank it, or to recognize itself in the giver's image, is not benevolence yet. They would take their hand from the warm wall gently, the way you take your hand from a sleeping animal you have decided not to startle.

So they would do almost nothing to the town.

Almost nothing, done exactly, and for a very long time.

They would not preserve it. Preservation would be the polite form of killing it: fixing this house, shoring that wall, keeping the map from going soft. The town is alive precisely because it is allowed to shed its rimward buildings like old skin and grow new ones at its leading edge. They would let the eastern houses fall. They would let the plaza stay clear because the heave is weakest there, let the lanes stay clear because that is where it runs. They would knock no stone loose to test its repair, and build no cairn to mark their visit, and leave no blessing carved anywhere that the cold could lift and sort into a wall.

They would walk the bowl once, perhaps, slowly, in the flattened grey light when shadows pool instead of sharpening, and they would walk the way weather walks there — making almost no sound on the pumice dust, spilling nothing, dropping nothing, taking no sample, no rubbing, no true and permanent plan. The moth in the roofless house would open and close its dust-coloured wings without urgency. The lizard with the translucent throat would watch from a crack and be gone. The rosettes would turn, all of them, a fraction, toward a warmth moving through a wall — and if, for an instant, they turned also toward a warmth moving *between* walls, the power would not stop to be recognized. There is nothing here that would step forward to meet you. They would not require it to learn how.

What they would do, they would do outside the rim, and ahead of it, and mostly to others.

Because the town is migrating — imperceptibly eastward, downhill, at the speed of stone rising through dust — and its greatest danger is not starvation or winter. It does all its own winters. Its danger is a future that arrives faster than it can: a road laid across its path a century from now, a settlement founded on the plain ahead by people who find, as all people find, that flat empty basalt looks unused; a well sunk; a wall built by hands that would stand across the slow leading edge and be mistaken, night after night, for an obstacle to incorporate, until the pattern broke against something that would not sort. So the wise power would go east, far beyond the rim where the plain resumes sharp and cool and apparently worthless, and they would do the unglamorous work of keeping a corridor empty. Not a sanctuary. Not a reserve with a name. Names draw feet. They would simply arrange, over generations, that the land in that direction remain unpromising in the memories of travellers, unmarked on the maps that matter, a place caravans cross without stopping and surveyors record without enthusiasm. They would turn aside the well-meant: the pilgrims who would come to live in the empty houses correctly, the scholars who would come to measure them lovingly to death, the kind kings who would roof every house and people them with the poor. They would do it without lying where they could help it, and without wonder. The best protection for such a place is not reverence. It is being, to everyone else, slightly forgettable.

And to the lesser lives in the lee of the walls — the ash-grey rosettes worrying mineral damp out of basalt grain by grain, the mustard lichen expanding in the cold, the unseen maker of three-toed prints that end where a wall arrived in the night — they would give exactly what the town gives them: margins. No new species introduced to enrich the community. No pests removed. No numbers improved. Those lives are not a separate kingdom to be tended; they are what the inhabitant permits, gut-flora and gleaners, loosening tomorrow's building stone, softening stone back into sortable rubble. To favour them over the whole would be to mistake the fringe of a body for its purpose.

At the end — if *end* is the word for a visit measured in cold cycles — they would return to the rim at the blue hour, when frost grows out of the shaded stone not as dew but as white hair, and is gone by midmorning without ever having been wet. Below them the bowl would hold its late-evening light a little longer, then give it up to granular dark. A lane of dust would slide sideways, paper-thin, in a wind barely felt. Somewhere across the plaza, a wall would settle with a warmth that had nothing to do with hands.

They would leave nothing behind for it to organize. That would be the offering: an absence clean enough not to become a wall.

They would not be thanked. They would not be noticed, except perhaps by the rosettes, turning a fraction toward a departing heat and then, after a while, turning back — not in disappointment, but because the heat that mattered had resumed moving through the stone. Below the rim the town would go on growing, folding, flowering into houses, subsiding into lanes, and growing again, migratory and self-repairing, with no one to live in it and no vacancy either.

And the wise power, going away eastward over the sharp plain to keep its roadless road clear for a few hundred years, would consider that not a failure of love, but its first accurate shape: to have seen a thing complete without purpose, alive without mind, inhabited without inhabitants — and to have loved it enough not to finish it.
