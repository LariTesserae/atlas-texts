First, it would make itself smaller.

Not out of humility performed, but out of physics. It would understand at once — the way you understand, putting a foot down there, that the tone comes up through the bone — that attention is a kind of weight in that place, and that a great power arriving at full size could do what an unguarded voice does in untuned ground. It could plant things. It could retune a valley for a thousand years merely by noticing it too loudly. It could finish things by accident, the way a hand can crush a soft egg it meant only to warm.

So it would come the way the locals come to one another: one at a time, without a roof over its intentions, mouth shut.

It would not bring rain.

That would be the easiest kindness, and the cruelest. It would look at the salt-white lake bed in the silent hollow, at the wooden boat resting there with such gravity, caulked with plant oil, waiting for water to become the sort of thing that can happen — and it would want, with all its ancient benevolence, to answer that patience immediately. To say: *you were right, you were not foolish, here is the element you prepared for.*

It would not say it. A flood does not bless a place that has not yet invented shores. Water would cut edges where edges have not been learned, drown the sound-drunk groves that drink through their leaves, fill the resonant bowls, silence the only rain those people have ever known. The boat was not a prayer to be answered early. It was a courtesy extended forward in time. A wise power knows how to receive a courtesy without hurrying it.

So it would go and sit in the boat for a while, in the dry lake, in the falling-feeling silence, and keep it company. That is not nothing. Loneliness acknowledged is not loneliness removed, but it is loneliness shared, and in that country even sharing must be done bone to bone or not at all.

It would not bring animals, either. No birds to startle from the ferns, no insects to stitch a second, smaller music over the great grinding note, no worms to turn the ashy soil. From a finished world it might look like absence. From inside it, it is a chord not yet ready for another note. The groves move all in phase, like hair underwater. Someday there will be rustles that are not caused by vibration. Someday there will be burrowing and hunger and death arriving with its necessary teeth. The power would not import that future out of pity for the present. It would not call the plateau barren just because it is early.

And it would not — this would be hardest of all — complete the mountain.

There is a temptation, for anything wise and strong, to *help along*. To harden the soft hills that are still inflated and provisional. To decide the valleys. To split the smooth basalt eggs cleanly, give every cliff its crack of character, set the stair on the south face back into a shape that stays, so the way up and the way down can be trusted. To make cause arrive in order. To shorten the shadows until they learn to attach.

It would resist it the way you resist speaking in a clear, loud bowl of ground you do not understand.

An unfinished thing is not automatically a broken thing. Sometimes it is only a thing whose griefs have not yet been invented.

Instead, it would learn the slates.

It would station itself — discreetly, at the altitude where attention can become weather — where the stair comes up from the older world, and it would be unremarkable there. No wall. No fire. No sign in letters that burn. Merely a stretch of path where caravans of the finished lose their appetite for finishing. Miners would remember, suddenly and through their teeth, that there is nothing up there they know how to take. Missionaries with names for everything would find their vocabularies going politely quiet in their mouths. Surveyors would pack up their instruments, not afraid, just certain all at once that the angles were not theirs yet. They would turn back believing it was their own idea. That is how the wise prefer to do that sort of work anywhere.

On the plateau itself, it would apprentice itself.

Yes. That is the thing it would do that would surprise the locals least, and please them most, once they understood it was happening.

It would pick up a stone hammer. It would learn to listen in the shins, in the sternum, in the fillings it does not have in teeth that therefore ache honestly at the high harmonic of the inflating peaks. It would let its forehead be flattened, slowly, by use. It would accept instruction from a child who cannot yet be trusted to speak aloud, her whole furious concentration coming through the small warm pressure of her brow: *here, not there; this cairn wants a stone lifted, not added; walk loud, rest less loud; never, ever sleep in the silence, not even you, especially not you* — for a great being sleeping untuned might dream a forest into a valley and wake to find it growing, green-black and glossy, saying its dream back for a century.

It would be corrected. It would allow the correction to stand.

When it moved a stone from a cairn — if, after years, it were permitted to move one — it would move the smallest stone its attention could find, and then it would sit down in the loud ground and listen for three days to find out what it had said. If the ferns for a mile around re-tuned themselves wrongly, if the tone in the pass thinned toward a barren octave, it would move the stone back, forehead low, without argument. No thunder. No lesson written in the sky. Just slate, left by the forge in a careful hand not its own at first, then gradually its own:

*I spoke here. I am sorry. I have put it back.*

It would eat what drinks sound. Ringing seeds gathered at the fading of their note. Black oil on cracked lips. The crisp hearts of the low trees, cut quickly and shared quicker, before the wound bead-seals shut. It would stand in the resonant bowls at the long dusk when the tone drops its quarter-step over six hours and the leaves sigh closed from largest to smallest, and it would not fill that dimming with any radiance of its own. It would let the bruised green phosphorescence of the not-yet-hard places be light enough. It would let the shadows fall upward if that was the hour for shadows to fall upward.

And if its own shadow arrived before it — out on the virgin pumice, sitting down at the edge of a silent hollow, clear of its feet by ten feet and trembling with the tone — the power would not command it back. It would sit beside it and wait politely for itself. Even for such a being, *especially* for such a being, there would be something it had already said in that ringing air that it had not caught up to hearing.

Perhaps that is why, in time, the locals would begin to remember it without ever quite having met it.

In the pooled memory they all share — the same hut from the inside, the same fire that never burned, the same explanation of how to leave, given brow to brow by someone whose face matters less than the pressure — there would be a warmth that was not heat, a presence at the edge of the dished floor that takes up no room. It would not be telling them to go. That would be a finished-world kindness, and wrong. It would be telling them that leaving is a real verb. That the stair is unreliable not because they are imprisoned, but because the country behind them is still revising itself, and they are permitted to revise themselves likewise. Stay, and become tuners of a world being thought into stone. Go, and carry the tone down in the hollow chambers of your legs for the rest of your lives. Neither is abandonment. Neither is failure.

A benevolent power does not only open doors. Sometimes it sits in a roofless room and makes sure a people knows a door is not a verdict.

In the end — insofar as endings can be trusted where a seed is heard before the leaf shatters — it would leave almost nothing behind.

No temple. Roofed things sing and tear themselves apart.

No name spoken aloud. It would have learned better.

Perhaps, at the forge, among the slates in many hands, one more slate, blank on one side. On the other, in letters careful as footsteps in virgin ash:

*Keep your mouth shut in untuned ground.
Say only what you are willing to harvest later.
Touch foreheads. Mean it.*

And perhaps one stone, moved at a pass where the note had been thinning for a generation toward silence. Not a great stone. Not a miraculous one. Just the right stone, an inch to the left, so that somewhere below, dust that had begun to fall out of its rings steadied again in the bright thin air, and a grove of ferns that had been dying crisply at its edges sent one ringing seed, one, ahead into the loud ground where its children would be.

Years later — earlier, sometimes — a local would walk that pass and feel the correction arrive through the soles of the feet and pause, surprised, forehead tilted toward a listening no one was visibly doing.

They would not call it a miracle. They have a better word, though they rarely say it where it might take root.

They would call it *tuned*.

And then they'd sit down a while, beside a shadow that had been keeping that place for them, and wait, very patiently, for the rest of themselves to arrive.
