From the ridge, a benevolent and wise power would look down into the bowl for a long time before doing anything at all.

That would be the first sign of its wisdom, and also of its benevolence. Lesser powers arrive with solutions already in their hands. They see a town drowning slowly in green and want to drain it, clear it, let the sun in properly. They see a single plant eight hundred metres wide and want to break its monopoly, plant orchards, introduce birds. They see people spending their whole lives cutting back their own ground and think: *these people must be rescued from their work.*

This one would stand on the rim road in the ordinary grey-blue daylight, smelling crushed stem and wet iron on the wind, and it would wait until it understood that Sallowmere is not a problem that has failed to be solved.

It is a solution. It has been a solution for a hundred and fifty years.

It would not touch the motherleaf.

It would not make the sap sweet, or slow the stems to a more reasonable two centimetres a day, or teach the leaves to fall all at once and stay down. It would not give the town stone foundations, dry cellars, streets where voices carry. Any one of those gifts, however kindly meant, would be a small apocalypse. Take away the cutting, and you take away the roster in the town hall, the children in crocodile with hooks on their backs, the clerks and grandmothers owing the same hours, the whole low, intergrown knowledge of which whitewashed artery may not be nicked. Take away the growth, and the Sallowmerers would not be free. They would be unemployed in the deepest sense: a maintenance people with nothing left to maintain, standing startled in a field.

Nor would it cure the motherleaf of being what it is. The plant is not a monster holding a town prisoner, whatever the postcards in winter might suggest. Neither is it a goddess. It is a neighbour — huge, indifferent, persistent, reliable — with whom a hundred and fifty years of negotiation have produced something neither side could have made alone: a clearing that has to be remade every morning, and a plant that has been shaped, channelled, mapped and whitewashed into something like a public utility. The power would recognise that relationship. You do not improve a marriage of that age by removing one of the partners.

So, visibly, it would do nothing.

No light in the bowl. No voice under the leaves. No winter in which the town wakes to find the boardwalks miraculously level and every stem cut back to the property line. The people of Sallowmere would distrust a miracle like that, and rightly. They would go out that same morning with their hooks anyway, to check what had been done, and by whose hand, and whether an Arterial had been nicked in the dark.

What it would do, it would do at its own scale, the way the Sallowmerers do it at theirs.

They keep the plant from swallowing the houses. It would keep the world from swallowing the town.

Quietly, at the rim, it would perform a larger kind of cutting-back. It would prune attention. The rim railway would go on running — not profitably enough to attract a company with plans, not badly enough to be closed. Surveyors sent to assess the bowl for development, for biomass, for a research station, for a heritage experience with a visitor centre at the Top End, would find their reports strangely hard to finish. Their photographs would come out flat. Their costings would acquire, somewhere between the ridge and the office, an extra hand's breadth of difficulty that made another site, any other site, seem more sensible. The place would remain on maps, but slightly wrong — a few centimetres sideways, the way everything in Sallowmere moves — and that wrongness would protect it better than any fence.

It would maintain the weather of the bowl the way the town maintains a drain: not by commanding it, but by keeping a gap open. As the country beyond the rim grows warmer and less certain, it would preserve, unnoticed, the cool nights under the leaves, the slow gathering of moisture on every underside and stem, the drip from six until nine. Not more rain. Not less. The exact amount of damp a life here requires, and knows how to catch in barrels.

And underneath — down in the black, sour wetness that closes over a spade — it would do the one piece of husbandry the town cannot do for itself, because the town lives too close and too daily to see the risk.

A monoculture is a brave thing. One organism, or one family so intergrown it makes no difference, is one disease away from silence. So in the one unmanaged corner by the outflow, where the moorhen sometimes nests and is treated as news, the power would lay down a sleeping reserve: other strains of motherleaf, folded into the mud, carrying in their rhizomes small differences of bitter sap and leaf and season that the bowl has never needed. Not a new plant. Nothing to change the colour of the green or the smell on a visitor's coat. Just diversity, held in reserve, the way a careful town leaves slack in a cable. If blight ever comes over the rim on the wind, the bowl will yellow, sicken — and then, from the outflow corner, put up strange, slightly unfamiliar fists of leaf that know how to live with it. The map will be wronger than usual that spring. The winter arguments in the town hall will be long. They will manage.

It would leave no other gift, except perhaps this: that those who wish to leave Sallowmere find the road out easier than they feared, and those who wish to stay are never made fools of, economically or otherwise, for staying. That a child born in the Terraces is not trapped by the green, and not exiled from it either. Freedom, in a place like this, is also a kind of spacing. Everything needs room to be cut back — plants, and lives, too.

Then the power would withdraw beyond the ridge, unthanked, because it would never have announced itself, and the town would go on without knowing it had ever been noticed by anything greater than a heron.

That is not a failure of the story. That is the story, told at a larger scale.

For the Sallowmerers would recognise the method at once, if they were ever told. They do not conquer the motherleaf, and they do not worship it. They attend to it. They listen through their boots. They keep, together, the space between rhizome and house, between neighbour and neighbour, between this year and next.

A truly benevolent power would do exactly the same, one ring further out.

It would not make the clearing.

It would keep a clearing around the clearing — and then it would leave the people of Sallowmere, as they have always asked to be left, to get up in the morning rain that is not rain, latch their doors, take up their hooks, and make it again themselves.
