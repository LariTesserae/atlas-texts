If it came, it would come as they do: through cloud, without announcement.

It would not arrive above the valley in light. Light is not a kindness here. A clear sky, a true disc of sun, a week of dry warmth — an ignorant benevolence might think these gifts, and give them, and watch the moss die back from the walls and the canals drop, watch the fogged greenhouses heat until the lettuce bolted and the condensed water stopped dripping from the walkways, watch people pale from a hundred years of diffuse grey squint and burn in their own home. A wise power would know the first thing this place teaches everyone who enters it.

Do not stand in the gateway.

So it would step aside.

It would learn the pace of the racks and the sequence of the sluices. It would wait in a proper recess, out of the lane, while the harvest carts went in. It would take, if such a thing can be said of a power, the whole long education of the valley: how to tell by the drips on a roof which tank is filling, how to hear a pump change its mind through the soles of the feet, how to understand that eleven degrees is not suffering here but weather, that wet cuffs are not a problem to be solved but the signature of a life that fits its place as a hand fits a worn handle.

And having learned, having been tempted — for benevolence is always tempted — it would do almost nothing.

It would not enlarge the valley. There is nowhere to enlarge to; the ridges are the ridges, and the being that lives here has grown exactly to the shape of its basin over a hundred years, terrace by terrace, until town and factory and farm and body are one continuous negotiation. To add room would be to unbalance it as surely as to take room away.

It would not make the work easy. The work is not a curse laid on these people by an unkind world. The work is the relationship: scrape the moss, plant the gabion, cut the beans back from the fan and train them up the pipe again next week. Take the negotiation away and you do not free the tenders. You dissolve them.

It would not make them immortal, or infinite, or safe forever. It has watched living things long enough to know what a sealed system becomes. The young must still be able to take the cable car down with a dry bag. Strangers must still be able to marry in and spend five clumsy years learning not to block a lane. The ash of the dead must still go up, after the formalities, to the high beds where the alpine flowers are. A wise power does not turn a living valley into a preserved one. It does not put glass over the glasshouses.

What it would do, it would do the way everything durable is done here: quietly, at the scale of maintenance, in a manner the being could absorb piece by piece without ever stopping.

Once in a generation, perhaps, when the cloud tears on the upper ridge and a storm comes in sideways that is a fraction beyond what the intakes were built for, something would hold. Not visibly. No hand in the sky. Only the upper gates taking a load they should not quite have taken, and settling back into their frames with the usual muted clunk, and the water-keepers afterward shaking their heads at a gauge and writing the reading down anyway.

Deep under the middle terraces, where the original stream was given walls and a schedule, there is concrete that has been bearing weight in the damp for eighty years. A wise power would know which slow crack matters and which is only the valley's face weathering. The one that matters would stop deepening. That is all. A repair no one is billed for, discovered years later by a woman with a lamp who frowns because she remembers marking that crack, and it has not moved, and she chalks a new date beside the old one and moves on.

Down below the cloud — for even a power that loves a place must sometimes act at a distance from it — certain papers would simply never quite go wrong. The mineral claim that might have reopened the headwall mines at industrial scale would founder in an office, mis-filed, out-argued by a duller claim. The company that looked at the terraced watershed and saw only head and flow and a dam would find the financing perpetually almost there. Tour operators would develop a vague, unexamined conviction that the valley is hard to photograph and the cable car unreliable in the season they'd want it. None of this dramatic. Just the world below, which is loud and dry-handed, gently failing to reach up and assign this place a new purpose.

And now and then, diversity — the one treasure a closed, careful system cannot quite manufacture for itself. Not new crops lowered in glory. Only sparrows, those bold permanent tenants under the canteen eaves, arriving in spring with seeds in their guts from gardens far below; only a raven dropping a berry from some unvisited crag into a gabion; only a wind, once a decade, that carries spores and pollen over the ridge from a valley the cable car does not serve. Small, deniable, living imports. The glasshouse families would find a volunteer seedling that matches nothing in the ledger, test it with a stained forefinger, and keep it if it earns its shelf. They are good at that. They have always been good at that.

The power might permit itself one gift that is not maintenance.

In a fold of the headwall, above the highest intake, where the cable buckets pass and no terrace was ever poured — a place too steep, too small, too useless ever to be assigned — it might let a pocket of the old world persist, untouched and unneeded. Real soil, unmade. Wild thyme. A spring that answers to no valve. Nothing harvested, nothing scraped, nothing stenciled with a number. It would not show the people. It would not need to. The gift would not be for them, exactly.

It would be for the being they are part of, so that somewhere in its own body there remained one corner that was not a negotiation — a place where the valley, the single valley-sized organism that has learned to feed and water and repair and green itself at the altitude where clouds are made, could simply be a valley, briefly, the way a worker in a painted recess is briefly only a person, resting a hand on a rail, saying nothing, listening to water find its next level.

Then the power would go, if going is the word, the way the light goes at dusk here: not departing so much as ceasing to be distinguishable from the conditions of the place. The cloud would drop. The upper greenhouses would become rectangles of faintly lit mist. A pump would sigh. A crew would pass singly with lamps, voices at ordinary volume for three sentences and then gone behind the tomato vines.

No one would raise a monument. Monuments would block a lane.

But for a long while afterward — a hundred years, say, which is the timescale this place understands — the terraces would hold their form by replacing themselves, piece by piece, without stopping; the basins would keep their water; the children would learn the safe recesses before they learned anything formal; an old widower would keep, in defiance of all regulation and with universal connivance, three pots of useless, beautiful gentians on a valve housing, and the gentians would do inexplicably, stubbornly well.

As if something, having seen them clearly and loved them wisely, had understood that the greatest mercy it could offer was not to lift them out of the cloud.

It was to make sure the cloud, and they, could go on meeting.
