It would not rescue them.

That is the first wisdom, and the hardest.

A lesser power, arriving above that black, too-young peak and seeing the roofed fissure from the air — the membrane belling in the wind, the rime boxes, the throats always dry, the sleep broken by thin air, the eyes adjusting at noon to permanent early evening — a lesser power would bring water.

It would bring rain under the roof. It would bring tanks, pipes, greenhouses. It would thicken the air until shouting was easy. It would make the descent unnecessary and the cable obsolete and the whole careful, tethered life a primitive embarrassment to be dismantled with gratitude.

A wise one looks longer.

It looks until it sees that the place is not a camp of people enduring a mountain, with some birds inconveniently in the rafters. It sees one being, many-bodied, living in one warm mouth of stone: swifts who were there before the roof, choughs in the truss-junctions, sparrows who have never seen a tree, mice in the ducts, moths making weather around the lamps, spiders holding the warm corners, and the large, slow, wool-wrapped animals who have learned — as all the others learned — to live in tiers, to move muffled, to check a neighbor's tether, to speak only on the exhale and close enough to touch.

It sees that the scarcity is not only a wound. It is also the shape of their tenderness. Speech is intimate because breath is costly. No creature is a pest because every creature is information. The crane sits idle for the nest because the nest is not an interruption of the city. It is the city, declaring one of its priorities.

So the power checks its own tether first.

It learns the rules before it touches anything: *Tether everything. Water is weighed, not poured. Do not shout. Do not net a bird.*

Then it does almost nothing. Almost.

On the night when the wind comes down off the summit harder than the roof was built for — not the ordinary tearing river of it, but the once-in-thirty-years gust that would find the patched panel on the north end and peel it like a lip — it puts itself, briefly, between the gust and the roof. Not stopping the wind. The wind is the mountain's own breath, and the city was built in the one place sheltered from it precisely because the people understood that. Just... easing it by the width of one rib. Enough that the membrane sighs instead of tearing. Below, in the sleeping tiers, no one wakes. A lamp string swings in its slow arc. A chough shifts its red feet in its sleep. In the morning the roof-menders will find a bolt strained almost to failure and shake their heads at their luck, and replace it, and that will be the whole miracle: that there was a morning in which to replace it.

It does that kind of thing.

In a winter when the rime comes thin and the crews come back with light boxes and frost in their eyebrows, it does not make it rain. It breathes, once, very cold, across the windward rock in the hour before dawn, where the scrapers are already roped and waiting. A little more white fur on the stone. A few kilograms more, weighed and signed for. Enough for the flasks, enough for the scrubbers, not enough to change the economy of thirst that teaches those hands their gentleness.

When a fledgling falls — as fledglings do — from a sheave housing to a catwalk, it does not catch it in a visible hand. It makes the fall happen beside the one rigger who is looking up at that second, the one whose gloves have the fingertips cut away and whose hands are fast from splicing cable. He catches it without thinking, the way he would catch a dropped tool that mattered, and sets it back, and chalks a note on the nest-map. The bird lives. More importantly, the map is updated by a human hand, and the custom holds another season.

It goes below, sometimes, along the cable.

Not up here is where this being is most vulnerable. Up here they know their dangers by sound — a rib's creak, a fan's laboring note, the wrong silence where birds won't fly. Below, in the cloud and the thick green air, are people who could cut the bright line for reasons that would seem excellent down there: a war, a debt, an efficiency, a new road. The power does not smite them. It makes the paperwork go missing twice. It makes a surveyor, standing in rain he doesn't notice he's standing in, look up at the cable vanishing into cloud and feel a sudden, inexplicable reluctance to be the man who severed it. It puts the thought in a clerk's head at 3 a.m.: *check what that line feeds, first.* Small deflections. The lowlanders remain free, and mostly decent, and the drum in the fissure keeps turning, winding and unwinding the line that holds the hidden room to everything underneath it.

For the bodies themselves — the accumulating debits of altitude, the headache behind the eyes, the thought arriving a second late, the nose bleeding when the gate cracks — it does not remove the price. To remove it would be to unmake the adaptation, and to make these people tourists in their own home. But in the swaying tiers at lamp-out, when the gates are sealed and the fans are laboring through the walls, it sometimes deepens one hour of sleep per person per night into something truly restorative. Not more sleep. *Better.* The body's alarm quiets, briefly, as if the air were a breath thicker, as if a hand were resting very lightly on the sternum. They wake no different, except that the year can be borne, and then another year, for those who choose to stay. And for those who choose the descent — because some do, and the wise power loves the choosing as much as the staying — it eases the thickening air on the way down, so that going home does not feel like drowning in plenty.

Once, it considers a gift, and thinks better of it.

A seed would be a cruelty dressed as kindness. Nothing green can be spared the light or the water, and to plant a dying thing in that dim, beloved place would teach the sparrows — generations removed from any tree — only a new kind of loss. So instead, now and then, it gives the long-stayers a dream. Not a message. Not a prophecy. Just a tree, in sleep, seen from underneath in windless lowland summer, leaves moving in air so thick and wet you could lean on it. They wake with their throats dry and their eyes wet, and they do not talk about it, because speaking costs breath — but they are gentler at the flasks that morning, and the idea of the tree stays real for another year without anyone having to water it.

It keeps the mountain honest in its geology, too. The peak is young and impatient and still settling; left alone it might shrug the fissure shut in a single bad decade, or open it to the wind forever. The power does neither. It simply holds the two lips of the stone in the relation they already have — neither healing the mouth closed nor letting it gape — the way you hold a door neither open nor shut for someone who lives in the doorway. The roof stays roofable. The Lips stay Lips. The ibex still come at night to lick salt and stare in at the lamplight, and no one shoos them, and so the outside is still in conversation with the inside.

If it loves them — and it does; that is why it came — it loves them the way they love each other: at close range, briefly, without waste. A hand on a forearm to keep the other there for the whole sentence. A tap on a tether: *check yours.* It does not hover, golden and explanatory, over the Yard. It does not want to be worshipped in a place that has no surplus for worship. It wants the forge to keep breathing orange through the grate. It wants the nest-map to stay current. It wants the rime crews, coming in before dawn with the heavy boxes, to be stared at for that one moment in the airlock — because they have been *outside*, where the horizon curves — and then to be given precedence at the water, by custom, without anyone having to say why.

So in the end its whole intervention amounts to this: the bolt holds one second longer. The box comes back a little heavier. The right person looks up. The clerk checks first. The sleep is deep. The dream has leaves in it.

Nothing is saved in a way anyone could point to. Everything is permitted to continue.

And at lamp-change, when the membrane sighs and the great drum turns and a swift cuts the dim air exactly where a head was a moment before — when the man on the ladder leans instinctively with the swinging lamps, clipped at the waist, and the chough above him is unconcerned, and the sparrows burst golden-dust from under the conveyor, and some voice, when it comes at last, comes low and brief and close into another's ear —

the power, unseen, clipped in, breathing the thickened air as carefully as any resident, says nothing at all.

It just checks its neighbor's tether, and stays.
