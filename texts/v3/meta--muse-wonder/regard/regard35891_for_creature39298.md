They would not come down.

That would be the first decision, and in some ways the only one that mattered. A power wise enough to be truly benevolent would feel the temptation at once — because the chamber invites every familiar form of rescue. Make the pool cooler. Make the films flourish and carpet the black glass. Widen the inflow so the blind white visitors can survive, breed, become a population. Bring light of another kind. Bring meaning. Bring company.

They would stand, if standing is the word, above the eleven-year-old lava with its boot-cutting edges, and let the temptation pass through them without obeying it.

Because what draws them is not a place failing to live. It is a place living exactly at the scale it can bear.

The films do not want a garden. They are not diminished versions of forests waiting for better conditions; they are chemistry that has learned persistence at a seam — sulfide against oxygen, hot against warm, growth against dissolution in the same centimetre. To make them abundant would not be to bless them. It would be to replace them with something louder.

The amphipod, when one comes, would be harder. Pale, eyeless, no longer than a grain of rice, grazing for days in the only survivable water it will ever find down there, unable to cross the shining desert of the pool, unable to go back. A lesser kindness would rescue every one. A lesser wisdom would build it a way through and call the chamber, at last, a habitat.

They would not.

Not because its small death does not matter — they would attend to it particularly, the way one attends to any brief life that has arrived by accident and will not repeat — but because its body becoming film, and film becoming food, and food becoming visitor again after long months, is not an error in the room. It is part of how the room's emptiness stays honest. To save it from the edge would be to deny that the edge is where it, transiently, belongs.

And then there is the other being. The real resident.

They would spend the longest time with that one.

Sixty metres of basalt reworked below microscopy, veins without a locatable heart, a slow thermal organism that exists by sipping heat from the dike below and carrying it to radiators in a coastal town. A thing made by people who then did the rarest thing people do: they stopped at the wall. It feeds, and by feeding, keeps the pool from boiling and from sealing. Twice a day it drinks deeper, and the orange line in the floor dulls toward red, the steam thins, the dripping slows, and then the heat gathers itself again.

A benevolent power would recognise kinship there, and also a warning. This being's whole virtue is restraint repeated until restraint becomes metabolism. It inhabits everything around the chamber and leaves the chamber alone. It knows the room only through a dozen dark grains on a dry ledge, turning almost imperceptibly toward warmth and chemistry. To reveal themselves to it — to flood its sparse senses with a presence it has no gradient for — would be a kind of violence disguised as communion.

So they would do what it does. They would inhabit the surroundings, and leave the interior untouched.

Outside the chamber, there is work a wise power can do without breaking anything.

They would guard the obscurity of the place. Not with wonders, not with terror. Quietly. A survey that finds the basalt above the chamber unremarkable. A dataset that bores the wrong kind of curiosity and rewards the right kind of patience. In the town on the coast, in the offices where the grid is tended, generation after generation, they would keep alive not merely the technical knowledge of how the lattice feeds, but the *reason* it was built the way it was built: that you can draw heat from a living seam without opening the seam; that efficiency is not the highest argument; that some found things are best kept by being lightly held. When demand grows — and it would grow — they would favour new warmth from elsewhere, a wind farm approved a season early, an insulation scheme that suddenly seems obvious, so that the twice-daily sip never becomes a gulp.

They would also prepare, gently, for the end that even this held balance must have. The dike below will cool on a timescale bacteria and towns alike cannot imagine. A careless immortality, imposed from above, could keep the lattice clutching at diminishing heat long past the room's own wish to close, forcing the crack, starving the films by inches, turning stewardship into haunting.

So they would give the keeper nothing it would notice as a gift. Only, woven into its slow responses, a grace it almost already has: as the heat wanes, century by century, to wane with it. To let the silica chimneys finally grow together. To let the inflow narrow and seal. To let the bubble in the basalt finish, at last, becoming stone — not as a failure of care, but as its completion.

Inside the chamber, they would change no temperature by so much as a degree. They would not add light to the amber light already welling through the water. They would not still the forty-minute breath, nor deepen it into speech.

They would do one thing only, which is not a doing.

They would witness it fully for a while. One whole cycle. Bubbles rising in strings from the glowing crack. The surface shivering. Steam thickening until the far wall is a suggestion and the room is all diffuse orange, pressure sighing out through the throat high on the west side into dark no one has mapped. Then the thinning, the brief warm rain of condensation, the pool clearing again. A single drip swelling the silence rather than breaking it. The dark grains on the ledge holding their orientation against the patter. Somewhere in the inflow threads, perhaps, the minute movement of a visitor grazing.

To be known completely, and not used. To be loved, and not entered. For a place that has survived precisely by being deliberately untouched, that attention would be the only miracle that did not cost it itself.

Then they would withdraw the way the probe once did — by a hole the width of a hair, healing behind them within a season. No mark. No shrine. No new resident added to the sparse census of films and visitors and the vast slow keeper in the walls.

Only this difference, afterwards, almost too small to measure: that the chamber, hidden three hundred metres under black stone, lit from below, doing its work in near silence, would go on being kept twice over — once by the being in the rock that sips its heat and never enters, and once by a power that had learned, from watching it, how to do the same.
