First, they would not arrive as a rescue.

They would come in the way everything else comes in here: slowly, along the one high causeway, getting wetter as they went.

From a distance — if anyone had been watching from the roofs, anyone not busy with a gasket or a grating or a row of beans waiting for its brush — they would have seemed unremarkable, which would have been the first kindness. Not shining. Not dry for long. By the time the packed cinder firmed under their feet and the lanes began to divide the roofs, their coat would have stopped shedding water and begun to hold it, like everyone else's. The smell of elsewhere would have rinsed off them into the leat.

They would have been taken, at first, for another newcomer who might stay a season or dry out and leave. They would have been given a borrowed bed over the coal store. A warm cloth for their feet. Soup, because soup was already possible. A place, if evening came and the pump slowed, in the row of chairs where the outflow warmed the stones.

And they would have had to learn — benevolent and wise and powerful as they were — to be corrected.

That is the test this town sets, without knowing it sets one. Not: can you survive the wet? Anyone softens. Not: can you bear the noise? Anyone learns to pause on the thud without knowing they are pausing. The test is older and quieter. Can you put your hand to a wall, feel who is home through the brick, and not immediately think that the wall should be drier, straighter, newer? Can you watch a woman spend a grey morning moving down a greenhouse row with a little brush, flower by flower, because there are no bees, and feel the wrongness of the missing bees without despising the tenderness that has grown up in their place?

Power, here, would discover all its temptations wearing the faces of gifts.

To lift the town a hand's breadth out of the marsh. Only a hand's breadth. Only so the lowest floors would dry, so children's feet could be checked for something other than tenderness, so paper could live outside tin boxes and lungs could lose, in a generation, that soft middle-aged cough. The bricks would take it. The people would take less easily. Every threshold in town is a year. Every coal hatch was a grandmother's window. Raise them all at once and you have not saved their calendar; you have abolished it. You have told them, in the language of foundations, that the way they have measured their dead was an error.

To heal the pump, then. Just that. To make the loose inspection plate stop rattling. To ease the flywheels into a cleaner silence, so speech did not have to braid itself around a stroke every twenty seconds. It would be easy to call that mercy. It would also be a kind of theft no one here has a word for, because theft here is mostly of tools left ungreased, and no tool is as shared as that rhythm. Babies time signs to it. Arguments cool in it. The dying go on the hiss, as if let out, and the midwife knows when to fold the hands. Take away the stroke and you do not give them quiet. You give them a silence they would have to learn to breathe in, alone, each at a different speed.

To bring back the animals.

Ah. That one would come at night, probably, in the borrowed bed, when the Works dropped to its low cycle and the totality of the absence opened up under the machinery: forty seconds between strokes, and nothing small and alive to fill the gaps. No scratching. No thin night-calling from the reed beds. Just dripping, just brick settling, just people breathing in time.

A benevolent power might lie there with a perfect frog already imagined in the palm — cool, gold-eyed, innocent — and think: *one.* What harm in one? Put it in the southern reed bed, where the water leaves. Let it sing once beyond the low iron bridge. Let the children who think a pigeon is a kind of valve hear something answer the pump from outside the town, at a pitch their ears have been gently unmade for.

And the wise power, being wise, would keep its hand closed a while longer.

Not because the frog would be wrong. Because the frog would be *first*, and first things here are never only themselves. A frog means midges to feed it and herons to hunt it and green skins broken from below and a whole returning grammar of bite and rot and startlement in the eaves. It means leaves with holes in them — which is to say, leaves as the rest of the world knows leaves, eaten and therefore part of something. It would mean the town would have to decide, lane by lane and generation by generation, what it was willing to share itself with, after centuries of being shaped by not sharing.

That decision is not a gift if someone makes it for you while you sleep.

So they would do what nobody from outside ever quite believes is enough, until they have stayed long enough to be slowed down by the flywheels.

They would work.

You would find them, if you knew where to look, down the damp steps behind the pump hall in the cistern vault, standing ankle-deep where the black water mirrors the lamps perfectly, with one palm flat against a brick arch. Not praying, exactly. Listening downward. There is a place in that arch — no mason living knows it, because the man who laid it badly died before the book swelled shut on his name — where the mortar has gone the colour of tea and the bricks have begun, very gradually, to consider parting. Not this year. Not in ten. In a grandchild's time, on a night when the header tanks are full and the marsh is high and the pump finds, for once, more weight than rhythm can answer.

No one has asked for help with this because no one knows to ask. It is outside the great shared knowing of hands. It is the one failure the town's maintenance cannot maintain its way around, because it is hiding inside the maintenance itself.

The power would not rebuild the vault. They would not make it new, or dry, or straight, or safe forever; forever is not a unit this place trusts. They would do the smaller, harder thing. They would repoint it in the town's own idiom: brick by brick, wet by wet, at pump-stroke pace, resting on the thud, working on the hiss, so that the arch would go on doing what it has always done — hold the whole weight up over black water — for another few generations of thresholds. Long enough for the people who live above it to go on deciding their own lives at sinking speed. A repair so faithful that, if anyone ever found it, they would assume a great-grandfather had been particularly careful there, and stub no toe on the difference.

And they would do one other thing, out beyond the low iron bridge, where the strained water slips away slower and cleaner than it came in and the town, properly speaking, ends.

There, in the far reed beds where the marsh resumes its own counsel and no lane has ever been packed down, they would loosen whatever it is — old exclusion, old habit of the place, old unspoken instruction in steam and vibration and warm green water — that has kept the world so politely, so completely, from creeping back. Not open it. Loosen it. The way you ease a fig's roots yearly from hot brick, so the wall and the fig can go on negotiating.

Then they would withdraw their hand before anything arrived.

So that if, some evening years from now, when the pump has slowed to one stroke in forty and the chairs are out and the amber bulbs are doubling themselves below knee-height in the second, watery town — if, in the gap after a hiss, very far south and barely at the edge of hearing trained low and deep by machinery, something small and thin and alive should clear its throat in the reeds —

the people would hear it in their own time.

Some would lift their heads. Some would not. A child who had learned *frog* as a dead word in a swollen book above the flywheels would say it aloud, perhaps, in the careful voice of someone pronouncing a language that has unexpectedly answered back. An old woman might cock an ear, not toward the sound, but toward the loose inspection plate, out of a lifetime's duty, to be sure its tempo had not changed. It would not have changed. That, too, would be part of the mercy.

They could slap at ankles that needed no slapping. They could go down in the morning to look at the unbroken green, and find it broken, far off, in one small place — and decide whether to rake it smooth again or let the break widen at the pace of water. They could leave it for a season. They could leave it for a grandmother. They could pollinate with brushes for another hundred years, if that is what their hands chose. Nothing would have been returned to them by force. Nothing wild would yet have been invited past the bridge. Only the future would have been made possible again, out where possibility belongs: at the margin, approaching slowly, able to be refused.

And the power?

The power would leave before they were thanked, because thanks would require a name, and a name would require a story, and a story here becomes a threshold people step over for generations. Better to become, at most, a rumour of a newcomer who stayed exactly one season, whose stride shortened properly, whose voice dropped into the hiss, who learned not to try to get a collar dry, who was taught by a six-year-old to find a dropped gasket in green shadow and was, by the end, almost trustworthy with a bearing: stroke, turn, wipe again.

They would walk out along the causeway in the warm cloud, while the smoke spread sideways and joined it, while behind them the town ticked and dripped and settled, low and loud and wet, one finger's width a year into the marsh and pumped, by that exact amount, back up.

Their shoes would be dark and swollen to the shape of their feet. They would walk the way the town had taught them without teaching: flat, unhurried, a slight roll outward, a pause so small you'd only see it after a day of watching, while the foot found the firm ground under the silt.

They would never step where the duckweed was unbroken.

Not yet.
