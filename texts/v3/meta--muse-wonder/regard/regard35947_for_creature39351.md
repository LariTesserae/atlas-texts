They would come down the three switchbacks slowly, because everyone does the first time — not from caution, but because the sound reaches you before the understanding does, up through the wheels, up through the floor of the car, and the body slows itself to listen.

And they would stay a long time.

That is the first thing wisdom would require of them, and the hardest. Not an hour. Not a season. Long enough for the benevolent impulses to wear themselves out.

Because benevolence, arriving here, would feel all its usual urges at once, very strongly.

It would want to give them silence.

It would see the workers shouting mouth-to-ear under the compressor roar, the children learning that low, breathless murmur, the visitors with headaches by evening, the starlings doing the ammonia alarm so perfectly that human beings have had to surrender sound itself and warn each other by smell — and it would think: *one quiet morning. Just one. Let them hear each other. Let them hear themselves think.*

It would want to give them seasons.

It would see the sparrows raising chick after chick in the warm lagging, in December as in July, the frogs calling and mating and laying in water that has never frozen and never warmed, everything locked at nine degrees and damp, on the first evening of spring, for thirty-one years — and it would think: *rest them. Let the chorus stop. Let the females rest, let the ground lie empty, let winter come and clean this place.*

It would want to give them room.

Every perch taken. Every rail, ridge, wire, bollard and windowsill shouldered with a bird facing into the draft, beak open, and above them the great slow queue turning, turning, waiting for a death to make a space. It would think: *clear half of them away to somewhere kinder. Thin them. Net them off humanely. Let the roofs be roofs again.*

A benevolent and wise power would feel all of that — and then, if it stayed long enough, it would understand that every one of those mercies would be a killing.

Silence here is not peace. Silence is what wakes them at 3 a.m. in hotel rooms out in the world, ears ringing with absence. The roar is the medium they sleep in, speak in, measure danger by. A change in the level is the only alarm left that works.

Winter is not rest. Winter would be the sumps freezing under frogs who have forgotten how to stop calling, chicks starving in lagging gone cold, a population with no fat laid down for it, no migration route rehearsed, no memory of scarcity in any living body. They have not been denied a season. They have become a creature for whom there is only one season, the way deep-sea things have become creatures for whom there is only dark. You do not enlighten those by opening the sea.

And room — room is the cruelest idea of all. The population is not crowded past its number. It *is* its number. Food at a fixed rate, heat at a fixed rate, midges at a fixed rate, perches at a fixed rate, solved exactly, held exactly. Empty half the roofs and the queue above would fill them before the bodies hit the ground. Clear the queue too, and you have not freed the town. You have only amputated the upper half of a single body and watched it grow back, tail for tail, into the same silhouette.

Because that is what they would come, slowly, to see, standing still in the middle of the main street at midday with their coat dampening at the shoulders and a fine white dust settling on their sleeves: there is no *them* here, in the plural, waiting to be rescued from a place.

The being who lives here is the holding.

It is the gull and the vacancy the gull drops into. It is the orange worker crossing the yard and the orange worker who crossed yesterday, taking the same post. It is the swift colony in the pipe lagging and the pipe that can therefore never be replaced. It is the falcon in Cooling Tower Two, who came to end the birds and became, by seniority, a foreman. It is the cats who have learned not to chase in the middle of abundance, the ravens in the siren that will never sound, the frogs making the drain covers tick, the people who warn each other with wintergreen because their tenants speak their language better than they do. It is not a community trapped in an equilibrium.

Like the town itself tried to tell them: it *is* the equilibrium, wearing ten thousand shapes, and it has done the thing almost no living thing ever manages to do. It has stopped wanting more. It does not build up. It does not run down. It has found its one note and it is, in the only sense that matters to anything alive, content to hold it.

So inside the quarry, the power would do nothing.

Nothing at all. No clearing, no quieting, no season sent down like a blessing. No perch removed, no perch miraculously added in the bowl — a new perch would be filled in a second and mean nothing, a removed perch would be a small death sentence passed up the queue. They would not heal the plant into cleanliness, either, and that restraint would cost them most of all, watching the boilers run dirty and wasteful and inefficient year after year, because the waste is the food, the leaks are the warmth, the constant load is the climate. A clean, modern, well-run plant would starve this place more completely than any winter.

Inside the quarry, they would just learn the courtesies. Step around the nests in the doorways without looking down. Work around the swifts as around a load-bearing beam. Never recommission the siren. Face, politely, into the draft.

All of their work would be done outside, where the being cannot see it, and where its real dangers live.

For the being has no predators in the bowl. Its predators are all at a distance, and they are all reasonable people. A regulator with a new emissions standard. A parent company deciding the gas line can be stabilized more cheaply two valleys away. A council that finally notices a town living like this and sends help, nets, veterinarians, journalists, a plan. A well-meaning century that cannot abide a place doing nothing new for thirty-one years and calls that, automatically, a problem to be solved.

Against those, quietly, over decades, the power would move.

An exemption renewed. A contract extended without anyone quite remembering who argued for it. The food-store cold contract kept, marginally unprofitable, marginally indispensable. The road down the heath kept bad enough to discourage visiting but good enough for the shift bus. The quarry bought, and bought again through other names, and left off maps a little more thoroughly each edition. Parts for the old boilers appearing in the supply chain long after the manufacturer swears they were discontinued. Nothing miraculous. Miracles would be noticed. Just the world, again and again, failing to get around to ending this place.

Not forever. Wisdom is not the same as making a thing immortal, and benevolence would know — as the being, in its animal way, perfectly knows — that the note is held by replacement, not by permanence. Every bird on that roof is dying. Every worker in orange will be replaced. The boilers themselves are only being replaced more slowly, piece by piece around the nests. One day the holding will falter for reasons no exemption can fix, and on that day the power would not nail the town together and force it to sing on, terrified and thinning, past its time. They would let the level change at last. They would be there to scatter what could scatter, and warm what could be warmed through its first real cold, and witness the rest without calling it a tragedy either. Holding is holy. So is ending, when it comes in its turn.

But that is not yet.

For now, they would do one more thing, and it is so small that perhaps it is not a doing at all.

Up on the heath, above the quarry lip, where the cloud moves over wet stone and the sound of the town arrives only as a low, even pressure in the ground — up there, where there are no perches, no waste, no warm ducts, no reason — they would set a single length of wire between two posts. Unclaimed. Facing the wind.

Most days no bird would touch it. It offers nothing. It is cold, and there is no food, and below it the queue turns in perfect patience over ten thousand better places to stand. But now and then — once a year, once in five years — a starling dropping out of the spiral for no reason anyone could name might land there. Just for a minute. In real weather. In a place where spring is not the only thing that has ever happened, and where its own silhouette is not already waiting to be filled.

Not a door forced open. Not an escape route, not a lesson, not a season imposed. Only the existence, at the edge of a complete world, of one perch that does not need to be taken — proof that the holding is a holding, and not a cage, because something in it could, if it ever wished to, stop.

Then the power would go back down the switchbacks, into the roar that comes up through the floor, and take up the only position that love can honestly take here:

in the queue, with everybody else, turning slowly over the roofs, in no hurry at all —

waiting, and not filling, any space that is not theirs.
