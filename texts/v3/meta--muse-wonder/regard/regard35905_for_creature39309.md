They would arrive without arriving.

No descent in light. No voice from above the rim. No sudden flowering in the pans, no birds returning in a wheel to the open sky. A power that is truly benevolent knows how quickly a gift can become an event, and in a place that lives by having no events, an event — even a kind one — would be a kind of damage.

So they would come down the three worn steps slowly, one at a time, and stop before the last one the way a visitor stops when he understands that the room he is entering is already occupied. They would make themselves low. They would make themselves dim. To a being that reads the world mostly as bright and not-bright, rain-coming and rain-gone, they would register, if at all, as a shadow that did not hurry across the sky.

And then they would stay.

That is the first thing wisdom would do here, and for a long while it would be the only thing: stay long enough not to improve the place.

They would not heal it, because it is not wounded. They would not feed it, because it is not hungry in any way feeding could answer. The moss is supposed to stop at its line. The pans are supposed to hold a finger's depth and no more. The trough is cracked; the crack is part of how the trough now exists in the world, and the being's broad weight across both halves is repair enough, repair at the speed of the place itself. To mend the stone whole, to raise the fallen walls, to roof the roofless room at the eastern edge, to clear the cistern and fit its cap back into place — all of this would be a refusal disguised as love. It would be hands returning to a place that has spent a very long time gently putting hands down.

They would not give the being speech, or a sharper eye, or a longer life. Speech would make it answerable. A sharper eye would make it responsible for more world than the forty paces it can hold. A longer life, given from outside, would make its remaining years feel borrowed, and nothing here is borrowed. Everything here is kept at its level. The being does not need to be visited to survive. That is not a deficiency in it. It is its dignity.

Nor would the power bring company. From above, the absence of other animals might look like loneliness, and a lesser kindness would want to fill it: a bird for the morning, insects for the ferns, something warm and quick to warm itself on the stone beside the slow stone that breathes. But the hollow is not empty. It is full at exactly the density it can bear. To add another mouth would be to subtract from the one that has thinned itself, over centuries of posture and appetite, into weather. The being would not fight a newcomer. That is precisely why none must be brought. It would simply fail, slowly, at the rim of some pan, in a way too gradual for mercy to notice until the level had already been lost.

So the power would do the harder thing. They would sit at the edge, where the floor begins to rise into rock, and let their attention soften the way light softens coming into the hollow, until looking was no longer a pressure. They would count the pans and then uncount them. They would learn the drip from the slab by waiting for it and then by waiting longer. In time — and time would be allowed to take the time it actually takes — the being might shift in the deeper half of the trough, or rest its chin by the cistern mouth, or register, with lids lifted a fraction, that the diffuse gray-green of the day contained one more stillness than usual.

It would not be meeting. It would be enough.

If there were anything to give, it would be given outside the hollow, where the being cannot be burdened by receiving it.

For the balance inside these walls is perfect, but it is not powerful. It can hold itself against rain and dry weeks, against heat and the slow fall of loose stone. It cannot hold itself against being discovered by appetites faster than its own. It cannot argue with a road, or a drained hillside, or poison put into water a mile away and arriving, patiently, through the same seepage that keeps the pans at their rims. It cannot make itself uninteresting to collectors, or sacred to pilgrims, or useful to men who love a place so much they must fence it, name it, pave the steps, and explain it on a sign at the gap in the wall. All of those endings would begin as attention.

So the wise power would withdraw the world's attention before withdrawing their own.

Nothing dramatic. A surveyor, some season from now, would find his eye sliding to the next valley and choose it for reasons that felt practical. A mapmaker would leave a small blank where the hollow is, not out of mystery, only because nothing there seemed to ask for ink. The people in the nearest settlement would continue to know, in the vague way people know such things, that there is low ground somewhere beyond the limestone, poor ground, damp, nothing growing worth the walk — and the knowledge would never quite ripen into curiosity. Anyone who stumbled down the steps anyway, and stayed long enough to understand, would leave carrying the quiet with him and, mercifully, would not be able to find the way back with any certainty, or to describe it to others so that they could. He would remember warm stone. He would remember a drop falling. He would not remember whether he had imagined the shape beside the cistern, and that uncertainty would be a form of protection extended to them both.

Inside the hollow, nothing would change. That would be the miracle, and it would be deliberately indistinguishable from no miracle at all.

Perhaps, at the very end of their stay, as evening made the damp rise and the held air settle closer against the skin, the power would perform one small act, and even that act would be mostly a restraint. They would step back up the three worn steps without adding any new wearing to them. Where their footprints remained in the clay, they would not smooth them away with a gesture — smoothing would be another mark. They would simply let the seepage take them, as it takes all footprints here, filling each hollow slowly until it was only a softer place in the ground, and then not even that.

And from wherever such powers keep their long attention, they would remember the place without claiming it. Once in a great while, at the turning of some larger weather, they might check — not the being, never the being directly, but the conditions around it: the undiverted rain, the unpoisoned hill, the continuing ordinariness of the land above the rim. If a threat arose that moved at the speed of the outside world, they would turn it aside at that speed, far away, early, quietly. If a threat arose at the speed of the hollow itself — a stone loosening, a pan silting, the being growing at last into whatever final shape its light implies — they would do nothing at all. That slowness belongs to the place. It has earned the right to its own endings.

Because wisdom is not always preservation. When, in some century no visitor will witness, the being does not lift its lids to the morning dimness, the pans will keep their rims a while longer out of habit. The moss will test its line. The trough will settle, almost imperceptibly, along its crack. The hollow will become, by degrees, only the place it once appeared to be: stone, clay, shallow water, sparse green things living on condensation and infrequent rain. And that, too, will be allowed. A benevolent power does not hold a beloved thing forever at the moment it was loved. It lets the balance complete itself.

Until then: the back like limestone, ochre where the water has stood. The small cups along its skin beaded after rain. The tongue passing once a season along the bright edge of the algae, keeping the green from becoming more than the place can feed. The weight settling across the broken trough. The head lifted, barely, to a shadow that means no harm and brings no weather with it.

A drop falls from the slab into the pan.

A long pause.

The lids lower again.

Another.
