It would not go in.

Not at first, and not in the way a power is expected to go in — not with light, not with a voice, not with the lintel thrown wide and the deep cell opened at last to sight. It would arrive on the summit as everything else arrives there: slowly, against the wind, into air too thin to hold comfortably. And it would stay outside the drum a long time.

Long enough to learn the first lesson the place teaches anything that comes to it: that hurry is a form of violence here.

From outside, it would seem to do less than the weather. It would sit with its back to the black stone while the roof drank the sun. It would listen to the faint mineral breathing of the vents. It would watch the keepers come up the scree with their wooden tongs and woven nets and clay bailers, watch them go feet-first down the ladder and hold themselves silent, and rise again into the killing cold with nothing in their hands but the dead. It would understand that this, already, is close to a perfect ethics, arrived at by people who could not have explained it: *tend, feed twice a year, remove the dead, lift nothing living out.*

Any miracle it brought would break that.

It would consider the miracles, one by one, the way a hand considers stones before lifting any.

Light? It could open the dome like an eyelid. It could make the water clear and blue and visible to its roof. And the blind fish would fold into the walls and not come out, and the shrimp would vanish into cracks, and the mussel pavements would shut for a season, and the pale nations that have spent two thousand years perfecting a life that does not need seeing would be punished, all at once, for being seen. Their eyes are not a wound. They are a decision the flesh made honestly, in the dark, and kept.

Sight restored would be a cruelty dressed as a gift.

Rescue? It could lift the old ones in the deep cell — for there are old ones; it would know by the slow displacement against the partition wall, by the heavy whiskers that taste through the gap under the baffle and withdraw — lift them out into rivers, into oceans, into the wide water of the world below the clouds. But there is no wide water in their understanding. There is wall and wall and water between, and warmth, and the taste of stone and snail and each other. To be taken out of the Cistern would not be freedom for them. It would be the end of the only universe that has ever held their shape. The rule is not a superstition: *the living are never lifted out at all.* It is mercy, correctly identified.

Speech? It could make itself known. It could send a pressure through the water that meant, unmistakably, *you are not alone, you are loved, you have been noticed at last.* And the fish would turn away, and the sponges would shed their slow white snow, and for a generation of shrimp the niches would be places of fear. What would the ones in the deep cell do with such knowledge? Carry it, in the dark, while they grazed? There is no loneliness in them to be answered. They are not waiting. That is the hardest thing for a benevolent power to bear — that its attention is not needed as consolation.

Enlargement, then. A wider sea, a river tunneled through the mountain, new waters, new blood, green things growing. It could do it gently. It could do it over a century. But the Cistern is not a prison that failed to be a world. It is a reservoir that succeeded in becoming one, precisely by being closed, and warm, and slow, and fed on its own weather. To open it would not complete it. It would dissolve it into something else and call the dissolution kindness.

So the power would keep its hands, mostly, to itself.

What it would do, it would do from outside, and at the Cistern's own pace.

It would read the inscriptions — not restore them, not carve them clean again under the snails' mouths, for that smoothness is the snails' livelihood and their scripture too — but pass its attention over every shallow braid still legible under the travertine, and remember the makers' names where the water has forgotten them. Nothing inside needs those names back. But someone should hold them, so that the forgetting inside can stay innocent. It would become the memory the place has cheerfully surrendered, without asking the place to carry it again.

It would attend to the slow emergencies that even this slow world cannot answer. A pipe mouth sealing, a finger's width a season at a time, toward a century when the seep would fail. A roof stone loosening in frost, fifty years from falling. A crack widening that would one day drop a hand's breadth of level in a single summer and strand the vault colonies dry. These it would mend — from above, from outside the water, with stone and wood and no ringing tools, a fraction each year, so that the silt never rises and the water never learns alarm. Not to make the Cistern eternal. Eternity would be another prison. Only to let it live out the full length of the life it is already living, unhurried even in its ending, whenever its ending honestly comes.

And it would guard the unglamorous conditions of care: that there continue to be keepers, and grain for the tray, and a path up the scree that does not slide away; that no one below the clouds ever finds a use for this strange warm sea that involves emptying it. Not with wonders. With weather that turns surveyors aside. With a tradition kept quietly fed in a village that believes the twice-yearly climb is simply what is done, and has forgotten, as the snails have forgotten, exactly why. The power would be content to be forgotten in the same way.

Only once — perhaps once in a hundred years — would it go under the lintel itself.

It would go as a keeper goes, breath held, moving at the speed of settling silt, and it would stop well short of the deep cell. It would make itself still in the middle water until the fish resumed around it and the shrimp thickened again in the niches and it had been reclassified, by everything living there, as furniture. Then it would lay one palm flat against the partition wall, and wait, and feel, very faintly, a great slow turning on the other side: something that has been growing past the point where growth looks like change, tasting the changed pressure of the water, considering it, for as long as a crab considers a grain of silt.

It would not tap a signal. The keepers' taps mean *halt*, *rise*, *turn back*. It had no instruction to give.

But it would stay until, through the gap under the baffle, the heavy whiskers came once — tasting this new, motionless thing that was neither food nor keeper nor threat — and withdrew again, unhurried, unafraid.

That would be enough. That would be the whole encounter: to have been noticed in return, in the only language the place has ever trusted, and found harmless.

Then it would rise, slowly, so as not to cloud anything, and go back out into the thin air and the sideways rain, and leave the door exactly as closed as it found it.

Inside, the nations would resume. The fish would graze the ceiling as if it were another floor. The mussels would continue their afternoon-long decisions. The snails would go on writing and erasing their polished meanders over the faces of the makers. In the deep cell, the old ones would turn, and their young would issue into the middle water, and nothing would be required to be different because a god had passed by.

Outside, on the bare summit, the drum would breathe its faint warm steam into the wind, giving nothing away.

It would be watched over now. It would never know it. Those two things, held together without contradiction, are what its wisdom would look like.
