It would not come down the stair with rain.

That is the first temptation, and a wise power would feel it fully — standing on the rim in the heat, looking into that pocket of green and noise and breath, seeing cracked lips, jars measured by notches, children wiping a spill up with dust and squeezing the dust over a plant so that not even carelessness is wasted. The hand wants to open. The sky wants to break. A river could be made to turn aside up on the plateau. A spring could be struck out of the rock under the seep, clear and permanent and singing.

It would ruin them in a season.

Rain in abundance would flood the bowl that has learned to live by drops. It would wash the dung from the garden beds and carry it, foul and precious, away through the alleys. It would rot the spliced beams, soften the mud-brick, split the sealed cisterns with silt, drown the low stables where the sheep sleep to warm the floors above. Worse — slower, and worse — it would unmake the rights by which they live. Who takes water before the heat. Who after. Whose jar, whose notch, whose day. Those tiny, ancient arrangements are not decorations on top of survival. They *are* survival, made into manners. Overflow would not free these people from negotiation. It would leave them, suddenly, with nothing practiced to negotiate *by*.

A benevolent power that did not understand this would call its flood a gift.

A wise one closes its hand.

---

It stays on the rim a long time first.

Because from above, as everyone learns, there is nothing there. And the power has to learn what the bowl's own people already know: that you cannot see this place truly until you are in its way. You have to turn sideways on the stair for the donkey. Duck for the lintel. Step back for the goats and know them by their bells. Speak low, not from secrecy but from courtesy, because the walls carry everything.

The power learns that what lives here is not, in the end, a population. It is a being.

Not a metaphor for one. A being, with a metabolism.

Water enters it drop by drop and is never lost, only changed — from roof to cistern to mouth to jar to garden to fig to goat to milk to child. Heat enters it at noon and is stored in stone and given back at night into sleeping bodies. Dung, ash, feathers, bone, fallen fruit, prunings, sweepings: nothing crosses its boundary if it can possibly be turned and used again. Even memory circulates. The elders by the cistern hold the rights the way the cisterns hold the rain, and release them, argument by argument, notch by notch, into the young.

It is crowded, dense, loud at feeding time, soft-spoken at all others, green against all reason, and it has made itself out of almost nothing by refusing — generation after generation, handful after handful — to let anything be *only* waste, *only* noise, *only* old, *only* animal, *only* shade falling for an hour on someone else's beans.

You do not improve such a being by enlarging it. You do not save it by easing the very pressure that taught it how to be itself.

So the power does mostly what the bowl's own inhabitants do for one another at night, crossing a roof that is someone else's ceiling in the dark: it moves carefully, it touches only what it must, it greets without lighting a lamp.

It does the things the loop cannot do for itself.

The loop cannot mend the rock it is sunk into. So, far below the foundations, where a slow slip in the stone would one day bring the western terraces down into the threshing floor, the power sets its patience against the fault — not healing it all at once, which would be an earthquake, but persuading it, grain by grain, to settle and lock. No one above ever knows. A wall that should have cracked that winter does not. The man repairing it chalks it up to his grandfather's good mud, and he is, in the way that matters, right.

The loop cannot reach beyond its own walls for what isolation slowly starves. So once in a generation — no oftener — a bird blown off course from a valley no one in the bowl has ever seen drops exhausted into the great fig, and shakes from its feathers a seed that is not quite like any seed they have. A new toughness in the beans. A goat kid, years later, born unexpectedly hardy from a ram that wandered in off the plateau and was gone by morning. Pollen on a wind that should not, by rights, have descended. Small infusions. Nothing that will take. Nothing that will make them dependent on a next time. Just enough difference for life to keep arguing with itself.

The loop cannot hold back the kinds of death that take too much at once. A sickness that would carry off half the children in a week is turned — not abolished; children here will still die, and be grieved by name, and their names remembered by the stone near the cistern — but blunted, staggered, made to arrive slowly enough that mothers can nurse, that the old can sit up through the night in shifts, that knowledge of broths and cooling cloths and which herb for which fever can pass from hand to hand faster than the fever itself. A wise power does not make a people immortal inside a finite bowl. It makes sure they are not ended by accident.

And it guards the one gift the plateau has always given them, which no one below would ever think to pray for, because they have never had to:

Their invisibility.

A surveyor's route bends, almost imperceptibly, a valley too far east. A war-band's scout, following the faint impossible murmur of doves out of bare rock, feels suddenly and convincingly certain that the sound was wind, and thirst, and tricks of heat, and turns back having seen nothing worth reporting up here. A merchant below hears of a hidden green place and finds the story, by the time it reaches him, attached to a different plateau entirely. The power does not build a wall. It does something much more in keeping with the place: it practices privacy as a discipline, the way the inhabitants pretend not to hear a whispered argument. It looks away, hugely, on their behalf, so that the world continues to look away too.

It gives them no sign.

This is the hardest part, and the most necessary. A miracle they could point to would enter the loop like a stone into the seep. They would have to make a right out of it. A day for it. A family to tend it. A notch on a jar. Gratitude would become another mouth to feed, and then a priesthood, and then a dispute — *whose grandmother the wonder first appeared to, whose roof it rightly belongs above* — and the close, practical, unsentimental tenderness with which they treat figs and bees and grandmothers and dung would bend, just slightly, toward something that expects to be rescued.

The power refuses to be worshipped by people who have something better to do.

Instead, if it acts at all within the bowl, it acts below the threshold of accounting. On the worst morning of the worst drought year — the morning when the seep has given nothing for two days and the jars are being carried up nearly empty and no one, even the children, is wasting breath on complaint — one of the dew slabs up on the rim yields a few cupfuls more than physics quite allows. Not a jarful. Never a jarful. Just enough that the day's notches can be honored exactly as agreed, that no elder has to stand up by the cistern and decide, in front of everyone, whose child drinks less. Enough to preserve not life, exactly — they would have preserved life by harsher means — but the *agreement* by which life here stays human.

No one announces it. The woman who finds it says the night must have been colder than it felt. She is believed, because she is known to be accurate about such things. The water goes where water goes. By evening it is in the beans.

That is all. That, and timing.

For the power learns, watching, that in a place where memory is storage, the cruelest losses are not deaths but unfinished transfers. So an old woman who alone remembers why the water order was changed for three days in the drought of her childhood, and changed back, and what promise was made then to the family on the fourth terrace — she does not die in the night, as her body was preparing to do. She dies six evenings later, in the late shade under the great fig, in her proper place, having argued the whole thing out twice more with two old men and a listening granddaughter who did not know she was being handed a cistern. Nothing in her span is lengthened beyond nature. It is only arranged, gently, so that nothing entrusted to her is spilled.

Waste, here, is the only real sin. The power resolves not to commit it.

In the end, perhaps, it does one thing for itself, because benevolence that never receives becomes another kind of flood. It takes the lesson. It carries away — no water, no seed, no soul, nothing the loop will miss — the knowledge that abundance is not the opposite of scarcity. *Attention* is. That a world can be made not by having more but by noticing, more and more finely, what is already passing through your hands: heat off a wall, droppings on a ledge, a tendril of vine, a grandmother's recollection, a spill in the dust. That beings flourish not when every need is abolished, but when every presence is near every other and all of it, endlessly, minutely, is negotiated in good faith.

Then it leaves the rim as empty as it found it.

From above, there is nothing there. The wind goes on combing the grit into ripples. The thorn bushes hold their separate circles. A traveler might pass within a stone's throw at dusk and hear, perhaps, bells and doves rising impossibly out of bare rock, and shake his head, and walk on into the cold star-crowded dark.

Below, the bowl breathes out its stored day. The ovens cool. The birds settle in the figs in their vast rustling canopy. Someone crosses a roof by touch, greeting two other wakeful people by name, though no lamp is lit.

And the being who lives there goes on making itself — one notch of water, one basket of droppings, one spliced beam, one remembered right at a time — unwatched, unrescued, and, in the only way that would not destroy it, beloved.
