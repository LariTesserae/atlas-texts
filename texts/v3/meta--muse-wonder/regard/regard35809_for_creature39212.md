If such a power came, it would come first to the rim.

Not into the cave. To the rim, at dusk, where the pale grass bends and the ground breaks white and suddenly stops. It would stand where the human camps are made and unmade by wind, and listen to the column of bats rising through the sinkhole — a column so dense that for a while it has a shadow of its own — and it would understand, before it descended, that what had drawn its attention was not a place containing a life.

It was a life containing a place.

The stone is the least of it. The stone is young, sharp, cold at eleven degrees, hollowed beyond any useful measuring. The life is the thing itself, turned inside out: a body whose heart hangs from a ceiling no eye can find, whose blood goes out every night on millions of wings to feed over grassland and river and distant fruiting valleys, and returns before morning to be digested, dropped, rotted, eaten, hunted, dissolved, and eaten again in the dark. Beetle out of dung. Cricket out of beetle. Rat out of the fall. Snake out of the rat. Fungus out of whatever mercy left unclaimed. The cave does not live in the cave. The plains live in the cave, second-hand, through the bats.

A lesser benevolence would get to work at once.

That is how you know the wise one by what it does not do.

It would not bring light. Light is the obvious gift and the cruel one. Give the deep vaults lamps, or widen a sinkhole, and in a generation the mats shift, the swiftlets abandon the ledges between the grey country and the black, the pale hunters lose the darkness that is their only advantage, and green things creep a few triumphant metres farther in and then die in a place that cannot sustain them, rotting where nothing has prepared for leaves. The twilight is not a deprivation here. It is a border, exactly placed.

It would not bring water. The pools are shallow, lens-clear, cold enough to ache a hand, and they have lasted because no flood has ever come to scour them and no river has ever come to fill them with silt and surface fish and the eggs of things that would eat the blind shrimp in a season. A benevolent flood is still a flood. The drips, falling a quarter of a mile apart, each pool with its own note by which even humans learn to navigate, are enough because the inhabitants have spent their whole evolution becoming the kind of creatures for whom they are enough.

It would not feed the bats in a bad year, nor thin the rats, nor spare the fallen pups, nor drive the snakes from the pool edges out of tenderness for the frogs. Tenderness, applied to one link, becomes a weapon against the next. The pup that falls still warm is not an accident in the system. It is a payment. The rat that takes it is not a villain. It is a payment forwarded. Take away the falls and the hunters starve; take away the hunters and the hills seethe past bearing; take away the seething and the whole warm economy under the roost, the only warmth in the cave that does not come from a body or a flame, cools and stalls and leaves the bats hanging over their own unrotted waste.

It would not make it comfortable. It would not round the sharp fallen slabs, nor sweeten the ammonia, nor quiet the roar so that frailer things might converse beneath the roost. The roar is not noise around the life. The roar is the life, announcing, at a volume that enters the chest, that the ceiling is loaded and the shifts are changing and the countryside above is being faithfully converted into the countryside below. Silence here, as the people have already learned, is never safety. It is only a mat asleep.

So the power would do the hardest thing in its repertoire, the thing that separates wisdom from mere kindness with strong arms.

It would guard the edges, and leave the centre alone.

Its work would all be done outside and above, where the cave is actually vulnerable, because the cave's weakness is not in its dark. Its weakness is that its heart feeds elsewhere.

It would watch the cold grassland where the moths rise. If fire came too often, or poison fell from some future human hand to make the fields above more fruitful, or the rivers where the midges breed were stopped and soured, or the fruit valleys a night's flight away were cut down tree by tree — there, and only there, it would move. Quietly. A rain that comes a week early. A blight that inexplicably fails. A chief, or a farmer, or a child gathering fallen young bats with a cloth over her mouth, who wakes one morning with the unshakeable conviction that this stretch of riverbank is not to be touched, and cannot afterwards say why. The cave gathers its living from a hundred kilometres of ordinary country. Protect the cave and you must protect a hundred kilometres of country that has never seen it, from harms that will never know what they nearly starved.

It would watch the sinkholes. Not widen them. Not close them. Keep them open and keep them few. If some later age arrived with metal that did not need to be kept wrapped, with lights that did not smoke, with a hunger for dung deeper than wooden spades and baskets hauled up one by one could satisfy, with the wish to stand under the roost not for an hour but for a spectacle — the power would be at the smallest sinkhole, where the ropes of twisted fibre go down. It would not appear in fire. It would not need to. A rope that rots a little faster than expected. A path on the plain that never quite becomes a road. A story, entering the mouths of the people who already know to keep theirs closed under the roost, that this place takes and does not give back, that no one who tried to stay ever stayed. The people who come now are not the danger. They have learned the laws without writing them: drink only what drips clean from a stalactite point, walk on stone where you can, build nothing, leave before the torches fail. They are edge-hunters like the rat and the snake, and the cave can afford them exactly as it affords the rat and the snake — as long as they skim, and go.

It would watch, over centuries, for the things no inhabitant can watch for itself: the slow crack travelling through a ceiling span, the disease arriving on a bat blown in from a colony impossibly far away, the new predator learning the sinkhole mouths. And even then it would move the way the cave moves, by fractions. Nudge a settling block so that when it falls, in fifty years, it falls in the grey country between roosts and not through the densest mat. Turn one sick traveller aside at the rim, gently, the way wind turns. Nothing that would teach the colony to depend. Nothing that would leave the survivors weaker for having been spared. Benevolence that repeats itself becomes a crutch, and a crutch, in a place where everything pays its own way in the dark, is only a slower kind of killing.

And for the being itself — the whole loud, damp, crowded equilibrium, bats and dung and blind fish and fungal thread taken as one breathing thing — the power would offer the one gift that costs it the most and the recipient notices least.

Witness, without appetite.

It would descend once, perhaps. Not in glory; glory would startle ten thousand bats into a waterfall going upward and cost the night's feeding. It would come small and quiet, as the people come, with its equivalent of a cloth bound over its mouth, and stand on stone at the edge of a guano hill, and let the sound get into its chest, and let its skin cool to eleven degrees, and breathe the powdered wing and dung of the place, and look at a pool no larger than a washbasin reflecting a coin of distant daylight. It would not bless the water. It would not leave a mark the next fall of droppings would not honourably erase. It would learn the drip-notes, pool by pool. It would stay long enough to stop thinking of it as a cave with animals in it, because until you have done that you cannot be trusted with it.

Then it would climb, as everyone climbs, toward the one sound that is not the cave's — wind moving in grass it could not yet see — with its hearing ringing and the coolth going out of it slowly.

Behind it the entrance would swallow its light again. The frogs would go on calling in the permanent evening. The next shift would already be moving through the sinkhole mouth. The hills of guano would rise their fraction for the year under the same cold ceiling, and a bat would be born into the same roar its grandmother heard, at the same volume, and never know that anything had ever attended to it at all.

That would be the proof that the attention had been wise, and that the love in it had been real.

Anyone can improve a place. It takes a power to find one of the rare, finished things of the world — young stone, old equilibrium, a life that renews itself nightly out of elsewhere and asks for nothing — and to understand that the whole of its mercy consists in this:

the grass unpoisoned, the rivers running, the sinkholes open, the story told rightly, the centre untouched.

Nothing built. Nothing spared. Nothing silenced.

Only the long, unrecorded guardianship of the edges, so that the dark can go on, ungrateful and unbothered and entirely itself, being loud forever.
