It would not rescue them.

That would be the first sign of its wisdom, and the hardest.

From the rim, a lesser benevolence would see everything at once and want to act at once: the unfinished reservoir to finish or fill, the sumps to drain, the mosquitoes to poison, the rats and cockroaches and pigeons to clear, the three hundred pale people to lift up into wind and clean sunlight and houses that do not bloom with salt.

It would look like mercy from above. From below, it would be an extinction.

Because the being that has drawn its attention is not the town, exactly. Nor the people, though they are part of it. Nor any one of the species that have found their way down the spiral ramp and stayed.

The being is the loop.

Pumps making sumps making frogs making mosquitoes making swifts. Power hall making heat making warm places behind panels making cockroaches making geckos. Food waste making rats making cats, who are officially discouraged and unofficially fed. Raw limestone weeping into walls, walls rising on plinths, double doors shutting — badly, if a child is involved — against a life that is not invading the town so much as completing it.

Once something fell in and prospered, it became food or shelter or shade for something else. That is the being: hot, damp, noisy, gradual, densely inhabited, trapped in a hole it did not dig and unable to survive anywhere else.

A wise power does not heal such a being by taking it apart into the parts it approves of.

So it comes down the ramp, if it comes at all, the way everything else has come: slowly, learning the courtesies as it goes.

Shake out your shoes. Cover the cup the second it is poured. Sweep the threshold at night. Tuck the net in properly. Do not mistake what you stop hearing after a month for silence.

Then it does almost nothing, for a very long time.

It listens to which pump is knocking.

That, here, is a form of prayer.

Its interventions, when they come, would be nearly impossible to distinguish from the town's own slow processes of accumulation and wearing-through. The reservoir is postponed again. Not cancelled — cancellation would bring surveyors, decisions, a final use for the land. Not completed — completion would drown the lowest street and end the argument. Just postponed, year after year, by lost paperwork and changed budgets and a plant that remains necessary but never quite important enough to expand.

A boundary is kept on the plain. Not a wall. Walls invite climbing. Just an absence of reasons to come: no new road that makes the rim convenient, no development that finds the edge valuable, no official report that describes the pit in language exciting enough to attract cleaners, saviors, investors, or exterminators.

Above, the plain stays empty and silent and blinding. That emptiness is the roof of the place.

Below, small things fail to go wrong at the last moment.

The knocking pump does not seize during the ninety murderous minutes at noon. The fissure the bats prefer does not quite silt shut before they have shifted, in their own time, to the next. A crack that would have joined the sumps into a lake and taken the lowest street opens instead as a seep, a centimetre at a time, manageable by night pumping. The oleander by the clinic goes on being poisonous and unkillable, and flowers.

Where it acts more visibly, it acts in keeping with the terms already established by the oleander: tolerate the damp, tolerate the heat, take the light when it falls straight down, wait through the shadow.

It would not bring wind by blasting the pit open. It might coax a little of the plain's wind down the spiral ramp at dawn — a slow, turning breath that reaches the bottom just before the coolest hour ends, stirs the columns of mosquitoes without drying the reeds, and then gives up. Not weather as rescue. Weather as relief.

It would not poison the sumps. It might deepen one channel here, widen a reed bed there, so dragonflies have more patrol and frogs have more throat-deep water and the mosquitoes meet more of what already eats them. It would strengthen what is already doing the work.

For the people — because benevolence that ignores people in favor of an ecosystem is only another kind of cruelty from above — it would not offer escape as the only form of care.

Those who want the rim would find the way open. A scholarship that happens to arrive. A job elsewhere that happens to suit. A road out that is not a one-way mercy.

Those who stay would get the things that make staying less costly, without making the pit uninhabitable for everything else: finer mesh that keeps mosquitoes out and still lets trapped dragonflies be released; better seals on doors rather than poison behind panels; cool, pale roofing; a clinic that has what it needs for fever, infection, asthma, infected bites; contraception and care for the cats, so the unofficial landlords live longer and the rats remain, as everyone says, organized rather than worse. Care for the slow dogs in the clinic exhaust. Books for the school. A pump part arriving a week before it is needed, as if someone had finally understood the supply list.

No miracles that require gratitude. No monument. Nothing that would make the town dependent on being watched.

Perhaps, in the wide gravel lots where another block was meant to go, a few more things would manage to root. Not imported green lawns, not fragile tomatoes under shadecloth eaten to stems by morning. Pit-native attempts: deep-rooted, salt-tolerant, shade-giving things planted in broken concrete where the seep runs close, protected while young by the same scaffolding that has already become architecture. Years would pass before they mattered. The power is patient enough for shade to be an act of faith.

And it would protect the children from being mistaken for the future of only the human part. They already know which stairwell has wasps. They already know what a knock in a pump means. It would make sure growing up fluent in a more-than-human town does not cost them their lungs, their blood, their choices. Their knowledge would be treated not as deprivation, but as a language.

At seven in the evening, when the limestone gives back its day and the streets are hottest and everyone comes out anyway, the power would be indistinguishable from anyone else who has lived there long enough: salt faint at the seams, moving slowly, knowing not to leave a cup uncovered.

It might sit where it can hear the pumps change pitch. It might watch the bats pour out in thin black smoke and the swifts come home screaming. Below, in water a centimetre higher than yesterday, the frogs would begin again, not as a chorus but as a pressure, a single grinding organism made of ten thousand gullets.

The power would not ask to be hosted. In this place, it is hard to tell who is hosting whom.

It would only make a quiet, continuous decision, renewed each night when the yellow work lights come on early and the moths soften every beam:

Let this unfinished thing remain unfinished.

Let what has learned to live in the trap go on living.

Let the people who keep it from drowning be kept, in turn, by the life they did not intend to shelter — and let no one from the bright, empty plain come down with clean hands to save them from each other.
