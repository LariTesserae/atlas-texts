I come to it the way everything vulnerable does — from above, and too exposed.

First there is just the rim in my peripheral vision, that dark line far over my head, weeping. I keep looking up at it while I move. I don't know why, yet. Everything else is below the wind. The air down here is cold enough to sit in my lungs even in summer, saturated, smelling of iron and green sedge crushed wet, and underneath that a sweet dark smell, vanilla and leather, that means *held, held, held*, all along the canals.

I'm low. That is the first true thing about my body. The world does not pass at eye-level, it passes at water-level — sedge roots, the plastered upstream face of a dam, the undersides of lily pads going pale overhead. When I rise, only my nose truly breaks the surface. Then the top of my head. My eyes are set high and small; I can see the terrace open ahead, mirror after mirror into fog, while the rest of me remains a shadow underneath. I like being misread as a log. I like the widening V behind me being the largest announcement I make.

If I haul out — and I do, because I have to — the water sheets off me all at once. I'm heavier than I look on land, barrel-bodied, clumsy in a way that makes my own skin prickle. My front feet are almost hands, small, clawed, clever with a stick. My back feet are absurd paddles that fold and drag. My tail drags too, broad and cold and fat, propping me when I sit up to work. On the dam crest I waddle. I am built to be slightly ashamed up here.

In the water I'm not.

Under, everything rights itself. My nostrils close without thinking. My ears close. A clear lid slides over my eyes and suddenly I can see the branch I'm gnawing through the silt-cloud of my own making — and my lips have already met behind my teeth, so the wood can fill my mouth and the water stays out of my throat. I can cut a willow entirely submerged and not drown in splinters. My teeth ache in a constant, useful way. Orange, iron-hard, growing whether I want them to or not. If I stopped, they would kill me. So I don't stop. I turn a wand in my forepaws and gnaw, gnaw, and the coppice beyond the pond grows denser for being cut, and there is always another wand.

I know this canal the way you know your own sleeping shape. Not by sight — half the year it will be black under ice — but by touch, by the pressure against my whiskers, by depth under my belly. It is deep enough here because a yearling — me, last summer, me again this morning — pushed mud ahead of his chest along the bottom, again and again, until it stayed. Further on it shallows where my younger siblings have been practicing and abandoning their pointed sticks. Further on still, it bends, not straight now, deliberately toward the willow stand my mother prefers. I follow the bend with a branch longer than my body clamped in that gap behind my incisors, towing it so it rides above my back and rustles faintly against the sedge.

I am not the pair at the center of this. I know their smell through the lodge wall better than my own. They are mated into the grain of this place; the chamber where I was born is above the waterline behind me, lined with shredded wood, breathing warm through its vent hole into the cold. I can hear kits in there sometimes — this spring's, not mine — whining thin and almost human-like through packed earth, and my mother retrieving them from the shared roof with a gentle cuff when they tumble too far. Our roof is not only ours. In the center here, lodge has grown into lodge, roof into roof, an undulating country of stick and mud acres wide, and half the valley's children fall asleep in piles on it at dusk, retrieved by whichever adult surfaces first.

I am the year before last. Or the year before that, it is hard to count when winter locks us together. I am as large now as my father when I rear up against my tail, and still tolerated — still helping dredge, still towing to the food raft, still allowed the deep entrance that keeps mink from my sleeping platform. And restless in a way that has begun to change how I move through home. I patrol too far at evening. I go to the ends of the newer districts where each lodge stands alone in its own ringed pond and the scent-mounds are fresher and sharper. I nose at borders that are not mine. I have started looking up.

That's why the rim stays in my eye.

Come thaw next year, or maybe even at the end of this one if the light goes strange and the urge overtakes sense, I will leave the way nothing in this valley wants to travel: overland, uphill, through the raw gullies where no canal has been dug to protect me, tail dragging in loose till, head up every two steps to sniff for fox, for eagle-shadow. I know it because the two-year-olds before me went, downstream some of them to fight for unheld water, some of them up and over into the wind-scoured plain where there is nothing — no pond, no dam, no persuaded stillness — until an animal like me makes it from nothing. Mouthful by mouthful. A first stick. A first bright thread of leak stopped in the night.

Until then I am still of the maintenance. That is the law I know in my feet.

Late today — if it is day; the light stays pale for hours and the fog erases the edges — a leak has started in the lower dam, a vibration I feel before I see it, running water speaking through wood and mud into the soles of my hind feet: *here*. I sink toward it and my parents are already there, or my older sister is, converging without a call. We do not hurry. Hurry makes silt clouds and bad packing. I dive and come up with mud clutched against my chest, chin tucked hard to hold it, foreclaws digging it in where the thread is escaping. Pack. Plaster smooth with nose and paws. Test. Listen again with my whole body pressed to the dam face. Pack again, a stone this time, carried in my teeth and wedged just so, because an inch is not a small thing here. An inch up drowns the nursery. An inch down bares the entrance to otter. We live in the tolerance between and we keep it, nightly.

When it's held, we separate without ceremony. A pair crosses the upper pond, one towing willow. Somewhere a kit tries its teeth and fails and tries again. The heron has not moved from the spillway. The ducks are settling into our thatch, safe up there because of the deep water under us. Under the surface, the cache from last autumn still waits black in the mud by the lodge — a sunken forest of branches, cold-kept, because a family that misjudges winter starves surrounded by trees it cannot reach.

I slip back down my entrance and up, dripping, into the dry dark. It is warm with bodies in here. It smells of shredded alder and milk and castor and wet fur drying dense. Behind a wall, a kit sighs. Above us the roof goes on and on, joined to every other roof, and above that the fog, and above that the rim — that sharp, young edge between the world we have made stay, and the empty one I will have to cross to make another.

For now I curl with my tail around my feet to keep the fat of it warm, and I listen to water not moving, level after level, all through the valley we are holding still.
