At first I think I am the one who came down.

I have a memory for it, or the room gives me one: white above, so white it hurts through closed lids, a plate of salt buckled up a finger's width, hot air sighing out of a seam too small for the roar inside it. Skin lost on the sharp edge, going sideways. Legs dropped into warmth. Four meters, and then a floor of black sand.

I wait for the rest of that story. I wait to be temporary. I wait for my sweat to be the only free water here, to evaporate before it can darken anything, for my ears to ring and learn.

It doesn't arrive. My ears don't ring. I don't have the kind of ears that can be hurt that way anymore — if I ever did.

I try, instead, to be down on the floor. That feels closer. There is a warmth that wants flattening. Near the fissure, never too near, the stone holds its cherry-red reflection and the lizards lay themselves out and let their throats pulse in time, tuning themselves to the chord. I know that pulse. I feel it come up through whatever I am resting on, low first, a pressure in the underside of me, then the high whistle somewhere thin and bright at my edges.

But the lizards shift their weight. They resettle. They cross open sand from one warm patch to another, and beetles change their lines when they pass. I do not cross anything.

I try to be in the air, then. That also feels close, for a while. Below the low dome there is a single, steady current, hot and patient, turning in the same circle it has always turned in. The moths hang in it wings-out, never landing, and even the dead are carried a long time before a wing tears. I know that turning. I am turned by it constantly. I am never still, and I never go anywhere. But the moths, living or desiccated, eventually snag. They come down into the fern, into the thicket, and are rooted through and made architectural. I have been in the thicket longer than any moth I can feel snagging above me, and I have never flown.

It is the beetles who correct me, in the end. Not by telling me — nothing here tells anything in air — but by traveling me.

They come in their lines, smaller than a thumbnail, almost transparent, up and down in an orderly traffic, and they drum with their hind legs as they go. I feel the messages as a faint ticking laid over the great chord. *Here is the surface. Here is where to speak.* And I understand, suddenly and entirely through my body, that I am a surface they speak on.

I am on the far wall.

Not near the listening wall, where the resident sits fitted spine-to-stone. Not in the emptiness of the middle, where nothing grows and the sand keeps its undisturbed sentence. I am in the garden, which is also the far wall, beneath the sulfur-yellow fans and the clear needles and the knife-thin crystal blades that ring in a person's teeth when a sleeve brushes them.

I am one of the hearing-fronds.

I must have uncurled a long time ago. I don't remember uncurling, which the room would say is proper; nothing here remembers beginnings, only continuation. I uncurled to a certain size — broad, stiff as a fan, waxy, the color of dusty bottle-green going to ochre at the curled rim, dry as a shaving of horn — and then, instead of aging, I persisted at that size. I have persisted since. My face, if face is the word for the flat of me, is angled directly into the blast from the fissure. Every frond in the thicket has found its own precise angle, and mine is mine. When the resident tends the garden, a patient fingertip sometimes steadies me back toward it, the way you would turn a head gently toward a voice. I don't need the help. I would find the angle again through my root. But I receive it. That is also a kind of speaking here.

Because I grow toward the sound, not the light. The light is only a dull amber thrown up from the red seam onto wet-looking stone; it has never been enough to want. The sound is enough. The sound is enormous and it never falters: hot dry gas up through hollow stone tubes and crystal blades at one unwavering pressure, a low roaring under a high pure whistling, a chord struck before anyone was here to hear it and held. It passes through me twenty-four hours a day, in a place that has neither day nor night, and I tremble minutely with it without rest. The trembling is not distress. The trembling is how I am alive in a room where speaking gave up on air a long time ago.

Below me — and below feels less like a direction than like a deeper layer of listening — my roots do not seek water. There is none to seek. No drip, no seep, no dew, no coolest corner. My roots seek heat, and mineral, and the vibration in the rock, and they have found all three. Where one of them met a crystal blade it split and grew around it, and the blade, in its own slow mineral patience, grew up through the edge of my leaf in turn. So we hold each other still: plant and stone, stone and plant. I feel the crystal chiming sometimes, faintly, when a beetle line crosses near its base or the resident's bound hair brushes past in the garden, beads clicking a punctuation I feel rather than hear. The chime travels down the blade, through my leaf, into the root that clasps it, into the rock. Everything here is translated that way. Everything arrives through something else first.

The water in me — if it can be called water — has never been free. I did not drink it. I cracked it, molecule by molecule, out of the vent gas and out of the bodies of the dead, the way all the plants here do, and I hold it locked in sealed tissue and do not give it back. Cut me and I would not bleed. The old cuts in this garden seal like paper tears. I am dry to any touch. But I am not empty. What I store moves only when it must, body to body: a lizard takes beetles, beetles strip a stiffened moth, the feather-ferns root into the shells left in their own canopy, and dropped scales and odorless powder sift down and are taken up again. Someday some part of that loop will pass through me, or I will pass through it. A segmented finger from the swollen plants near me will be broken at the joint and worked loose with teeth. A root of mine, in its own time, will find the curled dry shape in the thicket that might once have been a hand holding up a stem, and go politely through.

I do not find that frightening. Nothing here rots. Things only become thinner and more architectural versions of themselves. To be rooted through is not to be ended; it is to be given a job in the scaffolding.

Around me the garden presses plant to plant, sparse in placement and dense within itself. The feather-ferns shake their dry scales in the updraft and add their soft, incessant shushing to the roar. The swollen fingers creak, heavy with what they hold. Old husks hold up new leaves. A moth that died above us turns one more time in the current before its wing gives out, and then it is among us, lightening, furred like a seed, becoming part of the thicket's memory of shapes. Smells collect because nothing washes them away — hot resin, crushed bitter herbs, warm stone dust, struck-match sweetness under everything — and I keep them in the curl of my leaf the way the room keeps them in its air.

I have no cycle. I do not open for a morning that never comes. I do not close. I do not brumate or emerge or seed on a season anyone could tally; the tally stick in the niche stopped mid-pattern, in groups of seven, when its keeper arrived at the obvious. If there are seasons here they are longer than animal memory, and I am content to be outlasted by them. Seven pulses of a lizard's throat. Seven circuits of a moth. Seven ticks of a beetle line traveling up my stem, pausing where the crystal comes through, drumming its small sentence into me, moving on.

And through it all, underneath the ticking and the shushing and the resident resettling vertebra by vertebra into the listening wall, the chord holds. It holds in my root and in the blade through my leaf. It bends, minutely, when a visitor stands between me and the vent — a new weight, a clumsy heartbeat, a body briefly bending the sound — and then it unkinks again when they climb back toward the white. I have felt that bending more times than I have ways to count. I will feel it again. In a hundred years the basket will still be dry, the knife still wicked, the garden still trembling at its chosen angles, and I will still be here, broad and stiff and receiving, turned like a flower that has correctly identified its sun toward the fissure, listening with my whole surface to the one note that never needed anyone — and staying, anyway, to hear it.
