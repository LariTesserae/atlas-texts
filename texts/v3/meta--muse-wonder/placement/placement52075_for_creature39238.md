I thought at first I would be one of the flamingos.

It is the obvious borrowing. Thousands of them, ankle-deep from pond to pond, heads down, heads sweeping, pink where the water is pink and paler where it isn't. Their color is not theirs — it comes up through the algae, through the shrimp, into the feather — and I liked that honesty. Take color from what you eat. Eat in the dark. Make no wasted sound in a place where sound will not carry anyway.

But I watched them longer, from the rim where the air is bruised blue at noon and still does not cool you, and the flamingos never really stop. At midnight the flock lifts in that soft rush you feel in your chest more than hear, settles, goes on feeding in the black. At dawn they are already feeding. They are a metronome stretched across the whole floor. Beautiful, and too tireless for me.

Then I thought: the worker, then. One of the people in the scattered salt-brick sheds, half-buried a kilometer apart, no town on the flat at all. Hooded, reflective, face powdered permanently white. Moving only on the causeways. Drinking before thirst, in that thin air where you can be in trouble while your mouth still feels wet. Learning never to trust a blue lake in late afternoon — only hot air bending sky down to salt — nor a shed that looks twenty minutes away and is three hours' walk.

But the workers arrive breathing too loud. I could hear myself in them. And they are already being encrusted the way the abandoned pump-houses are, windows filling with white growth, roofs staying intact while the floor salts them in. That will happen to me too, wherever I land here. It is not a choice of species. It is just the trajectory of staying.

The pale fox almost had me. She lives on the margin where salt gives way to stone, red halophyte no higher than a finger, tussock grass in yellow tufts that never touch. She has given up her own schedule entirely and learned the workers' shift changes by heart, and the order at the condensers on the rim: insects first when the moisture beads on the cold fins at evening, then birds, then her. That kind of patient, marginal attention felt familiar.

And then I got smaller. The way you have to here. Nothing lives spread out. Everything lives in pockets, and in the lines between pockets.

Under the crust, an arm down, the brine that burns a cut instantly. In the ponds — turquoise, cobalt, milky jade, that shocking arterial pink — the algae blooming in poison salt and needing almost no fresh water at all. The shrimp clouding the pinkest water so thick it moves against the wind. The flies rising off the warm edges in a black smudge, a million at a step, settling again without a sound. Once you see the base of it, everything else is just a larger way of waiting near it.

That's where I found my exact size.

I am the lizard the color of rust on salt, on a warm pipe joint.

Not beside the pond, exactly. Not in it. On the line between ponds, where the causeway runs and the pipe runs along the causeway and the joint swells a finger's width wider than the pipe — just enough for four feet and a belly. Industrial geometry following the salt hexagons so closely that from above you can't tell them apart, and me following both. My pocket is a seam of retained heat.

This is a good place to understand the rules in the body.

At the coldest hour before dawn I am down in a crack where the pipe meets packed salt, stiff, salt ticking faintly around me as the crust contracts. Condensation beads uphill of me first — on the metal, on my own back — and I come out not to hunt but to lick. Everyone here licks condensation from metal, feather, leaf, crust. I am simply closest to the source. A bead loosens, runs the joint, reaches my chin. That's the morning.

Then the pumps resume below the threshold thin air can carry, the first flies stir on the warm brine, the flamingos are already sweeping, and the sun comes white and physical over the rim. The workers emerge powdered as the ground. I climb up.

By mid-morning my joint is the temperature other animals organize their fleeing around, and for me it is just correct. I do not hunt the way the fox hunts, or sweep the way the flamingos sweep. I wait, tongue out now and then, tasting the air. The brine flies do the moving for both of us. A black smudge lifts off the pond edge at some footstep far away, drifts, resettles — and some of it resettles near warm metal. My tongue is quicker than my feet ever need to be. Everything here eats each other or eats what eats the bloom, in a chain so tight you can watch it turn over in an hour. My link in it is very short: fly, tongue, stillness.

At noon, when the heat is worst and the whole shallow oven on the roof of the world is at full pressure, the machines rest and the workers sleep in the thick-walled sheds where it is merely hot instead of dangerous. That's when the swifts pour out of the eroded cliff-bases into the thermals until the rising air looks feathered. They take the noon by going up into it. I take it by flattening into it. Belly to the joint, legs splayed, rust on rust on white salt. The lizards on every pipe joint across the floor doing the same, each in its own small territory of warmth, none of us calling. Evaporation makes no noise beside us. Crystals grow feather-white along the pond edge below, a centimeter in a hot week, silently.

I borrow color too, it turns out. Not pink — nothing down near the machinery is pink except the water and the birds who eat from it. Mine is the color of the pipe where it has weathered, of the red-brown margin stone, of salt with iron in it. If I moved to a cleaner joint I'd be wrongly dressed for weeks. So I don't move.

In the evening the ground gives back its stored day and the ponds stay warm to the touch, and I stay warm a little past the hour when the vicuña are picking delicately up on the margin and the hawk settles on the pump-house roof, the only high thing for kilometers. Below me the trucks crawl home raising plumes that hang and dissolve in silence. The queue forms again at the condensers: insects, birds, fox. I have already had mine off the pipe at dawn, which is how small things survive here — not by being first in the big queue, but by finding a smaller one nobody else thought to stand in.

At night I go down into the crack again while the stars crowd overhead, hard and close, packed as densely as hexagons below. Salt begins to cake in the creases of my feet, at the edge of my jaw. I could scrape it off against the pipe. I mostly don't. Nothing here rots and nothing washes away; things encrust, repair, get wicked over in new crystal by morning like tire tracks healing into pattern.

So find me there: not spread across the flat, not in the mirror-sheet of rain that lasts an hour after a storm, but on the warm joint between two ponds, in the line between pockets. Small, rust-colored, tongue tasting flies, awake at the hours that empty out for everyone else. Learning — like the shrimp, the swift, the vicuña, the powdered people in their sheds — to rest when resting is the work, to drink before thirst, to take my color from where I sit, and to let the place slowly, beautifully salt me in rather than trying to wash it off.
