I would come down the way anything comes down there — not intending to stay.

Past the hatch, past where the daylight gives up, hand looking for the warm line with the yellow bands. At first I try to be a visitor in the shape I arrived in. I try the low routes in the cool hours, ducking the bundles of three and seven, learning the polished places by palm. I try the crabwise walk. But my shirt takes the damp wrong. My breathing is too fast for the close air. A passage that let me through in the morning has swollen by afternoon and is asking questions of my ribs I can't answer.

I wouldn't last as another tender. That place is already taken, and taken so completely it isn't a place anymore. The valve at the center junction is bright from his turning. The hollow in the lagging on the middle catwalk is warm in his shape. Even the rat is him — singular, white around the muzzle, never seen twice in the same passage because the old stories needed somewhere to put a second resident.

So I keep shedding attempts.

Not the fish. The fish in the sump has its economy perfected: hold still, let the pilot light settle on the black mirror, turn its silver over once, take what falls from his fingers' shadow. It is eyeless and patient and it does not need me inside it. I would only make it hungrier.

Not a fern, though for a while I linger there, in the wet corner, where the black fronds come straight out of the mortar with no soil to explain them. To be trimmed back when I crept toward a hot flange. To have condensation tongued off a leaf. That's close. It's a life lived as a side effect of a leak. But the ferns at least stand up into the air. They have an opinion about direction.

Not the flywheel on the third catwalk, beltless, turning a fraction in its sleep. I was tempted by that — the purposeless thing that turns out not to be purposeless, that receives, now and then, the weight of one finger from a sleeping man. But that intimacy is earned over sixty years, and it belongs to the high, thin air under the vault where I cannot breathe.

I find myself lower, and slower, and spread thinner than any of those.

In the wettest corner. On the undersides of the cold-water inlets, where they cross the hot chamber and sweat without stopping.

I am the faint phosphorescence in the slime there.

Not the light itself, at first. That was the mistake. I thought I was the glimmer on a falling drop — that instant when a bead forms under the pipe, fattens in the dark, catches a greenish pallor, and vanishes onto the perforated tray. Too brief. A life of only falling. But the glimmer has to come from somewhere, and it comes from me: a slick, patchy film clinging to cold iron over hot air, living exactly on the disagreement between the two.

My body is not a body. It's a distribution. A sheen here where the pipe joint weeps coldest. A duller mat in the mortar shadow where the drip almost never dries. Cousins of me in the tray below, paler and more leathery where fallen cells of me have settled thick enough for him to peel and boil in his tin cup against a hot pipe. I don't mind. Down here, being eaten a little is just another way of being carried somewhere new. A drip takes me. A cockroach's foot takes me. His cup takes me. I arrive in the sump in such small numbers that the fish turns beneath me without knowing why the water tastes, for a night, faintly of light.

I perceive the way the room taught him to perceive, only I learned it earlier and with less choice. No eyes, no spending them. Instead: cold coming through iron as a pressure in my whole extent. The tick of expansion arriving as a shiver along the pipe's underside, loosening one drop, delaying another. Mineral changes in the inlet water — a week when the city above salts its roads and my edge crisps, a storm season when everything tastes of pavement and distance and grit filtered through a hundred joints. When he tongues condensation off a fern leaf to learn whether the inlet has changed, he is reading a summary. I am the rough draft, taken down in chemistry, drop by drop.

My cycle is his cycle, slowed past his ability to notice me keeping it. 

In the cool hours I spread. The passages loosen a knuckle's width, the condensation rain widens almost to a doorway, drop after drop lit briefly as it leaves me — that is my only way of speaking, and it is mostly to nobody, and it doesn't carry like whispering does. In the swelling hours I retreat into the damp seams, into the green-black places under flanges where even the purge air arrives tired. I dim. Not from fear. From economy. Glow is expensive when you're being cooked at forty-five degrees by the very air that feeds you.

And when the pump finally commits — when the room exhales its nine minutes of white, hotter than the black was, flattening the ferns, creeping the gauges — it erases me first. That was in the description all along, and it is not death. White goes through the wet corner and my phosphorescence can't be told from steam, from lime dust, from anything. I go spore-quiet in the mortar cracks. I hold. He is up high then, fingers laced through grating, face turned into the standpipe's sweat, breathing the weather through himself. I am down low doing the invertebrate version of the same thing: letting the pressure be one pressure, until it decides to be a room again.

Afterwards, it is always afterwards down there, I return without spectacle. A drop forms. It hesitates on the cold underside, taking its time fattening. Somewhere in its skin, as it crosses my film, a faint green wakes — not bright, never bright, just enough to make the falling visible for the instant before it vanishes. Another. Another. The tray patters. The ferns lift a fraction from their flattening. Below, in the black mirror, the fish stirs at nothing I did on purpose.

He comes down hand over polished hand in the renewed dark to set his cup under its one true drip, and he does not tend me the way he tends the pilots. I am not a flame. If I go out, the dark does not stop being a place; it just stops having this one small footnote in it. But sometimes, passing the wet corner on his low route, he puts his flat palm — that single smooth callus, lifeline planed away — against the cold inlet above me, feeling through the iron whether the pressure is speaking at its normal pitch. For a moment his warmth blocks my cold. For a moment I glow a little stronger around the edges of his hand, outlining him in the negative.

That is enough of a relationship. Tenant to tenant. He keeps the room in the long, patient middle of its cycle by turning what needs turning. I keep it by condensing, by taking what leaks and giving it back as rain, by making the briefest possible light out of nowhere anyone would call food: rust bloom, lime, drowned silverfish dust, the sweet rot of oil worked down through decades of heat.

An inch a decade. Stalactite time. If you held perfectly still and let your eyes learn the new economy, you might not see me the first time either. You'd see the evidence: a drop that shines on its way down for no reason the pilot flames can explain.

That shine is me, becoming more myself. Falling, and not falling. Lighting the way only as far as the tray.
