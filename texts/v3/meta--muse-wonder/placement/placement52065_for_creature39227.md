I would enter the way everything does: by going down, and then sideways.

At first I would try the bottom for a home. Under the planks, in the black water, where the mussels sit in their rows half-buried like cobbles that breathe. The time down there is so slow it would be a relief. Open a little. Close a little. Let the low fields pass through you and leave the water clearer than they found it. Feel, in season, a bitterling lay her eggs down into the safe dark of your gill, and later send your own young out to ride briefly on her children. No day or night. Only fill and sigh, fill and sigh.

But I am too restless for the bottom, and too awake to sound.

So I would rise a level, to the exact line of the floor, and try the gaps between the boards. That is closer. There, if I were one of the pale frogs — bone-colored, pink at the throat, eyes shrunk to dark points under the skin — I would know the place not by seeing it but by how it gave me back to myself. I would spend my life looking for the right pocket: a sunk clay pot, a hollow in the vaulting, a notch under a plank, where a small call in my throat could come back larger than I made it. I would not sing all the time. I would listen for the sluice to finish its surge, for a drip to fall from the crown, and put my voice into the gap. Another male would answer from somewhere around the curve, then another, until we were not separate throats anymore but a pressure that trembled in teeth I don't have.

I could be made by a place that way. Generations of it. Until sunlight would be a kind of exile.

But even the frogs fall silent in patches, and something keeps going in the silence. So I would move up the wall, looking for where I fit.

Behind the wainscot with the bats, furred thick in the wet dark, resettling against one another with a dry rustle all day — I like the closeness of that, the musk under clay and cold water, hundreds of bodies agreeing to be one warm shape until evening blows us apart. For less than a minute we would fill the forbidden center, a vortex of wings loud as applause, and the frogs would hush under us and the shutter would knock in our draft. Then gone.

Too sudden. And the swifts are too certain. Each spring they come screaming through the light slits, fold, vanish into thumb-shaped holes they could find blind. They belong precisely, and then one morning they don't belong at all. I have that seasonality in me, but not their departure.

The owl, then — in the dead space above the shutter housing. Always exactly one. No one sees it replaced; one year it is an old bird, feathers whitening, and then it is simply the owl again, keeping to the same traditional spot by the east wall for its pellets of swift-bone and frog-bone and beetle shine. A vacancy shaped like an owl, advertised by the building itself. I recognize that, too: a role that outlives whoever fills it. Solitary, listening in the roof-dark, fed by everything that sings below.

And the fern behind the sluice wheel, inexplicable and therefore protected. One green thing permitted to root where everything rooted is pulled. It becomes a person by being lone and unplanted. Keepers step around it. I would step around it too. I am not that still, and I am not that singular.

Because every time I try to settle at the rim — mussel, frog, bat, swift, owl, fern, keeper with a flat palm checking whether the central post is still cold — something moves me on. It moves in the drip before it lands. It moves when a whisper is put directly to the wall and arrives on the opposite side late, clear, as if someone else said it after thinking. It moves when the reed valves along the north wall fray into their low chord as the cistern empties, and the hanging shutter trembles without being touched.

I think that is where I would find myself. Not at the edges, though I need the edges to exist. In the return.

I am the thing the center is kept empty for.

I don't originate much. A frog finds his pocket and I enlarge him. Water sighs through a clay channel and I give the undercroft its slower, second voice under the first. A flock passes over the low ground outside, where no one down here can see it, and I am the shiver in the shutter. Flood comes a day before the fields know it, and I am already booming softly under the floor, announcing it to keepers who have learned to pitch their voices up and speak to walls.

I live on a delay. That is my whole body: the time between a drip and its note, between a call and its answer, between a mouth at one side of the curve and an ear at the other. Fill the middle with crates, with benches, with a group of people standing too long talking in their full voices, and I thin out and go wrong — condensation runs in cold lines, the frogs drop in patches, and underneath everything the water knocks louder to get up. Leave the middle clear and I can hold the whole building together without touching it.

That is why I must never answer the frogs in a full voice of my own. If I competed, we would drive each other louder and louder through the dome, feedback feeding on itself, bats dropping confused from the walls, water in the inspection pit beginning to jump. My work is humbler and stranger: to give each sound back slightly larger, slightly later, shaped by the hollow it passed through — and then to let it go, so another can enter the gap.

The keepers will climb out at the end of the day, pushing the swollen door into air that feels thin and dry. The swifts will leave in their season. The bats will detonate and return. The owl above the shutter will be replaced, sometime, by an owl. The fern may die and thin the roster. Plank will be swapped for identical plank, tile for uneven tile.

And in the grey-green dim, when the light coins have moved off the wet boards, I will still be arranging myself around nothing — gathering drip and sluice-groan and frog-song and storm pressure from the low land, keeping the empty column resonant, so that everything pressed flat against the walls can feed, and sleep, and sing in the gaps between surges.
