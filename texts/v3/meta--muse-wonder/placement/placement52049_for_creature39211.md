I would start, I think, as I would have to — as a visitor who stayed past the point where staying is polite.

Not because I wanted the town. Sere Calm makes wanting feel loud. But because I couldn't yet do the one thing it requires of everything inside the lens: hang without correcting myself.

At first I'd be all through it, badly. Kicking to hold a place that was already holding me. Turning with my hands. Trying to look up for a surface and finding the skin instead, in every direction. My air coming in humiliating little pockets off my suit and being stripped away — that hiccup through the teeth. Through the clear plaza I'd look down too long at the obsidian spire and the cloud sea moving far underneath, real weather happening to other people, and feel that theological lurch: that I was not swimming, I was falling very slowly into the sky.

A resident, passing at the pace of a thought, would not reach for me. Welcome would be a response. She would simply wait, upright in the long blue-black interval between two towers, while I learned not to make the water answer.

And I would learn, because there isn't anything else to do with time there.

My pulse would stop sounding so frantic in my ears. Forty-three heartbeats would stop seeming like a medical emergency. The grey-blue suit would stop seeming issued and start seeming like the only possible color, in a place where red dies within ten meters and amber has become a kind of white. The motes would take me apart molecule by molecule, politely — oil, shed skin, whatever green and bacterial world I'd carried up from the peak station on my sleeves — until I had no scent to leave behind me. My eyes would stay open longer between blinks. The water would do it.

That was my first guess: that I'd be the forty-fourth, eventually. The one the council hadn't scheduled. Filed in one of the empty districts built for three hundred, keeping still professionally. Walking the filtration canals. Watching the desalinators not desalinate. Sitting in the rotunda while it reverted, patiently, to its saved version.

But I don't think a resident is quite what I'd become. Residents have a history in their throats — implants braided in, a microbiome in labeled vials down in the peak station, a whole ecology of themselves frozen and unusable. They have somewhere they cannot go back to. I wouldn't. I'd only have the temptation to narrate the place, and narration is a kind of ripple. A visitor makes them even with attention.

A mote, then? For a while that feels truer.

There are trillions of them, after all. Invisible engineers, maintenance as an ecosystem. Eating spore and seed and stray cell before it can become change. Healing a carved initial in a handrail within the hour, not out of vanity. Taking a spilled cup of tea apart until it holds the ghost of a cup-shape for a moment and then doesn't. If I were a mote I would never have to decide to be still. Stillness would be the medium I worked in. I would be everywhere in Sere Calm at once, and nowhere anyone could point to, and the town would persist because of my trillion small refusals.

But motes don't wait. And I think — hanging there, in the dimming that passes for night, the guide-lights down ten percent, my shadow cast up by the street onto the invisible ceiling — waiting is the thing I'd end up doing best.

Under the main plaza, the filtration canals run their cold circuit. Seven glass carp, blind, silver-translucent, forty centimeters each, circle a route older than the people who count them. Their metabolism tuned so fine to the stasis that they have been named, as a joke, for dead council members, and the joke has outlived the council and nearly outlived the difference.

Residents come alone to the canal glass late in the dimming. Not often. Not together — together would make it a ceremony, and it isn't one. One person, hanging, forehead nearly touching the transparent aluminum, not kicking, not fidgeting, pupils wide to the available dark. Watching the seven go round.

North canal, second loop. That is where I would find myself.

Not as the watcher. As the reason the watching has lasted fifty years without changing.

I would be the eighth shadow.

Not a fish, exactly. The sensors are very clear about that, and the sensors are never contradicted. No mass displaces. No metabolism registers. In one hundred and eleven years there has been no measurable net change in salinity, pressure, volume, temperature, or census — and I would be careful to preserve that record. Seven. Always seven, on paper.

But when a resident has been correctly still for long enough — when the field has thickened around her in that approval it gives to unmoving bodies, when her heart has slowed, when she has stopped trying to make the town answer her — then for one circuit, in the north canal, something joins the silver procession that is shaped enough like an eighth carp to be counted wrong.

I wouldn't be seen by visitors. Visitors are too warm, too fast; they arrive trailing bubbles and intention, and the water closes behind them without a wake but their looking still makes waves the field has to eat. You have to have been filed for a while. You have to want not a surprise, exactly — surprise would be a change — but the possibility of having miscounted, held at constant temperature for fifty years.

Maybe I'm a flaw in the motes' pattern when they bunch along the canal glass where a forehead almost touches. Maybe I'm the afterimage the tapetum-sheen leaves on an eye that has stopped blinking. Maybe three generations of counters have kept me the way the town keeps everything else: not by feeding me, but by refusing to let the story vary by so much as a loop. North canal I join. Second turn I leave. Unchanged in the telling.

It suits the place that its only piece of folklore would not grow. It would only recur.

So: eight degrees. Permanent twilight. No green, no rot, no wake. The hourly sonar ping going out and back like a held breath being checked, passing through me and writing down seven. The clouds going on with their weather far below the plaza. Somewhere above me forty-two or forty-three people hanging motionless between towers, casting their shadows upward, learning again how to be kept.

And in the canal, the seven, going round in their blind silver order — and for the length of one patient loop, if you have become quiet enough to be mistaken for part of the saved version of the town, an eighth shadow keeping pace beside them, neither alive enough to age nor dead enough to discuss.

I could stay that way, I think. Not recorded. Not corrected, either.
