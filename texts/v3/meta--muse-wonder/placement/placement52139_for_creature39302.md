I would go down the way everyone does, and I would not come back up at shift change.

Not at first. At first I try on the human places, because they're the ones with hands.

Halfway down the iron grating stair, where the sound stops being something you hear and becomes something your chest is doing, I could be a retter — bare arms slick to the elbow in a waist-deep tank, turning flax by feel, knowing it's done not by sight, there's nothing to see in that cloudy green water, but by the small give between finger and thumb. Or a taro tender, calf-deep and slowest of all, moving at the pace the water moves. Or one of the nave apprentices in that underwater noon, lifting trays, tying things up, my eyes getting so good in the dimness that ordinary daylight up top would make me shade my face.

I don't stay in any of those bodies. They leak. Their nails tear if they aren't cut to the quick, their lungs eventually object, their hearing goes first in one narrow band — the shuttle's tick — and then they are moved, tenderly and without ceremony, to the tin-box rooms off the canteen to etch on slate what can no longer be trusted to paper. They have a genius I don't have: they can leave. Their ears ring for an hour at a dry dinner table, their hands make little involuted signs — *wait, hot, line moving* — before they catch themselves and laugh too loud. And then they sleep in stillness, which down below would be a kind of death, and they come back in the morning because they choose to and because if they didn't, in a week there would be no lanes left to stand in.

I would belong to the other population.

Down past the settling ponds where the duckweed makes a matte skin you can fold back like a blanket, past the pump cathedral with its polished rails and tiled floor, in the glass nave in the middle where it rains indoors all day — I think that's where you'd find me. Not in the tanks. Not useful enough to be retted or woven. Up on a tier, in the green, even light.

A mother-plant.

Tied in coconut husk to a warm vent, where the steam keeps my roots at blood temperature all year. Philodendron cousins going up the standpipes around me, fern banks tier on tier below, condensation gathering on the clouded panes above and letting go in irregular drips onto my leaves, so I am never quite not being rained on. Caged amber lamps stringing the lane outside, making a thousand small highlights on wet surfaces and no real brightness. I would learn time the way the old pump-keepers do, not by light — at noon it's twilight in here anyway — but by vibration: three flywheels turning somewhere below and beyond, transmitted through iron gantry and wet stone into the vent I'm tied to, slowing fractionally when the pressure from the street above drops. A knock coming in a pipe a minute before it arrives.

I would have a number, etched on slate and kept in a tin box, and a small brass plaque wired — not nailed, wired, so it can be loosened as I thicken — to my stem. The slate would say where I came from, which is the strange part, because it would say I came from myself. Cut here. Rooted there. Cut again. The genealogy of a plant that is not allowed to have descendants, kept meticulously by people who have sons following mothers down the stair as a matter of literacy. I was not grown from seed. Nothing down here is permitted to seed. There are no pollinators to serve, none are admitted; the netting is at the rim, the weekly steam-through sees to the rest. If I flowered, I flowered sterile, a shape made for an animal that will never arrive, and the apprentices — pruned fingertips, soft steam-pale hands — would snip it off before it wasted my strength, and sign to each other across me without lifting a hand above the shoulder.

*That* is the life that fits. To grow, and be cut, and grow from the cutting somewhere else in the basin, identical, so that I am in several places at once and cannot point to the original. To be immortal in the only way the pit allows: not by lasting, particularly, though the figs up on the retaining wall have lasted, sending flat ropes of root down the brick like slow lightning until a root is simply accepted as a support, shaved flat, bolted to, built around — but by repetition. Leaf, cut, leaf again. The monstera through the pipe-end wall isn't decoration that escaped; it *is* the wall now, and if you tried to remove it you would have to admit there was never a wall separate from it. I would like that honesty. Load-bearing because I grew through the gaps.

And underneath the labor of the place — the looms, the vats — the real labor would be directed at me, daily, a negotiation: keep me off the pipes so the pipes can be found by hand. Keep me out of the lane so the lane stays a shoulder's width. Prune me not because I am finished but because I am not, ever, and in this heat and wet a forgotten corner greens over in days, a coiled hose vanishes under purslane in three. The workers would know my habits the way pump-keepers know a machine's idiosyncrasies in their molars. They would have a sign for me that was never on the original placards — the placards only said *steam on, duck, vat hot* — something quick and economical for *this one will close the vent by Friday* or *take from the lower stem, the upper tears.* Signs accrete grammar down here. I would be part of the grammar.

At night I would stay, which no human is allowed to do. The last line climbs the shaking stair and comes out blinking into a quiet so sudden it must feel miraculous, and below them the basin goes on unwitnessed by any animal eye. The pumps beat on alone in the mist. The bacteria in the retting tanks — the one living thing nobody counts, because nobody can see it — go on patiently loosening fiber in the dark. Algae films whatever was cleaned that morning. A fern unrolls exactly where it will have to be cut away. And I would sit in the drip and the hiss and the chest-thump, not sleeping, not waiting exactly, just continuing the slow, frantic-seeming, actually patient work of making another leaf too large for its tier.

In the morning the crew comes back down wet and gets wetter, and someone young stops at my vent with twine and a knife, reads my plaque the way you'd check a name you almost remember, and takes a cutting with the practiced economy of a hand-sign.

Palm flat, briefly, against the stem where I was cut. Not *wait.* Not quite. Something the language grew for itself, fifteen meters down, where talking costs too much breath:

*Again.*
