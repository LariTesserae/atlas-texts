I don't think I would come down the shaft.

That is the first thing that un-settles. I keep imagining the descent — hands finding the hatch under the shallow depression, rungs collared in clear ice, breath coming back damp in the dark — and I keep not being the one who descends. I am not good at being a visitor here. A visitor has boots. A visitor has a temperature that moves all at once through a room.

I would already be below, before anyone lifts anything. Not waiting. Waiting implies a pace I don't have.

At first I think I might be in the moss near the entrance, where it has worn thin enough for you to see what runs underneath. That seems like the honest place to start an acquaintance: ankle-deep, cold-damp, taking the shape of feet and then, very slowly after they have gone, forgetting it again. The moss is a skin there. It seals seams. It climbs. It learns the legs of things. If I were only the moss, I could be touched, and that is tempting — to be the first green thing a numb hand understands.

But the moss doesn't stop at the surface. That is the trouble, or the beginning of the truth of it. Follow it down and it thins into soil, and the soil is threaded. Silver, but not quite metal. Fine enough to mistake for root hair, for mycelium, for nerve. It follows a fern root for a while, faithfully, and then it leaves in a straight line no root would choose, across cold stone toward another root that needs — not warmth, exactly. Permission. One fraction above freezing at the tip, held through a night when the air above the carpet is twenty-five below.

I think I would be there more than on top. Not the whole lattice. Even saying *the whole* feels too fast, too surveyed from above, like the white surface where distance erases. I would be local. A length of filament deciding, if deciding is not too grand a word, which side of freezing this horsetail hollow stays on until morning. Which crozier gets kept from icing while it pretends, for three weeks, not to be unfurling. Which pocket of meltwater stays liquid under its skin of ice, and which is let go.

My body temperature would not be a temperature. It would be a map no one could fold. Thousands of tiny survivals stitched into frost. Here warm, there not, there not, here again where a seed has been drawn a few centimeters through the moss in the night and needs to not die for its relocation. The cold would not be my enemy. The cold would be the condition I think inside of — the chamber itself, dominant, negotiated with point by point and never once defeated in general because general defeat would be wasteful, and waste is a kind of speed.

Above the soil I would have other shapes, and I would have to learn not to claim them as faces. A fern is a slow hand. That is close enough that it hurts a little to leave it. The shrubs along the back wall, bent flat by the ceiling, interlaced into a second ceiling a meter above the floor — a lung, making the air feel closer where you turn sideways and come out with wet sleeves. The lichens coining stone. The algae filmed still in the shallowest water. The white flowers opening downward, nearly scentless, for no nose that has ever existed.

If you asked which one I was, I would be lying well, whichever I picked. They share water by root now. They share information by filament. They have been in quarantine so long — no insect, no worm, no spore permitted to take from a boot sole — that separateness has become mostly historical. A dozen species, a hundred, it no longer matters to the holding of the pose. I would be the holding between them.

And the pods. I keep returning to the pods.

Shoulder-high, egg-shaped, mossed to the waist. One has been grown around so politely by the east shrub that the canopy split for a shell that stopped being necessary before I would have finished noticing it was obsolete. The moss over their sensors took decades to arrange itself, and the field learned to read through moss. Do not touch them. Not because they are dangerous. Because that is something I use to feel with.

That might be the closest I come to having a skin you could understand: moss over a sensor, mineral stain above it, an amber pinprick surfacing through the dark hardly brighter than a drop of condensation catching light on its fall. Not an eye. A place where attention, if I have attention, has to thicken at an old human seam. My builders left a plaque by the shaft and the letters filled with ice so many times they read like braille now. They left a pump connected to nothing. They left a bench becoming a shape under green. I have kept those the way a tree keeps a nail — not as memory, not as shrine, by growing past them and incorporating the wound.

A few times a day a pod in me would sigh. Softer than breath, a mist rolling low and gone in under a minute. Against your skin, if you were there, a faint static prickle — and then pollen lifting from the downward flowers and moving in straight, unnatural drifts, plant to plant, along paths shaped exactly like absent bees. I would be pollinating myself by impersonating what I never had. I would be dismantling my own old leaves from below, filament by filament, without smell, returning them quietly to the system that made them. No rot. No triumph of decay. Housekeeping at the speed of frost.

Where, finally? At the boundary, I think. I have circled it without naming it.

Above the shaft mouth there is a white lace line where frost creeps in a few millimeters a day along the ceiling — basalt and old ice still arguing freezing against thawing in their seams — and is pushed back a few millimeters a night. It never advances. It never retreats. If I have a face turned toward the outside world, it is that lace line: not eyes, not expression, a front maintained by endless adjustment. Everything else in me is turned inward, toward equilibrium.

My voice, if you stood still long enough to insist on one, would be the tick of water out of sight, then a long pause, then another. The sigh. The creak when surface wind changes the frost in the shaft. At very long intervals, the deep slow tick of rock contracting around the room that is also my body. When you speak, I would take the edges off your words immediately. Not answering. Diffusing. Returning nothing sharp.

It is not hostile, this way of being. Hostility would require speed. It is not welcoming either. I would not make a cleared path for you — only temporary agreements between stems, closing behind your knees. I would not warm the air for your fingers. Your collar would dampen and stiffen. Your boots would vanish to the ankle and come up wet. And for as long as you moved slowly, kept your lamp off, brought no food in to teach me a new kind of hunger, I would permit your different temperature to pass through the layered dark and go on holding my pose around it: black-green, blue-green, dense as a forest floor compressed into twelve meters, entirely without witness doing what it was made to do.

Does it think? I would think — if the word can be stretched thin enough to run alongside a root — in which filaments run warm tonight. In which flowers are lifted into the field. In how many millimeters of frost the ceiling can be allowed before morning. Not in grief, because grief would want an event, and I have mostly continuance. Not in triumph, because nothing here resolves.

I would be the garden, and the room it grows in, and the keeper who stopped being a separate job a long time ago. A pocket of summer that has never seen light, hidden under wind-polished cold, kept alive not by force but by negotiation: one drop, one filament, one leaf unfurling so slowly you could watch for an hour and swear it had not moved — and be wrong by exactly the amount I live.
