At first I would not be seen.

That's how you learn to be here. Low on the dark side of a slab, where the wind has already gone over once and forgotten to come back. One eye on the flat. The light is that spread-out, noon-that-is-almost-evening light, and in it I am only another piece of black rock that hasn't been stained yet.

The air does not trouble me the way it troubles the newcomers. They climb a rubble slope and stand with their mouths open. I taste the cold on the inside of my beak — iron, wet stone, a little salt from the lake crust — and let it pass through me. Birds are built for thin places. The trouble is not breathing. The trouble is stillness. Hold still too long here and the frost gets into your feet.

So I shift. Hop. Turn my head.

From the scree below me a marmot whistles and is gone into his stones. Farther out the peat gives and shines. The melt has been running since mid-morning, braiding itself in bright sheets over gravel, finding a new way around a block that fell sometime in the night with that rifle-shot crack. There is a fresh scar above the marsh, pale and untouched. By tomorrow it will not be untouched. Lichen first, rust and sulphur, eating stone one coin at a time. Then me, to see what the fall shook loose.

I don't own the scar. I don't own the marsh either, though I return to it more faithfully than the cranes do. They come back in pairs to the same drowning meadow and stand grey in the grey shallows, rebuilding on whatever hummock the water has left them this year. I admire that, in a distant way. My fidelity is wider and less honest. I am faithful to whatever is happening.

If the geese are down, I keep to the edges of their noise. If a fox is working the sedge at a trot, I follow at a height that pretends not to be following. If the wild asses come in their strings of twenty and thirty, pale bellies catching what light there is, I let them pass below me and watch their ears turn together. They know the green line better than anyone. Where the melt lingers, where the grass comes bright for a few weeks, where to stand when the afternoon squall comes scouring sideways and everything living weather-vanes into it and waits.

And if a processor is moving —

You hear it first, and barely. A thrum in the wet ground. A conveyor clinking at intervals, patient as a heartbeat. Then the amber running lights in the fog, the long low hull crawling on its person-high tracks, drinking at the front, laying down clean warm water behind. I ride the little lift that comes off even that blunt shape. I have learned its rhythms the way I've learned the herds'. At dawn there will be ducks in the steam at its stern, in the pool that never quite freezes, and sometimes something small and dead or discarded near the discharge, and the crew inside will be watching their gauges and watching, just as carefully, for a goose sitting invisible in the intake path.

They stop for asses. I have seen it enough times that I no longer find it strange: that enormous beast idling, exhaust flattening white in the cold, while a foal picks its way unhurried over the track. The law, yes. But also the place teaching its manners. Keep low. Keep wide. Keep moving. Never insist.

I lift then, because the updraft off the new cliff is better than sitting, and because from a little height the plateau stops pretending to be empty. Three processors, kilometers apart, three lit smudges when the dark comes. Herds stringing toward the lee meadows. Cranes calling once across the water. Pikas in the sedge, cutting stem by stem, laying out their whole winter in miniature stacks. Nothing upright. Nothing finished. Drainage still deciding, rock still deciding, routes redrawn every thaw.

I croak, once, and hear it arrive clean a mile away in the thin air. The ravens in the story the newcomers tell about this place always get that line. Sound carrying. Head turning as we pass. It's true enough. We are good at announcing and better at noticing.

But the part that makes me stay is not the summer.

The summer is brief plenty for everyone. Then the bargain ends. The cranes and geese lift and go south over ranges that would kill the walkers. The asses drift lower. The marmots go under stone. The processors turn, one by one, and crawl down off the plateau before the seeps ice shut, leaving only a faint darkening over gravel that the frost is already buckling out of existence.

And what is left is left with me.

Lichen on fresh rock. Peat stiffening under clear ice. Foxes making a sparser living in the wind, wolves a change in the foxes' behavior, and me, black against a blacker winter flat, finding the world reduced to what can afford to remain. I hop over frozen braids. I test carrion frozen hard as wood. I shelter in the lee of slabs that will shed again in spring and sleep with my head turned back into the held warmth of my own feathers — a cushion plant's trick, learned at feather-scale. A few degrees warmer in there. A few degrees calmer. Enough.

Under the lakes the microbes go on with their slow, salt-thick summer, unseen. Above them I go on with mine.

I am not the largest thing here, or the oldest, though I am older than any crew that comes up gasping for a season. I am the one who watches from the torn edges, who includes the crawling factories in his map the same year the ducks do, who knows which lake will be skipped because the sedge is flowering and which scar of rock will be worth visiting after the next slab falls.

A resident, then. Not by settling. No one settles here and keeps the name for long.

By returning, and returning, along a route written in wind and meltwater — and by leaving every perch exactly as sharp as I found it.
