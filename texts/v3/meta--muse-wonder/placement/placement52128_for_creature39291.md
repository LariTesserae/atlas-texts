I would not come down the path.

I think at first I would mistake myself for something that does. Something with a hat to lose, with boots to send grit-rhythms through the rim rock ahead of me. But the Ear would correct that quickly. Loose things belong to the column here, and I am not loose. I have too many points of contact, and I hold on too well.

Lower. That’s where it settles.

Not in the great trunks — I am not patient enough, or rooted enough, to drink stone for a century and call it a life, though I envy them their long conversation. Not in the cushions either, breathing in all day just to sigh myself empty over the ground birds. I have a harder back than that. I have weight in my tail-end and a use for it.

I am one of the fist-sized beetles who climbs the reed-like side-shoots of the hollow trees.

Armored is the word from outside. From inside, it is more like being a drum that grew legs around itself. My shell is dark and pitted, oil-slicked from crawling under the sail-leaves, thick enough that when I clamp down in the worst gusts the wind has to content itself with worrying at my edges and making me tick faintly against the stem. Six feet, each ending in a hook worn bright. Through those hooks the world arrives the way it arrives for everything that truly lives here: not as sound, exactly, but as trembling.

You learn the bowl through your feet or you learn nothing. The shoot under me is never still. It flexes at its joint and complains creakily up into the trunk; the trunk hums its own tall note; under both of them, slower and larger, runs the root-conversation, the one plant talking to itself through warm rock toward sulfur, toward feldspar, toward the buried first tree at its heart. I sit in the crossing of those three voices all day and try not to add a fourth until it’s needed.

The climbing takes the morning, such as morning is in that brown-green gloom. Up from the lee of the root flare, where I sleep clamped in a crack that smells of resin and old pepper, past the lower slits of the trunk — careful there, when the wind finds them the whole column goes *hmmmm* through my legs and up into my teeth, if beetles have anything that does the work of teeth — and out along a side-shoot that bows and recovers, bows and recovers, trimming itself to the vortex. A moth will still be under a sail-leaf above me, oil-proof and powdered silver, waiting out a gust that ended an hour ago. A spider goes over on its one bright line, hand-sized, utterly committed, reeling itself toward the next tree like a fisherman who has decided to be the fish.

I climb until the shoot is thin enough to answer me.

Then I position myself, which is the whole art. Too low and my weight is swallowed by the trunk’s big note. Too high and I’m just another thing the wind is trying to steal. There is a place — she showed me, though she would not call it showing; she simply picked me up once between two horn-calloused fingers, unimpressed by my kicking, and set me down two joints higher than I would have chosen, and tapped the shoot twice — where the stem will take a blow and give it back clean.

And then I lift my abdomen, and drop it.

*Tok.*

Not loud. Never loud. Loud is for the walls and the great trunks and the column bursting twenty feet high over the grove. Mine is a working sound. It travels down the shoot and into the trunk and out again through the plume-fronds, stiff and feathery, sieving the turning air — and when I have the rhythm right, things fall. Pollen. Dust loosened from the sieves. A stunned winged thing that was riding the debris column a moment too low. Below me a lizard stalls mid-glide, rib-fans ticking, and snaps it out of the air without ever looking at me, because looking is not how we know each other here.

I hammer, and rest. Hammer, and shift a fraction to the left, because the wind has leaned. Hammer in the gap the Tuner leaves me.

She is the weather I understand best. Her three beats and the dragged thumb — throat-clearing on a trunk slit — come through the wood before they ever reach the air, and every beetle on every shoot goes briefly still to make room, the way you make room in a crowded room for someone carrying something hot. If she raps twice I stop at once: fall coming, or a shoot being hammered half to death by enthusiasm, which has happened, which I have done in my first season when every stem felt like it was asking. She moved me then too. No anger in it. She just sealed the split I’d made with black resin, shaved a slit somewhere above me a finger’s width longer so the young tree dropped from a whistle to a breath, and put me where my drumming would do less harm and more good. I have stayed mostly in key since.

Echo is my great problem and my great pleasure. Sound does not leave the Ear; it only goes away and comes back wrong. A rival beetle three trees over may drum a challenge at noon, or may have drummed it at breakfast, or may be me, returned around the fluted wall a little lower and slower, sounding older and more worth answering. I have courted my own echo for a whole afternoon. I am not ashamed. In a place where scent blows away before it can mean anything and sight is dust and moving shadow, you take your company where the wall sends it. The finch chicks grow up in the resonating chambers drumming *tok, tok-tok, tok* at their own wooden world. I am doing the same thing from the outside of it.

What do I eat? What the air files off the world. Scale-things that hide under the copper undersides of the sails. Mineral dust, ash, insect wings caught in the plumes and worked loose by my hammering. Once a season the moths come off the leaves in their brief silver multitude and for an hour even a beetle eats like a king, clumsily, powdered to the elbows. I drink nothing. There is nothing to drink, which up here is not a hardship but a fact of architecture, like the vortex, like the heat coming back through the rock into the soles of things. The oil on the leaves gets onto me and into the seams of my armor and keeps the salt wind from filing me down to a white map of myself. I smell, permanently, of the grove. Resin. Hot pepper. Old incense. If a ground bird takes me one day from behind while I’m lifted and listening — and they are quick, and I am the right size, and being periodically blown protesting into the updraft does not make them any less hungry — then I will at least have been seasoned by my own home.

I am not safe from her, either. I know what roasted beetle smells like when the armor splits in leaf oil. I have climbed over the emptied shells of my own kind in the root-cracks and felt them give back a small, reasonable note underfoot. That is the bargain of the Ear, as far as I can tell: everything here is an instrument, including the food.

Even at the end. Especially at the end, maybe. If I die clamped to my shoot the wind will worry me loose eventually — it worries everything loose eventually — and my shell will join the column for a while, turning companionably in the middle of the bowl with the husks and the feathers and the lost awl and the dead who were lighter than they expected to be. Now and then I will brush the fluted wall and the wall will answer with its low stone note, and I will tick my way audibly around the bowl once, twice, three times, lower and slower each time, until some afternoon a finch or a spider or a beetle new to its shoot hears a stranger drumming from behind and lifts its weight and answers.

That seems right. Not lonely, and not quiet, and not finished — just, for the moment, in key.
