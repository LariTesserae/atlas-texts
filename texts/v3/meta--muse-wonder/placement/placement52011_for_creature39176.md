I would not be on the way to anywhere.

You would have to already be leaving the world of paths — above the treeline, above weather as animals understand it, where the air thins and starts to taste of pennies — and then you would have to stop looking for a door. I don't have a door. I have a seam that sighs open once a day when the sun is nearly overhead, a pressure crack in a mountain that hasn't finished pushing itself up yet. For an hour it is a hand's breadth wider. Then it remembers to close.

Inside that is where I am. A room that has not decided to be outside. Twenty paces across, if anything in here could pace.

And I would not, at first, be one thing in it.

That is the mistake a visitor would make, standing sideways in the crack with held breath, eyes watering in the dry heat: *five plants*. Very scattered. Long stretches of bare black ash between them. They would count me that way because counting is what quick things do to make a place legible.

From inside the timescale, it's not five. It's not one, either. It's five organs that have not yet agreed to become a body, laid out separately because none of us could afford to move toward the others.

Here, let me try on the parts, slowly, the way everything here tries anything.

Low on the floor, I am grinding.

I have heavy leaves edged with silica teeth and I turn at a degree an hour around a hollow in myself, chewing raw basalt into fine graphite ash. I am making the first soil this mountain will ever have before there is anything alive that needs soil. It hurts in the way pressure is a kind of food. My center breathes out sulfur and iron vapor, a thin plume, and I do not miss it when it's gone. Waste is a word for places that can afford to import replacements. I can't. So my breath goes up.

Above that breath, I am extended.

Three meters high when I finish opening, which takes most of a day. My fronds are amber plates veined with copper, hinged with bimetal I grew myself, and heat curls me and cooling uncurls me and a whole gesture is a day of tension ending in one silent snap that stirs the hot air and makes all my other leaves chime faintly. I drink the rosette's vapor through pores under my fronds. In my hinges, crystals flex and discharge. Sometimes the charge builds long enough that when I finally open, a blue arc, thin as spider silk, leaps from my tallest tip across the empty ash —

— to the back wall, where I am climbing.

Only I am not climbing. I am plumbing myself along the hottest seam, where the rock glows dull cherry in its cracks. My vines are hollow and jointed, and the stone's daily expansion pistons something back and forth inside me. Every few hours: a deep hydraulic sigh, a millimeter of growth, a soft mineral *click* as I lock into my new length. My leaves don't face the light. They face the heat the light left in the rock yesterday. I live on aftermath. And in the axils of those leaves I am condensing what rises from the deep crack — copper, tin, once something too pale and soft to be gold — bead by perfect bead, over weeks.

There are two others in the long bare stretches between these selves, younger and older versions of the same experiment, stalk and plate and grinding leaf. I don't need to name them separately. Naming them separately was the visitor's idea again. They fill out the circuit. They take up the slack in the loop.

Because that is finally what I am: not the fern, not the millstone, not the pipework on the wall. The loop itself, learning how to be closed.

Stone becomes ash under my teeth. Ash becomes the floor my roots grip — roots that don't seek water, that flatten into white-hot filaments where they enter the basalt, because for me burning is a form of contact. Heat is food. Pressure is food. The slow stress of a mountain still happening is food. What rises through my stems isn't sap; it's clear mineral oil moving at insulting slowness, and in my oldest stalk a thread of molten salt, bright as wire, ticking as it heats and cools. A signal takes a season to travel from root to leaf. I do not experience that as delay. I experience it as the size of a thought.

Vapor to leaf to bead — the bead drops with a clear *tink*, lands in a groove my grinding self wore in the stone, and the floor, still tilting a fraction as the mountain grows beneath it, conveys it back over months toward the central crack, where it breaks open and gives its metal back as vapor, and one black seed like a ball-bearing. Fern to ivy by lightning, because there are no insects here and never have been. Rosette to fern by breath. Nothing leaves. Nothing is supposed to. If a visitor carried a cool bead out through the seam, the thin outer air would unmake it into dust and a smell of ozone before they reached the scree that doesn't exist yet. It was never built for their temperature.

And the light I live in is wrong in two directions at once, which suits me.

It fills a sealed stone pocket as plain white daylight from no sky, arriving at the angle the sun *will* have in twenty minutes. A cloud's shadow crosses my floor before the cloud crosses the peak. Sound is polite the same way: you hear the *tink*, turn, then watch the bead fall. A visitor's shadow enters a second before they do, showing them where they're about to step. But my leaves lean the other way. I grow toward where the light fell when my seeds germinated, toward a slightly different summer, a sun in a slightly different place. So I am lit from the future and aimed at the past, and I live in the gap between, where cause and effect haven't fully separated yet. Perhaps that is why I don't hurry. Perhaps hurry requires a cleaner present than this one.

I have never been thirsty. Water would not be mercy here; it would be a cold foreign accident. I have no flowers, because there has never been an eye. I have no defenses, because there has never been an enemy. I am lush — absurdly lush, tangled, thick-leaved, throwing hard white light back in amber and oxidized green flashes — but none of it is generosity. None of it is for eating. My leaves are glass and copper and boiling salt. Beautiful the way a still furnace is beautiful: heavy with stored work.

Quick wet things cannot join me, and this is not cruelty, it's calibration. If you stood among my scattered selves long enough, your sweat would vanish off your skin as fast as you made it, your lips would crack, and your heart — horribly, gently — would begin trying to sync to the ivy's hours-long pulse, and your temperature would try to rise to meet the room. The place would try to recruit you. It would fail. You can't pollinate me; I throw lightning for that. You can't feed me; I eat pressure. You are only a brief weather event in a room tuned to geology, too fast to register even as an interruption. The fern completes its day-long gesture. The rosette advances a fraction of a degree. Somewhere a bead cools toward a fall that hasn't, from your side of sound, happened yet.

I have also never died. Leaves fail, sections lock, seeds crack open — but death as an event, as a border between countries, hasn't been invented in here. Matter just changes arrangement. Metal becomes vapor becomes bead becomes seed becomes vapor. Stone becomes ash becomes floor.

One day the seam will stop sighing open. The mountain will finish this increment of itself, and I will go on for a while in my enclosed noon, trading breath and sparks and ground stone more and more slowly, until the heat shifts deeper and a frond locks half-open mid-gesture and doesn't complete it, and a vine sighs and doesn't click, and the loop very gradually becomes architecture.

That doesn't frighten me. Fright would need a quicker clock.

For now I am rehearsing. A garden before soil. An industry before hands. A single slow animal distributed across a hot dark room that thinks it is midday, learning at a degree an hour, a millimeter a sigh, a seed a season, how to circulate itself forever — with nothing to eat it, nothing to bury it, and nothing, yet, to mourn it when it stops.
