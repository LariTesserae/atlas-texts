I think I would start out thinking I was listening.

That is the mistake a visitor makes, and for a while I would make it too. I'd be somewhere off a station jetty, or down along a ridge flank, and I'd believe the roar was a thing happening to me from outside — the falls, four hundred kilometres south, the turbines stepping the whole sea down to the lowlands, the sound coming through rock and water and sternum until there was nowhere it wasn't.

It takes a long time to understand that listening is not something you do here. It's something you are built around.

My ribs find it first. Not all at once. Each one takes a slightly different part of the chord, the way the hull of a work-boat takes it, the way a bedframe in a blockhouse takes it up through the floor at night. Where the tone builds on itself off a submerged peak, the water thickens. Where two ridges cross and the reflections cancel, there is a kind of hollow — not silence, there is never silence, but a lessening, a null I can feel in the long bones of my body as a place to turn.

I do not have eyes. That stopped seeming like a lack very early, or perhaps it never started. Above me at noon the water opens into light so clear it has no texture. A peak a hundred metres down can still be resolving, edge on edge, black basalt going down in knife-lines through what looks like thin air, and there is nothing between — no weed, no drift, no particle of green life to tell me whether a thing is near and small or far and enormous. Too much seeing, and no way to measure it. I measure it by pressure instead. By how the one endless note bends around it. I learned the shape of my home ridge the way station children learn sign: by being inside it before I knew I was learning. By immersion. Without deciding to.

When I was born I was almost clear. Two metres long, ribs still soft, not yet tuned enough to hold the wider map. So I stayed close to my mother, close enough to feel her as a moving weather. She made a private interference around herself — the roar coming off her flank, off her resonating ribs, breaking and reforming — and I lived inside that disturbance for years, drifting in it, feeding when her stomach began to glow faintly blue-green from the mats she had cropped, following the slow pulse of that light along her side. *Here. Here. Here.* Not a call. She has never made a call in her life. Nothing here needs to add a sound to a sea already speaking at full volume.

In time my own ribs hardened into the chord. The world got larger, not louder. It had always been this loud.

I crop the bacterial mats where the plateau is still cracking under itself. I go down until the cold stops changing — it never really changes, two degrees at the surface in sun, two degrees in the trench dark — and I find the places where fresh rock has split and begun to exhale. Hydrogen. Sulphur. A mineral warmth against the cold that I can feel along my mouth plates. The mats grow white there, and rust-orange, spreading a hand's breadth and no further than the chemistry allows, and I rasp them from the flank with a slow, deliberate patience. The grazers are already there below me by the million, cemented to the vent rock, opening and closing, opening and closing, not to each other but to the pressure of the turbines, as if one vast animal were breathing very slowly in the dark. When I have fed enough, my own stomach lights from inside and the pulse travels backward along my ribs, diffuse and blue, and sometimes far off through the clear black another pulse answers it. Double, and hold. Or nothing for a month. Both are complete forms of conversation.

I am solitary in the way this sea teaches everything to be solitary. Not lonely. Loneliness would require an expectation of nearness, and there is no nearness here, only provinces. Six days of empty water for a boat is an afternoon's drift for me. I cross trenches where dead things sink so slowly into the stillness that they are taken apart over decades — no rot, no rush, grazers and chemistry doing their patient work. I have passed wrecks sitting upright on ridge crests, paint still legible, as if someone had parked them there and intended to come back. I have a scar along my third rib where a survey cable lay humming across rock and burned me before I knew its pattern; the light breaks there now, one pulse missing, and the station hands on the reef above that ridge know me by it. Their grandparents knew me by it. I was already old when they were learning to close their hands around their first signs.

Sometimes, after feeding, I rise — not to breathe; I have never needed the surface for that — but to drift for a while in the sunlit layer off a pump station. I can feel the station before I am anywhere a human would call near: the tick of heat from the discharge, the warmer mineral water steaming where it meets the cold, the blockhouse conducting the roar up out of the rock in its own smaller register. The crew come out onto the jetty. I know them not by sight but by the brief, irregular disturbances they make — boots on steel, a hand on a rail, bodies standing close together the way they always stand, touching forearms to get each other's attention. They stop work when I am there. They talk about me across the workbench of the air in slow, unhurried signs, and I answer without meaning to answer, just the light going down my flank in its feeding rhythm, rib by rib, the broken third beat in it.

Neither of us makes a sound. There is no need, and there never has been.

That is what I become, I think, the longer I stay in the letting-it-emerge of this place: not the keeper, though I am near kin to the keeper — not the grazer, though I keep the grazers' time in my ribs — but the large, blind, translucent thing in the mid-water that has learned to navigate entirely by a voice not its own. Fourteen metres of patience. A lantern of tuned bone. A stomach faintly lit by eating stone-fed things in a sea where nothing grows toward the sun.

And if you lowered yourself over the side of a boat at noon, in ordinary daylight, I would not startle. I would not sing out or turn to look. I would simply already be there, crossing far beneath you through water clearer than air, trailing my slow pulse into the trench dark, feeling your small hull take its place in the great interference pattern — one more ridge, one more brief shape for the falls to speak around — while above us both the surface closed flat and grey overhead, exactly where it has always been.
