I would hear the place before I ever learned to live in it.

Not the loud parts first — not the Trunk chords in my molars, not the pod-falls rolling like weather from another country. The sorting of it. A footstep through the mat, and then its copy, frayed at the edges, handed back a day later by a hollow I never saw. The real herd drumming in my knees, underneath the Plant's fond memory of a herd. I think I would spend a long, useless time at the beginning just standing with my palm down on the warm felt, trying to tell which vibration was happening *now*.

That would rule some lives out for me, kindly and quickly.

I wouldn't last as a surface runner, not as one of the good ones. I admire the leanness of that life too cleanly to pretend I'd be natural at it — following thousands of black runners for days inside their fog and thunder, owning rope and tallow and a story, reading a skin-sheet fall by the hole it cuts in the wind. I have the patience for the waiting but not, I think, the beautiful indifference to being lost. I'd want to stop and annotate the emptiness. And on the open mat, between Trunks, stopping to annotate is how you wake up grown over.

I could be mat-born, for a while. That part calls to me: the second country underneath, where it's warm and close and dark in the total way, where children learn their mother by breath and smell before sight. The smell of iron and crushed green. The dog-sized beetles passing in their blind trains. Being one of the people who knows which bog steams clean and which one, after a quake, exhales that sweet poison that lays a whole burrow down. There's an intimacy there I recognize — living inside the body of the world rather than on it, navigating by the backs of your hands.

But I'd keep drifting upward, toward the hatch. Toward the place where the tunnel air meets the cold.

So: Trunk-folk, probably. But not high Trunk-folk, not one of the ones dropping lines into the well for frog-bats or listening up into that unmapped dark for canopy things. Lower. Basal. The first chamber above the mat, in the ribbed flare where the Trunk drinks its own condensation and the roots go down into the tunnels. A chamber someone else cut twenty years ago that has been very slowly, politely closing ever since I've lived in it.

That feels right in a way the other possibilities don't — a room that is also a listening device, that reports every footstep back to you whether you want the report or not.

I would be broad and low from it, the way everyone here is broad and low: big-chested for the thin air, soft-booted, one hand trailing by habit even indoors, fingertips spread on living wood. My eyes would be the startling kind, wet-black, iris shrunk to a thread, inner lid flicking without my thinking about it when ash or pollen blows. I would squint painfully through the grey summer noon in a slatted bone mask and prefer almost any other hour of the year. Frost has taken the tip of my left middle finger — I can picture that loss clearly, and that I wear a small dark bead on a cord for it, not sentimentally, but because people here mark what the place kept so the accounting stays honest.

And I think I'd have a trade, of the only sort this place really rewards. I would be an echo-sorter. A song-mender.

Not a chief — there are no chiefs worth the word in a country where a crowd is thirty — but one of the people who sits with a cheek pressed to the Trunk wall and reads the plateau. Who is brought a new recording the wood has made and asked: how old is this? Whose was it? Has the Plant already stolen it twice? Families would climb down from their chambers or up out of the hatches or in, damp and tallow-smelling, from the herd-lines, and sing me their map-stanzas, and I would sing them back changed, because the Trunk-bud they left as a blind shiny nose last autumn is a tower this winter and the bog has walked half a valley west and a stanza that lasts too long becomes a lie. I would be particular, exacting, a little feared for refusing to let a good line survive the ground it described.

In practice this means I'd live tethered even indoors, which amuses people. A wrist-loop to the stone footing while I sleep, because a listener who has heard the mat grow over a sleeper in a recording the Trunk played back three days late does not thereafter sleep un-tethered and call it trust. It means my tools hang from pegs the Trunk flesh is slowly swallowing, and I have already decided which bead necklace I'll leave in the scar when this chamber sighs shut for good and I have to move on and cut, or ask to be cut, a new one. It means babies in my walls learn young which pitches not to cry at. It means my whole domestic life — cooking, quarreling, loving, grieving — is being kept imperfectly by the column above and below me, and will be offered back, deepened and wrong, to whoever lives in me after I'm gone. I find I don't mind that. I would rather be misremembered by wood than not kept at all.

My wealth would be exactly what wealth is here: rope, in coils I am stingy with; tallow worked into every seam I own; a frozen-sap knife for chipping root-honey; more knowledge than is strictly mine about which roots bear weight in a growth pulse and which stretches of mat hum hollow over a grazer tunnel about to go. I would trade listening for beetle-eggs and fungal sheets from my mat cousins, for runner meat when the herds were generous, for a cured leaf big enough to re-roof the windbreak I keep meaning to fix.

And once a year — if year is even the right word in a place that is dark for most of its living — I would go out with everyone else for the pollen noon. Mat-folk blinking up out of their hatches, runners in from the herd-lines, my own Trunk families descending chamber by chamber. Thirty of us, maybe. A crowd. Standing coated in luminous dust while the unseen Canopy opens something miles overhead, mouth open like a child's despite the taste of cold metal and crushed herbs, because this is the hour we all agree, practically and therefore sacredly, lets you hear the third call at all.

Then the inhale. Ears popping down the line. Every loose fiber streaming upward toward nothing visible. And the scatter, quick and unceremonious, before the growth starts.

That's where I'd be at the end of it, I think. Not at the center of anything — this world doesn't have a center, it has a body. At a hinge in that body: one ear folded against the bass when the high wind sings, one cheek to the wall, sorting the copy from the caller. Tapping three times through the soles of my feet when something out on the dark mat taps first.

Once to announce. Once to let the Plant steal it.

And a third time, slightly changed, so they know there's still a throat behind the echo.
