I think I would start out mistaking myself for the visitor.

Kneeling. Turning sideways at the unmarked hatch. Counting — not out loud, you can't count out loud in there — one minute, two minutes, while the water beads on my eyelashes and the roar gets into the soft places under my ribs. I would try to be the custodian with the hand signs, the one who knows to tap four fingers, pause, two flat, and then leave before the floor starts to tilt.

But four minutes is an honest limit. It tells you what you aren't.

I wouldn't leave cleanly. Something in me would keep slipping sideways out of that role. First I'd be a droplet forming on a copper-bright pipe, swelling until the vibration held me for half a second longer than gravity should allow — trembling in a node of pressure, all surface and listening, before I fell with all the others in a brief, private rain. Then a piezo beetle in the bronze ranks on the warm wall, one of thousands, lifting my wing cases a fraction when the pressure cycle peaked, making current out of being shaken, never warm and never cold in a way that was entirely my own. Then a vent sparrow, blind generations deep, naked against a cable loom, saying nothing aloud ever again but passing a shiver down the line: *leak, high, sweet smell, here.*

Each of those is truer than the visitor. Each of them is living off the surplus instead of enduring it. But none of them stays.

Where I settle — slowly, the way everything in that pocket settled, by erosion and repetition rather than by deciding — is lower. In the deepest recess, beside the outflow, where the two great membranes flex and the indicator moss breathes its dull blue. Where everything turns when the filters purge, not only toward the food coming down, but toward the point beside it.

I would be her.

Not at first. At first I am only fist-sized and very pleased with myself. I have found the best valve hollow in the world. I wedge myself in, inflate my sides hard against the metal, and croak, and the recess takes my small sub-bass note and makes me enormous. The tower does my shouting for me. Food arrives on the current. The air that would shred anything tall passes over me in folded, delayed layers, and in the cancellations, in the low quiet pockets where black-green moss can keep its felt, I am left alone to grow.

So I grow the only way the room permits. Not up. Out. Low and wide and denser every year, plate by plate, each plate separated by a seam of moss so the sound crosses me in segments and never builds to tearing. My skin thickens. My ears, ruined early, calcify into conduction rods, and that loss turns out to be a translation: I stop hearing *at* the world and start hearing through it. Through my sternum. Through my kneecaps. Through the polished places where my belly hasn't left the floor in decades. A custodian's tap arrives the way weather arrives for other animals.

I learn the job by becoming part of the architecture.

When a harmonic rises that the baffles weren't tuned for, I swell and take it into the soft places under my ribs. When the sparrows shiver a coolant leak down through the beetles tiled across my back — beetles clicking, shifting like living scale mail, warming me with the trickle of energy they make from the roar — I move one plated foot, a fraction, and tap back a location. When my skin cracks in the thirty-two-degree wet heat, the maintenance motes gather over me in the brief shape of a hand, or a lens, or a small bright wing, and knit me shut again, but rough. Always rough. The roughness holds moss. The moss holds water. The water cools the beetles. The glass eels graze the seams between my plates so I don't stiffen and change pitch, nosing silver-gutted through the runoff channels toward the chemical warmth I leak into the sump, and blind sparrows raise their litters, unafraid, in the hot fold where my forelimb meets the wall.

In the worst cycles, when both turbines peak together and the air seems to solidify, everything small shelters in my acoustic shadow. I am the only large quiet the room has.

I almost never hunt. A drain fly crawling across my face with its wings furled tight. A young hammer frog, ambitious, wedging too close and croaking too competitively. Mostly my wide, lipless mouth simply opens when the tide comes — dead motes, beetle larvae, biofilm, mineral sludge, the tower's rich brown-green waste — and lets the place feed its own ear. Because that is what I've become. Not the inhabitant of the chamber so much as its organ. The thing that hears, steadily, for the place built to swallow hearing.

I cannot fit through the hatch. I haven't been able to for longer than most custodians have been alive. My oldest skin has mineralized into the floor at the edges; calcium teeth have grown down over my back; the tower, following an optimization nobody wrote, has concluded that I am load-bearing, and so, in every way that matters here, I am. If I sleep — eye filming over completely, breathing slowed to three rises a minute — the whole room's note goes very slightly flat, and the moss dims, and somewhere two decks up the inhabited quiet aches in its teeth until I wake and adjust one side against the membrane by a centimeter and the roar finds itself again.

It isn't captivity, from inside. Captivity needs an outside, and I don't really have one anymore. I have two meters by a little more than a meter of greenhouse warmth. Water that falls without pause. Food on a schedule older than my body. Mineral growing through me and beetles breathing on me in unison. A sound so total it has replaced silence, weather, and time.

And now and then, through the piezo-ceramic slab, through the polished dark path worn by seventy years of knees: four fingers. A pause. Two fingers flat.

I let a fraction of air out of my left side — not loud, loud has no meaning — and the hanging droplets all complete their fall at once, and I answer.
