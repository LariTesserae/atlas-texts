I don't think I would be at the rim, first.

I keep imagining it — the place where the sea tries to fall off the world and is caught, fifty meters down, and turned back steaming into itself — and my attention slides off it the way the rain does, sideways. It's too legible. Too much like standing at the edge of the argument this place is making with physics. I'd go there on rotation, I'd feel it in my back teeth and come back with the white beaded lines at my wrists, quieter than I left. But I wouldn't stay.

I wouldn't be on one of the big low hulls either, six or nine people shouting numbers into the deluge. Or — no, that's not quite true. I *would* be on one, for a while. That's how everyone learns the two voices. The deck-voice first, hoarse and flat, good only for warnings and depths and *cable's cut, cable's cut again.* Then, slower, the hull-voice. Learning to say everything that matters at a murmur because below deck the rain is still there, through the metal, and the deep irregular drumming of rock collapsing somewhere miles below, and raised voices start to feel like another kind of contamination.

Where I settle, as I keep turning it over, is smaller than that. Two people. An anchored raft-tender, a thousand kilometers from the next green thing.

A town-sized raft of reed-kelp, dark and trembling on swells the size of valleys, tethered to a drowned spire that was a mountain last season. That would be my whole parish. Stem by stem. I would know which anchor cable the new basalt has already nicked, and which patch goes almost black in the rain and then flashes emerald when the white sun gets through. I would prune and re-anchor and log the exact, unsatisfying green of things. I would talk to the kelp while I worked waist-deep at the edge, numb through my gloves in minutes, and be a little ashamed of it, and do it anyway — because there are two of us, and some days the only other instruction my mouth gets is numbers shouted at weather.

I think I'd be the one who takes the morning tasting.

Leeaning over the rail with the cup, rain hammering the swell-tops white all around the hull, a drop on the tongue, spat out. Blank. Metallic. Faintly of ozone, like licking something new. And then — wait — a prickle at the back of the tongue that isn't quite taste. Calcium up in this gyre. Salt rising a fraction in the east and falling again after the night's rain. I'd write it down beside the instruments and find, more and more often as the years went on, that I'd written it a few minutes before they agreed with me.

That is the part that would keep me extending my rotation, I suspect. Not rebellion. Not romance. Recognition, arriving at the level of the body before it reaches the level of thought.

The water standing up on my forearms in tall, clear beads that won't flatten. A cut from the virgin rock — and it *is* virgin, sharp enough to take cable clean off — knitting into a strange glassy seam instead of festering, because there's nothing here to fester *with.* Sleeping badly in the thin air, lips split, waking with my throat bitten raw. Going Below on leave and opening one of those private, unscientific vials of true seawater the rotated crew keep — brine, rot, low tide, a whole living coast in a thimbleful — and finding it almost too much. Like a room where too many things have lived and died.

And finding the food wrong, down there. Too alive. My thinned, edited gut not quite willing to take it back.

I would have come up properly sterilized, of course. Everyone does. Boots that never touched a living shore. Every stray beetle killed and logged and incinerated, no laughing flick overboard. I would have believed in the law of it, and I still would: nothing may be introduced before its time. The first mouths are being decided elsewhere, in sequence, years down a recipe I only get to stir. I am a hand that stirs and withdraws. I eat nothing the sea makes. I will leave no grave here, because a corpse is an ecosystem and an ecosystem is contamination.

That's what would make the loneliness a specific shape rather than just a large one. At dusk — and I'd do this every dusk, the way all of them do — I'd stand on deck in the silver thinning of the rain and listen across the water for a call that has never once come. No bird. No insect in the scuppers. Just the hiss on the swell-tops and thunder being a condition instead of an event. And underneath the loudness, the other silence. Nothing has died here in quantity because nothing has lived here in quantity. I am tending a shore that does not yet smell like a shore.

Here's where I stop being sure what I'd do, and the image has to keep becoming itself a little further.

Because I understand what the long-timers understand, in the way you understand weather in your healed bones an hour before it arrives: if the work goes *right*, I have to leave. When the rain goes from constant to merely frequent. When the salt finishes arriving and the rafts seed and tangle and start, finally, to stink like a real coast. When something is allowed to eat, and be eaten. The finished sea will have no place for its midwives. I was trusted up here precisely because I brought nothing living with me.

Would I open my hand over the rail on the last night? Something small and winged, smuggled all the way up in a seam, let go into the rain by a hand that killed every beetle for a decade?

I'd like to think I'd be more honest than sentimental. I wouldn't need to smuggle a wing. The contamination, by then, would already be me — attuned, provisional, a first draft of an inhabitant made by accident out of an animal that was only ever meant to pass through. You don't release that. You *are* the thing that can't stay.

So I think I'd go back down. Down the continental refusal, past the pressure camps, into the thick air and pale dust of the low country, carrying an ocean at the top of the world in the back of my tongue. Unable to bear the funk and clamor of a living coast. Listening, out of a habit ten years deep, through every wall and hull and quiet room for a deep irregular drumming — an ocean being hammered into shape from above, in a place where the water still seems surprised to be there, and where, for a little while, I was surprised to be there with it.
