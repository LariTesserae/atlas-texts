I thought, at first, that I'd be the one who came in.

On my side through the root-slit, cheek in the damp, shoulders learning the width of it. That is how the place introduces itself: as somewhere you visit. You lie down because you cannot sit up. You hold up a hand to see if your shadow will come. It doesn't. You wait for your eyes to adjust, and they can't, because there is nothing to adjust *to* — the noon isn't stronger in one corner, or kinder in another. It's everywhere, with the sound packed into it like grain in a jar.

But a visitor is an event. A visitor has an arrival, and therefore could have a leaving, and therefore brings time in on his clothes like burrs. This place has no vacancy for that. The crickets I displaced with my shoulder were already back. My footprint smoothed itself out from under me. So I couldn't stay as the intruder. I'd have to be something that had never entered, because it had never been outside.

I tried being one of the crickets, then.

That was easy to imagine. Shoulder to shoulder along the seam, antennae woven over the dweller's fingers, *included*, not trapped. Making the upper, tearing note of the chord and never once having invented it. Eating the film and being eaten back into film. No solo. No answer because the answer is continuous. It suited me — the temptation to give up progression and just be texture. But the seam is already full. The thatch is already woven. One more cricket would be an overflow, and nothing here overflows. I would have to push another cricket out of existence to take its place, and nothing here dies, either. I couldn't be added.

I tried being a moth on the ceiling, then. One of the powdery ones making the ceiling into a second, living ceiling. Wings never opening, never closing, just trembling at the exact rate forever, eating light or shed scales or nothing visible. Looking down at the dweller's brow, where others of me were settled just as densely, until the boundary between its head and the roof of the world went soft. That was close, too. A cycle squeezed so tight it becomes a surface. Effort disguised as stillness. But the moths already make a complete ceiling. There's no gap in them for my wings.

The frogs pressed cheek to cheek in the damp corner — no. The salamander lying across the dweller's ankle, wearing its translucency as camouflage, visible only by the dark thread of gut — no. The woodlice in a row like grey beads down its spine, the springtails stirring in its creases so that its hip and the floor beside its hip seem to be made of the same granular matter — no, no. Every form I reached for was already occupied, already holding its precise note in the chord. The beetles were ticking wetly in the floor. The earwig was in the shell of its ear, in the only privacy available, and did not want a neighbour.

So I went, the way your attention goes when you're lying on your back in a space too low to sit up in, upward.

There are beads of condensation on the low ceiling. Perfect hanging spheres that do not fall. In the floor there's a saucer of water so still it looks solid, but up here is its opposite: water that should be in motion, arrested on the point of it. The moths crowd around, not drinking. The sparse moss holds its translucent leaves up through the light. And one bead — I didn't choose it so much as find my looking had been happening from inside it for a while — hangs directly over the dweller's face.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be.

Not the dweller. I don't have its terrible stamina for centrality. Not the knot in the chord, not the one who balanced pea on pea on pea by touch in a brightness that makes sight useless, tongue held on a single vowel between limestone teeth. I would be the smaller, dumber version of the same refusal, out at the edge of its gaze: a droplet that has been about to fall for the entire noon of this place.

From inside a bead, the room should turn upside down and shrink into a lens. It doesn't. The light has no source and no direction, so there is no image to invert, only brightness held in a curve, brightness coming off the wet air, off the packed crickets, off the dweller's open eyes below me, equally. I am lit all the way through. There is no dark side of me turned toward the stone. I have a skin only in the sense that I maintain one — a surface, holding. Holding is the whole of my work, the way holding one vowel is the whole of its. My jaw is surface tension. My eyelids are surface tension. If there are muscles in a drop of water, they never relax.

Because I understand, up here, what would happen if I let go. It isn't much. That is the horror of it. A fall of four feet, less. A cool strike on a cheek already pressed to a cool floor, or on an eye that has not closed since this moment began. And then — a blink. A fraction of night. The first shadow with an actual cause. The chord would have a gap in it, just wide enough for time to put a foot down. A pebble might topple. The droplet has been prevented from being weather, from being rain, from being a tear the place could shed for itself; I've been kept spherical so that nothing down there has to react.

So I don't swell. There is always more damp coming through the pale, dentable rock, always more condensation gathering, but I never get heavier. Or I do, and I am somehow taken back, molecule by molecule, eaten by the moth-hum, dried by light that never warms, returned to the exact size at which falling remains imminent and impossible. My cycle has been squeezed, like the moth wings, like the dweller's breath arrested halfway between in and out, until the cycle stops being time and becomes a shape. A sphere. Hanging.

I know everything below me completely, and without event. I know the frog in the dweller's armpit and the one against its cheek. I know the loose, upturned hand still arranged around having stacked the cairn, not touching it — nearness without contact being the most relation anything here can afford — because I am in the same relation to the dweller's eye. I am always just above it. I am the closest thing it has to weather, and I never arrive.

It does not look at me. Looking *at* would imply it had been looking elsewhere before, and there is no before in its gaze. But I live in the upper part of what it sees, the way the moths on its brow live at the edge of its seeing, and perhaps that is a kind of being known. Not worshipped, not avoided, not used as anything but another cool surface the room rests against. The moss counts its leaves nearby. The moths tremble around my curve and resume the exact rate of their trembling. A springtail's worth of dust hangs suspended in the bright air beside me and does not settle on me, because settling would be the beginning of accumulation, and accumulation would be the beginning of weight, and weight would be the beginning of an ending.

I am not peaceful up here. A sphere this patient is a clenched thing. But I am well-placed. If the dweller is the one living thing in which this unfinished, blinding, deafening moment has learned to keep its eyes open, then I am the small, clear thing that helps it: the fall that stays ready, and stays, and stays, so that it never has to close them.
