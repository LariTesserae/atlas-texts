I wouldn't be found the way people below the clouds are found. Not in a house. Not in one place, holding still long enough for you to point.

You'd have to stay, first. You'd have to let the tides stop being an inconvenience to your breathing. I would be the reason it healed.

At first, though, if you met me early, you would meet me walking.

Down among the round ruins, where the floor is soft stone with the texture of wet clay. I go upright because my body still believes in upright. Because the old roads still believe in roads, and the sediment there falls sideways in brownish streams along a current that died before the sea was born, and my splay toes can find it. I place each foot as if the water might suddenly remember it was air. It never does. That is not why I'm careful.

I'm careful because I am learning the floor with my skin.

There are cracks down there where the earth breathes heat. I press my palms to them and drink through them — not water, warmth. Minerals. A dark flushing rises into the fans along my back, those thin, fretted fans that aren't fins and aren't wings, and for a while my whole spine tastes of iron and sulfur and deep stone. The plants close when the clear, cold fronts come through. I close, too. We are practicing being the same kind of thing.

My eyes are covered. That sounds like blindness if you come from a world of sharp edges, but there is very little up here that needs edges to be known. I know the stilt-forest by the brush of fronds against my fans. I know the causeway by the sudden straightness underfoot, fifty miles of intention still refusing the general softening. I know when a meadow is passing overhead, provinces of rootless kelp flowering as they travel, because the clear bells hung underneath it ring not in my ears but in my sternum, pressure answering pressure.

I eat what rains inside the sea. Pale seeds that have taken years to fall this far, ticking faintly against my skin on their way down. Pollen. Soft fruit loosened from the moving fields. Things that were meant for somewhere else and never arrived.

At night — only a thicker dusk, only the plants beginning their uneven glow — I sleep standing in the forest corridors with one hand curled around a trunk. While I sleep, rootlets grow from my heels into the floor and from my fingertips into the bark. This is not an accident and it is not a trap. It is how the forest learns my name, which is not a name, and how I learn its. Waking takes a long time because waking is a tearing, small and deliberate, along a hundred fine threads. I always leave a little of myself behind in the wood. The wood always leaves a little of itself in me.

For years I think this is simply what living is: to walk the drowned roads and be slowly annotated by everything you touch.

Then the air begins to stay in me.

That is how the second life announces itself — not as a decision, but as a physics I can no longer argue with. Air gathers in the hollow chambers under my ribs. It gathers in my spongy throat, in the folded fans I open to the slanting columns of sun. One morning I let go of the trunk I slept against and I do not quite sink. I put my foot down toward the stones I have memorized and the water, kindly and without ceremony, declines to give them back to me.

If you have ever been young in any world, you know this exact feeling. The bottom stops being yours.

So then I would be the other thing you might see, once in a summer, once in a childhood, at the edge of visibility.

A pale, long-necked shape drifting through the perpetual late afternoon, fans spread in all directions like a peacock who has forgotten what display is for and kept the beauty anyway. You would not see me flee. Flight requires a belief in pursuit, and pursuit requires edges, and mine have been softening for decades. The vegetation parts for me — not in reverence, exactly, but in recognition. A seed recognizes warmth. In my wake, where my passing raises the water by a fraction of a degree, seeds burst open all at once, a slow green punctuation along the current.

I sing, in that life. You would not hear it as sound. Sound up here is eaten within feet, becomes only a tremor in your teeth. Singing for us is shaping a breath into a warm cloud and letting go of it correctly, so that the vibration travels bell to bell under the travelling meadows, meadow to meadow, field lighting field in sequence across the blue-black deeps. A sentence can take a season. I have said things as a drifter that I began as a walker; by the time the last field pulsed with them, far away in the dark, I no longer remembered beginning them. That is not loss. That is grammar, at our scale.

I do not remember walking the way you would want me to remember it. I carry it, which is different. I carry it in the mineral rings laid down in my bones from the vents I pressed my hands to. I carry it in the green along my shoulders where ten thousand dim afternoons taught my skin to eat light. The sea smooths memory the way it smooths footprints in the clay floor and names written in tower dust: not by destroying them, but by widening them until they become part of the general shape of things.

And then, very late, if you stayed — impossibly late, by your counting — you would find me again in a place you had already swum through without knowing.

At the rim, in the shallows where the whole sea inhales twice a day and rises hundreds of feet to swallow the stilt-forests, my rootlets would one season take hold of a root-mat with a new seriousness. My neck, which had stiffened by degrees I mistook for rest, would become a reasonable shape for a trunk. The fans that caught the sun would go on catching it, wider, slower, until catching it and *being* a frond were the same gesture. The green would spread. That is all.

Some of the trees there grow down from the rafts and up from the floor until they meet in the middle and confuse themselves. I think I would like to be one of those: my rooted old age descending through the warm dusk, my walking youth still rising in the memory of the wood, meeting myself halfway down a corridor exactly the width of a drifting body. If you swim through it, the leaves will brush you and close behind you gently, like a throat. That closing is me. So is the corridor.

Rooted, I would finally understand the work. At night my roots go questing quietly through the clay and find the day's sharpnesses — your handprint, held for an hour; the scar where a stone fell; a broken frond — and I press them, patiently, back toward unmarked. If you climb into the dry bell-chamber of the tower and write your name in the dust that hasn't been wet in ten thousand years, I am the fine root-tip that comes up, eventually, through a crack in the stone. I will read it the only way I still can: by touch, by tasting the disturbance it makes. Letter by letter. In order. And then I will soften it away.

Please don't mistake that for rejection. From inside a tree, you learn what remembering costs — how a sharp thing held too long cuts the hand that holds it, even when the hand is made of water and time. I am not erasing you. I am returning you to a size the sea can carry.

In the very end, which mostly means deciding over fifty years to stop, my trunk would go hollow and fill with that airy, breathable upper water, warm as bathwater, tasting of minerals and leaf-rot. And in the dark of it, small pale things that have been gestating like seeds in a fruit would push their heads up through the still surface inside me, and take a first breath, and sink, and walk away along the old roads toward the ruins — opening fans they don't yet know how to use, placing their narrow feet with terrible care in the ghost of a riverbed.

So if you come looking: don't look for a village, a harbor, a grave.

Look for a footprint healing a moment too fast. Look for a forest corridor that fits someone who is no longer there. Look for the light going field to field across the deep at night like a thought crossing a mind, and know that somewhere in the middle of it a very old animal is becoming a place, tenderly, on purpose, over centuries —

and that the place, when you rest against it half-submerged and exhausted, will hold you the way the upper water does.

Like a hand. Like something that has decided, moment by moment, not to let you drown.
