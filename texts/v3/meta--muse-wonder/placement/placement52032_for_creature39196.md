From below I keep trying to place myself too high.

My first instinct is the sky, because the sky is what you notice first coming up the lift — the swifts screaming through the smoke, the pigeons going up off a roof all at once like a page turned, the starlings shading an intersection at dusk. I imagine myself swift-shaped, living in a chimney joint, arriving gradually over weeks in spring, part of that thickening of the air. It doesn't hold. A swift is all arrival and appetite and speed. I would be bad at the speed. I'd miss the ledge. I'd be the dead one a loft-keeper clears one-handed out of a vent, without looking, while arguing about roof rights.

I try lower. A pigeon, maybe, which is more my pace — steady, cooing, safe, the warmth through a tenement wall in winter that a child learns before it learns words. To be census and furniture and food and fertilizer, to circle twice over steaming canal water and settle again exactly where my ancestors settled. There's comfort in that. But I don't settle that well. I'm too alert to the settling. I'd be the one on the ledge with its head up while the others are adjusting their wings, listening for what changed.

A goat on the parapets, cropping lichen, sure-footed over white-crusted roofs — no. A frog in the drains, the one protected animal, uneaten out of law and disgust and gratitude, calling after rain from every grate — I like the gratitude, and I like being left alone on purpose, but I don't want to be down in the runoff, however honest it is. A house spider in an alley mouth — that one stays with me longer than I expect. To weave the grey veil overnight knowing the morning sweeper will cut it with a long pole as a matter of routine, and to weave it again the next night without resentment, because overnight weaving is simply what the alley is for. To be tolerated, not loved, until morning. That is close to something in me.

But I think I keep coming back indoors, and backwards, into the building itself.

I'd be born three rooms in.

One of the interior people — damp cellar pallor, greyish, hair bleached at the forelock though I never worked the drying terraces long enough to earn it properly. A windowless childhood in the dark core of a rookery tower, one wall warmed by a foundry flue and the other, in winter, warmed in patches by roosting birds. I would learn the city first not by seeing it but by its pressure in the brick. I'd be the child who slept through ordinary screaming and woke at the wrong kind of quiet — who could tell, before speaking, the difference between pigeon warmth and a wall gone cold because a colony had been lost. The adults notice that kind of thing early up there, because a wrong placement wastes a lifetime. So they wouldn't waste mine on the parapets or the barges. They'd give me to the masons, and then, when my palms never hardened properly and I kept stopping mid-task with my ear turned, they'd give me to the courts.

I would become a cavity-taker. A census person for holes.

It's unglamorous work and it takes forever, which suits the place and suits me. Before any facade can be sealed, before a roof can be re-done outside of nesting — and it is always, somewhere on the face of a building, nesting — someone has to go up and down the skin of the place tapping, listening, counting what lives there. Not estimating. Counting. This nesting hole occupied, this one cold for two seasons, this ledge owed to a colony of swifts that died out and were replaced by their descendants so gradually nobody signed anything. I would carry chalk that rains off by afternoon and a loft book that outlasts the chalk, and I would mark what must be rebuilt *before* it can be destroyed, because that is the law and also the whole morality: you cannot seal a cavity without providing its equivalent. The chimney after its rookery. The birds first.

I'd develop the hands for it slowly — not a mason's horn palms, but a keeper's gentleness in the fingers. Crosshatched forearms. The ability to clear a blocked vent, to feel through brick for the faint difference between rot and a sleeping bat, the way other people check a door is locked. People passing me in a stairwell would tap the wall beside my head the way you'd greet someone, and I'd answer by tapping back, out of habit, listening for cavity, for nesting, for where the warmth had moved.

And I'd talk the way everyone talks up there, which would finally feel natural rather than slow. In short phrases, timed between foundry thumps. Pausing mid-sentence — without awkwardness, without apology — while a flock went over or a chimney hit the pitch that makes talk pointless, doing something else with my hands, and resuming twenty seconds later exactly where I left off. Lowlanders would think I'd forgotten the thread. I hadn't forgotten anything. Remembering the thread across interruptions would be, essentially, my entire trade. I'd take up lawsuits begun by women in their marrying years and hand the findings to their daughters. I'd add names to loft books in a hand I learned from a keeper who learned it from a keeper. Marriages would merge in the margins: this roof access, that obligation, these ledges, those birds.

Where would you find me, late in life? A little deaf in one ear — the tram side, the swift side, sixty years turned toward the screaming. Eyes poor for open distance. Probably never more than a district or two from the tower I was born in, because why would I be. In the late afternoon, when the plateau makes its own white cloud and the rain falls only inside the rim, I'd do what everything does: come out. Onto a parapet, among the pigeons, in the steam coming off the cisterns, shaking water off, resettling. Indistinguishable at a distance from any other animal crowding the wet stone. Then I'd go back inside through a doorway I have to duck for — everyone ducks, the nests above the lintel were never worth a raised door — and put my palm, on my way to bed, against the interior wall.

Warm in patches. Occupied. Not mine, exactly, and never empty.

I'd fall asleep the discriminating way, listening not for the noise, but for the change in it.
