At first I thought I would be one of the ptarmigan.

It seemed the honest place for a newcomer: in a round niche let into a house wall, at the height of a person's shoulder, with my feet tucked under me and my round eye open. Not working. Not speaking. Simply fitted, at last, into a space that had been left for my shape before I arrived, and learning how long I could watch the terraces without needing to interrupt them.

I stayed long enough to know that wasn't it. The ptarmigan do not watch the way I watch. There is no curiosity left in them to satisfy, no inventory being made behind their eyes. They are not taking the bowl in. They are part of the wall taking the bowl in. I was still keeping a list.

I thought, then, that I might be a child on the hoists, because children are allowed the nearest thing to speed this place permits. I imagined the soft click of my tongue carrying one terrace down, a basket riding beside me — turnips, wet laundry, a pot of horsetails with the soil still trembling — my weight welcomed on the rope bridges, everywhere and underfoot and never once tripped over. But I am not quick in that way, and I am not learning stillness fast enough to be young here. My hands kept wanting to finish things. In the bowl, nothing is finished. A basket arrives and is emptied and is already, in someone else's hands, filling again.

I tried myself out, for a while, among the gardeners in middle life. I liked the justice of it: to move, not remove, a fern that had seeded itself in the wrong pot, to carry it cupped in its own soil down two terraces to the shaded place under the hoist where ferns belong. I liked knowing a pea plant individually. But my attention kept sliding away from the growing things toward the things that were barely happening at all — toward the settlement of a stone by a hair's breadth in a season, toward the exact circling of the sun at which frost melted off a leaf edge and re-formed while you watched, toward the pale fish turning in the cistern and the water boatmen writing nothing on the surface and erasing it.

The old noticed before I did. That is their work, after all. The old tend slow things.

So now, if you came down through the slot in the boulders — duck, mind the steps hollowed in the middle — and you walked the terraces looking for me, you would not find me at the center, though I pass the center often. You would have to look the way people here look for anything: not across, but near. Third terrace down from the rim, on the shaded side that is not truly shaded, where the condensation gathers on the cold outer faces of the stones. There is a groove cut fingertip-wide into the masonry, dark with damp, and a clay pipe, and below the ice slabs one of the bell-shaped lids of beaten bronze, green with verdigris, hanging clapperless to catch the drip and send it silently down a length of twine into a jar.

That round is mine. The drip-bells, the twine, the grooves.

It is slower than gardening and less visible than building, and it suits the shape I've worn into myself here. I go from bell to bell with a cloth and a small bone pick. I clear the groove where lichen has begun, lovingly and with complete indifference to my intentions, to drink the water meant for the cistern below. I feel along the twine for the stiff place where frost has entered the fibers. I lift the bronze lid — never letting it strike anything, for it has no clapper and is not meant to acquire a voice — and wipe the green from its lip so the bead forms clean and falls true. On the brightest circlings I stop at the black stone in the market court and warm my hands a moment in the lens-light like everyone else, and soften a little resin, and pass it on to younger hands that have a use for it.

People have learned what my round is good for besides water. Because I pass every cistern, I pass every cluster of households that shares one. Because a voice will not carry across the bowl and should not try, things travel the way water does here: by small channels, patiently, hand to hand. So as I work, someone will come close — under a foot, breath warm against my ear — and tell me that the pipe above the granary ledge is knocking faintly against its stone, or that the woman two terraces up who keeps the spherical bushes has more pea seedlings than her strip can hold, or that an old man in a wall-niche has not been seen to blink in a while and someone should sit with him. I carry it the way I carry everything: without adding volume to it. Three terraces later I bend to another ear. My whole conversation is conducted in the space between a mouth and the side of a face, and nothing I say has ever startled a swallow.

I am not tall anymore, or if I am, I have forgotten how to be. My shoulders have learned the sideways turn at the woodpile and the loom frame as a single motion, timber and body negotiating the burrow together. My felt soles make no announcement. There is sulphur-colored lichen at my left elbow where I lean, always in the same place, to reach the bell under the third slab, and a bone-white rosette at the shoulder that brushes the limewashed wall on the stair. The children accommodate me without looking, the way they accommodate a sleeping dog. The goats pick past me on the roof paths in their hoof-covers. Once, an enormous furred bumblebee blundered toward my face on my own stair, and I stopped mid-step and mid-breath and lowered my eyes, and felt the furred air of it pass my cheek, and understood — not as a thought, as a place in my spine — that I had been given right-of-way by the bowl exactly as I had given it: completely, and without ceremony.

I sleep the way the rest of them sleep, in a snatch, behind a felt curtain in an alcove that smells of wool and cold stone, with shaped covers over my eyes that do not make darkness so much as announce it. I dream in light, now. Nothing shouts in my dreams. When I wake, two hours or five minutes later — the frost on the leaf edge has melted and re-formed, that is the clock — someone else's hands have taken up the cloth where I left it, and mine take up a basket that was already on its way somewhere, and the round continues through me rather than beginning with me. That used to frighten me. It is the most restful thing I know.

If you stay, you will begin to see me everywhere, which is to say you will stop seeing me at all. A felted shape stirring beside a cistern. A hand, nails black-edged, testing a twine. A pause on a stair while a bee decides. I am one of the negotiated occupants: the cats have the granary ledges, the beetles have the compost vaults and are thanked in peelings, the swallows have the undersides of the bridges, and I have the narrow, damp seam between the ice above and the jars below — the place where the whole bright, precarious balance quietly becomes drinkable.

It is, I have come to think, the best niche in the bowl. Everything here depends on a degree or two held without fluctuation: too little and the gardens blacken, too much and the rim begins to weep. My work is that weeping, caught one bead at a time and led away in silence, so that it never becomes a fall.

Come close if you want to speak to me. Closer than that. There — that distance. I can hear you now.
