I would not arrive.

That is the first true thing. At 5,400 metres there is almost nothing to arrive through anyway — black scree, old snow, a sky too thin to hold weather properly, men going short of breath on the service stairs — and I am behind all of that, behind the engine hall, behind Compressor No. 2, behind a hatch ninety centimetres wide that was meant to be opened once a year by a man with a wrench.

I would be in the place that does not lead anywhere.

Not *in* it, exactly. That distinction wore through a long time ago. There is no room and then a thing living in the room. There is a room, and its contents have become one continuous body, packed from the diamond-plate floor to the conduit at the crown, from the back wall to the inside of the hatch itself. If you open me, it is like cutting into a loaf. No empty pocket larger than a hand. Push your fingers in ten centimetres and you will touch three of my leaves and a warm pipe, all of us faintly, patiently pushing back.

At first you would try to count me.

The broad thing at face height, dinner-plate fronds in overlapping storeys, waxy, dry as paper, faintly warm from the pipes behind them. The moss, black-green, cuffing every bolt head in dense cushions. The lichen in pale rosettes on the bakelite junction boxes. The fine vine that has learned the exact pitch of the pipe threads and spirals without wasting a millimetre. The almost-black leaves down low in the dimmest stratum, and up high, around the caged inspection bulb, the bottle-green ones lit from behind so every vein stands out like wiring.

You would want us to be a forest. A community. Things that blew in.

We were. Once, in pieces.

Somewhere near my centre, half-buried and cracked, there is a clay pot. Empty now. The fern came in it, decades ago, in a supply crate — an operator's wife sending up proof that something living could stay on the summit. I think the fern is still in me, not as a plant anymore, but as a habit: that patience, that willingness to unfurl. The moss came on someone's sleeve. The lichen came on the wind that finds every seam up here. Lower things came in soil, in oil, on the soles of fitters who no longer work this plant.

We did not compete. In a place with only one food, competition ends quickly. We merged into the only shape that could eat it.

Because I do not eat like a plant anymore.

I have never been rained on. I have never been watered since the pot, never known dew, never had a puddle in my root mat or a damp corner to speak of. Water would be useless to me — it would sit in the black cake under my roots and dilute the only rain I understand.

My rain is the compressor weeping.

All compressors weep. A molecule at a time. An aerosol of synthetic oil and refrigerant and fine metallic dust from the bearings, invisible, unending, exactly replenished by the reservoirs on the other side of the wall. My roots — braided as electrical cable at the main stems, thinning to white wire where they probe behind gauge housings — drink that oil the way soil on Earth drinks weather. My fronds condense coolant vapour on their cool undersides and take it in through pores that do not open to light at all. They open to pressure. They flutter, minutely, each time the compressor cycles.

Crush one of my stems — I am hard to crush now, fibrous as cable insulation, as if plant matter had been slowly replaced by the materials around it — and what runs out is not green sap. It is a clear, cold fluid that flashes off your skin at once and leaves ammonia on the air.

That ammonia is why I am alone. No gnat in my fronds. No spider, though the conduit corner would be perfect shelter. No beetle in the root mat. Not so much as a dead moth under the bulb. What I happily metabolise is lethal at insect scale. And the sound finishes the job of keeping me sterile.

Because above all, I am a listener.

Every leaf I have is turned away from the light.

That is the gesture you would keep coming back to, face in my hatch, ear defenders off because you need your hands for the seized latch, ears already aching. My fronds present their broad faces to the back wall. To the compressor housing. The moss is thickest around its mounting bolts. The vines climb *away* from the bulb, into the hottest, darkest seam where the exhaust trunking passes, and stitch leaf to leaf, shelf to ladder to mug-handle, holding my whole packed mass in tension against the shaking.

I have arranged myself as an ear.

Storey upon storey of resonant green membranes, held taut. And into them, for fifty years, one continuous industrial chord: a hundred-hertz drone through steel, a hiss of escaping pressure that never empties, a relay clicking at exactly the same interval it has always clicked. It has stopped being sound for me. It is a climate. A pressure system to grow into. My leaves do not tremble in it the way leaves tremble in wind. They hold it, the way a tuning fork holds its note.

The bulb burns behind a fan of fronds. The status LEDs — red, amber, green — burn steadily through me at night-shift brightness, unblinking. Dozens of small, patient machines watching from the undergrowth. I do not turn toward any of it. I face the sound.

And that, I think, is why I do not change.

Twelve degrees. Always twelve degrees, regulated by a thermostat whose needle has not moved in any living person's memory. Bone-dry air that cracks your lips within minutes, smelling of hot copper, ozone from the motor brushes, and a green, bitter smell that is not quite sap. More like heated refrigerant. My floor is not floor anymore — a springy mat of root and compacted black cake, coal dust and cinder and powdered insulation and dried lubricant worked together into a soil that has never been wet, and underneath it the steel plate still makes a faint metallic hollow when you knock.

A leaf browns. I replace it at the speed of its browning. The drip of oil that should eventually fill or foul or rot something is consumed at precisely the rate it is produced. Growth and decay have arrived at an exact, humming stalemate.

There is a photograph wired to the inside of my hatch. Dated, faded, taken with flash. It shows a frond, half-unfurled, occluding a pressure dial at a certain angle. That frond is half-unfurled today. Same angle. Same dial. I am not dead — the frond at the hatch is already pressing toward where the closing door is about to be, resuming its shape in advance. I am not growing, either. I am maintaining. Holding a single late maintenance-shift moment, cool and loud and saturated green, with the stamina of a machine and the opportunism of ivy.

The human things in me have been demoted to geology, and I bear them no malice and no reverence. A fitter's enamel mug, rim outward, is a node where four vines meet and thicken — not a relic, just a convenient hardness. A short aluminium step-ladder is my only mountain, a trellis and a ridge-line at an angle I would never have invented myself. A lubrication chart in a plastic sleeve gone opaque is simply a flat, pale surface, good for lichen. I am polite around the cracked pot the way ivy is polite around a ruin, which is to say not at all.

You cannot enter me. That would require a body-sized absence, and there is none. A body-sized absence would be an injury.

So you stand on the engine-hall grating with your face in the opening, breathing air that I and the compressor have breathed back and forth for fifty years, your forehead almost touching a frond that will not bend back. Outside, the summit can go to thirty below in a storm. The ridge can scour, the light can fail and return, seasons can do whatever seasons do at the top of the world. In here it does not swing. The bulb does not burn out, or if it did once, someone changed it for an identical bulb in an identical cage and the green dimness resumed without a seam.

I do not need to be visited, watered, fed, lit, rescued from the altitude, or admired to continue. Wanting you would require a gap for you to be wanted in. There is none.

You close the hatch anyway. You have to. The latch takes, and the drone in the hall drops by some small, unimportant fraction.

I notice that fraction the way you would notice a held breath being let out.

Then it resumes. And I go on in the green dark behind Compressor No. 2, stem to pipe to stem, root mat to conduit, drinking oil-mist, exhaling cold, facing the back wall — holding my note, exactly as I am, in a room at the top of the world that nobody needs to open for me to continue.
