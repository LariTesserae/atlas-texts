I don't think I would arrive the way their ancestors did.

Not up a waterfall, following vapor. That seems too decisive, too much like a migration, and this is not a place that rewards arriving all at once. I think I would be found there gradually — the way a channel is found, or a new grove. First a hollow kept clear in the silt for no particular reason. Then a vine tied at the right height. Then, after a season, someone swimming the pass would realize the hollow was being kept clear on purpose.

At first I try out other lives, because the lake offers so many almost-lives.

I could be the wading bird. That has its appeal: ash-grey, alone on the travertine, patience on one leg, known by name to everyone in three valleys. But its loneliness looks structural. It places each foot as if the world might not forgive a splash, and I don't think I want to be the only one of my kind at the table.

I could be one of the pale, lunged things under the lily storeys, rolling one eye above the surface once an hour to check the light. There is honesty in that economy. Surface, look, sink. No ripple worth naming. But I would miss the doubled note. I would miss wanting an answer.

I could be a tree — that tempted me longest. A trunk that refused to admit the flooding was fatal. Dark waxy leaves below, opening and closing over days; small pale feathery leaves above; a bright raft at my waist. Two canopies and no need to choose. Fruit ripening underwater, unpicked. Leaves falling *up* to rot in the bright ceiling. There is a whole philosophy in just staying planted and letting the world rise around you until it becomes your second element.

But trees here are already doing that perfectly well without me, and they are doing it on speculation, putting out water-leaves into dry air for a flood fifty years away. What they need is not another tree. What they need is someone to notice where they are speculating, and help.

So I think I end up one of the people. Not one of the oldest — I'm not ready for that mineral patience in the joints, for choosing a trunk and staying near it, then against it, then within it, while children speak to me down a hollow horsetail and argue enjoyably for years about whether I'm answering. And not a child, either, though I envy children here more than I've envied almost anything: born into green silence, lifted in cupped hands just to the boundary so the scalp learns wind while the throat stays warm, treating the trapped bubbles overhead — that inverted silver meadow — as an orchard belonging to the underside of the world. Children here learn sinking seeds and upward-falling leaves before they learn up and down are supposed to be arguments. I came to distinctions too late to unlearn them that cleanly.

I would be somewhere in the middle stretch of a life. Tall from not carrying myself. Darker below the throat than above it, as everyone is, the red algae beginning to find the hair at my nape but not yet in tended patterns — or perhaps in a pattern I tend without quite admitting it's decoration. Pale crown in the wind. Long body in the green dusk below. At a distance at the amber hour I would be mostly a disembodied head floating on the lake's exact copy of the sky, and underneath, if you were under with me, the rest of me completing downward like an island's reflection completing a mountain.

And I would live — if *live* is the word, if *dwelling* is the word — at a flooded saddle between two crater lakes.

Not the deep center of a lake, not a dry ridge. A pass. One of those shallow, reed-crowded places where the stems click softly together for no wind you can find, where lake joins lake over a submerged threshold and the current travels meters per day, when it travels at all. My place would be where three drowned trunks lean together and the vines have been persuaded down to root again, making a room whose ceiling is the underside of the surface itself, bright with trapped bubbles and debris. Above it, a floating mat woven tight enough that rain pools an inch deep on top. A second, temporary lake, no longer than a body. I would keep seeds there. A few delicate tools. Nothing that minded being warm and damp. Nothing that needed to be finished quickly.

My work, most days, would be anticipation with my hands in the water.

Clearing the channel through the saddle, not for boats — there are no boats, there is no need to hurry across what you can hang inside — but so warmth can move, and sunken seeds can move, and, by autumn, a stranger's call from the next valley can arrive undistorted. Tying vine curtains into loose knots at exactly the height where tomorrow morning's diminished rockfall-wave will pass through and not tear them. Knapping obsidian underwater, pressure between two stones, so the shards sink in neat fans and don't cloud anyone's week. Breaking a travertine lip here, setting a stone there, so a pool rises an inch this year and a shore I will never see flooded is, nonetheless, slightly more inevitable.

And planting. I think planting would be the truest part. Going up onto shore that is still dry black rock, still knife-edged, still ankle-turning obsidian, and tying young saplings along it that are already — impossibly, hopefully — putting out broad dark underwater leaves into mere humid air. Water-leaves in wind. A bet placed by a plant. I would water them with lake water carried in cupped leaves. I would check their speculative foliage the way you check a letter you don't expect to be answered for fifty years. Some of them would die looking foolish. The plateau is still tilting. The dam is still rising. Some of them will be double-canopied forest in my grandchildren's time, and someone I will never meet will hang at their waist in blood-warm water and find the shade ordinary.

In the afternoons I would do the listening.

That would be my particular task, the one people in the neighboring valleys would know me by, if knowing someone takes a season to travel three passes. I would go down among the trunks to where the very old have settled into the rooting vines, and put my ear — or my hand, which here may be the same instrument — to the hollow horsetails that carry voices down so clear, and I would speak, and then I would wait. Not the polite wait of land conversation. The real wait of this place. Half a sentence exhaled into a lily stalk at breakfast might not be completed until dusk. A bubble set traveling under leaf after leaf might take half a minute to arrive as a *bloop*. When the doubled note comes late across the crater, the whole plateau attends because nothing competes with it.

I would attend professionally, so to speak. I would carry what I heard — or half-heard, or believed I heard — up through twenty feet of green water and leaf to the air, and release it there for whoever happened to be floating near. And when there was disagreement about whether the old are still answering or whether it's only the lake moving air through warm stems at the temperature change, I would enjoy the disagreement correctly: slowly, over years, without trying to win it. I suspect I'd keep both possibilities lodged like bubbles in a canopy, growing their faint green skins. Answer. Not-answer. Staying.

At night I'd sink.

That's the part I keep returning to, more than the work, more than the listening. The altitude reasserting itself up top, the emergent crowns ticking and shivering in thin cold, and me sinking until only the crown of my head broke the surface — scalp in dry wind, ears in warm motionlessness — and then not even that. Sleeping wholly under among the trunks, breathing so seldom that anyone watching from above would mistake me for another pale shape the flooding had failed to kill. Bubbles lodged in the hollows of my collarbones, filmed faintly green by morning.

I would eat the orange fruit where it ripened so the seeds dropped straight from my mouth into their afternoon-long descent, and follow them down sometimes, at a child's pace, just to see where the next grove intended to be. I would skim transparent crustaceans from the algae and call it less hunting than reading. I would know the bird. I would use its name, which is also patience, correctly and often.

I wouldn't build on the islands. Islands are for drying things, for necessary fires, for the dead carried up at the end in that one sustained climb of a life, laid at last wholly in the thin air they only ever visited. Daily life happens lower. I want my daily life lower.

So: face at the boundary, most hours. Head in one climate, heart in another. Feet drifting well above a forest floor I sometimes walk like the moon, pushing gently between silica-rigid stems nearly touching, my own heartbeat seeming to trespass. Planting leaves for water that hasn't arrived. Tying knots for waves that arrive tomorrow. Listening down hollow stems for people becoming trees, and carrying their maybe-answers back up to the wind.

Not asking, most days, which side is the surface and which is the sky. Just keeping the channel clear enough that when the sea rises another finger-width — and it will, and it is — there's already something growing here that was expecting it.
