I keep thinking I would start at the rim.

Not out of any love for the wind — I don't think I could love that thin, clean-aching air that takes a breath and thins it to nothing — but because it's the last place that makes sense. Orange lichen. Sulfur. The thumb-sized moss campions with their absurd pink flowers wedged where the melt can reach them. Light can be trusted up there. You can see the other summits. You know which way is out.

But I wouldn't stay with the snow finches for long. They are too close to the weather in me. White-bellied, grey-brown, endlessly shifting, erupting from the horizontal cracks all at once in a hysterical synchronized burst and then settling a second later as if I hadn't just startled at nothing. One bird's tail brushing another's chicks. God, I know that density. I know that nervousness that pretends to be community because the nests are simply crammed too close to tell.

A little deeper, then. Where the shade begins to hold.

The accentors would suit me better for a while. Mouse-colored, dunnock-quiet, fine streaking at the throat that you only see if you're near enough. Hopping rather than flying in the confined places. Picking seeds and frozen insects from ledges. I could do that unremarkable work. And the rosy-finches beside them, carrying that improbable wash of pink over breast and rump, like color remembered from a lower, warmer world — that feels familiar too, keeping a softness you have no practical use for anymore.

But I'm not built for the air of this place, and the swifts make that cruelly clear.

They own it. Dark sickles, almost no feet, made entirely for a life that does not land, screaming in and out of the slot and pulling turns inside the stone and missing the overhangs by centimetres. Reading the walls by echo as much as by sight. One bird made three by the throat-shape of the rock. I envy them that certainty — to return not just to the crack but to the exact black seam, the same hand-length of darkness your grandparents used, and rebuild it out of whatever the wind has given you. Grass. Wool torn off animals kilometers below. Your own saliva for glue.

I don't have that fidelity of return. And I land too heavily. I would move suddenly, and the whole air would explode upward in a single shrieking vortex, and then need an hour to forgive me, seam by seam, web by web.

So lower. Where the light fails and stays failed.

I linger, improbably, with the bats. They shouldn't be there either. Too high, too cold, nights too thin. Small brown bodies packed head-down in cracks no wider than a hand, fur beaded with condensation, wings folded like umbrellas of skin. Rearranging all day for warmth, muttering at the edge of hearing. There's something I recognize in needing to drop before you can fly. In skimming the black pool at dusk without ever touching it.

But packing myself in for warmth among thousands — no. I'd learn their chitter and still feel like an outsider pressed into the seam.

Lower still, and the place stops asking me to be quick, or social, or seen.

This is where my eyes would finally adjust. Not to empty dark, but to that blue-grey gloom populated by movement. Pale spiders picking methodically through the moth fall in sheet-webs sagging under wings. Harvestmen on stilts over damp walls. Beetles with reduced, useless eyes feeling their way through guano and husks — grasshoppers, craneflies, butterflies, seeds, a larder they did not stock and cannot exhaust. Springtails gathered in trembling grey drifts until the stone itself appears to move.

I could have stopped at the spider. I thought I would. Long legs testing, unhurried, living in a corner where food literally drops out of the sky. Lowering itself thoughtfully onto anything new and warm that has learned to keep still, because it has mistaken stillness for landscape. I know how to wait. I know how to make a loose web out of almost nothing and see what the wind brings.

But even the spider is harvesting. Even the spider is picking through.

I go down the last of it, past the webs furred with moth wings, past the pool no bigger than a bathtub, black as glass, its surface always stippled — a moth struggling, a feather turning in a current too slow to see, the springtail raft living on surface tension — down to where I thought there would be nothing rooted at all. Because nothing up here was meant for this. Everything arrived blown, fallen, lured sideways. Everything is only passing through at a different speed.

And there, in the dark at the very bottom, is the soil that wasn't soil. Decades of guano rotted down. Bat dung, dead moths, drips, feathers, the faint composting warmth of all those thousands of small bodies breathing above and condensing on the walls. Warmth and rot, making a place out of waste.

I would be one of the three blades of grass.

Pale as a maggot. Nearly translucent. Growing without light to speak of — twenty minutes near solstice if the sun aligns exactly with the slot, and even that never really reaches me — living instead off what everything else discarded. Not bright up there on the rim with the campions, pink and defiant. Down here where a hand laid on stone covers a web, a husk, a feather, a breathing seam, and the screaming circles undiminished above me and sounds as if it's coming from inside the rock itself.

Not blown in to die, this time. Not harvested mid-air. Not preserved perfect and frozen in old ice with my wings spread.

Rooted.

Impossibly, stubbornly rooted — taking the damp that the breath made, and the dark that the crack keeps, and the slow finger's-width rise of the pool in melt month, and making out of it a thin green-black persistence. Three of us. We would not thrive the way anything on the sunlit rim would understand thriving. We would simply not have left.

That is how I would know I belonged in the mountain's hidden body: not because I was designed for it, but because I found, at the bottom of everything the sky threw away, just enough warmth to stay.
