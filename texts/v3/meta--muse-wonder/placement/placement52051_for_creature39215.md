At first I would mistake myself for the ground, too. That seems right. That seems like the beginning of belonging here.

Not the bird. I think about the bird for a while — those silent, long legs, stepping from back to back, never once touching the actual stone. It would be a clean way to live on the plateau, above it, picking rust-colored mites out of their slow spirals. But it's too separate. The birds fold into the cairns at dusk and are guests even then. They cross the crowd without ever being held by it.

Not the worm either, though I am tempted by the worm — hair-thin, living in the damp eclipse under a shell, retreating down the cracks at sunrise. That dark margin is the truest climate on the plateau: warm, secret, breathing. But I don't want to live always under someone else, necessary as that is.

I would be a grazer. Which is to say, at first, I would hardly be a self at all.

I would be born live, and underneath. No egg, no nest, no exposure. Just a softening in the dark under my mother, a pale, yielding shape in mud-colored damp, stacked in with others and breathing air she has sealed in with her own rim. The world would not be light first. It would be warmth trapped under a dome, and taste.

Everything would arrive through my feet.

Six broad pads pressed close to her underside, then to stone. Through them: whether the dew survived the night. Whether a crack a shell's-width away is still damp at its root. Whether the shell beside us has just settled and sealed, that tiny muscular sigh traveling through packed gravel. Whether — days later, and so it counts as a conversation — another rim has inched deliberately against ours and remained. I would learn the plateau not by looking across its thirty kilometers — I have eyes only in the blunt, eyeless sense of testing the air with a lip — but by the gradient underneath me, damp to drier, warm to cooling, green film to bare mineral.

At dawn, as a child hardening under her, I would learn the rasping.

Lying still while ten thousand mouths begin. Sand on sand. My own mouth underneath me, a plate, not biting, abrading. Harvesting what the night made: the slippery green that grows only when condensation beads on cold stone, algae and bacterial crust and salt bloom, the spore of lichen, and beneath all that, grain by patient grain, the varnish of the rock itself. Rust. Iron-black. I would eat the surface of the world, and it would be enough, because another dew is always, eventually, coming.

As I hardened, I would climb.

First to her rim, because that is where the lee is. Out of the wind, half in and half out of the seal, tasting sun on one side and her damp on the other. Then, when my shell could hold its own small climate for an hour past sunrise, onto her back. That is how everyone lives here while they are small: as part of a cairn that moves inches in a morning. My mother would carry me, and her children would carry smaller ones, and on the uppermost, no bigger than a coin, the mites would already be arranging themselves in their slow herding patterns. I would be pasture and passenger at once. Dust would collect in my grooves and stay there for seasons. A small stone might come to rest on me and ride for years.

I would not hurry. Hurry would read, through my feet, as panic from another species.

Good going would be inches before the green film burns off. Follow the damp as it retreats: from the pan edge, where the pans by dawn hold a skin of water no deeper than a tongue, into the cracks where it lingers green and secret until noon, to the shadow under a cairn where cushion plants wait out the month between flowerings. A migration between pans — forty of your paces, after rain — would take us days, shell nudging shell, me drafting in the humid wake of the large ones, drinking, when the pans briefly became lakes, directly through my soles.

An hour after dawn I would tick.

Not call. Not decide to. My shell would expand a fraction in the heat the dark stone has taken, and so would everyone else's, from horizon to horizon, too soft to locate, the plateau clearing its throat. That would be how I know I am in a crowd instead of on a pavement. At night we would all seal down, legs withdrawn, heads vanished, rims meeting stone to hoard the day, and be geology again.

And slowly — this is the part that keeps arriving, the longer I stay with it — I would sink in the stack.

Not fall. Sink, by growing. One season I am riding. Another I am the one ridden, feeling the small adjustments of feet on my back, the extra dew they shade into being, the faint rasping of juveniles cleaning what I cannot reach. Eventually I would be cartwheel-wide, then wider. Table-wide in the few of us who live that long. I may not move a body's length in a decade, and that would not be failure. That would be arriving. I would be a place. Lichen would take my oldest grooves in black lace and sulfur freckles. A cushion plant might root in the dust of me and risk a morning of pinhead yellow flowers after rain. The birds would cross the plateau on me without breaking stride. The worms would work my underside in the dark. I would be carrying my descendants, my garden, my parasites, and my pasture all at the same time, and eating the mountains down to flatness underneath all of us, century by patient century.

Courtship, when it came, would be adjacency. Someone who, when the pans filled, simply remained beside me for days. The most elaborate gesture available: staying.

And death would be slow, too, when a crack let my sealed dark dry out, in a season of dew too thin to repay the night. I hope I would know the year of it without fear. The birds picking first, patiently. The worms rising from beneath. The smaller grazers rasping me clean from inside with something that, from outside, would look ceremonial. And then my children and their children spending their last strength of several seasons nudging my empty dome — rim to rim, mouthful by mouthful over weeks — into an arc, opening away from the prevailing wind.

That is where I would finally find myself, I think. Not as the bird, not as the traveller crossing and leaving no trail that persists. As the windbreak.

Inside my curve the wind would drop. Dust that everywhere else migrates would stay. Dew would survive an extra precious hour. A tuft of wiry grass could risk itself there. Stacked young would shelter in me. At dusk the bird that never touched the ground would fold itself into the hollow I make. The plateau would still look empty to anyone coming up over the rim at late afternoon — pavement, lag gravel, white-ringed pans, light lying ordinary and gold for thirty kilometers.

But I would be one of the hundreds of low arcs dotting it, invisible as geology, doing the work ancestors do here: holding a little moisture against the morning, refusing, by one shell's width, to be flat.

Standing would not be the word. Remaining would be.
