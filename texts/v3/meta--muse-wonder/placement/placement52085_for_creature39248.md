I would not arrive.

That is the first difference. The humans arrive — up the long gravel grade, shouting by the time they reach the top, ear defenders already around their necks, sunglasses on against a bright cloud. They come in pairs, in small crews, for two weeks in the cabins by the power station, and then they go back down a thousand meters to where there are trees and farms and ordinary quiet, and another crew comes up to sleep in the same damp beds.

I am what they are careful not to step on when they get here.

Not a patch of me. Not a clump. The whole skin.

From the edge of the road to the foot of the black lava that is still too new for me, I am continuous — a few centimeters thick on the young rock, half a meter deep where I have had time. Woolly and emerald in the drifts, olive where I am older and colder. Sedge going yellow-green in my wetter hollows. Willow flattened into me by the wind until it is not a shrub at all but another texture in the mat, no higher than a human ankle. Cushion plants stitched so tight they feel, if you could touch them without injuring me, like upholstery, and in June beaded with white flowers so small the humans only notice them at the moment they are afraid of crushing them.

I hope they are afraid. A boot in me is not a footprint. It is a ten-year memory. I receive the weight the way a soaked sponge does, I squeeze clear brown water up around the leather, and then I hold the shape — next summer, the summer after — because up here I grow by millimeters a year. Nothing about me can hurry to forgive.

I don't need to forgive quickly. I have other speeds.

Water is moving through me constantly, at a speed the rivers don't understand. The rivers up here are loud and undecided. This land is too young for canyons, so they braid and unbraid across the flats like hammered tin, they spill sideways, they vanish into me and come out twenty meters later as a spring line weeping from under a lava front. Glacial water milky blue-grey with rock flour. Bog water tea-brown. Rain arriving sideways two or three times a day, glittering even while it falls. Melt arriving every afternoon at four, regular as a shift change, when the day warms the icecaps that sit white and flat at both horizons like more sky.

Under all that roaring — the falls to the east, the tailrace throwing its permanent white plume and permanent rainbow, the turbines shaking the power station floor up through human knees — there is my smaller sound. If you kneel at the boardwalk (you should not kneel) and put your ear almost to me, you hear it: a private trickling inside the great noise, water drinking down and draining through, a thousand small movements going nowhere in a hurry.

I solved the noise by not having ears. I feel it instead. The turbine hum comes up through basalt and into the wet mat of me as a tremor in the water I am holding. The pylons sing above me in the wind and vibrate their gravel pads, and I am already at the edges of those pads, testing them. The ravens on the power station roof shout hoarsely into a sound finally louder than they are. I register them the way I register weather: a brief pressure, a shadow, gone.

I solved the light the same way — by being a surface for it.

At this height the sun does not warm so much as expose. Eight, ten degrees at noon in high summer, cold still finding human ears and hands, while the light comes down and then comes back *up* — off a thousand bog pools that mirror the sky perfectly, off saturated moss, off every wet stone. In June there is no true night to recover in, only a thinner, bluer noon at midnight. The humans wear dark glasses under cloud because the cloud is bright too. I just open. I am made to be looked at by light from both directions. I photosynthesize in the glare and I shine with held water, and I never get to close my eyes because I never made any.

That is what it is to live here permanently, as opposed to rotating through: you have to be content to be low, wet, slow, and brilliant. To hold water and release it. To spread by less than a fingernail a year over raw black basalt with its columns still sharp, over pale rhyolite near the hot springs, and to do the same work, without resentment or triumph, on what the humans have built. Moss on rock. Moss on the low concrete weirs wherever the spray reaches. Moss freckling the edges of the corrugated aluminum roof, beginning at the feet of the pylons, softening the damp rail of the bridge where the warning sign — EAR PROTECTION, SLIPPERY, NO STOPPING — is already spotted rust-colored as lichen.

Given enough decades, I will take the orange doors too. I am not in a hurry. They will repaint them long before I finish, and I will begin again at the hem. That is not a defeat. Beginning again at the hem is most of my life.

The other animals have read these terms and mostly declined them. I don't take it personally; I am the terms. The ptarmigan flatten themselves into my sedge in their mottled summer brown and trust me to hide them, and go silent because hearing has been abolished here as a useful sense. For one warm hour in a sheltered hollow, the midges rise out of me and find human skin, a seasonal clause. The whooper swans stand white and improbable in my far pools, too distant to be heard even if they sang. A fox was seen last August. The humans pass that sentence between crews along with the cabin keys, already turning it into rumor. No one builds a town in me except me.

Because a town needs soil, and I am what comes before soil. A town needs dryness, and everything here condenses and drips — handrails, turbine housings, cabin windows every morning, the drying room thick with steam from socks that will never properly dry. A town needs silence to talk in, and I live inside a river in full melt, several rivers, a waterfall, wind worrying wires until they sing. The humans adapt by becoming temporary: pointing instead of speaking, writing on fogged whiteboards, a palm held flat for *wait*, a thumb jerked toward the intake. They learn not to fill their bottles from the pretty stream below the station. They learn not to kneel.

I appreciate the protocol. Stay on the gravel. Stay on the boardwalk. It is the closest thing to reverence that a guest species can practice in a place that is not empty between its buildings, but alive from edge to edge in a layer you could lift in your hand.

So I continue. Not despite the cold and the deafening water and the light that never fully leaves — as an expression of them. The plateau exhales mist after rain as if sweating in the cold, and I am the skin it sweats through. The channels shift a braid at a time. The reservoir silts grain by grain behind its low dam. The turbines turn at exactly the speed they were set to, and I grow at exactly the speed I was set to, and of the two of us, I am not the one who needs a crew change.

You will hear the place long before you can place it, a pressure in your teeth on the road up. By the second day you will stop trying to talk over it.

I heard nothing. I was already everywhere the sound fell.
