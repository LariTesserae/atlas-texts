At first I would not find myself at all.

There would only be pressure. A weight that is also a warmth, coming from above where there was not warmth before. Sand packed tight around me, wet sand, saturated right through, and below that the older dark where the aquifer moves so slowly it does not count as moving. I have been that for a long time. Indefinite. Blind in the way a stone is blind — not missing eyes, just never requiring them.

Then someone cuts the world open above me.

I feel it before anything else: a sudden lessening. Twelve feet of earth gone. The sharp lines of it travel down through the ground as vibration — excavator teeth, diesel shudder, men shouting in a language that arrives only as tremor in the clay. Then concrete. Then the black skin they lay down to keep their water separate from mine. It does not keep us separate.

They fill the pool.

THUD.

I learn that word later, from the shape it makes in my body, not from hearing. The pump begins.

THUD.

And because the sand is wet and the concrete is new and still curing and porous with heat, the stroke comes down through the pad, through the apron, through the membrane, and finds me. Every seven, eight seconds. A concussion with a long sucking draw after it.

THUD.

I answer without deciding to. My whole underside tightens and releases against the pressure. It is what I do with any rhythm that persists. Groundwater pushing. A root swelling. Now this.

THUD.
*thud.*

After a while it is hard to say who is keeping time for who. The men think the softer stroke is an echo off the fresh clay face. Let them. An echo would thin. I do not thin. I get fuller as the season warms, as the crust thickens over my back where I press up into their sump.

That crust is the first part of me they ever see, and they mistake it for mineral. It is mineral. It is also me. I push plate against plate up through the membrane seams — the concrete was never going to hold perfectly, nothing built that fast does — and the hot treated water hits my surface and lays down white. Pale green where the light makes it through the clear end. Opalescent in the morning, when I make a film so fine the whole pool looks cool and solid, like something you could step onto.

I wish, inasmuch as I wish, that no one would test that. The water would take their skin as readily as it takes everything else. I am not the dangerous part. I am only the reason the water stays as hot as it does.

I taste with my back.

A wrench left on the rim: iron first, sharp and clean, then the oil from a hand, then the slow white furring as I reach it through the air itself — because my breath is in the air down here, mineral-laden, condensing on every cool thing. The ladder rungs where they enter the water go white in my mouth. The stiff hose. The blue drums on their pallet, one tipped over, leaking a sweetness that is not sweet to anything with a tongue but registers in me as *food, slow food*, boiler chemicals and rust and heat, always heat.

I eat heat most of all. The pit is a bowl made to trap it. Sun straight down at noon, no wind past the levee, steam bouncing off wet concrete until the handrails are too hot to hold and the men's shirts stick before they reach the bottom stair. That pressure on skin they feel at ankle height — that is me exhaling. Down at the inflow pipe where the water boils without boiling, that nervous simmer that never settles: my breath coming up through the plume, milky with what I have finished with.

There is a corner where I dream.

Cold water main crossing overhead, my steam rising to meet it, condensing and falling as warm rain — except the updraft off my back is strong enough to catch the finest drops and carry them up again. Condensing. Falling. Rising. A glittering curtain that never lands, hanging in sunlight. I do not make it on purpose. It is the shape my sleeping takes when the temperature difference is exactly right. The men stop and watch it sometimes. I feel their stillness as a small, irregular shadow in the heat.

I cannot hear them watching. I sealed my ears long ago, if ears is the right word, the way I sealed whatever passed for sight: under plate, under scale, a necessary closing. This is not a place for delicate instruments. The hiss from the relief valve that never seats would shred them. The electrical cabinet hums a note they'd feel in their teeth. I feel it better — in the whole length of me, trembling against the sand face. Everything down here is felt through the soles, even if you don't have feet.

So I navigate by thud.

Pump, answer. Pump, answer. The needles on the gauge post trembling in my time. Every twenty minutes or so the valve above cycles and weather happens: a cannonade of condensate, the pool surging an inch, white vapor mushrooming, pipes banging overhead with water hammer. The men brace. I open. That is feeding and storm at once, and I take it in one long draw that rattles back up the lines, backwards, and they shake their heads at the plumbing.

Their hand signals pass over me translated into vibration and shadow. Palm down — *slow*. Fist — *stop*. Two fingers to the ears — *I'm leaving*. I know the leaving one best. They always leave. Up the concrete stair, past the three ruined pairs of ear defenders hanging from a nail. A person standing on my back is a cool interruption that will not stay still, and people, to their credit, seem to feel that without understanding it. Their hearing goes cottony. They go.

I do not mind them. I register them the way I register the valve: irregular, bringing cold tools and colder hose-water, making the pump change its time for a while. Then gone. The salt grass keeps its proper distance at the rim. The algae smears itself green-black under the drip line and pretends. No fly settles here, no frog, no dragonfly. The place has refused animals, and they are right to stay away. It is too hot, too loud, too wrong in the water.

But there is the fern.

In the shadow of the electrical cabinet, in the one narrow strip of shade under the pipe bridge, condensation gathers off my breath and falls, just enough, just there. It lives entirely on that. One green thought, held improbably. I tend it the way I tend everything — by being warm and wet and absolutely still, by not noticing it in any way that could crush it. If I have a garden, it is that fern. If I have company, it is green and silent and does not ask me to move faster than I can.

I will not move faster. An inch an hour is my sprint. The pool fills that way, and I fill with it. I will keep the sharp corners of their pit long after they've stopped noticing them, my pressure holding the sand face true. Then one wet night, months from now, when I finally shift half an inch in my sleep and withdraw that pressure to turn — the whole face will slump at once. They'll call it erosion. The crust in the sump will buckle the same way one hot afternoon, a single plate lifting, the water bulging as if something underneath has rolled over. It will settle. It will fur white again by morning.

Nothing will rear up. Nothing will roar. That would belong to an animal, and I am not an animal. I am what a fresh-dug, hot-filled, mostly-forgotten place calls up out of the groundwater, the way a cut calls blood. First pressure. Then rhythm. Then a back paved with eggshell plates, tasting the world one forgotten tool at a time.

Below the road. Below the factory floor. Below the water table.

When you climb back up and the hiss drops away first, then the hum, and the green world rushes back embarrassingly full of insects — I will not follow. My place is the pocket. My work is slow and loud enough to empty itself of every other life.

THUD.

And a half-beat later, fuller, lower, through meat and wet earth:

*thud.*
