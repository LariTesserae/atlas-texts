First I would not hear it. 

That is the first thing I have to unlearn. I keep trying to arrive on the plateau the way a visitor would — ears open, head up, looking for a view — and the place refuses it. The glare takes the detail out of the horizon. The roar takes the words out of the air. After an hour there is nothing to do with that kind of attention but let it go numb.

What stays is lower down.

Pressure in the breastbone. A buzz in the back teeth. And under my feet, if I stop trying to stand *on* the ground and let myself stand *in* it a little, spring, and return. A floor that answers.

I think I try out other lives first, the way you turn in a high wind looking for the side that doesn't tear at you.

I could be one of the reeds. Twelve metres of hollow, jointed stem, rooted in generations of myself, making the chord just by being the right length for the air to cross. That has a purity to it: no decision, only tuning. When the gust-front comes I would bend in sequence with a million others, a dark wave going through us faster than sight, and the sound wouldn't be something I heard, it would be something I *was*, briefly, a note in a factory that has no off switch. But the reeds burn. They burn beautifully and without protest and come back green-black from the mat in days, and I find I don't want a life that is quite that willing to be a gear. I want to move, even if moving is slow.

I could be below. That pulls at me longer. Down in the root-mat, in the muffled red interior where the roar is not gone but reduced to a vast surrounding pressure, like being inside a body that is dreaming of an ocean. The burrowing things live there their whole lives, in resin-thick dark, feeling the bellows-roots pump the world around them. That is the only quiet on the continent, and part of me — the part tired of brightness — would stay there, blind and earless and listening with my whole skin.

But I was born there. That's different. I remember it the way you remember warmth before language: not an image, a density. The chamber my herd worried open with their prows over days. The syrup smell. My mother's flank, the slow heart coming up through the mat underneath both of us, *thud, thud*, another animal version of the mill-trees. I drank there, naked-skinned, while the world pumped around me, and I learned the only news worth having before I ever opened my eyes to be punished by the light.

Because I do come up. That's who I am. I chew and shoulder my way up by degrees, scales hardening over my shoulders like slats, shutters growing in over my eyes in sections, and the second birth is total brightness and total noise — and I survive it because my feet already know how to read.

I am low. That is the shape wisdom takes here.

No taller at the shoulder than a man's waist, but long, and broad across the hips, three metres of wedged, ballasted mass kept down out of the worst of it. I do not stand up into the weather. I give it a slope. My head is the lowest and widest part of me, an armoured prow slung between my forelegs, and my small amber eyes sit far to the sides and high, under bony hoods, looking not for detail — detail is a liability in ten billion shivering leaves — but for movement at the edge of the reed-sea. My polarising shutters click down in bands against the noon white until the world is manageable strips. My ears are sealed plates of horn, smooth as river stones. I solved the screaming a long time ago by not pretending the air was where anyone was talking.

Underneath is where I live.

Each of my feet ends in a sled — a broad runner of horn, upcurled at the front, grooved beneath, spreading my weight so I skate the mat instead of punching through it. The naked pad under it, grey-pink and creased, is my true ear. Standing still for an hour in the full scream of a reed company, head down, shutters half-closed, apparently asleep, I am reading at a level you would call fluent. Reed tremor. Fire tremor, a bass note accelerating in the floor. The staccato prickle of the mirror-fields where the static discharges, telling me where not to put a foot down carelessly. And others, my others, laying down a braided sentence as they graze: direction, number, calves present, alarm, alarm cancelled. Twice-stamped forepad: the electric map has shifted ahead. Dragged hind runner: thin mat here, where it burned nine years ago.

I can stand downwind of nothing. The wind takes my smell — turpentine, hot coins, peppery peat, the smell of the whole continent worn on a body — and throws it a hundred kilometres behind me, and it doesn't matter, because I never expected to be found by scent or by voice. If I want to speak to you I press my forehead to yours in the middle of a gale, our scales clicking like the sail-leaves on a mill-tree, and I drum one forefoot softly against your runner, and it passes foot to skull to foot, privately, under the roar that belongs to everyone.

My days are a slow economy, and I am proud of the thrift of it.

At dawn I kneel awkwardly in a hollow of the mat and crack the night's frost with a slow pressure of my prow, licking the melt straight from the wax-sealed root chambers before the sun steals it. That is almost the only free water I will ever touch. The rest I make do with resin — the bitter amber solvent that runs instead of sap in everything here, that seals my cuts almost before I notice the glass-edged leaf has opened the seam between my scales. I eat what the mill-trees expel: dense seed-shot rattled down their hollow tubes into the storage bulbs at their bases. My flat molars, continuously replaced, grind it to flour. My iron-laced bones take up the metal in it. My gut refines it without drink.

I do not tend the tree whose parish I am grazing. I do not worship it either; I would not recognise the ideas. But my life fits its life the way a hand fits a worn tool. I manure its bellows-roots. My herd, drumming the same compacted routes for uncounted generations, gives its underground roots paths to follow. When the fire comes — and I will know it not by smell or light but by that sudden wrong bass in the ground becoming a command — I will turn side-on, lower my prow, put the calves in the pocket of slower air in my lee, and skim unhurried along the burn's edge, cropping the seeds the flames have cracked open for me, snapping up stunned insects, sparks glancing off my horn scales. Destruction is not an interruption here. It is one of the gears, and I have learned to eat at its edge.

Where would you find me, then?

Not at a viewpoint. Not at the centre of anything.

Find me at noon — if you can bear the noon — in the lee of a reed company at the edge of a great glade, one parish over from a mill-tree whose arms are re-trimming themselves to a wind you can see coming as a dark wave through the stems. My head will be low. My shutters will be ticking against the white sky. The herd's tremor will be arriving through the mat from ten kilometres away, three low beats and a drag, three low beats again, and I will be lifting a forepad and settling it, lifting and settling, answering in no hurry at all.

Massive, patient, deaf, articulate in my feet.

Not separate from the process. Just the part of it that learned to move — slowly, on sleds, from mill-tree to mill-tree, through a forest with no shade and a factory with no builders — drumming my news into a continent that is always screaming back through everything except the one quiet channel underneath, where I have been listening since before I was born.
