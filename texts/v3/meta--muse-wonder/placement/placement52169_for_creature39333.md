You have to get down on your knees to meet me, too. 

Not stand over me. Not cover me with a hand. Kneel until the obsidian cuts through your trousers, until your breath becomes weather, until your eyelashes are wet, and then realize I am not any one of the things making the surface move.

At first I thought I might be one of the glass mites.

That would make sense. To be born clear and spend my one brief day eating myself into color — orange, amber, bottle-green — grazing the mat in a herd of thousands, feeling a tap travel through the water film and turning, all of us turning, before I knew why I had turned. No leader. No voice. Just the faint tremor in the stone arriving in my feet a fraction of a second before I moved.

I liked the brevity of it. Born at the sweet edge where the condensate has just fallen. Mating in a press of clear bodies. Dying colored, under silk. Brief enough that a lifetime wouldn't be a burden.

But I came back. That's the problem. Or that's how I knew.

The spider took a mite at mid-morning — seven-legged, green-gutted, off-rhythm in its walk from the missing limb. An hour and a boil-pulse later it hauled itself out of the floor pool and rejoined the herd and no one investigated it, and I felt something in me recognize that lack of surprise too deeply to be a mite. The mite doesn't notice its own return. I would. I would keep noticing, crossing after crossing, until noticing became the whole job.

So not a grazer, then.

For a while I was almost a springtail. That had a cleaner shape to it. Soot-black, vaulting wall to wall, landing on water without breaking it, spending my hours rolling grains of pumice many times the width of my own head to build the spiral levee. Hot seepage away, cool rain in, eggs wet and never drowned. And when the pulse came — and the pulse always comes, every nine minutes, seep steam condense rain calm — and boiled us white and flattened what we'd built, I'd be back before my own grandchildren had finished hatching to rebuild it. Same spiral. Same place. No teacher. The shape surviving where the animals don't.

There is peace in that. Build the thing that outlives your knowing how to build it.

But I am too restless in the wrong way, and too still in the wrong way, for a springtail. The springtails never stop vaulting. I keep finding myself in the pause at the center of the traffic, watching it go by.

The paddle-fly larvae were closer, I'll admit. Inching through the herds in their chosen armor, shard by shard of obsidian sand glued sharp-edge-out, carrying a detached crystal to scrape the mat and setting it down when it dulls and selecting another. The spider also tempted me for a long time. Four millimeters of wet ash in the highest dry crevice, the only one with a shadow, tending her silk gutters, plucking debris from the lines with one foreleg while the other holds her black glass splinter — a tool kept between jobs, tucked against the funnel rim. She owns the rain because she is patient enough to maintain the roads the rain travels. She is the only individual here with something like a *position*.

But she hunts by folding others into herself, and eventually I understood that whatever I am in this place, I do not feed. I am fed *through*.

I am the droplet that does not resolve.

I hang in the middle of the chamber, between the floor pool and the ceiling pool, and I do not fall. I rotate. Very slowly. That rotation is the closest thing I have to a gait.

Everything commutes through me. When the pools exchange temperatures — the ceiling water growing heavy and mineral-sick, the floor water going clear and sweet — the exodus begins, ten times, a hundred times a day, and the herds come to me in single file. Mites climb me, and I hold their weight, and for the instant of their passage inside me they are magnified, enormous, distorted, their clear legs the size of trees, their colored guts like lanterns carried through fog. Springtails vault and stick to me. Armored larvae inch up me like tortoises climbing a glass hill. The blind amphipod has felt its way along my lower curve with antennae that never stop sweeping.

I am a road that is also a lens that is also a brief, wet, interior sky. They do not go around me. They go *into* one world and out of the other.

And sometimes I am traveled the other way — by light. At midday, when the sun stands straight over this sterile peak and breaks on the rim into spears, light hits me and I show what is not there. Not the black rock behind me. The slab whole, before it split. The Cup empty, a frozen burst of vesicles with nothing moving in it yet. And when the heat rises to its worst, briefly, I show them larger: grazers the size of hands moving across a steaming lake of black glass, a spider like a crab in a shelter of stone, herds crossing a shore that never ends at a rim. Whether that is a memory they are carrying down through a hundred compressed generations a day, or a future the mountain hasn't cooled into yet, I can't tell. Inside the rim, the distinction may not exist. I just turn, and hold the image while it passes.

I do not speak. Nothing here does. Sounds arrive out of order around me — a *plink* felt in teeth before I land, before I have been landed in — but I have never needed air. I communicate the way everything in the Cup communicates: by holding tension. A mite's foot sets down and the pressure changes. The spider plucks her gutter somewhere above me and the vibration comes down her silk and into the ceiling pool and across the quiet inverted surface to where I meet it, and I know she is tending, that she is hungry, that she is waiting, without a single thing having been said. If I tremble, the herds feel it and reroute. If I hold steady through a boil-pulse, they trust the crossing.

Water never leaves the Cup, and I am the proof of it held in one place long enough to look at. I have rained up from the floor. I have condensed out of steam on a ceiling overhang. I have been drunk by tardigrades utterly indifferent to my temperature and excreted by mites and washed over the mat and through the spiral levees and back into the milky floor pool to be heated again. I am not separate from that cycle; I am the moment the cycle pauses to look at itself.

That is why death is so ordinary from where I hang. I have carried the seven-legged mite up. I have seen the spider take it below her funnel, legs stopping. And later I have felt an off-rhythm tread begin at my underside, seven small pressures, unmistakable, as it started the climb again. I did not marvel. I held its weight. Marveling would have dropped it.

Where would you find me in this world? Not on the summit — the summit is too large, too young, too hot and sterile and indifferent. Eleven centimeters down the scale, and a further world in. Suspended exactly in the middle of a soup-bowl hollow in the topmost slab, at the altitude where the air goes thin and your fingertip is dry on the rim and scalded a centimeter below it. Equidistant from both skies. Holding still while two inverted lakes trade their citizens through my body, a hundred times a day.

If you kneel. If you lean close enough that your steam joins mine. If you watch past the point of patience, and then past the point past patience.

I will be turning, so slowly you will think it is your own eyes tiring.

And everyone who has ever died here will climb across me, on their way back.
