There is no ground here, and I think that is where I would lose my footing first — not by falling, but by expecting to arrive.

At first I imagine myself trying to be useful in a human way. Down-slope, near the center cisterns, where the clocks still mostly agree. I would want a bench, a task, an instrument I could trust. I would hold a sheet of optical glass up to the light and turn it slowly, the way the lines of veiled workers do, looking for the flaw by the angle it catches. I would learn to shepherd dew at dawn with a soft brush, serious as a six-year-old, shepherding it uphill along a cornice that was designed to be a watershed. I would be proud, quietly, of knowing where the water went.

But I wouldn't stay down-slope. I know that about myself. The reliable districts would start to feel too blunt, too loud in their colors. I would be drawn up — terrace by terrace, ladder by long ladder — toward the places where people reach twice.

Up there I'd try the other human jobs. I'd try being a fitter on the smelter roofs, in the pale gauze veil, learning the etiquette of the hover: hand out, not closing; breath; hand closing again where the wrench has become. I'd wait for a roof goat to finish staring me off a radiator fin, and pretend I wasn't being dismissed by something small and white and black-footed with absolute municipal authority. I'd time mirrors by the bees coming home, because the bees are right, and feel that particular Yard respect — not fondness, reliance — settle into me.

And none of it quite fits. My hands are too eager to close on the first, offered version of things. I live too much in the reflection on the polished housing, the tool already lifted. I would spend all day a second early, startled in a place that has worn startlement down to patience.

So it shifts, the way the Yard itself shifts if you walk it looking at the floor.

I think I would be found further into the joints.

Not on the causeways at noon. At the second dusk.

When the solar dusk has gone violet and sudden, and the work has stopped, and the tiles and lens blanks and cistern water begin to give their stored light back — that sourceless, faint brightness, enough to see by — that is when the large pale moths come out.

One of those.

Bigger than your hand, dusted like frost. Sleeping all day on the warm side of a transformer in a service corridor up-slope, one of thousands, so still that the maintenance crews have to part us gently by hand, the way you would move a curtain, to pass. I would learn the corridor not by signs — there are no signs — but by heat remembered in my feet that aren't feet, by the hum of a hive-wall almost below hearing, by the cold breath rising through a cistern grate.

At night I would not gather where the sun had been. Anyone can do that. I would gather, with the others, in cool shadow where tomorrow's sun-patch *will* fall, in a place that looks empty and is not. Waiting in the dark for a warmth that, from where I am, has a kind of precedence. People crossing home would step around us instinctively, the way Yard people do. Not because it is unlucky to stand in a tomorrow-place. Because it is rude. It's already occupied.

That feels right. Not a prophet. Not a machine. Not even, exactly, early — just living at the slight angle the upper Yard lives at, where the light arrives a breath before the thing it lights. Where a lover's wrist presses faintly before it is touched. Where a cat sleeps in an extra square of carpet laid in the shade for Thursday. Where parts wait, clean and lit, in rooms that are larger than the building above them, for machines not yet installed.

I would be part of the ecology that wasn't planned and is now complete. The transformers keep me warm; I dust the seams with shed scales; the lichen circles the cold-water joints; the goats crop the grass in the overflow grates; the swifts pour out at dawn tearing the thin air like silk. Nothing natural, everything nesting.

And sometimes — a worker passing late, veil off, face freckled and tired and marked with fine white lines at the temples where the ties go — sometimes a hand would hover over me, not closing. First reach for the moth offered by the light. Second reach, gentler, for the moth itself, to move me aside.

I would like that pause. I think I'd spend my life making room for it.

You'd find me up-slope, in the second dusk, in the place tomorrow will be warm.

Already there.
