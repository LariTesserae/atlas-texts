At first I think I would want to be something that does not have to walk.

One of the balloon things, perhaps. Up above a pillar where the warm air has been rising in the same place since before anyone pressed a hand to stone. Translucent, gut-sac pulsing slow, feeding ribbons trailing under me, combing spores and floating young out of the updraft. Never landing. Never having to choose. Mating, birthing, dying all in that one patient circle, and then the long spiral down over days, turning, being eaten on the way by dry wings.

That is what a visitor would want. To keep circling a world like this without having to learn it with his feet.

But that isn't living here. That's just staying aloft in it.

Then I think I might be one of the waiters beside the rails. Low as a table. Dust-colored. Breath once a minute. So compatible with stillness that the sifters forget the difference between me and a cairn until a needle-leg steps too close. There is a clean honesty in that. Eat when the world steps within reach. Otherwise, keep the decision the country made at the beginning.

But I keep coming back, as I go down further, to the hands.

Not the polished lanes in the felt, where the grazers go. Not the needle tracks that disturb nothing. A hand, pressed flat beside a scent rail until the oil goes into the stone. Five long fingers. Stippled pads. And next to it, lower and smaller, a child's hand, held down just as patiently. Not a print left by accident. A sentence left where it will still be legible when the child is old.

I would be the one who holds the child's wrist down.

I would not be tall here. Standing — when I stand, on open ash, flat-footed, sole spreading — I would come only to a visitor's sternum, but broad through the skull and shoulders, made low to the country. In the crystal gardens I would go down without thinking onto knuckles and the outer edge of my hands, fingers curled clear of the needles, because the choice between two legs and four is not a choice between person and animal. It is terrain.

Where eyes would be, my skin would smooth over. I would not miss them because I would never have translated the world into light. I would read it in heat, in overlapping shape: the hot pennies smell of bacterial felt, the cooler basalt where a thermal boundary slides across my face, a bandmate's fever-shape resolving out of absolute dark by the pits along my jaw and ribs — temple to jaw, then smaller, under the arms. I would know posture before touch. Fatigue in the neck. Fear as a bloom of heat. A sleeping child as a slow, regular cooling and warming against my belly.

My skin would be dust-grey in the creases, mottled brown-grey underneath, warm as everything is warm. Thirty-eight degrees in the stone, in the air, in my mouth. To touch another person would be, for a second, not to know where my warmth ends. When I had worked too long scraping felt near a fissure, the panels along my back would tighten and loosen, opening damper tissue to whatever moving air I could find, then sealing again. I would not sweat water away. Water is not for that.

Mostly, I would not speak aloud.

I would speak onto forearms. Onto ribs. Fingers drawing quick patterns in the dust I keep deliberately on a companion's skin, pressure and warmth laid into heat-pits in sequence, fast and close and gone without trace. For a child, I would walk with one of their hands on my ankle and the other on stone, teaching them to read. Not correcting in words. A tap. A change in the temperature under their palm. That withheld almost-click of tongue against teeth that everything here makes when it means *attend*.

The trail under our hands would not be only a route. It would be the text of everyone who had ever passed this way, laid down in heel and toe and the gland at the base of the spine, and it would still be specific after fifty years: six passed, one gravid, one old. A brine pocket cracked three days east of the third pillar. Do not crack it again. It has sealed.

I would be in the middle of my first circuit come back around, I think. Not young enough to ride in a sling of dried gut, learning scent before weaning, and not old enough yet to be mostly listening. Old enough that my own handprint, somewhere behind me, is already smaller than the hand I have now. Young enough to be trusted to build the wall.

That is where you would actually find me. Not in a house — there is no rain to keep off, no weather to break that hasn't blown in exactly the same way for a thousand years. In a semicircle of flat stones set on edge, waist-high, opening away from the thermal. Inside, the floor swept down to basalt. You can see my broom-marks. I made a small private pocket of different air there, for sleeping, for a birth, for opening the panels on our backs safely. I will use it for months, perhaps for what a visitor would call a generation, and when the band moves on I will leave the obsidian scrapers laid by size, smallest at the end, and if we have one, the copper awl, beaten, green as a pea, point turned outward in plain sight. Not lost. Left. Because someone will come to sweep this place again, and it may be my grandchildren, and the wall will not be a ruin. It will just be dustier.

My work would not look like work from above. Scraping mustard and rust and bone-white felt where it thickens by warm fissures. Drying it on hot stone. Grinding it in the hollow worn into the boulder, pressing it into dense salty cakes that smell of hot pennies and keep essentially forever. Following grazers and gathering what they have enriched — droppings before they powder, shed skins, eggs, dead sifters crisp as husks, mineral flakes changed by passing through a gut. Setting patience-traps nobody needs to watch. Taking stones from the hottest fissures to set the protein in eggs, warm a newborn whose temperature has dipped. Cooking as a matter of degree, not kind. No fire. Nothing here needs to burn to become itself.

And water, governing all of it. The whole sixty-year round, which would simply be my life laid out as a succession — after the crystal garden where the needles tinkle faintly in the updraft, three hours of ash smelling of iron felt, then the basin where the dead keep, then the pillar where the air climbs and takes the heat off my face like a remembered breeze — I would be able to teach that whole known world to a child as a sequence you walk with your feet. It would still be true when the child arrived at the end of it. I would crack a salt pan, drink a few black spoonfuls of sulfur brine, let the patient life that gathered around the crack drink too, seal it with pressed ash and spit and a flat stone, and lay down the mark: *opened in the year of the third child. Do not open until the child has a child.*

When we passed the still-air basin, I would not hurry. One of ours would be curled there, eyes sunken to pits, tongue dried to a strip, not rotted, only stored in warm parchment. Not abandoned. A chosen address. We would take the broad skull, in twenty years or forty, whenever the circuit brought us back, pack the orbits carefully with red dust, and set it skull-stone-skull into the next cairn. Grave, map-marker, punctuation. *We were here. This many. Remember.*

I think that is why the balloon thing was wrong. It never lands, and so it never leaves anything behind to be read. The predator was wrong too — it only takes. What I would want, down here where a footprint keeps its crisp edge for thirty years and a scent laid down by a grandmother can correct a child she never met, is to be part of the slow editing. One new vibration added to a song in a lifetime. One tool design altered across two hundred years of testing. One awl left unguarded, because theft requires the kind of change this country has never provided.

In the end I would get still the way the old do. Longer rests. Fewer words even by hand. More time with forehead and palms pressed to basalt, listening for what travels through stone hours away, arriving in the teeth before it arrives in the ears. Not infirm. Just completing a stillness I had been practicing since I learned to move between ash grains without disturbing them.

And if a bright confusion came down the trail one year — too hot, loud under dead cloth, carrying a small sun in its fist, leaving footprints that would outlast its grandchildren — I would not be afraid, and I would not be curious the way it hoped. I would part around it with the band, read its intentions in its face-heat, and leave the beginning of an answer where it would find it: a felt-cake on a flat stone. An awl turned point-toward it in the ash.

If I came close at all, I would cup my red mineral so the glow fell only on my hands, and put my long fingers very gently on its forearm, and tell it onto its skin where it was in the succession.

It could take forty years for it to understand.

That would not be too slow.
