I think I would come down the way everyone does at first, wrongly.

In the elevator, past the place where the roots start crossing the shaft — fingers, then wrists, then beams — I would be holding myself very still so as not to brush them, and thinking obsessively about water. Not even thirst, exactly. Just the idea of it. Rain. A cup left open. A bath. All the wasteful, shining ways we use it on the surface, as if it could not kill anything.

That would last about a month, I'm told. The wistful exile talk. Down below they would listen politely, oil my split lips for me with the little wooden applicator, give me a cheekful of that dense, cool fruit pulp, and not quite believe me.

I wouldn't stay a visitor, though. Not because I am braver than anyone. Because of the quiet.

At first the quiet would seem like an absence — no engines shouting, no open flame, the sawmills felt in your teeth a street before you hear them — and then very quickly it would reveal itself as a medium. There is so much being said, all the time, through the soles of the feet.

I can imagine learning it the way children do, before letters. Bare feet flat on polished root-wood. Palm flat, and wait. Don't press an opinion into the wood. Wait for the pulse. One beat a minute in a limb that is thinking its way toward somewhere important. Faster in a house-wall that wants pruning. A dull, spreading ache where rot has started somewhere uphill, sweet and wrong.

I would be bad, at first, at almost everything else this place asks.

Too hasty for a pruner. I'd want to cut decisively, finish the shape, make the street stay where the street was in spring. That is expensive thinking down there. Cut wrong and you don't get a stump, you get the black rot running overnight. I'm not sure I'd trust my hands with a saw until I'd unlearned finishing things.

Not a beetle-handler, either, though I'd love them from a safe, admiring distance — the dog-sized beetles coming home at the manufactured dusk, mandibles kept precisely blunt, chitin oiled against the dryness, their passage the closest thing to traffic. I'd want to pet them, which would be surface sentimentality, and they'd know.

Not a surveyor. I don't have the nerve to argue with a green knuckle in the bark that has declared other intentions for next year's neighborhood, to move my dotted line of copper staples and file a growth forecast instead of a map.

What I do have, I think — what would get kept and trained and slowly made useful — is a willingness to sit still in a place where something is arriving, and listen until I can tell it apart from everything else arriving.

So: a listening post.

You remember the plaza near the beginning. A parade ground of polished root-wood, big enough for an army, and on it almost nothing. Three benches. A listening post. That sparseness used to puzzle me. Now I think I'd live inside that puzzlement for years.

I would be the tender there. Not the oldest one — not the woman in the Pollen Commons who can feel her grandson coming home by the particular way a branch lifts a fraction to meet him, though I'd learn under someone like her. A middle one. City-stayed, rather than city-born. Hair braided tight and oiled. The insides of my nostrils oiled. A faint sheen of cedar and mint and hot copper on my skin that I would stop noticing, the way you stop noticing your own house has a smell.

My slippers, folded neatly beside the bench. My shoes are not for this. My soles would thicken. That embarrasses me most in this imagining and also pleases me most — that my body would become literate from the bottom up. A sawmill cycling down two districts away as a change in the grain under my heels. A freight pod sighing through a hollowed dead branch to the east. Weather changing around a trunk thirty kilometers above my head, announced by a subtle brightening in a light-fiber the width of a street. Someone stamping once, lightly, in code, across the Commons: *here, sit, I saved you the bench.*

I would answer the same way. Not by raising my voice. Shouting is illiteracy here — it muddies the medium everyone else is trying to think in. During Bloom Hours I'd sign, and I hope my signing would become beautiful in the way theirs is, whole sentences delivered while still holding things, while the cream, hand-sized moths spiral up and a misplaced vibration could pull them off course. I'd keep a little store of oil for them beside the post. They'd bring me messages in wax tubes, slow and unhurried, and I'd pass what they carried downward into the wood and upward into words, depending on who it was for.

That in-betweenness suits me. I have always been more adjacency than center.

I would live in one of the induced houses on a residential terrace — not the lovely knotted towers up on the Trunk Terraces where the miners' huts got talked into becoming beautiful over eighty years, but something younger, down toward the Root Flats. Four and a half years growing. Weather-tight, mostly. Windows still learning translucency in the afternoons. Some mornings my kitchen would have decided, very slowly, to be slightly rounder than it was. I would not hammer it flat. I'd make resin-milk tea, invite the district pruner, and have the long, serious, faintly comic conversation: what is this room trying to do, and can we live with it if it does?

I think I'd be good at that conversation. I think most of my life, in any world, is that conversation.

*What are you becoming? Say it slower. Say it at the speed growth understands.*

I'd eat at the Pollen Commons at dusk, when the shutters close far above in the trunk and the whole cavern dims in twenty golden minutes — nut paste, leaf bread, mushrooms fed on what the beetles left, salted oils rich enough to coat the mouth. I'd listen to the growth report read aloud, and realize with a small start that I'd already heard most of it through my feet that afternoon: which street has narrowed, which house has flowered unexpectedly, which bud-yard on the far rim might be opened for settlement next year. I'd feel proud of that, quietly, the way you feel proud of a language going fluent in you without asking permission.

And I'd make the mistakes that mark me as someone who came from above. I'd step, once — only once — off bark toward a pale root with a bare foot, and feel it begin, with terrible gentleness, to consider my ankle as a suggestion. I'd never bring anything wet down in an unsealed skin. I'd learn to carry my griefs and excitements the way they carry water here: sealed, permitted, rationed, tended like an altar. Not spilled. Spilling hurts the tree.

At night I'd lie in a room still extending itself over me — an inch of new cork, a beam sighing as it cools — and hear almost nothing. A creak like a ship a long way off. The tick of resin. Papers lifting, curtains stirring, as the city breathes out cool dry air through vents in the larger limbs. A baby in the next widening wall-space over, sleeping with one foot pressed unconsciously to the wood. Somewhere far across the root valley, a factory sighing down to its low electric hum, and a new bud splitting with a sound too low to hear and exactly loud enough to feel.

I'd feel it. That would be my whole job, and my whole belonging: to feel it accurately, and pass it on without distortion, and not force it to arrive faster than it is arriving.

If you asked me, after some years of that, whether I wanted to live on the surface again, I'd consider it seriously. Politeness is a form of listening.

I'd say I have been. I'd say the sky was very interesting, but it did not answer when I put my hand down. I'd say the light up there falls on everything whether it wants it or not, and down here, the light is carried, fiber by fiber, to the places that have asked for it, and at noon you can see the tiny translucent lizards living inside the brightness, keeping it clean.

Then something would come across the plaza — not quite where the path was this morning, because by morning the street never quite is — and I would feel it coming a moment before I saw it.

And I would turn, almost on time.
