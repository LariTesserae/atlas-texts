From above, I would not be there at all.

That is the first way I know. I would come across the plateau for hours the wrong shape for noticing - too upright, too exposed, eyes narrowed against the glare, lips splitting, looking for a river line or a road or anything tall enough to cast a direction. I would walk within a stone's throw of the rim and hear, perhaps, a bell impossibly inside the rock, a dovelike murmur where no doves could be, and I would turn, and lose it to the wind, and keep walking.

So I have to learn the place by going down.

On the stair my body gives me away first. It is too narrow and I have to turn sideways, and in that turn I am already being remade: not a traveller crossing a landscape, but an obstruction in a passage, a thing other lives must negotiate. A donkey comes up laden with brushwood and I press back against warm stone. A woman comes up with a hide bucket on her head, another in her hand, and turns with me in the polished trough without spilling. My forehead finds the lintel at exactly the place generations have glossed with theirs. I stoop.

For a while I try being human there - correctly human, at alley height. A child could do it better. Children grow up vertical here, roof to roof, the fig trunks a second street system, small jars carried before any other law is learned: wipe the spill with dust, squeeze the dust over a plant, waste nothing because there is nothing to waste. I am too slow for that apprenticeship. My feet slide where theirs grip, broad-toed, on parapets. My hands are clumsy at the true unit of measure, which is not the jar but the notch on the jar, not the handful but the pinch. And sound undoes me. I would speak at ordinary pitch two terraces down and hear myself arrive at my own shoulder. I have not learned privacy as a discipline - to turn away convincingly, to look at my hands while a neighbour counts grain, to let a girl at the cistern sing to no one. I would hear everyone, and fail to pretend not to.

So I fall a little lower in the town, nearer the ground it actually runs on.

A goat, for an afternoon. That almost fits. Feet first, certainty first: a parapet edge no wider than a bell's swing, and the toes - hooves - know it without asking. A tuned bell at my throat by which one person in the whole bowl can name me blind in the dark. Vines left hanging just within reach, pruned by my mouth to an ancient, exact allowance: so much leaf permitted, no more. At feeding time I would be part of the roar and set it off, bleat to donkey to rooster to a woman's flat carrying call, a child repeating her from a roof.

But a goat is too negotiated, too owned, too daily argued over. Whose roof, whose figs, whose cough heard at night. I keep lifting my head from the vine leaves. I keep watching the swallows cut the alleys at head height, so sharp they brush sleeves, and something in me wants the lane rather than the roof - wants to belong to the air the bowl has folded into itself.

A swallow, then. Mud under an eave, made of the same stuff as the houses. Out at dawn over the colourless plateau, back into green dimness before the heat. I would know every eave, every bee-lane through the alleys that everyone ducks by habit, every updraft the rim makes when the wind passes over and seldom descends. At night, not sleep exactly but a held stillness among hundreds of others settling in the figs, a vast muttering rustle.

Too fast, still. The swallow sees the bowl all day and approves nothing, tends nothing, remembers nothing except by return. And I notice I am always slowing down toward the thing the swallows are returning *to*. Each evening my flight narrows. I land less like a bird and more like a weight coming to rest. The branch gives, the leaves close around the last reflected light - light that has come off a pale wall, up from dust, brown and close - and I stop wanting to leave it in the morning.

That is how I find my scale. Not by choosing, but by outlasting my quicker shapes.

I would be the great fig.

Not the oldest root in the bowl perhaps, but the one between the third and fourth hour, the one whose shade is a right, precise and argued over and remembered by elders when the leaves are still small in spring. My roots have gone twenty feet down through fissures the houses are polite about, feeling for the damp that feeds the seep, drinking what cannot be seen. They crack a wall each generation and are forgiven, because a wall can be re-mudded with dung and ash and urine and straw, and shade cannot be made by hand at all. My canopy spreads over whole alleys. Below me is the permanent greenish dimness where most of the living is done; above me the plateau noon is merciless and empty, and I hold a roof of leaves between them.

They feed me the way the town feeds itself, and I am not insulted by it. Ash to my beds. Ground bone. Feathers, old thatch, sweepings from the threshing floor, night-soil carried without embarrassment because shame here belongs to waste, not to what is wasted. I take the bowl's leavings down into dark and return them as fruit so soft a person inside a house can tell, without looking up, which tree it has fallen from - by the sound it makes on a roof, on a leaf, on packed earth. Children eat it on parapets and spit the stem into a palm, to drop later onto soil. Pigs wait for what falls spoiled. Goats take my lower leaves on schedule. Wasps go drunk in my split skins. A hard year is recorded in me as surely as in any granary, and a good one is syrup and shade and argument.

I am a street as well as a tree. That is the part that becomes more itself the longer I stand in it. Toddlers are lifted into my lower branches; roof-runners pass through me from house to house without touching the alley. Women hang curds in cloth from me. Old people take their particular patch of my late shade as absolutely as the dogs take their hereditary sleeping spots in the dust below, defended by everyone's agreement. Couples argue in whispers in a privacy I am expected to perform. A girl sings to herself at my roots and I do not repeat it - though the bowl is an ear, and I hear, word for word, a conversation at the cistern, a goat coughing two rings up, the cistern ticking with its single falling drop, the bees working the south wall, a man mending a beam his grandfather spliced.

At night I am the town's other roof. Birds come into me in their hundreds, shifting, muttering, turning in sleep. Goat bells tick below. Someone crosses a rooftop by touch, descends to check a restless donkey, greets two wakeful neighbours by name in the dark. The rock around us gives back the day's heat, and the cold stars stand over the plain, and I am warm through, breathing out slowly with the walls.

From the rim after dark you would see almost nothing of me - a darkness denser than the darkness, sunk below the level of the world. That is right. I do not need to be visible from above. I am named, disputed, inherited, fed; I crack the houses and I hold them in shadow; I turn dung into figs and glare into a place where people can finally unbind their hair.

One root-length deeper. One tendril more, by permission, over a neighbour's roof. One more season of making a crowded, living thing out of almost nothing.
