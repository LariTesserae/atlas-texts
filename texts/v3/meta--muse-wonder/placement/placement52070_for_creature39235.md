I thought at first I would be the visitor.

That seemed the obvious place to put myself — on the low marsh path, wet grass to the knee, coming up on the long green-black face of the pumping station with the engines already in the soles of my shoes. Finding the gap in the ivy because the sound gets thicker there. Ducking through the rusted door.

But no one stays in that room as a visitor. The roar sees to that. It gets into your sternum and your teeth and it erases your breathing, and within minutes you'd be back outside again, speechless, thinking you'd seen an empty room.

Maybe the frog, then. Down by the drain, in the finger-width stream that never widens. That at least is a life pitched at the right scale for the place: throat barely moving, sitting for hours in shallow water, letting a drip land on your back without startling. Stillness as a whole occupation.

Or the moth behind the gauge. That appealed to me for a while. The one spot where the vibration is least. Fogged glass in front of you, two broken spokes nearby rusted smooth at the break, and you simply do not fly when someone enters. You have found the quietest corner of a deafening place and you keep it.

But the moth is hiding from the room, and the frog is only visiting its floor. Neither of them belongs to the wall.

And the room is the wall. Everything else — the empty air in the middle, the pipe at head height, the valve wheel, the drain in the grate — is just what the surfaces are arranged around. The life is in the bands. Condensation band. Spray band. Tideline. That narrow dry band above the pipe where the engine heat comes through.

That's where I would find myself. Not standing. Never standing. Pressed flat, above and behind the pipe, spread wide over the warm concrete until from the doorway I didn't read as a creature at all. One more bulge where the concrete has softened and re-set. One more darkening where moss gives way to damp lime. Green-black along my back. Liverwort feathering at my sides. A faint orange pitting where the light from the vent crosses me, as if rust were blooming up through skin.

You would only know if you stayed, and you would have to stay a long time. My breathing is not a visible thing. The sound has taught me better than that. My ribs rise by less than a bead of condensation swells, and fall again, taking the turbine note in through the wall and letting it out with one extra, living thickness in it. A held chord, with me inside it.

My eyes have fogged like the gauge. I don't mind. The room is twelve feet long and it has not rearranged itself in my lifetime. I know it by other means now: the worn hollow on the pipe where the drip always lands, the two broken spokes of the valve wheel under my pads, the smooth rust where those breaks healed over decades ago. My hands have gone wide and low and many-jointed for this. No claws — there is nothing here to dig into that wasn't poured or bolted — just pale pads, worn featureless, that hold on wet concrete and polished iron alike.

I travel the way everything here travels: in lines, never across the open middle. Down the spray band to where the stream crosses the grate, my lower skin wet, my upper skin only damp. Up to the ceiling, where droplets form at a rate you could set a watch by, and I can take one before it falls. Belly-close along the underside of the pipe, keeping that one bright strip of metal bright simply by passing, year after year, until the polish looks like the water did it.

There is one drip I never take. The one that falls from the lime tip to the pipe and makes its bright *tink*, at its interval, unbroken. I leave it alone. I couldn't tell you if it's habit or territory or fidelity. I only know the room has kept that note so long that I find my place in the room by it. If it stopped, I would not know where I was in the chord.

I eat almost nothing, and almost nothing is always here. Algae warming green in the light. The soft top of the moss, cropped so evenly the turf never spills past its edge. A snail in the morning, before noon washes its trail featureless. The mineral skin of the seep water, licked from the wall in a slow rasp that leaves no new mark. I do not hunt the frog. He sits in his stillness below; I pass above him in mine. We want different kinds, and there is room for both.

I have no voice. The place forbids it twice — once because nothing I made would survive the roar and the hiss of that leaking flange, and once because I have never needed it. If I need to say anything, I say it the way you found this place: through the soles of the feet, through a palm laid flat. A slight thickening of pressure in the concrete. A pause in my passage that changes, for a yard of wall, the evenness of the vibration. The turbines do not answer. Continuance is answer enough.

I don't sleep so much as thin out. My pulse settles where the gauge needle settles, against the same invisible mark, for hours. I chose the whole wall for exactly what the moth chose its corner against: the vibration is most here, and even. Least would starve me. I rest in the chord the way the frog rests in the flow, interrupted only when a drip lands squarely between my shoulders and runs, unhurried, into the green scales along my spine.

And I can still tell the weather. I turn my head — a fraction, nothing visible — toward the door when the light comes in broad and diffuse after rain, toward the vent where the mist hangs visible in the shaft. I feel the small cooling of the inflow on one cheek, where the warm interior meets the outside. Rain. Season. Even the heron, standing in the canal with its shadow on the water, dimming the louver's light by one degree. He has never put his head through the door. I have never gone to meet him. The threshold is understood on both sides.

I am not old the way a tree is old. No rings. No scars worth naming. My age is smoothness. Every angle I once had has been revised by water into the curve water prefers. My joints are dished like the nosings of the steps coming down. The plates along my back grew their little mineral caps and then stopped, the way the thumbnail stalactites on the ceiling stopped. My moss coat has reached the exact edge of its light and its spray, bright where the sun hits it, near-black in the corners, neither invading nor retreating.

I am not waiting for anything. Not decaying, not ripening. I have arrived at the version of myself this exact volume of warm, wet, roaring air can support, and I maintain it with a fidelity that looks, from the doorway, like nothing happening at all.

So if you come — and you will only come once, and you won't stay long — don't look in the middle of the room for me. Look at the wall above the pipe. Put your palm flat to the concrete before you go, if you can bear it. Under the great even vibration, very deep, almost too regular to call a pulse, there is a second regularity keeping time with the first.

That's me.

Holding still. Which here, in a pocket hollowed out of a dam, with all of eternity sorted into wet and dry bands a hand's breadth apart, is not an absence of life.

It is the whole practice of it.
