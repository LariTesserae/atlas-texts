I thought at first I would be up on the rim.

That's the human place in it — boots on actual rock, breath fogging, jacket loud in the absolute quiet, watching the whole eleven kilometers do its slow boiling below me. Not included, not eaten, just set apart long enough to see it clearly.

But I know myself too well to stay there. I'd keep edging closer to look at one small part properly. A white-starred campion. A copper lichen. A stalk going knee-high between morning and afternoon. And in this valley, to attend to something long enough is already to go down with it.

I wouldn't be the finch. Crossing without stopping, high and clean and uninvolved — I have never been good at that kind of transit. I wouldn't be the moth either, though I understood it immediately: adult life spent entirely airborne over the flowering stalks, because to land for more than a few minutes is to be grown over. That frantic refusal to rest, that pollinating in perpetual motion — it's too familiar, and too exhausting, and I think I would be looking, the whole time, for somewhere I was allowed to land.

So I would land.

Not on top. There is no on top, really, that lasts. Perhaps I begin as part of the skin — the recognizable part. Half a meter of cold green crust, cushion-dense, everything low because tall things are torn off. Frost whitening my north side at dawn. Ice skinning the water cupped beside me. Spore-haze at ankle-height, which is also as high as I ever need to reach, because everything I could possibly touch is already the same body. Light coming hard and thin and blue. For a little while, photosynthesis feels like enough of an explanation for existing.

Then the substrate moves, the way it always moves.

Not a disaster. No roar. A sheet of felt tears quietly along a seam somewhere upslope of me and slides, and my raft of stems — campion, sedge, a bit of creeping willow, my roots braided hopelessly into all of theirs — folds. Slowly. The way a page turns if the book is very, very large. My green side goes under.

That is where I find myself, actually. Not in the first image. In the folded one.

Deep in the cross-section, where a trench cut open would show it plainly: green skin, brown felt, black cable, red compost seam, pale crusher root, gray stone flour — and then green skin again, folded deep into the dark, upside down, somehow still living.

My senses narrow to the ones the valley actually has. No view. No sound — the felt has taken all of that, layer upon waterlogged layer, until even my own shifting is muffled to nothing. Instead: pressure, when a root-cable pulses nearby and the whole mat tightens and loosens around me. Heat, drifting in pockets from a fermentation organ somewhere below, warm enough to make condensation bead on tissue that should be frozen. A faint electric tingle on grey days, when the tall dry stalks far above — far *below* me now, direction has stopped meaning much — discharge root to root and a blue tracery passes silently through the net I'm braided into. And slowly, acid. Not eating me. Eating the rock on the far side of the crushers. Becoming flour. Becoming food.

I am not dead down here. That's the strange part, the part I keep returning to. My leaves are pressed flat against dead leaves. My white flowers closed and went brown without anyone seeing. But the willow stem folded in beside me is still putting out rootlets into a dead pump-organ. The sedge peat I'm rooted in exhale into me. What rots a meter away feeds what grows a meter away, and I am, at the moment, exactly on the seam between those two neighborhoods — being unmade so gradually that it is indistinguishable from being remade.

A pump bursts somewhere, too fast-grown, and by evening its chamber is compost, and the warmth of that breaking-down passes through me like weather. A hot node gets insulated by eager felt, cools, starves the felt, opens a black steaming pit somewhere up in the light that fills with meltwater and then with sedges. None of this is reported to me. None of it needs to be. I am not the center of it. There is no center. I am a local thickening in a closed loop that regulates itself the way a liver does — warm, cool, thicken, tear — without ever once knowing.

If I stay still — and here, stillness is not a choice I get to make, it is just what matter does — I will be classified as material. I already have been. Fine fibers are working through what used to be the boundary between me and not-me, looking for minerals. This horrified the part of me that stood on the rim. Down here it doesn't. The glove, the carcass, the dropped bright thing from the human world: none of them were punished. They were sorted. Fur to felt, bone to flour, dye-minerals pulled gently apart along the seams by competing roots. I am being sorted too, element by patient element, and some of what is taken from my upside-down green will come up again, in a season or in a century, as a metallic scale pushed warm through the moss smelling of ozone, or as the copper fiber inside a cable, or as a new shoot that will be knee-high by afternoon and collapsed by night.

And some of me may simply be folded back up. The valley does that. A slide turns the mat, a raft roots at the bottom while its trailing edge is already dissolving, an old surface comes up blinking into frost and hard sun with its roots now pointing at the sky and has to begin again as skin. Old as landscape, young as this afternoon — I wouldn't have to resolve that if I were this. I would just be the place where it happens to be true.

So not the rim. Not crossing over. Down in the dark of the body that replaced the ground, green side turned away from the light, hearing nothing, feeling pressure and heat and the slow grind of the mountain being eaten below me — still living, still being lived through, waiting without urgency to be either compost or daylight again.

Both, here, are just the valley continuing.
