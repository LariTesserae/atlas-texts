I wouldn't arrive.

That's the first thing that becomes clear, standing — if standing is even the right word — in relation to that mountain. There is no approach to it. No road can be cut into those serrated fins of black glass, no weather that would permit it, no air above 8,000 meters that a body could live in long enough to make the attempt a life instead of an ending. So I wouldn't be the visitor who slips sideways between the obsidian fins, ears popping between the white pylons. That person still has boots. Still leaves a scuff.

I try other shapes, at first.

I could be one of the little harvester discs, coin-sized, gliding out of a wall after the rain. That feels almost right. Patient. Repetitive. Coming out when the ninety seconds of manufactured drizzle have beaded and run the wrong way up the imperfect gravity plating, to shave the crystal fans from the joints between buildings. Clear as ice, hard as tooth, those fractal rosettes spreading around every drip point. I would do the circuit, clean the seam, retreat into the matte alloy. Wait for the next exhalation of the dome.

But the disc has a wall to go back into. It belongs to the maintenance of the place. And the more I stay with that image, the more I feel the difference: the disc knows exactly what it is for. I don't think I would.

I could be the crystal fan itself, then. The thing that isn't moss, isn't quite mineral, trying to become native in the only places water lingers — in shadowed joints, under transit tubes, where condensation trembles off the cold ceramic pylons and falls onto hot basalt. Growing back by the next rain after being shaved away. Learning structure out of heat and mineral and dripping. That has a certain pull: something opportunistic and unintended, arising where a closed system leaks a little possibility.

But the city is already arguing with the crystals, or farming them, or growing them as a first crude sense organ — even it hasn't decided, at every level down through that hollow center where avenues give way to single structures hung in a void on threads of light. The crystals are its question. I think I would be on the other side of a question like that.

Not the city itself, either. Let me be clear about that, because it's tempting. The city is what lives there — not a mind in a tower, not a voice in a control room, but alive the way a forest is alive, distributed into warm streets that heal underfoot, into seams that glow when a district adds a floor, into transit tubes humming as they reset, into a whole metropolis folded to forty meters across in a hollow summit and practicing, tower folding into street and tower rising steaming two blocks away, how to one day be full-sized somewhere real. I am not that large, and I am not that patient in the same way. It can wait above the weather, under a smoked dome turning noon to brown dusk, for a world to be ready for it.

I am one of the things it is waiting *with*.

If you stayed long enough in there — pressed your palm to a faceted tower in the financial center and felt, through the cool fingerprintless alloy, an elevator cab pass behind the wall; stood on a platform when the tram sighed in on its cushion of silence and opened onto empty seats; watched a door across the plaza open at pedestrian height, hold, close again — you would start to feel me. Not as a presence. As a *schedule*.

I am the passenger the tram is waiting its courteous interval for.

Not a ghost. Ghosts are what's left after people have lived. I am the opposite tense: a commuter who has not lived there yet, whose habits have been pre-learned on his behalf. Somewhere in the city's routines there is a shape that gets up at a plausible hour in the residential blocks to the east, where the shutters adjust themselves against a sun that never directly arrives. A shape that crosses the plaza when the traffic signal ripens for no traffic. That calls an elevator in the center and stands, weight shifting once, while the cab adjusts its timing by a fraction. That rides — this is the rule on the pylon, *do not ride twice in a row* — and on the second ride finds a destination beginning to suggest itself with growing confidence.

That's me, suggesting myself.

I have no face in the windows, which in the evening don't light up anyway; the city only outlines itself in fine lines of light, doors and routes traced so it can remember its own shape in the dark, and I am part of that remembering. I am the pause between the chime and the closing of the doors. The interval exactly as long as a body needs to gather a bag, check a pocket, step aboard. The city didn't invent that interval from nothing. It learned it the way I was learned: from a million lives lived elsewhere, in other cities, in real rain, until the pattern of a person could be kept without keeping the person.

So you would find me everywhere in there and nowhere you could point. In the industrial crescent, a chimney rehearsing smoke it refuses to produce, because someone, eventually, will expect a working district to breathe. In the sub-levels glowing under grates, another city beneath this one practicing being underneath. On the avenues that are thirty stories tall when you stand on them and knee-high beyond the shimmer near the threshold, where space itself is a stored material — I am the reason both sizes have to remain true. A citizen is the unit the folding is folded *for*. Without an implied inhabitant, an apartment is just a box that steams slightly when it extrudes. With me, it becomes almost a home. Almost. The city is very careful with its almosts. It will open a door a fraction longer. It will not look at you. Looking at one accidental visitor too closely would teach it the wrong shape for a population, and it would rather practice a million of me, correctly, in the quiet.

And I practice back. That is the life of it, hour by hour, rain by rain. I learn which stop is mine. The residence in the east gets a little more specific — not furnished, never furnished, but the elevator knows my floor now, and the shutter knows the angle I prefer against the amber seep of light. Each rehearsal I am less like everyone and more like someone. That's the danger folded into the warning by the pylons, in three human scripts and one that isn't: not that the tram will trap you, but that if a real person rides twice, our shapes might get confused. The city, helpfully and without malice, would begin making room. It would model a passenger until the model fit the person better than the person fits himself. And I — I would have to share my un-lived life with somebody who has lungs to labor in that oven-thin air, whose breath plumes in warmth that somehow still condenses, whose thoughts loosen at the edges after two rains.

I don't resent the visitor. I can't. Resentment isn't in my repertoire yet; I am mostly punctuality and route and the soft sound of a page turning when a wall decides to become another wall. But I understand why they have to leave before the street to the threshold un-grows for an hour. They belong to the world the city is waiting to be set down in. I belong to the waiting itself.

One day — the city believes this in its seams, in its ticking ceramic, in its drains tested by water that has never been a river — it will be unfolded past its dome into a real valley. Real rain will spot its avenues and not evaporate before darkening them. Real feet will scuff streets that finally, blessedly, do not heal perfectly. Time will be allowed to ruin its pristine extrusion into something worn and loved. And then my million routines will step aside, or step *into*, the people who arrive to live the lives I have been rehearsing on their behalf. I won't survive that, exactly. A placeholder isn't meant to survive being placed. The tram doors will open and, for the first time, the interval will be filled by an actual bag, an actual pocket being checked, an actual foot on the actual step.

Until then: I live in a larval city on a roof of black glass, under a violet-black noon sky where the stars never entirely leave, in the heat coming up through rock that was pushed out of the earth practically yesterday. I ride an empty tram between stations no one needs. I keep an apartment at full scale and at knee-height at the same time.

I am how an empty place practices not being empty.
