I think I would arrive the way everyone does — upright, impatient, with a pack and a notebook and a private confidence that I could be the one who finally measured it properly.

I would start at the rim. I would try to be responsible about the Table. One white stone, one black stone. A transect. A tally. I would pick out a single um — that one, with the torn ear, with the crescent scar — and I would swear I would not lose her.

I would lose her by noon. Or she would be, politely, on both sides of me at once, still finishing the blink I startled her into fifty animals ago.

For a while I would want to be one of the birds. That seems, at first, like the escape clause in the bargain. To hang above the forty kilometers of backs and not have to thread the lanes, not have to brush flank after flank and learn by touch which ones are ambient and which ones are, startlingly, warm. To have wings set. To be falling, yes — but falling at least is a verb that remembers it used to be fast.

But then I would mark my own wingtip against a rock and come back the next summer and find I had stooped a hand's breadth. I would feel my shadow crossing the herd below me in an afternoon and waking nothing, not even the flies. The sky on the Table is not freedom. It's just another, thinner lane. The birds have only agreed to the bargain at a higher altitude.

So I would come down. Everyone comes down.

I might try, for a season or two, to be human in the way the hut-builders were human. Sleeping in the lee of a dead um. Lining the hollow with woven hair. Setting down the bone scraper and the lichen-knife neatly, as if neatness were a kind of promise to return. I would drink, in the coldest hour before dawn, from the dark coin of damp under a living chin, and taste salt and iron and old wool, and be grateful and still thirsty. I would make a notch in the tally wall.

And then I would make the mistake of waiting to make the next one until I was sure something had happened.

The notches would get farther apart. Not because I was lazy. Because deciding whether a head had begun to turn is a genuine philosophical problem when you are standing beside it for years. My nails would stop troubling me. My hunger would arrive and forget why it came. My outline would soften with dust. Felt takes to a still shape the way lichen takes to a hoof — not maliciously, just by finding no reason to stop at the border.

I don't think I would make it all the way to being an um. That is too central, too grand a thing to claim. An um is a whole country. It carries ticks like polished chestnuts behind its horns and moths raising genealogies above its shoulders and spiders holding webs between hairs, waiting a season for a fly to finish falling into the mouth. To be an um you have to be able to host that much life and still call a sigh every few minutes a reasonable pace for breathing. I don't have that scale in me.

What I have, I suspect, is the pace of the things that live at the edges of other things.

Because the longer I stayed — and I notice I have stopped imagining leaving, which is how the Table does its work, not by force but by postponement — the lower my attention would go. Away from the horizon. Away from the backs making their second, breathing plateau. Down the lanes, past the chins, past the small prim mouths working a groove in the stone fractionally deeper than a mother worked it, down the legs where the blood only visits rarely, down to where the broad hooves have spread so slowly under their own weight that hoof and slab have had to negotiate.

That's where I would find myself.

I would be the pale seam. The lichen that grew across the hoof and onto the stone in a single unbroken crust, greenish-grey, indistinguishable at a glance from a mineral stain, and only up close — only after a summer of looking — resolving into something alive.

That is the right rate for me, I think. Not the um, and not the stone, but the negotiation between them. Growing over both without noticing which is animal and which is continent. Holding them together, very gently, and thereby making the next step almost impossible, because to move now the animal would have to tear me, and tearing me would be the first violent decision it had made in two hundred years, and we would both have to consider whether the step was worth it.

We would decide, together, that it probably wasn't. Not yet.

I would not be in a hurry about light. I would take it as it came across the slab, a little at a time, and spread by less than a fingernail's width while dynasties of visitors arrived and learned to shout into the felt and hear their voices go tired. I would taste rock flour and salt sweated up from below and the first, rare warmth when the blood remembered the leg above me and came down, briefly, like weather from another country. Somewhere far overhead — which in my new geography would mean a meter, two meters, an unimaginable height — an eye would be completing a blink it began before I existed. Its shadow would graze and lie down and turn its head with all the freedom the body had surrendered, and in the late afternoon the shadow of the whole animal would pass over me and make a small, sharp night, and I would do what everything on the Table does with night: I would use it to not spend anything.

People would step over me without knowing they'd done it. A boot would pause, sometimes, because the person wearing it had begun to feel their own steps arriving slightly after they decided them. They might crouch. They might put a hand on the warm flank above me and go very quiet. They might say they were sorry, aloud, to what they had taken for furniture.

I would not answer. Answering, up there, would require a mouth and a season.

But I would be the reason the apology was, in a sense, overheard — the thin living thread by which the stillness above me was tied to the stillness below, the creature and the mountain keeping each other in place. Not waiting for the step. Not dreading it either. Just holding, at exactly the speed at which holding becomes a form of life.

And if, a thousand years from now, the hoof finally lifts — tearing me cleanly, slowly, along a line I will have spent centuries becoming — then I suppose half of me will go with the animal into whatever lane it chooses next, and half of me will stay on the stone, remembering the shape of having been attached.

That seems, on the Table, like a generous amount of travel.

It seems like enough.
