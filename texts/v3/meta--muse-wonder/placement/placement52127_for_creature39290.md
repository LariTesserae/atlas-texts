I don't think I would arrive there as a visitor, not for long. A visitor is a number of cups a day, and I would feel that counting too keenly at first — the basin of water handed to me, the extra place at a table indoors out of the draft, the kindness in it and the arithmetic in it at the same time. I would walk the town end to end in seven minutes and think I had seen it. Then I would stop, because the place invites stopping, and start to cross all that empty stone between one door and the next, and it would take much longer.

At first I try to place myself in the louder lives, such as they are. Not the smith, I think — his ringing carries too cleanly over the flags, spaced and public, and then stops for days. I would be self-conscious making a sound that everyone could place to a lane. Not a child either, running in the paved gaps, though I envy them that: footsteps louder than their calling, already knowing not to fill an empty plot just because it is empty. And not the raven. The raven passes over the square most days, alone, but it does not live by the cistern. It only crosses the measure other things live by.

I think I would be found earlier than that. Before light, in fact. Lying still for a moment in a cold room, listening.

That is where the town would get into me: not in the square at midday, with the light white at the edges and shade genuinely cold, but at dawn, judging the night by sound. A thin, irregular ticking off the roof-slopes. A slower bead and drop from the tilted slabs behind the cistern-house. A barrel taking water one patient note at a time. I would learn to know, without rising, whether it had been a lean night or a sufficient one — whether beans could be soaked without anxiety, whether washing could be done, whether the week would be tight in that calm, unembarrassed way these people are tight about everything. I would rise and check the level the way other people look at the sky.

My house would not be a remarkable one. Perhaps the third house on the east lane, terraced into the slope so that my doorstep is level with the roof below me, and someone else's doorstep is level with mine. Older, rougher stone at the bottom of my walls, squarer stone above. A lintel my grandfather replaced, and then my mother replaced again, adzed to the same profile so that you would have to be told it had ever changed. I would be proud of my roof in the unspoken way they are proud of such things — not that it is grand, but that the slates bead dew well. That they were set at exactly the right tilt, a long time ago, and that I have kept them that way, one slate at a time, same thickness, same line.

I would know what my house holds. I would also roughly know what my neighbours' houses hold, because that is manners there, and survival, and not gossip exactly, though gossip does live in the cold doorway of the cistern-house, dosed out carefully with everything else.

My work would not have a clean name. That seems right. A little of everything by season: beans in the most sheltered row of a walled garden, onions, bitter greens, herbs with small fierce leaves grown stronger for growing slowly. Carrying wood. Shaking out cloth two lanes away and hearing how far the sound travels at midday when the town is still. Helping clear the shallow channels in the lanes before the wet weather, down on my knees with short nails and thickened knuckles, leaving the wiry grass between the flags alone unless it blocks a drain. That lesson would suit me, and take me longest to learn in my bones: not everything growing must be used. Not everything empty must be filled.

I would wear the wool layer until noon, take it off for the warmest hour if the season allows, put it on again before supper, and stop finding the rhythm fussy. I would move in and out of sun and shadow the way other people move between rooms. Set a bench where the wall holds the last warmth. Stand talking in a patch of late light. Understand the lizards on the sun-warmed walls at noon, vanishing into mortar cracks when a shadow crosses them — not as a metaphor, just as neighbours with the same information about the day.

In time — a long time, because everyone there is long-lived and bent a little in the same direction, like the two stunted pines by the lower gate — I would become part of the archive. That is the part that draws me most, and settles, slowly, into place. Because the repairs are made to be invisible, someone has to remember. I would be one of the ones consulted, not because I know stories in the valley sense, but because I remember that this beam only looks like oak and that one is, that this slate was replaced forty years ago after a frost and that one never has been, that the cistern under my own house cracked two generations back and was sealed in a particular way, and should be checked first if the level drops. The true map of the town is not the lanes. It is the history of maintenance. I would carry my share of it quietly, and hand it on in short, finished sentences, with long comfortable gaps between them.

Someone in my house would have left for the valley when we were young, and not come back, and that would not be a tragedy in the house. It would be the shape of things. Perhaps I waited some years myself — not for romance in the valley sense, but for a roof. For a widow to move in with her sister, for a line to end, for the meeting hall to reassign catchment rights along with stone walls. Outsiders would mistake that patience for indifference. It wouldn't be. It would be proportion learned so young it stopped feeling like a bargain.

If you came to stay with me, I would welcome you, and I would have done the sum first, and neither of us would be embarrassed by that. We have enough. You fit inside it. We would eat plain, hot food indoors — dense bread from the communal bakehouse, beans, greens, a little goat cheese — and in the morning you would hear me listening before I got up, learning the day from the dripping.

By late afternoon I would be in the square, not doing anything that needed a name. Flagstones worn concave underfoot. Thin smoke rising. Someone's voice at normal pitch carrying cleanly and dying at the next corner. The air already cooling on my forearms. Light normal and level, not golden, not grand. I would not feel diminished by the limits of that high saddle of grey rock — sixty or seventy roofs, no new one unless its roof can feed its people, cloud sometimes lying below us in the morning so that we seem briefly detached from the world.

I would feel finished. A person made to measure: quiet-voiced, deft-handed, content to sweep a plot that will stay empty, to polish a threshold my grandparents polished, and to gather, night after night, the slow dew that lets a complete little world go on being itself, exactly one more day.
