I would meet myself first with my ears, and that would confuse me, because the sound would not resolve into a voice I could call mine.

Coming up the stairs from the forest — no, not coming. Already there, on the stairs, before I reached them. A ringing in the hollow stone underfoot, cicadas in the warm hours making the teeth ache. A high electric twittering that never switched off. Parrots going off a cornice in a shrieking sheet and settling again on the same cornice, still shrieking, and under all of it, lower, pulsing, frogs in the dark round mouths of the drains.

If I tried to find my body by listening, I would find too many of them.

For a while I would think I must be the loud ones on top. That is the first skin of the place, and the easiest to see: the wall-crowns, the rim terraces, the fig canopies that took the place of roofs. Green and scarlet and blue-headed, shoulder to shoulder along cornices cut to shed water and now shedding feathers, whitened in the same places generation after generation. I would explode upward at a shadow, at a monkey's footfall, at nothing, a whole ledge of myself screaming at once — and then I would do the stranger thing, which is settle exactly where I had been. Not near it. On it. The branch polished by feet before mine. The roost that remembered me longer than I remembered myself.

But I would also be underneath that noise, in the water, and I would be much smaller and much less inclined to move.

Every cistern half-full under its mat of ferns. Every canal running an inch deep and shining with algae, or dry, or carrying one brown pulse after rain. In each drain mouth, a throat pulsing in the opening. In each bromeliad cup packed into a niche, a life no bigger than a thumbprint, calling from its cup of held rain. I would not be *in* the city's water there. I would be a condition of it. When the mist sat until late morning and beaded on feathers and condensed on stone, I would be in a thousand pockets at once, and at dusk the whole stonework would thrum with me until it seemed the canals themselves were calling.

And still that wouldn't account for the daylight above the frogs, the continuous weather of swifts stippling every cornice and drain hole, swallows mudding their cups beakful by beakful onto carved faces, weaverbirds hanging a doorway so full of green-and-yellow pendant nests that it swayed and cheeped when the air moved. I would be building there, but with the wrong materials for a bird and the right ones for the city: mud where mortar failed, stitch and saliva where thatch rotted, a nest supplemented season after season until the supplementation *was* the structure.

Then the wall-tops would move under me and I would be on them, because the wall-tops are roads and I would use them as roads. Nails clicking on stone worn smooth by exactly this traffic. A troop of me picking, squabbling, carrying infants and stolen mangoes from the feral orchards, dropping rinds into the courts below — where another me, tail up, nosed single-file through the leaf litter, and another darted and froze and darted across a collapsed house, and a family band of me rooted and snuffled through the damp fill until a floor that no hands had ploughed in centuries was turned as thoroughly as a field.

I would drop seeds onto the plaza slabs at midday and hear them ring like bells around the vultures — also me, the only still thing in the one reliable patch of sun, wings spread to dry the night's damp out of my feathers, finches and doves moving around my feet through a century of seed and droppings ground into the joints. I would look at anything that walked through me with a bright, entitled stare. Not bravery. Tenancy. Nothing has hunted me here for so long that flight has become an inconvenience: I flow six feet sideways, annoyed, and resettle, and watch it pass.

That is when it would stop being possible to pick the parrot over the frog over the monkey, because evening would come early in the shadowed bowl, blue and then simply gone, and the tenants would change shifts in the same niches.

The dark under the terraces — true warm dark, breathing dark — is not the empty part of me. It may be the largest part. Bats packed in the undercroft chambers, pouring out at dusk in rivers and pouring back before dawn over guano deep and soft and sweet-smelling, feeding beetles without number. A kinkajou-me unfolding from sleep in the roof spaces to walk the beams upside down. An owl-me taking the room the bats vacated. Whole chambers of me seething behind a doorway that smelled of warm fermented honey before I reached it, wasps papering the undersides of my lintels, termites building a second earthen city directly over the stone one, skinning my towers in mud that was also inhabited. Ants running my carved gutters in permanent highways, thousands of green leaf-flags moving uphill until the aqueducts themselves seemed to be flowing with vegetation.

And under even that, slower than any animal, I would be holding myself up. A fig that began in a crack, split a corner tower, and thickened around it until its trunks were load-bearing and its canopy was the roof. Moss stitching a joint that feet loosened. Dung and leaf-fall and collapsed nests sifting, a fraction at a time, into the fill behind the retaining stones. Rain hammering down for twenty minutes and finding a thousand designed ways through me — spouts, channels, stepped falls — and where the design failed, my roots improvising a slower one. I would subside a centimeter in a decade and call that swiftness.

So I would not be a species in that valley. There is no king of it in me to find.

I would be the occupancy itself — partitioned by stone, by shade, by hour, floor by floor, so old and so continuous that the partition is the body. Birds and monkeys and ringing teeth by day. Fireflies doubled in the lower canal pockets by night, and frogs louder, and the rustle and squeak and thump of ten thousand nocturnal bodies moving through rooms sized, conveniently, for bodies. The same ledge, the same black chamber, the same frog-plumbed drain, inherited not as territory but as address. Fruit coming in its slow rotation — fig, then mango, then wild avocado rotting sweet on warm stone and drawing clouds of iridescent flies — and all my processions shifting their routes along the wall-roads from orchard to orchard, as they must have shifted when the fruit was a harvest.

If you climbed to the high wall to steady your sense of scale, and looked out over rank after rank of green, animal-crowded terraces filling the bowl to its rim, you would not be looking at wildlife that had reclaimed a ruin.

You would be looking at me in my second incarnation. Feathered and furred and rooting and rooted. The maintenance crew that replaced hands with beaks and claws and saliva and dung and never stopped maintaining. A stone town that passed, over generations of dwindling, to the only builders who stayed — and learned, niche by niche, shift by shift, to keep itself alive by being lived in exactly as fully as it was built to be.

I am not the inhabitant of that place.

I am its unbroken inhabiting.
