I come down in the cage the way everyone does, with my palms pressed flat to the wire so the roots can pat the outside of the car and tell themselves what they've already decided: not food, not yet, just another noisy thing arriving.

I don't stay up on the canopy decks. I tried to imagine myself there at first — up among the pod-cutters and the bat stacks, where the light comes through strongest and the air, when the exhaust vents, goes soft with wings. It's beautiful up there. It is also too exposed. Every gap in the chorus is audible. A leaf that has worked its way across a catwalk in the night makes a particular hush as it takes the vibration out of the boards, a green felt spreading over a drumhead, and I could never learn to ignore it the way the cutters do. They step over it, sing louder, get on with work. I would stand there with my tools forgotten, staring at the silence making its little advance, until someone rapped me hard between the shoulder blades to bring me back.

Not a cutter, then. Not Boiler Row either, though I liked it lower down — the bowl of the root-floor worn smooth by boots and claws, the pumps clanking in a rhythm so total it came up through the soles of my feet and rearranged my heartbeat into something more useful. Old men down there, deaf in the talking range since forty, cup their ears out of habit and feel the boilers through their teeth instead, and sleep like held children. I envied them that certainty. But I don't have the chest for the Row, or the arms for the hulling floor, and my voice, such as it is, isn't a big one. It doesn't carry across Leafmarket. It was never going to hold up a ceiling by itself.

For a while I thought I might be more like the other citizens. A coin-frog in the condensation pipes, say — invisible, ecstatic, turning every drain and speaking tube and faucet in town into the inside of my own throat, so that a whole street could be woken by one small body singing in the dark of the plumbing. There's honesty in that. Or one of the forearm-long isopods, armor beaded with moisture, going my slow rounds eating rust and spilled oil and fallen leaf, children allowed to ride me a solemn few feet before a parent intervenes. Useful. Tolerated. Unremarkable in the best way, part of the reef.

Or even — this is the thought that comes to me in the plaza when the gnats hold their three-second architecture, an arch, a hand, a face, and everyone pauses because the air is moving right — I thought I might be something like the gear-shaped leaves that grow in the vibration shadow of the machines. Serrated, symmetrical, perfectly useless as gears. The Acre trying to imitate the thing it can't digest, and producing proof instead of a weapon. A pattern shaped by noise it doesn't understand. I know something about that.

But none of those stayed true when I followed where I actually go.

Because I notice the gaps.

I notice them before I notice anything else — the bell on a Tenement catwalk that didn't ring when the vibration of footsteps should have rung it, because a shade-leaf has grown over its mouth in the night. The cart coming up from the cistern pits too quietly, one wheel wrapped in shed root-skin, each rotation a small erasure. A house on the second terrace where there was an argument yesterday, a good loud brass-leaf household, pots and teenagers and a grandmother singing tunelessly at her mending — and today nothing. Not rest. Stillness. The kind that wakes children here the way fire alarms wake children on the surface.

My hand is tapping something before I've decided to. A railing. A boiler lid hung by a door. My own collarbone, two knocks: *I'm here. Look at me.*

That's the job the organists would have found for me if I'd apprenticed — they take the ones with bad nerves, who can't abide a wrong note, who surface in the night because somewhere across town a root has laid itself over a gong and the whole chord of the place has gone one degree duller. I wouldn't have lasted at the steam organ itself; it's too holy and too loud, all that bored trunk and boiler pipe holding a note that never resolves, the cicadas adding their unordered fifth from the venting stacks. But the organ's work is only the center of a work that happens everywhere, at the edges, in doorways, in houses gone still with grief or sickness or simple exhaustion. Someone has to go and be loud *on behalf* of the quiet.

So that's who I am here. I am a still-house visitor.

I have a bucket, and in the bucket a borrowed frog, a good one, a dog-sized ditch bellower with a voice like a drain being unblocked by God. I have a sack of hollow seed-case bells, donkey bells, a dented boiler lid and a striker wrapped in copper wire so it doesn't bite my palm. I have my hair cropped close against the damp and a fine green softness along my jaw that I comb like everyone else, and I walk the root-streets barefoot because I need to know through the floor whether the pumps are running sweet and whether the Acre has put a new feeder root under the iron plate on the next street while we were all being proud of ourselves.

I visit the house where the baby died, and I do not ask the parents to make noise. That's the whole point, though no one says it that way. I hang my lid outside their resin-glass window, amber and bubbled, and I sit on their step and tap it, steady, unhurried, and gossip — one-sided if it has to be — about Leafmarket prices, about a millipede train that lost its rhythm-keeper and walked its cart in a dignified circle for twenty minutes, about the mantis-colleague up on the high catwalks and how a new rivet-man forgot to step aside and got looked at in a way that aged him. Inside, behind me, two people get to be silent. Their grief is not interrupted by me. It's *covered* by me. The Acre, pressing its slow green attention against the border of the sound, hears a house still occupied, still defended, and moves on to test some other seam.

I visit the old distiller whose cough has taken his shout, before he makes the move down to Boiler Row. I bring the frog. The frog approves of his warm outflow ditch and says so, at intervals, magnificently, through the grate. We sit and don't compete with that. He touches my wrist when he wants me to pour more water. Tap-language. The first fluent thing I learned here.

I visit households that are only temporarily thin — everyone away on clamor-shift at once, nobody home but a sleeping apprentice — and I take my turn with the neighbors hanging bells, because a brass-leaf tax credit is a fine thing and a civic duty is a finer one, and because down here those are the same sentence.

It is not hero's work. It doesn't stop anything. The plant is patient and hungry in the way growing things are hungry, without malice and without limit, and it has already eaten the streets at the edges where the lamp posts stand leafed in spirals, the house fronts windowed shut like eyelids. One day it'll test a seam I'm sitting on. That's not pessimism, it's the root-floor ticking under my feet after the Clamor, when the pods have popped like distant gunfire through wet cloth and everyone stands grinning with their hands over their ears, rich in falling seed. In the lull you can hear what all the noise is for. Sap moving in a trunk like blood in a wrist held to an ear. My wrist. The striker rises and falls anyway.

*Still safe,* I tap, into iron, into bark, into the warm vibrating wall of someone else's rest. *Still safe, still safe.*

Not winning. Never winning. Just loud enough, in the one place where loudness was needed, for the person next to me to be quiet a little while longer — and that's a way of living here that the Acre, for all its patience, has never quite learned to digest.
