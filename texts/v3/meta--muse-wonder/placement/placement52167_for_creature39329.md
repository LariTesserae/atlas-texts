I wouldn't be out on the eastern rim with the windmills. That's too far from the lanes, too much horizon. And I wouldn't be in the western sheds for a whole life, either — though I would have done my years there, like everyone does, and kept the iron in my fingertips long after I left.

I think I'd start where Merrow children start: near the roof.

Born small, as we are, kept for weeks in the steamy upper rooms where the drying laundry hangs and a parent's chest does the work of teaching you the air. Slow breaths. Slower heart. Learning altitude before cold, damp before light. Then old enough to be sent up before dawn, barefoot on slate I know by toe, sponge in one hand and bucket banging soft against my shin — not really awake, arguing in whispers with a cousin about whose turn it is to do the north pitch, wringing cloud-water out by feel while the town is still only thirty meters wide in the mist.

That's where my hands would come from. Short nails. Palms ridged from wringing. Fingers that learned surfaces before faces, almost — the tick of a clean fin, the drag where lichen is starting, the place a slate has sagged just enough that the water sheets instead of beads.

Grown, I wouldn't inherit one of the great condenser walls down by the mills. Those belong to the old families, a hundred meters of finned metal and a hundred years of obligation along with it. I'd belong to one of the roof cooperatives instead. We string nets between chimneys. We share angles. A good member isn't the strongest — it's the one whose nets are up when the white river comes crawling in from the west, whose gutters were clean before anyone had to ask.

So my day wouldn't belong to the sun. It would belong to the ticking.

I'd wake at three in the morning because the eaves have started. I'd lie there a minute in the dark, in wool that never quite dries and never quite needs to, listening into the house: cistern-frog quiet under the floor, grandmother shifting in the steady middle rooms below where the temperature holds, steam from yesterday's cooking still sweet in the walls. Then up, hood stiffened to shed to the shoulders, the little copper gutter at my collar already cold against my neck, funneling a clean thread of mist into the flask at my hip as I cross the covered walkways roof to roof.

In fog I would work close and quiet. A hand on someone's forearm to speak. The small signs we all learn before proper sentences: *open, close, listen, your sluice, pool rising, come down.* I would be good at the listening part, I think. Not dramatic-good. Merrow-good. I would stop mid-step with my head tilted when a gutter starts spilling at a joint instead of filling even. I'd know whose roof is leaking by its rhythm. I'd hear a frog chorus stop on the far side of the lane and know a hollow is about to overtop before the bright thread of water ever reaches a workshop door.

And I'd mistrust anything too sharp. Too loud, too sealed, too certain. I would never strip moss from a north wall, not even my own if it meant leaving stone to freeze and split. I'd keep my surfaces ready and forgive almost anything before I'd forgive waste — a fouled fin, a net left furled through good mist. My feuds, if I had them, would be small and long: a meter of wall, a drip-line a neighbor's new fin has stolen from my roof garden, an angle my grandfather set that I will defend for thirty years out of loyalty disguised as engineering.

My garden would lean away from the sky, toward the longest weep. Bitter greens and sour things in tubs, coaxed along a drip. I'd eat downstairs where it's hot and preserved and sharply flavored against the damp, and I'd stand too close to people in the market during the two clear hours after midday, talking a little too fast in the pale light, blinking, enjoying clarity like a festival before we all pack away again ahead of the evening water.

On my way home I'd do what everyone does without deciding to. Passing one of the old boulders we build around — moss to the second story on the wall beside it, lichen white and orange in the cracks — I'd lay my palm flat against it. Still noon-cool at sunset. Still wet. Faintly metallic. Checking the day. Greeting it.

By night I'd take the quiet shift sometimes. Not sleeping, not exactly working. Walking the lanes where sound lies to you — a factory whistle landing intimate in your ear from across town, your own footstep muffled to nothing — and listening for the town doing its real work. Roofs ticking. Fins weeping. Pools rising by fractions in the black hollows beyond the last houses. The trams stopped. The sheds down to a few amber windows.

I wouldn't be a mountaineer. I wouldn't want a peak, or a view that insists, or a sky that aggressive blue the lowlanders brag about. Leave Merrow long enough and I'd get homesick for exactly what visitors can't stand: flat horizons, weather you can hear coming for hours, voices kept low, hands that know a wall in the dark.

I'd be waiting, mostly. Broad through the chest, cloud-pale, chilblains netted across my knuckles, boots silent on wet stone. Ready not because I'd conquered anything up there —

but because when the mist comes in, sideways and total, I'd already be clean, angled, and listening.
