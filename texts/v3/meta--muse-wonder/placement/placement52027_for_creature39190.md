I would enter by going down, and I don't think I would make it all the way down at first.

At the rim I'd be useless. I'd stand there squinting like they do, only for the opposite reason — not blighted by the wind, but unwilling to trust air that stays still. I'd keep looking across, expecting distance. In the pit there isn't any. Nine minutes end to end, an hour bottom to top, and all of it folded over itself. I would keep losing the street I was just on behind a cistern, behind a kitchen, behind a boiler shed that had a bedroom built on its roof because there was nowhere else flat to put a bed.

I wouldn't be a boiler tender. I thought I might be, at first — drawn to the center of it, the lagged engines, the pressure you feel in your teeth. But those people read the plant the way you read a sleeping child's breathing. They hear a mood through rock and sheet iron. I don't have that kind of attention. Mine snags on small things.

Not the rim courts, either, where they argue shade in deeds and inheritances. Not the freight lift, though I can picture myself there for a while, riding up and down with the sealed crates, everything tinned and frozen and never having walked, watching people's faces change as we pass out of the sodium orange into the green galleries. The lift goes everywhere and belongs nowhere. I'd get restless in it. Or maybe I'd get too comfortable.

I'd settle, the way water does, where the temperature changes.

Halfway down. The middle terraces.

That's where the rain starts and doesn't finish. Grey under the cold loop, beading, falling, thinning in the rising heat, gone before it touches the hot roofs below. If you stand there long enough your shoulders get stippled — not wet, exactly, but mineral. A thousand drops almost landing on you and leaving salt where they evaporated. I'd learn to wipe it away with the back of my wrist without thinking about it. I'd learn to walk the way they walk, as if through shallow water, because hurrying is just a way of cooking yourself from the inside.

I think I'd be the one with the brush.

Not own it — nobody owns it. It passes household to household along the terrace, because the beans won't set without it. They grow in troughs under the reliable drips, pale, stringy things, leaves almost white, and there are no bees in the pit. No flies, no wings crossing the forty minutes of noon sun, nothing to do that small, necessary moving-between. So people do it themselves, one flower at a time, in the coolest hour, with a concentration that looks like prayer until you get close enough to see it's also gossip, and tiredness, and hands glad of delicate work after a day of pipe joints.

I'd carry the brush from door to door.

That would be my way of belonging to places I didn't live. Announcing my steps on the ladder bolted through somebody's kitchen, looking at the rungs and not the room. Not hearing what the partition made it impossible not to hear, and having that not-hearing returned to me, generously, when my own partition was thin. A hand at my elbow in an alley the width of my shoulders. A palm, flat and brief, against my back on the stairs, to pass. Never an embrace. Embrace is heat.

I'd learn the terraces plant by plant. Which trough belongs to which leaking joint. Which household's pipe runs clean enough that the third drip can be drunk, and which one still tastes of the north boiler running lean, iron on the tongue. I'd scrape the bright moss out of the joints with a spoon sometimes, while I waited for the flowers to open — delicately, because you don't tear it. It's tea, and it's a poultice for the soft infections that bloom where a cool cistern sweats into a hot alley. I'd get good at knowing which handrails are handrails and which are pipes that will take skin. My hands would get educated before the rest of me did.

There's a kind of animal the pit had to invent because it killed all the others, and I think I would end up, quietly, being part of that invention. Not grand. Not the engines. The substitute bee. The pollinator. The thing that moves between flowers because nothing else with wings survived the week down here.

At shift change I'd stop wherever I was when the felt-wrapped bell struck once, and stand in that sudden, synchronized silence with four thousand other people, brush in hand, and rest in it the way you rest in shade.

And at night — real night, up above, only the pit glowing orange and green from below — I'd be out on the iron balcony with everyone else who wasn't tending anything for a while. Wet curtains pushed aside. Bean plants ticking with condensation overhead. The rain falling a little further than it does in the day before the heat takes it. I'd turn my face up toward the rim, toward the cold loop where our weather is born, not expecting it to reach me.

Just almost. Just enough, and no more than the heat allows.
