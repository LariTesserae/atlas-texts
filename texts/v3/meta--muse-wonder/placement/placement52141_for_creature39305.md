First I would hear it, and then a long time after that I would arrive.

That part, I think, would not be optional. I wouldn't come up the three thousand steps as a visitor quite, because by the time my body got there my bones would already have been living there for hours — the note up through the soles, up through the hammer I didn't yet know I was carrying. I'd be tuned before I was present. My shadow might beat me by more than that.

At first I'd try to be the wrong kind of person very diligently.

I'd try to map it. I can feel that in myself — the finished-world instinct to make a record that stays put. I'd carry slate after slate, I'd write down distances between the huts and the cairns, I'd time the crossing of a grove I could throw a stone over and get annoyed when it took an hour. I'd write *north* and watch the word become meaningless in loud ground where distances swell. I'd keep talking, too, at first. Not shouting. Just explaining. Filling the thin air with thin sound that the air immediately took, while the real sound went on underneath, unimpressed, through my teeth.

The plateau would be patient with that. Nothing here is in a hurry to correct you. It just continues adding itself to itself until you run out of the habits you brought.

My lips would crack. I'd learn the black oil, copper and mint on my mouth. My ears would become almost decorative — I'd stop turning my head toward sounds, because there is only the one sound, and it doesn't come from a direction. It comes from below. From before. I'd start listening the way you listen to your own pulse: not with attention, exactly, but with the whole architecture. Sternum first, I'd like to think. Then the shins. A low knowing in the long bones.

I'd ache between the eyebrows for a week from learning to speak properly.

That's where I'd begin to find my actual place, I suspect — not in the speaking, but in the small bow before it. Forehead offered. Forehead withheld. I have always been better at the pressure of a thought than at its pronunciation. Here, that would stop being a flaw and become an etiquette. I'd press my brow to someone else's and feel how much care it takes not to press too hard. Too hard is shouting. I'd learn the exact weight of *good morning*. The exact weight of *no, leave that cairn alone*. The exact, furious concentration of being understood without making anything grow that I didn't mean to plant.

Because I would be afraid of speaking aloud. Rightly.

I'd become a slate person. Given a slate before a name I could say — well, I already had a name, in the world below, but up here I'd let it go quiet in my mouth until it stopped feeling like something I was allowed to spend carelessly. I'd write young again. Short sentences. Chalky, careful hands. *Walk in the loud places. Rest in the moderately loud.* I'd copy the wisdoms by the forge not because they were rules but because my hand needed to learn the slowness my mouth hadn't.

And at the forge — I keep coming back to the forge.

Not to make anything edged. There is nothing to kill and edges haven't been invented yet in any lasting way, and I find I don't miss them. I'd be the one who tends the resonators. The frames of woven reed-fiber stretched with plant hide, the primitive quiet-rooms you stand inside when you need to think in softer bone. I'd learn how long a person can stand in one before they come out blinking and a little unstrung, loosened by the near-silence. I'd hold the frame steady. I'd pass them their slate when they emerged because their first impulse afterward would be to speak, and speaking just after quiet is the most dangerous kind — too much pressure behind it, too clear a vowel.

I'd do other small tunings. That's the common work and it would suit my hands: moving one stone on a cairn in a pass and then standing very still through my feet to feel whether a mile of country had re-tuned or begun, crisply, to die. Dishing a floor. Repairing a roofless hut knowing a roof would sing itself apart. Carrying copper spikes I rarely used. Gathering the seeds at the fading of their note — I'd get good at that, I think, at hearing the bell *before* the leaf shatters and moving my hand an inch to where the consequence is about to be. Black-shelled. Oil and ash and that almost-green bitterness on the tongue afterward.

I would live alone, the way they do. One hut, maybe two of us in the cold season, never more than could touch foreheads in the hollow of the floor. Company up there is too intimate to be casual, and I think I'd be grateful for a world that finally admitted that. I'd visit rarely and mean it completely when I did.

But the place I'd really settle — it arrives late in my imagining, the way light arrives late on the plateau, twenty minutes after sunrise, an hour — is one of the burst huts.

The one choked from the inside by a single glossy tree, leaves recurved and cupped like mouths around a vowel someone spoke a generation ago and couldn't take back. Still growing very slowly. Still saying it.

Someone has to tend those, surely. The word-trees. The honest members of families. I'd volunteer before anyone asked. I'd clear the pumice from around its roots. I'd read, on the old slates left in the hut, whatever context survived — whose child it was, whose grief, whether the word was *home* or *stay* or just the open *oh* of someone surprised by their own life. And when the dusk came down over half a day and the tone dropped its quarter-step and all the groves sighed shut, largest to smallest, I'd put my forehead to that trunk and listen to a person still finishing a syllable they'd begun before I was born.

I would not try to finish it for them. That's the discipline I'd spend my life learning. Not to argue with precedence. Not to correct an echo.

Maybe, eventually, I'd say one word of my own. I'd choose the ground the way you choose a grave or a marriage bed — walk out with one or two people who knew me by my brow, out to a grove already speaking, waist-deep ferns shivering at the low octave, dust hanging in its rings and lattices at leaf-height. I'd say only what I was willing to harvest later. I won't write what it would be. Even here, even hypothetically — keep your mouth shut in untuned ground. Some words you don't rehearse in a lesser resonance.

And if I died up there — when — I wouldn't expect a grave. I'd expect someone who loved me to walk to a pass and change a note by one small stone, and a mile of plants to re-tune, and somewhere a grove to thin where I would no longer be fed. That would be enough of a burial. To continue as a local alteration in what can grow.

In the meantime, my shadow would get ahead of me more and more often. On the virgin ash on the far side of a grove I'd find my own footprints already softening at the edges, tiny black seedlings unfurling from compressions I hadn't made. And I'd do what they do, what I'm apparently already learning to do just by imagining it: sit down beside it in the loud ground. Not frightened. Not impatient. Forehead bare. Mouth shut. Slate on my knees.

Waiting politely for myself to catch up to someone I have, in the ringing air above that unfinished mountain, evidently already become.
