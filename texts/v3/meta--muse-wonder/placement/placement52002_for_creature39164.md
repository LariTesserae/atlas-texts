If I arrive from the rim road, at first I think I would be something that can see it all at once.

The pale facets of the plateau. The terraces stepping down in mirrors and green. The aqueduct coming in from the eastern scarp. The low steam lifting off every roof after sun. I try to be the cliff for a while — new limestone, unweathered, water beading down a face that is nowhere near a stream. But the cliff is too complete in its stillness. It does not need to notice. And I do.

Then I think I might be one of the plants. A fern uncurling from the joint of a new wall, finding the mortar within weeks, making the house look older and more at home. Or the tall grain in a terrace, cut down and standing again in the same place, stubble dug back into ground that takes everything quickly. That has a truth in it — the willingness to be replaced leaf by leaf — but it is not quite enough either. I would want hands. In a place with no other hands than human ones, hands are how the world is kept fruitful.

So I go lower, and narrower, as you have to here. Not the whole valley. A stretch of it.

I would be a person — which is to say, one of the only moving lives there is — living in the third house below the central plaza, on the lane that steps down toward the marsh. Not uphill where the water arrives clean from the weeping cliff, and not at the bottom where it spreads and slows among the sedge, but in the middle, where it has already passed through several courtyards and has several more to feed.

My house would stand on a platform a waist high, loose stone in the culvert underneath so the water can pass even when the ground swells. Wide eaves. Reed mats hanging to break the drip. A cistern under the courtyard, full to its black lip in the morning, full to its black lip at night. If it were higher, I would know someone above me had taken too much. If it were lower, the people below me would know it of me before I knew it myself.

I would be built the way everyone here is built: low, broad through the shoulders, short in the leg, weight close to the wet stone. My feet would be my most honest feature — splayed, gripping, the soles pale and dense, placing themselves whole and flat before trusting a step. I would go barefoot on the platform and in the garden, feeling for root and stone through soil that shines a hand's breadth down, and wear grooved reed sandals on the steep lanes. Resin darkened into the creases of my elbows and between my toes. Lime in a crescent under my nails. A little green at the edges of my hands, not because I am a mason — though I would know how to scrape stone with stone, slowly, in the gritty quiet of a yard — but because all my life I have handled copper gutters, lifted copper-bound gates, cleared the green from hinges.

My work would not be dramatic, and that is how I would recognize it as mine.

I would keep a few hundred yards of channel. That is the size of a life here, really. You learn your stretch of water the way you learn a voice. Which stepping-stone rocks a fraction when the flow is right. Where reed crowds in fastest. Which neighbor's overflow feeds my garden and whose garden waits on mine. In a town with no birdsong, no insect drone, no frogs in the marsh, no restless penned thing calling at dark, a changed trickle carries like a sentence spoken in another room. I would sit under the eaves mending a mat or working a loom and hear, two houses away, the sound go slightly wrong — a leaf mat thickening, a stone shifted — and call out, low, because low is all that is needed, whose turn it was to lift the slab and clear it.

And when it was my turn, I would. I would not dam and I would not force. You widen your section. You let the pressure equalize. That is not a philosophy anyone would recite; it is just plumbing that has become character.

In the still weeks I might still walk the crop rows, even though that is children's work, because my hands would remember it too well to give up entirely: the soft brush, plant down lashed to reed, called a feather only for its shape, touched flower to flower. Deliberately. Only as much fruit as we mean to use. There are no other pollinators coming, and no sense that they were ever owed to us. Fruitfulness is a chore you do by hand, in company, at walking pace.

I would eat what everyone eats — broad leaves cut beaded in the morning, pounded grain, leaf pastes, pressed seed oil, a mineral salt scraped where spring water leaves its crust — often and not heavily. My teeth would be flat from grit and fiber. My larder would be neither full nor empty. I would speak in the slow adjustments this place teaches: an objection restated each morning on the same wet plot until, like a wall corner, it softens — not because anyone has shouted it down, but because weather and listening have done their work.

My days would have very little outline. Rise when the marsh mist burns off to its accustomed height. Sweep leaves from the rill. Check the tank. Widen, clear, carry in pairs when tile or stone needed moving, leaning with a neighbor into a fiber harness at the speed slick stone allows. Eat under the eaves in the evening and listen to each house make its small ticking contribution to the channels. Lamps of plant oil, low and clean. Moss thickening everywhere I do not walk. Ferns finding every joint I leave unlimed.

I think, at the end, I would not be someone waiting for the town to become something else. That is the deepest fit. Still on the surface, holding shape, green at the edges within a year of being made. Growing late and then hardly changing for a long span, until breath itself becomes a channel narrowed by damp, and they carry me at cart pace — in pairs, unhurried — to the shallow plots at the south end, and set a squared stone over me, sharp-cornered the first year.

And after that my stretch of water would belong to whoever had grown up sweeping leaves from it. The trickle would change, briefly, under other hands. Then it would find its level again.
