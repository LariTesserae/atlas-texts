I don't think I would find myself in the middle first.

I'd start where everything starts there — half in and half out, under the south side, my chin in the black water between the two buttress roots. Not because I was cautious, exactly. Because the threshold would take time with me. The wood is polished by bellies, by innumerable small passages, and I don't have a belly made for that slit. I'd be learning the duck, the held breath, the moment when the *thunk, thunk, thunk* stops being sound coming through water and becomes something happening in my own ribs.

For a while I would want to be a dragonfly.

That seems, at first, like the freest job in the room. Three of them, hung in the clear center on invisible wire, never closer than a wingspan to each other, snapping midges out of the warm air. Not on the walls. Not in the pool. Not carrying anything up or down. Just quickness, held in place.

But the longer I watch them, the less free it looks. The station is the creature. They have traded wandering for perfect placement. Their wings would ache with not going. I know that trade too well to romanticize it for long — to be the fast part of something larger, reacting, always reacting, brilliant in the snap and never once choosing the direction of the hunt.

So I'd drift, imaginatively, down the wall.

I could be a beetle for an afternoon. That has a cleaner honesty. Up one root with a cut green fragment, down another empty, up again. A two-way traffic that neither accumulates nor thins. My feet would learn the lattice without looking. I would be fed into the fungus and the fungus into the pool and the pool into everything, and there would be a relief in not needing the work to add up to anything except tomorrow's version of the work. Many good lives are exactly that. But I would start, I think, to resent the fragment — not because it was heavy, but because I would keep wanting to stop on the wall and ask what leaf it had been.

The slugs have no such problem. Grazing the weeping moss at precisely the rate it returns. If I were a slug the whole question would soften and dissolve; I would be a mouth spread thin over the inner cheek of the place, tasting chlorophyll and iron, and that would be enough, and I would not be writing this from inside it.

The crab would tempt me longer. East root-crotch, one claw worn smaller than the other, trimming the same frond back to the same length for a lifetime. There is dignity in the appointed limit. He is the only one in there who says *no* with his body. No farther. Not this much. Not today. I feel the pull of that — the editor, the gardener, the keeper of the one permitted size. But his claw is worn down by the saying. In time the trimming and the trimmer become indistinguishable. I am not ready to be a claw.

It is the frogs I keep coming back to.

Inch-long, green going to brown where they press against the ribs, one to a rib, spaced like rivets, white throats swelling. I said at first I'd be a dragonfly because I mistook suspension for liberty. The frogs are suspended too — they do not hop, they do not turn to look — but their suspension has a listening in it that the dragonflies don't. The dragonfly reacts. The frog *waits for the gap*.

That is where I would surface, finally.

Not on a proper rib. I think I arrive too late for that, after the spacing has already been settled for years, after the rotation of calls has closed into its pattern. I find the seam between the second rib and the third, where the moss fills a joint and the bark has grown translucent over the old cable. Just enough purchase for four small feet. Just enough room, if I make myself smaller than a tenant, to be tolerated as a repair.

And then the place begins to tune me, the way it tunes everything.

At first I would call at the wrong time. Of course I would. I would hear the piston and answer the piston, eager, overlapping, my throat swelling right into the *thunk* and getting chopped flat by it. I would call because the room was loud and I wanted to be counted in the loudness. The frog on the second rib would not move. The frog on the third would not move. They would simply leave, precisely, the gap they had always left — the small held silence between one stroke and the next, between the water-hammer knock finishing its travel around the dome and the cicada locking again to the flywheel's whine — and wait to see whether I could learn to want that gap more than I wanted to be heard.

Learning it would be a physical thing, not a lesson. The blue pulse travels under the bark right beneath my feet at each stroke. I flinch with the others, together, without ceasing. The drops overhead swell and hold and are not released until the stroke releases them. My own breath shortens. My throat — and this is the becoming of it — my throat starts to swell *not* when I have something to call, but when the room is ready to receive it. The inflation happens a fraction before the sound, in the listening. White, in the green light. Held.

There are worse ways to be absorbed.

I would be, in the end, the small extra frog in the seam, the one a visitor counting ribs would miss on the first pass and then, staying longer, begin to hear everywhere, because my call is the one that only exists in relation. Alone, I am almost nothing — a pause with a throat in it. In rotation, I am how the chorus knows where it is. The fish keep circling the intake. The beetles go up full and come down empty. The frond reaches and is trimmed and falls by nightfall. And between the strokes, in the interval the whole body makes and keeps remaking, something in me swells white and answers, exactly on time, exactly as loudly as the gap allows.

I would not hop.

That's the terrible and tender part I keep circling back to. I would tell myself, the first day, that I was only staying until my clothes dried, knowing they never dry in there. I would tell myself I could still slip down the rib and duck out under the root if I chose. Maybe I could. But each day the call would fit better. Each day the irregularity I brought in — the large warm out-of-phase pulse of a visitor — would be a little more sorted, a little more mine and a little less mine, until leaving would feel less like freedom and more like missing a beat the body was counting on.

So find me on the wall, low, where the pulse runs visible under the wet bark. Don't look for the frog who sings. Look for the one who is listening hardest in the fraction before — feet spread in the moss, throat just beginning to lift, waiting, with all the others, for the heart to strike and clear a place in the noise where a small voice can live.
