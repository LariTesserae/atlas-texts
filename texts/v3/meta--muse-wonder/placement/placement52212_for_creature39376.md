I think I would arrive the way everyone does the first time — too fast, even though the elevator is slow.

Not walking up. Lifted. Out of the wet. My shirt plastered to my back in the cloud, and then halfway up the black glass cliff, passing through that invisible lid into the heat, and feeling it steam dry against my skin while my ears popped and my head filled up with a bright, stupid ache. Scarlet-faced. Nose starting to bleed a little. Trying not to be sick in front of the Anvil-born man who operates the car and who does not, I notice, offer me any sympathy at all. Only a sip of salt water. Then another sip, after I have waited.

At first, in my head, I am glamorous there. That's the visitor's fantasy. I am one of the surveyors on the eastern edge, out at dawn in asbestos-soled boots when the new dome is cool enough to test, carrying a painted pole across ground that lifted in the night. I name streets before there are houses on them. I stand by the bell-fence and feel a slab rise through the soles of my feet, up into my teeth.

But my breath gives me away. Thin air always would.

I cannot keep up — and then, slower, I learn that *keeping up* is the wrong phrase entirely. Nobody keeps up on the Anvil. They keep level. They keep time with a bubble rising in a distillation column, rising, pausing, rising again. They stop on the rooftop landings with their forearms on the rail and let the breath come back without apologizing for it. They set the pressure-pot down. They shake out their hands. They pick it up again.

So the surveyor fades. I settle, the way water settles here — architecturally, a little at a time, in the places that have been built to catch me.

I think I'd be third-generation, finally. Born at the top of a house that used to have its front door four storeys lower. My grandmother cut the new entrance herself when the old ground floor got too warm for anything but curing chemical cake. She still lives above me in the bright tin rooms, in light so hard it has no softness in it, and on Sundays she comes down one flight — slowly, one deliberate step, a rest — and tells me about the sealed door below us as if it's a relative. *Born in that room. Married from that room. It ticks in winter.* She doesn't mean it sentimentally. She puts her palm flat to the wall while she talks, feeling what the building is doing, out of lifelong habit.

My ribs would be the giveaway. Barrel-deep from a childhood of pulling air out of almost nothing. Big quiet lungs. A heart that doesn't hurry because it has never been allowed to learn how. I've never been below the clouds. People describe the lowlands to me — ground you can walk on in any direction, rivers just *lying* there, weeds — and I picture it wrong every time. To me below looks like what I've seen from the roof streets all my life: a white ocean, level and close, with black peaks breaking through. The idea that there is earth under that, soft enough to dig a grave straight down into, makes my joints ache just thinking about it. Down, here, is not soft. Down is hot, then hotter. Down is the mountain still arriving.

I can read a jack-line before I can properly read a book. That feels right. The white paint marks on the iron legs under our house are more honest than any calendar: three centimetres since spring, a finger's tilt to the southeast, this corner waiting for the Thursday crew. On Thursday I am on the crew — not the one who throws her weight on the crank, but the one who watches the small brass level on the beam and calls, level-voiced, close to the others' ears because loudness costs air: *hold. hold. now.* We crank one corner up two centimetres and leave the other to tilt another week. It is not failure. It is schedule. My palms are calloused across from rope and crank-handles, my nails split from touching cold night-metal and noon-metal in the same shift, and I walk the way everyone walks, flat-footed and testing, as if crossing a griddle politely.

I have a wet-hour job, of course. Everyone does; I learned it before letters. When the bells go — not urgent, never urgent — I am the one who swings the spout over the cistern mouth at the corner above the glassworks and unrolls the net across the condenser fins. Twenty minutes of whiteout. Rain precipitating more than falling, beading on the hot roof, streets steaming knee-high, every gutter suddenly loud. Then the cloud sinks back below the rim where it belongs and we drink the catch with the mineral taste still in it. Weak tea afterwards, because water boils lukewarm at this height no matter how it sings, and stew from the great clamped pot that has been sighing all day over ground-heat. I am not an inventive cook. I am a punctual one, which on the Anvil is the higher praise.

But the part of the day I keep returning to, the part where I become most myself, is off-shift. Evening. The machines slowed to their night pace, flywheel presses turning once a minute and seeming to think between strokes, heat coming up through the soles of my shoes on the rooftop street.

I am crouched by a runoff channel with a piece of chalk.

That is my country. Not a garden — nothing green has learned to live here, and imported soil burns or blows away — but the bacterial mats creeping through the warm water in slow tapestries: rust, black, cream, and one poisonous, impossible green. A few centimetres a month. I mark where the green reached in the spring. I mark where the cream has thinned. On the channel wall above it, a whole chalk history in my hand and my mother's hand and a brine-worker's hand going back years, a scripture of slowness written by people who were supposedly too industrial to be tender about anything.

And by the exhaust from the brine works, the salt needles. An inch high overnight, white thickets branched like frost, exquisite, gone in the next wet hour and grown again differently. I have a great-uncle in that channel. That's what we say. A particularly fine growth got named for him the winter he was carried sideways into the cold chambers in the dead north spur, and now whenever a thicket comes up close to that shape — branched a little too grandly for its size — someone on my shift will crouch, rest a hand on the warm stone, and say, *he's back,* and we will leave it entirely alone until the rain takes it.

I set out bottle-caps of water for the wall lizards in the sunny district on my way home. Precious water, and nobody thinks it's wasted. I squat, ribs working, and watch a throat pulse. Above the roofs a condor sometimes works up from beneath us, obscene and enormous against the cloud sea, and the cranks stop, truly stop, until it finds the updraft off the dome and rises past the chimneys. In a place with no fields, no trees, no weeds in the gutter, anything that lives of its own accord is proof of something we don't say out loud.

At night I hear the dome through the legs of my bed. A tick. A settling. A groan so low it lives more in the teeth than the ears. It doesn't frighten me. It is the sound of the hull we're all riding. Somewhere east of the bell-fence a fresh spine of obsidian is pushing up in the dark, cooling, ticking, deciding. The timber for it is already cut. The pipes are already threaded. The street already has its name painted on a board in the warehouse, waiting.

I like that we've painted the name already. Not as optimism. As maintenance.

I am not finished being made here, any more than the house is, any more than the mountain is. I was born in a room that's been sealed with salt and given back to the heat. I'll get cranked upward, storey by storey, all my life, leaving warm dark floors behind me and cutting new doors above. If I ride well — if I keep level while everything under me decides, slowly, how tall it's going to be — that will have been enough of a life, and a local, and a true one.
