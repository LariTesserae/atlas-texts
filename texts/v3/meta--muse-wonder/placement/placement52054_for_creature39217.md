I would not begin on the plain.

Up there the wind finds everything. It would find me too quickly — strip the damp out of my coat, widen the light until it hurt, thin the sound until there was nothing left to navigate by. Twice I have known animals go up the ramp ahead of the chisels, and I understood the turning back. It is not the cold that stops you. Cold is honest. It is the silence opening, and the sky becoming too large to read.

So I would be lower than that. Lower than the boardwalk. Lower, most of the time, than the surface at all.

You would walk the Cut for a whole rotation and not see me, and that would not mean I had hidden well. It would mean you were looking at the wrong side of the world. The mat is not ground to me. It's a roof that became a sea. Green and rust and oxblood from above, dark and wet and root-threaded from below, smelling of peat and iron and the faint aluminium tang where the pipes tick in their lagging. I travel through it the way something slow travels through water, pushing a tunnel that sighs shut behind me. Twenty centimetres down, the roar is already only a pressure in my teeth. Half a metre down, there is almost peace — fungus, sedge rhizome, insects moving at the pace the cold allows them, and my own heart doing twenty beats to the minute, in no hurry to get anywhere.

I am that.

Low. Long. Heavier-looking than I am, because most of what you would take for my back is not me. Sphagnum seeded in the guard hairs when I was young and never left. Feather moss threading the undercoat. A pale patch of reindeer lichen between my shoulders that has outgrown two molts and will, in the end, outlive the shoulders. In summer I am bottle-green and mustard and rust, another hummock that breathes very slowly. In winter the garden freezes iron-grey, the snow stays on me, and I can lie beside a pipeline sleeper for an entire shift while orange figures work twenty metres away and learn nothing.

My feet are ridiculous and they are the reason the place forgives me. Wide, splayed, fringed stiff, the hind ones nearly ladles. I do not so much walk as distribute myself. Let the weight sigh out. Let the sponge take it and give it back. A booted human wounds this country with every careless step off the duckboards — crushes twenty years of growth, opens the frozen dark to the summer. Step for step, I do less damage than the fox whose neat tracks last until thaw. Mine last minutes. The moss springs back over a round depression beside the boardwalk and is already forgetting me by the time you look again.

And I have no ears to show you. That surprises people, when they think about it at all. Only smooth furred swellings where the skull thickens, the canals sealed shut not long after I was born blind and squeaking — once, wetly, the only unmediated sound I have ever made, before I was licked quiet. I am not deaf. The Cut would waste nothing that useful. I hear through my jaw pressed to peat. Through my breastbone. Through the soles of those wide feet. 

Which means the stations are not noise to me. They are geography.

Station One shakes the valley one way. Station Two, a fraction rougher, a worn edge to its frequency I would know in my sleep. Station Three has the double beat where its housing meets the bench rock, and the heat, and the ravens on the warm side of the exhaust. I live in gradients the way a fish lives in current — this direction the tremor thickens, that direction it thins into the colder, darker stretch between lights where no sensible thing builds. Near the red lines painted on the concrete, where your tea jumps in its cup and even the loose ice dances off the pipe insulation, I press my chin down and go still. My heart quickens there. Syncopates a little. Learns the rhythm. It is the only warm thing I know that never pauses, and I build where it shakes because the shaking keeps the ice from knitting solid in my bedding.

My nest is not much to look at from above, because there is nothing above to look at. A felted hollow in the peat, cotton-grass down, shed garden, in the penumbra of a valve housing — never in the worst of it, never out of it. In the willow thickets where a flange leaks heat and the twigs have grown waist-high and black with rime, we winter stacked: old ones on the outside going grey with frost, young in the damp centre, waking in rotation to lick condensation from the underside of the pipes and crop whatever foolish, slow thing overwintered near the warmth. I eat the underside of this place. Fungal threads. Crowberries kept cold-preserved in the mat since August. Lichen I do not so much digest as farm, and return altered. Once in a while a raven drops something by Station Three. Once in a while a crew loses something off a boardwalk at lunch and learns, wisely, not to go back for it.

If I need to speak, I speak through the ground.

One soft beat of a forefoot against a sleeper. The mat carries it a hundred metres — a felt syllable. Two beats, pause, one: a boundary. A deliberate shivering against frozen sphagnum: young here, keep your weight off. The humans talk down here in hand signals, fluent as speech, because the roar has taken speech from them the way it took my ears and made something else of both of us. The older hands knock twice with their frost-mallets on a railing before they start work on a section, and wait a breath. I have lifted a forepaw beside the transformer fence and held it, and lowered it again. Threat, curiosity, habit that has repeated itself into dignity — I no longer remember which it was the first time. I only know I would feel the absence if they stopped knocking.

My eyes are the poor concession. Small, black, lidded half-shut against what passes for daylight. I see orange work-light better than sun, movement better than shape. The blue-grey dimness of the floor, most of the year, is varied enough and sufficient. A fox could sit beside a pool skinning over with ice at noon in July and I could wait it out, because predation here is not a matter of strength. It is a matter of being heard, and under the roar nothing hears me move, nothing hears me breathe. It is a matter of being seen, which is a matter of impatience, and I have bred impatience almost entirely out of myself. I grow slowly. I breed slower — one pup every third or fourth year, raised first in the dark tunnels, taught stillness before it is taught food. I will live, if the benches let me, the way they live: forty years, perhaps, by not being weathered very fast.

So in midwinter, when you stand on the rim at noon and look down the eighteen kilometres of it — the orange lights burning small and steady in the blue shadow, the vapour from the stacks flattening and travelling sideways and never clearing the walls, the black smudges of willow where the heat escapes, the snow lying apparently unbroken over all that green, quiet-growing emptiness — you will not find me.

I will be under your looking. Ankle-deep. Jaw-deep. Garden-backed and earless and at home in a noise that drove everything else away, listening with my whole body to the one fast thing this young, cold, cut-open valley was given to do. And now and then, where the mat runs under a boardwalk and the compressed dark is already sheltered, I will answer the working world above me — not in sound, never in sound — with a single soft beat that travels through peat and sphagnum and frozen root, felt rather than heard, gone before anyone up on the silent plain could know there had been anything there to feel it.
