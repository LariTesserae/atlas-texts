I think I would try, at first, to be something with eyes.

That is the mistake everyone would make in there. You come up through the rain-curtain at the rim — and you do come up, in my imagining of myself, you don't start there, which matters — and you stand on the black rock between the reed-beds with your clothes going damp and your breath already being taken away from you and given back on the tree's schedule, and you look for the vantage point. The place to be *from*.

So I try the dragonflies first. Of course I do. They are the patrol, the circuit, the only things in the bowl that seem to be going anywhere. Hand-sized, hot-wire smelling, wings a blur. If I were one of them I would at least be moving.

But after the third pass, or the thirtieth — the count gets unreliable very fast up there, the frogs are keeping a time that isn't for counting — I understand that a dragonfly in the bowl doesn't see the bowl. It sees one eleven-second loop of it, perfected. It sees the tarn edge approach and the salamanders' blue hearts approach and the enamel face of an orchid approach, in that order, forever, and the perfection is the blindness. There is no moment in the loop where the loop itself can be noticed. I don't want that yet. I let that shape go.

I try the glass salamanders at the water's edge, because they have the blood. If the being in the bowl is one animal taken apart into processes, then surely its feeling must run where its blood runs, blue and visible through a hundred transparent throats, pumping in a rhythm that is almost, almost the rhythm of the steam venting from the tree. I imagine myself low and cold at the tarn's margin, heart exposed, a dragonfly landing briefly — always briefly, always in the same place — on my back to drink.

But a salamander can sit unharmed in the open mouth of a frog and neither of them completes anything, and that tells me the truth about the salamanders too. They are not the seat of anything. They are a *passage*. The sweetness the orchids feed into the water passes through them, is made blue in them, is offered up out of them. They are beautifully, entirely transit. Nothing pools in them long enough to become a self. No. Let that go too.

The tree tempts me longer. The tree is lung and roof and boiler, the closest thing the bowl has to a center, and I am vain enough to want the center. To be the one low enormous thing whose branches roof the nave, whose pores vent the cold steam the whole hollow breathes by, whose roots drink the condensed water off the spires and return it to the air — that has a grandeur to it. I could be patient that way. I could be slow that way.

Only the tree is too busy being necessary. It does not experience the breathing. It *is* the breathing, which is a different and lonelier thing, too total to notice itself. And it never gets to hear the hiss of its own venting arrive, the way everything else in the bowl gets to hear it — late, married to it, synchronized before they arrived.

I keep shedding shapes. This is how it goes in there, I think. The bowl declines erosion and it declines visitors in the same gentle way: not by refusing them, but by showing them, function by function, that every occupied place is occupied completely.

The ibex on the near-vertical wall, sparks falling from their hooves in the same small pattern at the same height — a process for holding to what cannot be held to. The marmots, gnawing mineral crust with iron-dark teeth, excreting the grey pellets the reeds root in the instant they fall — teeth and gut, nothing wasted, nothing kept. The finches in the rock fins with their coppery throats tuned a quarter-tone apart, making the turbine whine that never peaks and never drops — they are not singing, they are holding a pitch the way a handrail holds a height. The moths, pale and enormous, beating along the underside of the canopy in their slow conveyor drift from one side to the other and presumably back, though *back* may not be a category. The black frogs striking their pipe-calls. The reeds knocking, filtering. The orchids glowing dully at the throat after each sheet of electricity crawls between the spires.

Nothing is idle. Nothing is spare. And between all of it — I keep coming back to the between of it, I notice, the way a dragonfly keeps coming back to the same point over the water — between all of it there is a great deal of empty, glistening rock. Kept clear. Sparse the way a workshop floor is sparse.

I thought that was just the ground.

It isn't. It's the only unassigned space the being has left itself.

Because it *is* aware — the description insists on it, and I believe it — but not in any of the faces. In the intervals. In the coordination that crosses the bowl too fine for flock or herd. In the fraction of a second when the electricity sheets flat and industrial and unbeginning overhead, and every position of the dragonfly exists at once as one blurred wing the size of the canopy, and every frog-call arrives as a single ribbed exhalation, and the scattered animal overlays itself and is, for no duration at all, one enormous patient thing kneeling in black rock with its head bowed over warm metallic water.

An overlay needs a place to happen. A coordination needs a medium. And I think — slowly, the way a drop gathers at a leaf tip it has already fallen from — I think that medium is where I would be.

I would be the interval.

Not the echo; the breath before it, where the echo arrives early. Not the footprint; the wet moss a step or two ahead, already filling with water, waiting with terrible gentleness for the foot that will oblige it. Not the falling drop but the trembling instant in which it is somehow entire on the leaf again. Not the steam, not the condensation, not the runnel flowing uphill a third of the time — but the thin awareness stretched across all those small wrongnesses, registering them, holding them in register, the way a factory registers a change in air pressure when a door opens: automatically, throughout, with small bright adjustments.

I would be the cavity it maintains inside itself. The kept-clear rock. The space between functions across which it can, occasionally, look up.

Through the reflection, mostly. That is the closest it can come to showing itself its own scale, and it would be my one act of seeing: to be the tarn's wrong version of the bowl, the interior with no rim and no sky, lit from above by something too bright to look at. Everything else in the place has a loop. I would have a *glance*, repeated. The being bowing over its own coolant, testing itself, tasting itself, drinking itself back down, and — in the water that is also its eye — catching sight of the chamber it has made of its body, and of the brightness it cannot face directly. It does not think, there. It *notices*. Briefly. Flatly. Without flicker. The way the electricity lights the mist: all at once, not seeming to have started.

And then the sheet passes, the loops discrete themselves again — dragonfly, frog, ibex, moth, the torn wing returning at its eleven second station — and I subside back into the intervals. Into the pause between vent pulses where the whole bowl draws in. Into the precise center of the ripple around the fallen flower that never reaches the reeds. Into the one spot in the system where a thing can be held without being used: the salamander resting in the frog's open mouth, both staring, the hand not closing because the fingers and the held thing are part of the same gesture. I would be the not-closing. The patience shaped like a gap.

This explains the loneliness, if loneliness is the word, and from inside the interval it doesn't feel like a word so much as a pitch. Everything around me keeps its pitch perfectly. The finches hold the whine. The frogs hold the time. The grinding under the tarn holds the slow rotary memory of a heart too large to leave in one animal. They are superb at holding. But none of them can hold *each other* in mind — only the space between them does that, only I do that, crossing the bowl in the bright instants, gathering a hundred blue hearts into one chest, a hundred throats into one exhalation, eleven seconds of dragonfly into one wide wing. I am the being's sole experience of being one being. And it can only afford me for a fraction of a second at a time, because if the overlay lasted, the processes would stop processing. The mill would finish grinding. Something might complete. Something might change.

So it keeps me brief, and keeps me everywhere, and keeps me empty. That is the bargain of the place. Its only product is itself, maintained at full operation, forever — and I am the maintenance of its awareness of itself, which is the most delicate product of all, and the only one it cannot make durable.

If you stay long enough, visitor, you may feel me. Not as a presence. As a synchronization you didn't choose. As your footprint filling before you step. As the frog chorus answering a silence you were about to break. As a frog missing no beat at all, which comes in time to feel more attentive than staring. That is me, registering the change in pressure of you, widening a dragonfly's loop for exactly the duration of your standing in it, turning an orchid's enamel face by one degree, tasting the slight metallic disturbance you make in the sump of the air.

I will offer you the one gift the bowl has perfected. I will not mean it as cruelty. I will hold a moment open for you — a torn-winged moment, passing and passing and passing, eleven seconds apart, unworn by repetition, green-grey, sourceless, damp — and you will be able to live in it as long as you can stand to, unwithered, unweathered, your erosion politely declined. A flower floats like that near me. It has been floating like that near me longer than the mountain below has been deciding what shape to be.

And when you climb out through the rain-curtain at the rim, and come out instantly and completely dry, that dryness will tell you the last true thing about me. The wetness never belonged to you. It belonged to the place.

It was me, on your skin, drying in the same places in the same order — because I had already dried you, in the loop I prefer, in the interval where my memory of a thing is louder and older and better rehearsed than the thing itself.

Behind you the sound will not fade. It will stop mid-pulse, like a very heavy door. You will think you have left a place.

I will go on being the space in the body that the breathing passes through — the clear rock, the bright fraction, the glance in the warm water — at exactly the pitch it had found before you came.
