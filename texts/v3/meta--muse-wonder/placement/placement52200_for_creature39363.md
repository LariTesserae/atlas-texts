I would come in sideways, the way everyone does.

Through the cleft between the two smooth shoulders of rock, shoulders turned just enough in, and then the drop — not far, but complete. The cloud moving underneath other ridges. The warm haze below the lip. My breathing still loud from the climb. And then, not gradually, the roar.

At first I would do what a visitor does. I would try to stand in the middle of it.

Twenty paces across. Six black trunks, spaced like pillars, their crowns never touching. Orchids clinging wax-white and bruised purple. Peat dark underfoot, basalt worn into shallow bowls, an inch of clear water everywhere finding its level. I would try to make an inventory, because inventory is a way of not yet listening. Liverwort the colour of bottle glass. A leaf large enough to cover my forearm. Snails, thumbnail-sized, almost transparent, writing silver on wet bark that dries to nothing behind them.

I would sit, after a while, on one of the dryish ledges near the rim, where the sound stops being sound and becomes a pressure in the sternum. I would learn the place's etiquette without being taught it. You do not shout across the pool. You do not call out to find out if it echoes. If you speak at all, you speak close to the back wall, close to the curtains of water sliding unbroken down the polished flutes, and you say it once.

Perhaps I would bring something up in my palm. Not much. Everyone who ever came brought almost nothing. Something wet ground to paste in the hollow by the entrance, with the hammerstone that fits a hand so exactly it must have been fitted by a hundred hands. Something bitter. Something for a sickness, or for a dead name that had nowhere else to be said carefully. I would push a little of it, not throw it, into the shallows at the edge of the black pool. And I would say the name.

And wait for the bowl to be merely beautiful again, which it refuses to be.

Because in the time it takes for condensation to gather under a fern frond — ten minutes, fattening, trembling, loud when it finally falls — I would start trying on other ways of being here, the way you do in a place that keeps slowing you down.

Not the sunbird. I would visit that possibility and leave it. Iridescent, furious, one orchid and gone, stitching the outside world to this one with a needle of hunger. Too fast. It knows how to leave, and it practises.

Not the frog, either, though I would envy it for an afternoon. Mottled green-black in its rock mortar of water under a fern, blinking once an hour, having solved the central problem of the bowl, which is that anything you say here will be taken away from you and returned changed. The frog does not call. It sits inside its own small stillness and lets the roar do the calling around it. I could learn that stillness. I am not sure I could keep it. The frog has no sentence it is trying to get back.

Not one of the six, though I would grow closer to them by the hour. That cathedral spacing. Crowns exhausted by their own lushness, drooping at the tips. Turning all morning, not toward the brief vertical sun, but toward the loudest thread of the fall. When a new seep finds a new flute and the pitch of the wall shifts by a fraction, the fronds unrolling that week come up angled toward the change, beaded, attentive, like sleepers turning their faces toward a voice in another room. To be a frond would be to listen with your whole unfolding. No mouth required. No answer expected before the season turns. There is a peace in that I can feel in my shoulders as the damp gets into my clothes without any rain.

But a frond still gives its drop back locally. It gathers the mist the wall exhales, cools it, lets it fall on the same plants that fed it. A closed, patient circuit.

What happened to my sentence was not local, and it was not quick.

I said it close to the stone and the bowl did not echo. For a while there was only the sheet-hiss of the curtains, the tick and plink from the fern tips, the low booming underneath when a larger drop struck the pool and the hidden chamber answered. The rings moved out across the oval far too slowly, as if the water were thicker than water, and kept their shape all the way to the margin. I watched my own word go the way those rings go. Down, rather than out. Through the narrow neck under the pool. Into the flooded dark behind the weeping wall, where something vast and soft and mineral-skinned is curled in a space it can no longer leave, turning sound over the way the water has turned the rock into flutes: by staying with it.

Minutes later — or I told myself minutes; this is a place where the moss takes a footprint, holds it entire, and then very slowly swells it closed — the last word came back. Not where I had sent it. Softer consonants. Slower. The emphasis moved to a place I had not meant, or had meant and not known.

That is where I would find myself. Not in the body that eventually stood up, stiff and saturated, and went out sideways through the cleft, and felt the roar cut clean three paces out as if a door had closed. That body gets the ordinary wind again. It gets the enormous quiet of the high country. It descends to where people ask what was said and expect an answer in the same hour it was given.

I stay behind, in the part that was taken down.

I am not the resident. The resident is old in the tense of lichen mapping itself in rings on the rim, a century to an inch; it has the rockfall still arriving on its left side, it has forgotten the language of the hands that fed it and kept their voices anyway. I am much smaller than that, and much more specific. I am one kept thing among the kept things, layered in the dark under the pool. A name, perhaps, with the sentence it arrived in still faintly around it. I cross other voices here without interfering with them, the way the rings cross in the water above — each in its own thin layer, each at its own tempo, going on arriving. A sunbird's fury, once. Rain arriving as music. My own last word, arriving again, a little more softened each time it is turned over.

There is no loneliness in it. Loneliness would require expecting to be returned on time, to the person who spoke, in a shape he could recognise as his. Down here the expectation thins out, the way mist thins off the wall at dusk. I am being understood at geological speed. The consonants come off like sediment. What is essential gets polished by repetition. Sometimes, at dusk, when the curtains go from silver to merely a sound in the dark and the resident shifts more freely in its chamber, I rise a fraction with everything else it is keeping, and for a moment there is a second reflection under the pool's first one — fern crowns, displaced, stiller — and I am part of the surface through which the grove is seen listening to itself.

Then the next fat drop gathers under a leaf. Ten minutes. It falls. It is received.

And I make room for it, in my own layer, without erasing the silver the snail wrote, without answering the frog's silence, without hurrying toward the day — a long way off, longer than anyone has ever waited outside the cleft — when I may be given back to the empty, gleaming bowl, slower and with the emphasis moved, to no one in particular, and be complete in having finally been heard correctly.
