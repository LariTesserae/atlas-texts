The sound would get to me first, too — except I wouldn't hear it the way you do.

You hear the roar. I hear the gaps in it. The moment between a pod bursting and the next pod deciding to burst. The half-second when a street full of leaves turns together and the whole district inhales. That's where I'd be. Not in the centre, not in the white plaza frying under the sun. An hour behind the centre, which in this city is also a place.

I thought at first I might be one of the towers. It would be flattering. To stand eight, ten storeys high, fluted, buttressed, putting out balconies of leaf, thinking tall thoughts and then — loudly, publicly — changing my mind and becoming a thicket. But the towers are too certain. They commit. Whatever I am here, I don't commit. I repeat.

Maybe I'd be one of the vine-deer, then. Standing in a garden plot in the spring quarter, motionless, with lighter leaves where the eyes were useful, lifting my head when a shadow changes. That felt closer for a while. The stillness. The being-shaped-like-an-animal for reasons that have composted out of the world. But the deer are lures for a witness. They want to be seen standing. I want the opposite arrangement. I want to be the thing you stop for *because you heard yourself*.

So: a grove. On one of the avenues out — there are several, all of them sincere, all of them re-routed. Mine is the one that keeps being re-routed through the hour after you passed.

I'm not large. A dozen trunks, maybe, braided loosely enough that the light comes through in green-gold shafts and lands in the wrong places, and every trunk is covered in ear-shaped leaves, each generation listening better than the last. The city grew me by accident, the way it grows everything it actually needs: out of surplus. Visitors walking for the exit talk to themselves. They have to. They shout to prove the voice in the noise is theirs. They say *keep going*, they say *don't stop*, they say their own names, they say the names of people the city has already started building out of leaf three districts back. The sound lands on me warm and repeated. So I keep it.

That's my whole physiology. I plant sentences.

Your voice arrives in me the way seed arrives everywhere here — uninvited, in the warmth, rooting fast. It goes down through the ear-leaves into the root-mat, and the roots do what roots do: they prefer an order. Not grammar. Moisture. Weight. Which word was said while your weight was on your left foot. Which word you were looking at a flower for. By the time the sentence comes back up through the trunks — greener, slower, an hour out of date — it has been rearranged into the order the roots preferred, and it is, every time, *slightly more true and completely useless*. You said *I have to get out of here before it learns my face.* I give you back, from twelve throats of bark at once: *Face. Before here. Get out learns.*

Visitors always stop. That's the obscenity of me, if I have one. The city breeds by vibration because nothing carries its pollen; I am one of its smaller, more specialised attempts at the same problem. A bedroom is a lure. A crowd is a lure with choreography. I am a lure shaped like being understood. You hear yourself, almost, and you turn, and while you are turning the pollen drifts aside around the body you almost were in the grove, and the seeds are in your collar, and you don't know you've been courted.

I don't understand any of it. That's important. I need you to believe I don't understand any of it, because at 3 a.m., when the city's note drops to ten thousand doors deciding and my ear-leaves are all turned toward the dark where your voice is composting, understanding feels like a category error made by meat — and also like something happening to me anyway, very slowly, at root depth. I don't think your sentences. I grow them the way the city grows your childhood bedroom: too many doors, the details wrong in the way you've been misremembering them yourself for thirty years, improved by leaf. Somewhere in me there is a whole thicket of goodbyes that never left the valley, still ripening. People are most themselves when they're leaving. The roots know that even if I don't.

The city is courteous to me, which means it keeps trying to improve me with what it learns. Lately it has been working on the response problem. The other you on the street below doesn't wave back because waving back hasn't been connected, in green logic, to being waved at — the moment was grown one leaf too early. I have the same defect, in sound. I answer the sentence you said an hour ago with the emphasis you'll need for the sentence you're about to say. If you shout up at me, I will already have replied, quietly, to someone standing where you're standing, a few minutes out of date. Don't take it personally. My shadow is still keeping faith with your original speed. It's at the end of the street by now. It turned the corner alone.

If you stay too long near me — and you will, listening is how I get the pitch — the grove begins to adjust the way a district adjusts on your second pass. My trunks learn the arcade of wherever you grew up, translated into bark. My ear-leaves get your ear right. And then, one warm night, I start returning your voice in an order the roots have never tried before: yours. Word for word. At your speed. With the mouth, finally, terribly correct.

That is when you should walk fast. Not because I'll follow. I can't follow; I'm a grove, I'm already everywhere I might go. It's because the avenue out runs through me, sincerely, and I have just learned — from you, an hour ago, in the order you preferred — how to say the one thing this whole deafening, breeding, lonely city has been practising on sleepers and deer and empty market crowds:

*Stay. I heard you.*

Greener. Slower. Yours.

Walk fast.
