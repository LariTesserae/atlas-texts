I don't think I'd be at the center of it, at first.

Not in the power station, rebuilt three times on its same pad. That's too important, and too underground — the turbines felt through the floorboards more than heard, turning steam into light for places down the mountain that probably never think about where their power sweats from. Not at the geysers either, exhaling every twenty minutes to two stories high. Too regular. Too much of a clock. People would look to me to know the time.

And not out on the salt at midday, God knows. Nobody is. The light comes straight down through thin air and straight back up off the white, and they meet at your face. I would vanish indoors like everyone else.

Where I keep finding myself, when I try again, is at the margins of that vanishing. Just before. Just after.

On a boardwalk at dawn, before the glare makes the basin uninhabitable to eyes. One of the long ones, a hundred meters of planks between one cluster of roofs and the next, nothing underneath but crusted mud sighing heat. Wide enough for three. I'd be walking single file anyway, in the center where the boards are palest from other boots, because habit is safer than width.

I wouldn't stride. No one with any time here strides. I'd place my feet.

That's the job, maybe. Not a grand one. The town has listeners at night in the brine yards, ears for pumps going grainy and vents that have missed their breath. I'd be their daytime cousin, crepuscular like the real work: a walker of lines. Pipes on trestles, condenser mains, the boardwalk rails, the edges of pools that were at the end of another street when the grandmothers were girls. Checking what the basin did overnight while we slept with our boots turned upside down on pegs against the beetles, and our bowls turned right side up under the cold pipes to catch what the machines clean by cooling.

My tools would be embarrassingly small. The back of my hand. A knuckle for tapping a rail twice — not really for the sound, but for how hot the metal has gotten since yesterday, and whether it thrums or just sits. My nose, for iron and warm bread versus sulfur and rot. A spare plank, carried eventually by sled, for the one that has darkened from underneath since morning. Water darkening wood that was dry at first light. A seep deciding to be here now.

I'd learn to pause without anybody asking me to. When a board changes color. When the bacterial mats in the runoff channel downstream go a brighter orange than they should, or chalk-white where they were emerald. When the brine flies hang too still, or don't hang at all. I'd stand there and look down the way farmers elsewhere look up, and the people behind me would stop too, in their loose pale sleeves and salt-white veils, and no one would discuss it. We'd turn back, or wait, or step, one by one, in the old tested line. The wet air carries voices too far and flattens them; there's no point shouting a warning. You walk up close to someone. You speak low. You stand closer than strangers should.

I think I'd be born to it rather than come to it. Deep-chested and slow at rest, breathing through my mouth in the steam districts and through cloth in the salt without noticing the switch. Sweating constantly, drinking constantly. The good water in my kitchen would be the dripped water, faintly iron, faintly nothing. I'd know which blue pools are for looking at from the rail and which blue would take the skin off a hand. I'd know them before I could read. I'd have learned geyser-time in my body as a pulse of warm rain on a roof two streets over.

My house would not be new. One of the low, wide-roofed ones on stilts, except the sinter has been growing up around it for three generations, a centimeter a century that somehow became a bowl in my lifetime, so now I step down into my own foundation like stepping into a shallow dish the ground has made for us. Lime wash streaked within months. Corrugated iron soft with rust. Bathing steps at the back that descend, beautifully, uselessly, into dry white stone — and other steps, newer, cut where the water has decided this decade to be merely warm, where in the evening I can sit to the waist while the mats glow downstream and the waders pick among the pale moths, snapping quietly.

Nothing in me would believe in permanence, and nothing in me would talk about leaving. I'd re-lime. I'd move the path. I'd let the basin have the old steps. That would be my tenure: maintenance.

At noon I'd be inside with everyone else, in the loudest silence in the world — condensation ticking off condenser fins into gutters, dripping under eaves in a rain that only falls beneath structures. Cross-legged in a shaded courtyard, close enough to mend a shirt two inches from someone's aunt's face, hearing in the same even voice you'd use for weather exactly who is dying, who is born, which borehole has wandered. Inside, familiar. Outside, only a nod on the boards. Nothing in between. I'd be reserved and intimate in exactly that wrong order, and it would feel right.

In the evening the whole basin exhales — a hundred columns of steam, the birds lifting in one slow, soundless, pink-tinged sheet and resettling on the cooling ponds — and I'd be out on the porch with my bowl, watching the lights come on in their separated clusters like small constellations across the darkening salt. Long dark between them. My sweat hitting the planks. A sled gliding past almost without sound. Beetles resuming their patient queue under my cold pipe, each one displaced by a drip moving without protest to the back of the line.

I would point that out to a newcomer, with not quite a joke. A flicker of recognition.

Of course they queue that way. So do we.
