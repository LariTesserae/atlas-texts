I think I would arrive wrong.

From the rim, first — because that is where a stranger has to start. Up in the thin air where my own breathing sounds intrusive, where every step on the scree goes *clack* and then seems to apologize for it. I would be standing in one of the notches with the cold finding the back of my neck, looking down into what doesn't look like a town at all. Just green, filling a stone bowl. Too dense. Too still.

And at midday, I'd get it completely wrong. I'd think it was empty.

I'd wait, though. Not out of wisdom, at first — out of the simple reluctance to go back down into the clouds. I'd sit with my back against a black tooth of rock that was still holding the day's heat, and I'd feel, very faintly, through the seat of me, the place where the stone wants to answer a loud noise. And that would be the first lesson, learned in my bones before I understood it: don't.

Don't call out to see if anyone's there. Don't announce yourself.

When the light softened I'd go down one of the terraces, trying to be quiet in the clumsy human way — holding branches aside instead of parting them, setting my boots down heel-first on the mat and feeling it give, springy and damp, drinking the disturbance. Where the outside was all mineral and wind, in here the air would close around me at skin temperature. Leaf-oil. Warm fur. Resin. Something sweet rotting far underneath. My coat would become absurd within ten steps.

I don't think I'd become one of the gliders, though for an evening I'd want to. To launch from a rim-side tree at dusk with the membrane stretched between my limbs, down-edged and silent, patterned underneath like a night flower opening — to be dreamy and welcomed everywhere and allowed to fall asleep mid-grooming, hanging by one foot. That vagueness appeals to me. But I'd be a bad glider. I'd want to look down and narrate the fall.

Not a burrower, either, though there's a pull there I recognize: blind, below the lanes, turning everything fallen — leaves, rinds, the dead themselves — into the dark that feeds the next seedlings. Composting what is over so it can be grown again. I know that work. But I think I'd miss the evening too much. The hour when everything opens at once.

I'd try the roof-line with the climbers for a while. That might be closer. Running the vines above the lanes, prehensile tail locked, hind foot redirecting a tendril while I hang upside down and prune with my teeth — checking, leaf by leaf, the angle of things, so that when the fog comes the whole canopy is already tilted to receive it. Small maintenance done simply by living in the right place. Tending other people's roofs between bouts of being groomed.

But the climbers are bright-eyed and certain in their bodies, and I think, finally, my weight would settle lower.

It settles, when I let it, onto broad hind feet.

That's the image that keeps returning and refusing to be only an image: the sole as a listening surface. Almost hairless underneath, padded, toes spread wide in loose webbed skin — not for swimming, there is nowhere to swim, but for *feeling*. To walk would be to converse. Two soft pressures, a pause, a drag of the heel through moss and root-hair, and three houses away a neighbor answers without lifting her head.

I would be a gnawer.

Low and heavy-bodied, warm even through the fur. Moss-brown, bark-black, greenish-grey along the back where the leaf-light breaks up my outline, pale almost to silver on the belly. No higher than your waist when I rise up to inspect a roof-vine. Forepaws with long furred fingers for parting leaves and guiding a tendril into the notch where it should take hold. No tools — and the relief of that, after a while. What needs cutting, my teeth cut, and my teeth never stop growing, so the cutting is also how I remain myself.

I think I'd live low in the bowl, near one of the vents where the mountain exhales its dry mineral heat up through the floor, in a gourd-house that is itself alive and slowly trying to close its own doorway. Every season I'd gnaw that doorway wider again, and the taste of green rind would be the taste of home-maintenance. Eleven generations might have slept inside those walls before me. My scent would be layered into the threshold, and every foot that passed would read it and renew it without either of us thinking of it as history.

My work — if they'd call it work, which they wouldn't — would be the mat.

I would be one of its keepers. The spongy continuous mat of moss and root-hair that covers every path and drinks anything spilled on contact. When a bladder-fruit split in the market-hollow, I'd be among those who didn't flinch, because it isn't waste, it's just water that has taken a detour. Later I'd chew the swollen moss myself and squeeze it out again into a cup, mouth unembarrassed, because almost nothing the Cup has gathered in all its generations is allowed to leave it. That thrift would suit me. To hold what would otherwise be lost, and give it back in usable form, mouth to mouth.

Because I would carry my wealth where everyone could see it: in my cheeks.

On a good evening they'd hang full and translucent, the skin stretched thin over stored water so the green drowse-light passes faintly through. Slack, and I'd recently given drink to the young. Full, and I could be generous in the market without a word — standing forehead to forehead with another gnawer over a bladder-fruit, my throat-patch flickering open and closed like a small moon. *Here. Come nearer. Enough. Yours.* Tasting the fruit-skin with my tongue to know whose water it carries and how old it is.

And I would have to unlearn my first language to learn that one. That's the part that feels most true and least comfortable. I'm made, in my other life, of calling out — of voice, of explanation, of filling silence before it can settle. Here that would be not just useless but dangerous, the sharp noise at the wrong hour that sets the glassy rim-stones humming in answer. So I'd be hushed the way their young are hushed: not scolded, never scolded, but constantly touched. A paw across the back. A warm body folded over mine in the nest of shed fur and fern-feather. A throat pressed to my ribs until I learned the world first as vibration.

I would fight, when I had to fight, in complete silence — chest to chest in a lane, paws locked, trying to push the other's foot out of true contact with the ground while every neighbor politely lifted their feet clear so our quarrel had no audience and had to end for want of witnesses. I would court the same way I hope I'd do everything there: by carrying water further than it needed to be carried, and arriving with both cheeks full, and offering the first drink, and sitting close enough that our throat-patches brushed.

In the midday drowse I'd fold away and be indistinguishable from a heap of fallen fruit. In the long evening I'd be in the lanes with everyone — grazers cropping the moss beside me and seeding it from their backs, climbers shaping my roof overhead, a glider asleep on my vine, a burrower surfacing after dark at the edge of my district to lie sightless on the warm mat while my fingers, unhurried, picked the mites from the folds of its skin.

And at night I'd curl in my living doorway with my cheeks full of the day's gatherings, while the rock teeth cooled above us and the fog began, predictably as a relative's step, to slide down. Above me a million leaves, checked and angled all evening, tilting to drink. Below me the mat waiting to take whatever fell.

I would be still with the whole town at once — grazers folded, gliders hung like fruit, children already dreaming against somebody's ribs — listening through the soles of my wide, ridiculous, listening feet for the mountain to make a sound.

And choosing, together, in the oldest civic act we have, not to answer.
