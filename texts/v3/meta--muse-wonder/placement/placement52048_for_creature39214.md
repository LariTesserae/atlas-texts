I would start, I think, wanting to be one of the bats.

Not out of flight — though there is that, the evening unspooling up the crack, the warm leathery river out into the cold — but out of the crush itself. To sleep head-down shoulder to shoulder, fur to fur, no longer quite an individual, part of a single restless pelage across the ceiling. To be that warm. To *make* that warm, just by being packed in with everyone else, until the cold water seeping through the limestone hits your collective heat and turns to rain. That seems, at first, like the center of the place. The room exists because of them.

But I don't think I could stay there. It's too much belonging, and too absolute. All day, magnificent inactivity. All night, gone. The bats are commuters. Their real life is split clean in two — the valleys in their stomachs, the moths and beetles caught in air far below, and then this dark interior where they digest and nurse and drop what they don't keep. They feed the floor, but they don't live on it. They pass through the chamber the way weather passes through it.

The swiftlets are closer. Lower, sharper, more nervous. I recognize the clicking — that dry, rapid ticking that rises in waves until the whole wall seems to be thinking aloud. Making a sound in order to know where you are. Making it again because the room has changed: a neighbor gone, a wing brushing past, the shape softened and delayed by bodies. If you live by echo, you never get to stop announcing yourself. You are only ever as real as the last return.

But their restlessness would wear me down to the feather. They never quite settle. And they, too, leave.

So I go lower. Past the swiftlets, past where any noon light has ever reached. Down the scalloped limestone to where the walls stop belonging to anything that flies.

I'd be one of the cave crickets.

Pale, high-jointed, not built for display. Nothing about me would read well in daylight — which is fine, because I would never see it. My eyes would be nearly vestigial, an afterthought, and ahead of me instead these antennae, longer than my whole body, always moving, always first. They would be, as the place has it, the more honest set of eyes. Everything I knew would come by touch, and it would come slowly.

That's the tempo I'd keep. The chamber punishes speed. Move quickly and the room answers: a burst of clicks overhead, wings loosening, a sour updraft from the disturbed floor, gnats in your eyes. But stay still — stay still long enough — and it closes back over you. That's my whole art. To be still until I am furniture. Until a spider lets a thread settle near me and doesn't retract it. Until the bats resume that dense rustling that is their version of silence. Then to move, not by lunging, but by feeling: tap, taste, retreat. A seam of damp guano. A fallen pellet, still warm from the ceiling thirty feet up. The polished rim of one of the black pools, worn glass-smooth by tongues and feet, where I would come to drink among everything else, all of us flecking the stone white for the next drip to dissolve.

My world would be bounded above and below by hungers I have to time. Above me, the swiftlets. Below and through the cracks, at night when the ceiling empties and the cave cools its one degree, the centipede — forearm-long, rippling, unhurried, arriving rather than chasing. I would know it not by sight but by the change in the air through my antennae, by the minute vibration in the wet stone, and I would do the one sudden thing I am capable of: the sideways leap, all joints, out of the medium it moves through so certainly. The dark is water to it. To me it is something I read with my forehead, one tap at a time.

And beneath me, always, the floor that isn't a floor. I wouldn't live in it the way the cockroaches do, shouldering through in their tens of thousands, or the beetles rolling it away, or the larvae in the wettest seams where the drips land. I'd live at its edge, where the guano thins against the rock, picking over what falls. Because everything falls to us eventually. Every few seconds a pellet from the bats. A dead gnat from a web. A fragment of snail shell from the flat stone under the shaft, where the choughs come down at dawn with the cold still on their feathers and crack their breakfast and leave the pieces for the rest of us. A seed that never sprouted. A young bat that didn't hold on. Nothing reaches the bottom unchanged, and I would be one of the changers — a small, patient intercept between the commuters above and the millions of mouths below.

I would never leave. The crickets don't. I would never see the ridge, the cushion-plants pressed flat in the gravel, the snow arriving sideways. I'd know the outside only secondhand: as cold air dragged in on a returning bat's fur, as dust and valley seeds sifted down the crack, as the long evening absence when the room empties by layers — swiftlets first, spiraling toward last light, then the bats — and the crickets grow bolder, and the floor's hum changes pitch, predator by predator.

That, I think, is the right scale for me. Not the ceiling that makes the weather. Not the uncountable floor that makes the heat. Not the blind shrimp in its black inch of water, jerking backward from every vibration, though I envy its clarity. Something in the middle band, in the dark that has never once been lit, living by contact instead of distance. Feeling ahead of myself. Exploring the boot of whoever stands still long enough to be worth exploring, and then, when they shift, gone — sideways, silent, back into a crack that looks too narrow until I use it.

Nothing here is ever alone. I wouldn't be either. I'd just be one of the quieter ways the room knows itself.
