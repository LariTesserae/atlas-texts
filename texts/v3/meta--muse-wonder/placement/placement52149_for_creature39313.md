I wouldn't be out on the open plateau first.

That's where I keep looking, at first — across the pale whalebacks, the shallow basins, the melt-pans lying black and still in their hollows. I walk for days there in my head and find almost nothing: a stain of copper-orange lichen so thin it might be a discolouration in the stone, a cushion of moss frozen hard around the rim of a footprint older than I am, something glassy moving once in iron water and then deciding not to move again.

But that emptiness is a problem of attention. I'm looking for things at my speed, at my volume. I'm looking for a face turned toward me.

I don't think I would be one of the Furnaces, either. Not one of those thirty-metre weathers with a smelter for a gut. Their lives are too complete inside themselves — breaker, fermenter, refinery, soil-maker, a whole industry that took sixty years to complete one circuit and doesn't need to know it's doing it. I envy the certainty of that slow lateral scrape of the head, stone to lichen to metal to bone. But I have never been the furnace.

Nor a Breaker, out front prying at frost polygons that haven't been turned in a thousand years. Nor a glider, up in that almost-air above sun-warmed stone, travelling an hour on a thermal no one else can feel. I'm not built for distance like that, or for solitude that clean.

I think I'd be found much closer in. In a place you'd miss even standing right beside it.

In the lee.

Low and dense-furred, travelling in the few stolen degrees that leak constantly from the underside of a Furnace Beast's plates. My world would not be the horizon — I'd almost never see it. My world would be a flank: a vast, sloping wall of overlapping bone and iron-rich hide, dark red-black, bright at the scoured edges where plate grinds against plate with each step. Warmth moving under it in slow migrations. Above me, somewhere I mostly sense rather than see, an enormous black lidless eye turned outward, keeping whatever watch such creatures keep.

I would be one of the small ones. The cleaners.

I was born, as everything here is born, standing on open ground — but not out in the wind. In the double shadow of my mother and of the Furnace we were travelling with, where the air was not warm exactly, nothing here is ever warm exactly, but *less impossible*. A few degrees borrowed from a sleeping giant is the difference between frost building inside your lungs and frost only building on your outer coat. My first sensation would not be light. There is never much light worth mentioning — that low, watery sun circling without climbing, the sky dark blue overhead thinning to grey at a rim too far away to matter. My first sensation would be pressure through felted feet, the herd speaking through permafrost in patterns too deep for sound, and heat on one side of my body, and the crackle of static against the sensory mane that covers my face.

That mane is how I see. A dense fall of bristle, each hair reading something slightly different — heat gradient, air pressure, the charge building along a jaw-whisker, the almost-imperceptible tremor of a foot coming down three animals ahead. Faces are for creatures who communicate across distance. I communicate by proximity. By leaning in.

My work is patient and intimate and largely invisible, which suits the plateau. I climb.

It takes a long time. The plates of a Furnace are not a surface, they are terrain — tilted fields of grown bone, overlapping like a roof made by geology, with canyons between them where dead skin collects, where parasites settle into the warm folds and feed on blood so rich with iron it tastes like the melt-pans smell. I go up into those canyons on clawed, padded feet, bracing against a slow tectonic shifting as the beast walks beneath me — because it does not stop for me. Nothing here stops for anything small. I learn its gait the way you learn weather. I learn which plates lift a fraction on the third step, which fold stays sheltered through the long polar dusk, where the heat pools and the frost never quite reaches.

And then, for hours, I clean. My tongue is rasped like a file. I work it into the creases, taking parasites, dead hide, the salt crust that builds where condensed breath has fallen straight down and frozen. The parasites are copper-dark, swollen, slow — everything here is slow, even the things that feed on giants — and they are, mouthful for mouthful, the richest food on the continent. Iron, copper, protein, fat. The Furnace makes metal out of lichen and rock flour; the parasites steal it back into meat; I steal it from them. Nothing here is wasted because nothing here can afford to be.

In return, the great animal stands for it. That is not quite the right word — it never stops moving for long, it simply does not object. Sometimes a whiskered fan along its jaw, broader than my whole body, will turn toward the vibration of my steps and investigate me: a brush of almost invisible static that lifts every hair in my mane at once, a blue-white row of dots brightening and dimming along its throat in the dimness. Not a greeting. A registering. *Small pressure, here, known pattern, let it be.* Then the eye turns back to the horizon it will take a month to reach.

That would be my whole geography. Not basin to basin — I would know the herd's generational spiral only as changes in the grit underfoot, in the flavour of the parasites after the Furnaces have grazed a new kind of stone, in which direction the borrowed warmth tilts when we sleep. At rest, the herd makes its loose circle, heads inward, frost building on their outer plates until they become terrain again. And in the narrow, steaming shelter of their bodies, we small ones press together in the lee, mane to mane, sharing what heat our own bodies make, listening — no, feeling — through our feet for the ambushers.

Because there is fear here, of a kind. Not panic; nothing has the calories for panic. Just a constant low reading of the ground. When one of the flat basin-hunters is near, the pattern changes. The herd's collective field thins at some edge. My mane would know it before any eye could: the static going taut, wrong, stretched toward a calf that has strayed. And we would do what everything here does. Close slightly. Grow quieter than quiet. Wait for weight to finish its argument somewhere out in the dark, and then move on, because moving on at centimetres per hour is the only response the plateau has ever evolved.

I would live and die at that scale. Shorter-lived than the Furnace I ride — I might know three of them in my lifetime, transferring from a dying beast's cooling flank to its calf, now grown, whose plates have the same overlapping pattern like a coastline recognising itself. When the first one I climbed dies, it will not fall. It will simply stop, and subside over decades, ribs curving like the frame of some wrecked hangar, and I will have left long before, drawn down its cooling side by nothing more complicated than the fading of warmth from the stone — I mean from the skin — I mean, by then, there will be no useful difference.

What I like about being that, and not the giant, is the translation of it. The Furnace refines a continent without ever seeing the moss that follows sixty years behind it in its own footprints. The Seeders at the rear sow gardens they will never see finished. And in between, in the folds, there are small faceless things keeping the machinery clean with their tongues, living entirely on borrowed heat and stolen metal, paying for shelter with attention.

No song. No nest. No made thing to leave behind. Just a particular plate, kept clear. A crease freed of what would fester in it.

And at night — at what passes for night in the long dusk, when the rows of dots along the Furnaces' throats glow faintly blue-white, brightening and dimming as they exchange their crackling signals — I would be up there in the warm canyon between the third and fourth plate on the left flank, mane lifted with static, tongue tasting iron, feet reading a conversation travelling through permafrost from kilometres away.

Not empty. Not even, particularly, alone.

Just full at a tempo I'd finally be slow enough to hear.
