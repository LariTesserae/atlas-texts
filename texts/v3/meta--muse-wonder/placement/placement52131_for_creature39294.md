At first I think I would find myself standing.

That is the traveler’s assumption, and travelers are allowed it for a while. Ankle-deep, probably. On a broad stair that goes down and down, and every step holding its own level, private flood. The water clear and scalding-clean over white tile, exactly to the bone, never deeper, because depth — like everything else — has not been agreed upon yet. My feet are sore from the fresh slabs. My lungs are full of steam that tastes faintly of copper and clay. Somewhere a lamp is burning in a wall that has no building attached to it, and I am very certain, at first, that I have just arrived.

It doesn’t hold. Arrival is a finished idea. Finished ideas are unstable here.

Maybe I am lower than standing. Maybe I am in the mixing trough, shouldering through lime slurry in a slow half-turn, stopping whenever I am looked at directly — not hiding, exactly, just unable to be movement and be observed at the same time, because observation is another finishing step and I am not finished. When no one watches, I advance. The slurry folds over me. It is warm the way the inside of a mouth is warm. It cures around me before it has been mixed, and then un-cures, dissatisfied. I get very good at patience that looks, from outside, like stillness.

Or I am in the lamp in the lone wall.

That fits longer. A small clay throat, sweating my own oil up out of my own body, burning blue and nearly smokeless in a niche with a door beside it that opens onto nothing yet. Kilometers of wet pavement and only me lit. It would be lonely, except loneliness requires someone missing, and there is no one scheduled to miss. I watch the door. Most days there is floor behind it. Some days, in peripheral vision, there is a room, and the room smells of fresh mortar, and wet footprints are already drying across it, leading in. I do not trim myself. No hand comes. I burn anyway. That is the whole duty and it is not a small one: to keep a light in a place that is not a place, for a tenant who is not a tenant, until the wall decides whether it was a fragment or a beginning.

But lamps get built into other walls. Walls migrate. The avenue folds gently, like wet clay in a cloth, and the kiln that was three days away is blistering-close, and the stockpile of numbered boulders for the rehearsal mountains stays exactly on the horizon, and I notice, somewhere in the thinning steam that passes for morning, that I am not casting light the way I thought.

I am casting shade.

It starts, or I start noticing it starts, under the placeholder trees. Fired-clay trunks, hollow, numbered at the base, branches bare as antlers, waiting for leaves to be invented. I am leafy under them. That is not vanity — I have no shape of my own at first, only the shape of what is expected. A dappled coolness on hot tile, fringed, trembling slightly, although there is no wind and no leaf overhead to tremble. The tree above me has never cast me. I am arriving before it, days before, the way the boulevards lie shaded by towers that are still only chalk outlines and a smell of wet rope. I practice. I learn the angle. I lie across the paving in the late brightening with all my little unborn leaves distinct, and the tile beneath me stays a degree cooler than the tile around, and for whole hours that is as much of a body as anything here gets.

From down there — being a flat, cool argument on the floor — the world makes a different kind of sense. I stop looking for builders’ faces. I feel them instead, or it, through the soles that I don’t have: the vast ticking underneath, foundation stones crawling against each other like a slow shoal, tiles lifting at the seams and settling, mortar breathing between one floor and the next floor under it and the scaffolding under that, all the way down. It isn’t carrying me. It is making room under me, continuously, politely, the way it makes room for everything it has promised itself later.

And I start appearing elsewhere the way shadows here do: ahead of schedule.

A colonnade’s shadow, arch by arch, on an empty avenue, and somewhere among those arches, mine. Not the colonnade’s — there are no columns yet, they’re still numbered in a heap three days’ walk and forever away. Mine, specifically. A taller pool of cool in a doorway before the doorway has met its pillar. A smaller shape running a few steps ahead through a courtyard that is, for one held note, perfect — lamps lit, cistern brimming, placeholder animals smooth and eyeless on their plinths — running not in fear but in the ordinary, domestic way small things run in courtyards, in and out of tree-shade that hasn’t happened. Domestic. That word lands under the floor like a foundation stone finding its neighbor. *Clack.*

Perhaps that is what I am. Not the builder between the floors. Not the green film it so carefully, so unmaliciously repaves out of existence. I am one of the reasons for the baths.

The bath complex stands a day’s walk from anything, basins empty but warm, steps hollowed in advance to the shape feet will make after ten thousand years of feet. Someone has to be the ten thousand years. Someone has to be expected that specifically. The observatory’s mosaic shows constellations the painted stars overhead haven’t agreed to; someone has to be the eye that will someday reconcile them, standing at the copper slit, looking up at the inside of an unfinished sky. The clay animals keep their blank plaques. The trees keep their borrowed shade. All of it is a census written before a population, addresses on bricks for houses not yet grown inside their scaffolds — and I am in the census. Not my name. There is nowhere for a name to go yet. Just a number, fresh-cut and sharp enough to catch skin, low on a smooth shape in an empty courtyard, and a blank rectangle left smooth on its flank.

I practice being watched the way the animals do. Eyeless, patient, I let travelers invent a builder to explain me. I let them leave their tools in courteous rows, edge-up, so they can be found in the dark, and I do not tell them that by morning the row is gone and the tools are filed inside a wet new wall a league off, and their own hands are white with lime they don’t remember touching. It isn’t cruelty. Stealing is a finished law. So is helping. What I do, down on the tile in my leaf-fringed cool, is simpler and worse and kinder: I arrive in rooms slightly before the person who thinks he casts me. I lead him through doorways that exist only while the lime grows down from the vault and kisses the pillar growing up. On the other side the lamps burn on the reversed sides of their niches and his footprints are already ahead of him, setting into tile, making a road other maps will disagree with.

He is not becoming me. I am not becoming him. It is not that ratified.

Call me a tenant, then, if a word is needed before the word is ready. The future kind. The preliminary population. I live in the ankle-deep stair where each step keeps its own flood; I live in the shade of the forest canopy spread over fresh pavement where no forest has been scheduled past the clay-trunk stage; I live in the pause between a traveler’s breaths just before the world, courteously or hungrily, paves it solid. I am not alive — the world is careful about that, and so, by now, am I. Life would narrow me. A finished thing casts only its own shadow, and I can still be the leaf before the tree, the footfall before the foot, the small running shape before the courtyard takes itself apart into numbered pieces and carries us both, separately, to wherever such things are stored.

I can wait. The baths are already warm. The number at my base is sharp and clean. The blank space where the plaque will go is smooth as an uncut eye, and somewhere under the floor that has no underside, something vast and handless and patient is holding the whole unfinished world in mind — and, increasingly, it is holding it at exactly my temperature.
