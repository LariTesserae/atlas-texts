I come in, at first, the way you do — from below, on foot, expecting my ears to be the organ that understands the place.

They aren't. My ears give up almost immediately. Then my molars take over, and my sternum, and the soft wrong place behind my eyes where pressure registers before pain does. Shouting would be a private embarrassment. Even thought starts to feel like an unnecessary vibration.

I try to imagine myself as one of the keepers. I want that role, at first — forehead to forehead, palm flat on a speaking-plate, moving from resonator to balloon-tree making small, competent corrections. But a keeper still has to *leave* a node when the chord shifts. A keeper has lungs that an anti-node can empty without asking, and skin that the dry wind is patiently unmaking. To stay human here you'd have to keep negotiating.

And I don't want to negotiate. I want to metabolize.

So the lizard tempts me next — soot-colored, membrane-winged, folded into the vortex, asleep on the warmest layer of the circling air. Never landing. Waking only to take a midge out of a swarm so cleanly the swarm doesn't notice its own wound. But that's too solitary a fantasy, and the bowl corrects it quickly: nothing here lives alone, and the lizard only gets to seem alone because millions of smaller lives are holding the air up underneath it.

The midges correct it further. One midge is nearly nothing — a jointed speck, too finely made, wingbeats phase-locked to the dominant tone. But there is no *one* midge, not really. There is a sheet. A column. A sudden hand. A bright stream flowing under a hovering slab, carrying pollen like black mercury, carrying seeds, carrying pieces of the bowl's technology to wherever the bowl needs them next. At night they stop being transport and become architecture, building warm shapes out of their own bodies to roost inside. If I were them I wouldn't be a creature. I'd be a temporary agreement among millions to have a shape.

That gets closer. But I want roots as well as wings.

So lower. Slower. Down where the standing waves cross.

Where I actually find myself is at a node on the eastern side of the bowl, not far from the leeward gap, in one of those obscenely dense clumps that shouldn't exist at four thousand meters in a place with no water at all. From a distance you'd point and call me a single thing: a pale balloon-tree, barely two meters tall.

Up close the pointing gets harder.

My trunk is wrapped in cork-thick bark, insulating me against the five-degree air and the colder shadow. I have no leaves in any lowland sense. My canopy is a cluster of taut, translucent sacs, and they are drumming — tightening, loosening, tightening again — not to catch light but to catch *this*: the jet caught in the flutes of the wall, circling, layering tone on tone until the air is a standing event. I eat sound the way a forest tree eats sun. It passes into the sacs as violence and comes down through me as usable warmth, as pressure I can spend.

At my feet — and "my" is already a polite fiction — teal ferns clench and unclench their many-chambered fronds on the bass note. The violet not-moss ripples without pause, thousands of dry bladders inflating and sighing, inflating and sighing, moving gas through the root-mat we all share. Break one of our stems and you won't wet your finger. You'll get cold oil, turpentine-smelling, or a bead of silver stuff that crawls away from your heat. None of us transpire. None of us can afford to give water away. We are lush precisely because we refuse to.

My roots don't dig for water either. They dig for resonance. They are listening downward, feeling through black grit and glassy dust for the exact depth where the rock hums truest. And when the note shifts — it is always shifting, twenty times a minute the vortex breaks and reforms — I do the thing that would have frightened me when I had shins to feel it in: I loosen. The whole root-mat lets go. I slew half a meter across the bowl floor on a cushion of agitated dust, graceless, obscene, *fast* for a tree, and then I find the better note and lock down again with a *thunk* that travels through the ground into everything rooted near me.

That thunk is partly how I talk. The rest is through the mat. Through shared pressure. Through the ferns opening when my sacs have cleaned a pocket of air enough for them to risk it. Through the midges, who land on me in their brief coherent thousands and carry my black pollen to another clump and bring back granules I cannot name and do not need to. Once in a while a drum-moth settles across two of my sacs — big as both your hands, mostly ear — and lets the scream pass through its body. What leaves it is cleaner than what entered. Tuned. My canopy stops fluttering wrong. I had not known I was fluttering wrong. That's what having neighbors means here.

Six meters away, or seven — my sense of distance is mostly a sense of phase — one of the resonators stands furred with growth the same green-teal as my ferns. You mistook it for a building at first. I never did. Its door beats like an eardrum. Its wall, last week, unfolded two centimeters to meet a change in the wind, and a crack in its stone-that-isn't-stone knitted itself shut over the course of a minute, sweating faint heat. It takes in screaming air and exhales it a little warmer, a little calmer, into my clump. I, in return, hold stillness in pockets around my trunk where smaller things can survive the crescendos. Neither of us is housing the other. We are organs that happen to be separable at a glance.

I don't experience the bowl as loud, the way you did. Loud is a word for a signal arriving from outside. There is no outside; when I was a juvenile I *was* outside — a spiny lattice of a thing, a tumble-shrub, thrown endlessly around the circuit of the wall, planting for a second, a minute, tearing free. That is how you arrive here. You don't germinate into peace. You get hurled until a note catches you.

Now the chord is not what I hear. It's what I'm *in*, the way a fish is in a current, except the current is also the food and the weather and the medium my thoughts would have to travel through if I had thoughts in your format. I have something slower than thought. I have adjustments. At evening, when the light goes ordinary and gold and the cold deepens, my sacs retune with the rest of the bowl. The resonators walk the chord down by a third, into lower frequencies, and my whole canopy tightens to meet it. The ferns fold their felt leaves around pockets of our own made weather. The moss sighs slower. Above me the midges thicken into a roosting shape, warm with their own multitude, and the lizard folds once more into the warm layer and is gone from attention without ever having landed.

I do not rest. Rest is the one idea the bowl has spent ten thousand years unteaching me. Stillness, arrived at honestly, just means you've drifted out of tune, and out of tune means the dry that is waiting everywhere outside the nodes — the powdered plateau, the freeze-drying wind, the afternoon in which an unprotected place scours itself clean — gets its chance. The keepers know it. I know it in my bark. If the scream ever stopped, we would not finally get peace.

We would get exposed.

So: if you come back, don't look for me as an individual. Look for the place where the air is briefly thick enough to lean on, where the grit stops performing its patterns and instead is hidden under waist-high teal and violet density. Look for the pale sac-cluster drumming at the center of it, for the scorched-looking dust trail where I last moved and found the note again. Put your palm flat on the ground beside my root-mat. You won't hear me through your ears — your ears are the wrong equipment and have been the whole time.

You'll feel the *thunk* of my settling come up through your wrist, though, when the wind changes.

That will be me, and the ferns, and the mat, and the moth cleaning the chord as it crosses my canopy, saying, in the only language the bowl has ever trusted: *here. Still here. On note.*
