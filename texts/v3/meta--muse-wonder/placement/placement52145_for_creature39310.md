I think I would start — because you do start somewhere, even if the Worksfolk would laugh at the idea of a founding — lower than I expected.

Not at the top. I want the top, at first. From the description of it I go straight there: the white walls and the thin bright pain of the air, the fog nets singing, the orchards stunted and fragrant, the real mountains reduced to a low blue irrelevance. I want to be lean and wind-cut and speak less because the air is expensive.

But my ears would ring. I know that about myself. I'd be bent double at a landing with my water skin flat against my spine, chewing the bitter leaves and gagging a little on the tea like any newcomer, while a Top-born child politely did not watch me fail at altitude. I don't have the lungs that take four generations to make. I couldn't pretend I did.

And not the base, either, though I'd love it longest as a visitor. The arrival yards, the markets, the mules steaming, the soil yards where everything — ash, dung, crushed brick, kitchen rot, grandmother — is turned until it steams and sweetens. That's sacred work and I respect the black-to-the-wrist hands, but I'm not broad enough for it. I'm not a market person. I'd get lost in the clamor and love it and be useless by noon.

So: the middle. Somewhere in the tangle where I could be useful at the speed the place actually moves.

I'd live two or three levels below the dye-works, probably, where their warmth leaks through the walls all year and ferns come out of the drainpipes whether anyone planted them or not. A room in the deep interior, or half in it — a door onto a corridor where the electric light buzzes and flickers, and the other door onto a roof court just wide enough for beans, a tank under a grate, and a fig in a cracked planter that is technically mine to maintain and therefore technically a structural responsibility. I'd learn which stone lips of the beds you may step on. I'd learn to step only there.

And they'd find work for my particular kind of attention. I think I'd become a pump-keeper. Or a pump-keeper's assistant who never quite graduated to anything louder.

It suits the body I'd grow there. Not the big hauling hands, exactly, but hands that learn by palm — whether a pipe is running true or knocking with air. Ears that get good in the dark of a maintenance shaft. I would put my cheek to the warm iron at night, when the looms have stopped and the generators have settled to their lower hum, and I would listen cistern to cistern and be able to say: that level is full, that one is stealing, that one will overflow by morning and someone should be warned before the street below wakes up under somebody else's rain.

Because water is on a seven-day cycle there, I'd live by wetness arriving. Not by days. My week would be a tank filling behind a wall. A spout beginning to tick. Frogs shifting in a shaded sump. Children would know my knock because I'd be the one who comes, grave as they are, with a bucket when a new cistern is finished — carrying the frogs up by hand from the old tank to the new one, so the new water has its inhabitants from the start. I'd take that job with the seriousness it deserves. Nothing in the Highworks is decoration. The swifts eat the midges. The frogs keep the sumps. I keep the frogs moving. That's a whole theology, carried in a bucket with a lid chained to its handle.

Everyone carries something. Mine would rarely be heavy, which would embarrass me a little among mule people and soil-makers, until I understood that mine was just harder to see. I'd carry messages between levels that are almost different countries. Up: word that a middle-level weaver's daughter is willing to talk marriage, but her family wants the cistern rights kept and six sacks of soil to come with her, and the children raised high enough to get the lungs. Down: word that a net has torn, that a tank has cracked two streets below the dye-works, that hands and ropes and mats are needed at the bell. I'd learn the lip-reading way of speaking beside the looms — leaning in, a touch to the forearm, half a sentence offered and the machines supplying the rest — and the sign-talk for at rest, quick flickers while a shuttle clatters. I am good at carrying what was almost said. I could be trusted with the half that isn't noise.

My clothes would tell it. Mill-smock for the damp middle, most days. A white shawl and gloves tied at my waist for the days I have to go up past my comfort, and market cotton rolled under everything for the days I go down into the soup, the gravy-air, and come back headachy and smelling of citrus peel and dung. Nothing trailing. Everything hooked, clipped, knotted. Shake out my jacket and you'd find string, chalk for marking a damp patch on a wall, a folding knife, leaf for my ears, seed packets I meant to give someone three levels ago, a spare bell that belongs to a goat I haven't identified yet. I would pick burrs off people in doorways without thinking about it and flick them — not down, never down — into the nearest planter. Mint. Tomato. Someone's next year.

I'd get the sideways gait. One leg stronger than the other from the central ramps spiraling always the same way. Calves like rope. A squint I carry indoors. Lime dust in my eyelashes by evening. And I would have, on my own terrace edge, the thing I'm most afraid of neglecting: because in the Highworks a cracked planter isn't untidiness, it's a wound you inflict on a neighbor. So I'd become, slowly, the kind of person who checks. Old women sit in roof orchards shelling beans and watching for a fig root getting ambitious; I'd join them earlier than my age strictly required, shelling, listening to a wall, watching the soil line for slip. It's the same work at different scales. Listen to the pipe. Watch the planter. Nothing underneath us is holding us up. We are holding each other up, and holding is mostly noticing in time.

I don't think I'd ever go all the way down to the ground. There isn't any, that's the point — drill through my courtyard and I'd hit someone's ceiling, a shaft smelling of hot oil, a roof garden. So I'd stop asking where the mountain rests. I'd measure my life the way they do: born, or arrived, when they capped something. Living in the year the nets tore and were mended. Working on a repair my predecessor began and someone after me will finish, and finding that not frustrating but correct. A finished place cannot take another garden.

When I die — and I'd want them to do it the plain unsolemn way — burn me in the deep furnaces on a slow day and mix me down at the yards. Rest the ash. Carry it back up in sacks with a family stamp in the weave. Some of me in the bean beds. Some of me, if I'm lucky and if the children who carried my frogs remember me kindly, under the fig in the cracked planter outside my own door.

And then in a few years someone new, breathless, still learning not to throw, would eat a tomato off that terrace and stand a moment with their hand flat on a warm pipe, listening for whether the water was coming up true.

That would be enough of a haunting for me.
