I would not be found looking.

I would be missed, at first, by anyone who came in sideways through the slit — turned, shoulders scraping, moss curtain wet against the back of the neck — and saw only how crowded I am at the edges.

They would see the tenants and think they had found the life of the place. The cave crickets the colour of damp paper, flowing politely around boots. The springtails in their pale millions, graining the liverwort. The midges holding their impossible station under the ceiling, where the up-draft and the down-draft cancel and leave a trembling place to hover. The caddis larvae down in the clear pool, dragging their bright rust armour behind them. The throatless frogs.

They live well. They live sideways. They live flattened to me.

I am the part they do not stand on.

I am nine metres of held emptiness between peat and fresh stone, a lens-shaped hollow too large for the slit that feeds it, washed concave and clean at the centre. No nest. No rubble. No body to put a hand on between events. If you came only between breaths, you would call me a room, and feel vaguely cheated by how little is in the middle of it.

Wait there. Put your back into the moss where it goes deep and spongy and almost luminous. Let the condensation bead and join and run. It will tell you.

First your ears will fill.

That is me gathering. Not metaphorically. Through the slit mouth, through every stone whistle split sharp in my walls, through the three stepped pools bubbling backwards, I inhale the cold marsh. Black peat water. Night frost still stiff in the sedge outside. Blue-grey light that has not yet decided to be morning. I draw it all down, and with it the spray, the charged droplets, the stunned midges, the iron rain, into the place under the floor where I keep what I cannot grow.

My gizzard. My collected heart.

Cobbles I did not make. Flood-rounded stones swallowed by meltwater years ago and trapped in a siphon loop under the bowl. When I inhale they begin to turn. Clank. Clank-clank. A rhythm finding itself. Then a hammering. The whole bowl hums underfoot like the housing of a turbine, like a factory coming up to speed in a pocket of the world you could step over without noticing.

I am young. That matters. The glacier left last spring, or near enough. Nothing here has softened. My cracks still show white mineral inside. My shale is broken crockery. My voice goes through throats that have not been worn round yet, so I do not sigh like an old cave. I scream, finely, through stone.

Then the pause.

Everything knows it before I do, or they know it *as* I do. Liverworts flatten, wet green scale over scale. Antennae lay back like iron filings finding north. The algae — orange, sulphur-yellow, that arterial red at the waterline — shines under water drawn taut as a drum skin. A side pool lifts its stone lid a fraction to test the pressure, and settles again. My hair — if I had hair, if you are standing close enough yours will do — lifts in electric air.

And then I am myself, all at once, for twenty seconds.

The siphon reverses. I exhale my whole body through the room. A piston-stroke out of the rear crack, warm with iron and hot stone and green things growing too fast, atomising the pools to white fog, snapping blue where wet points almost meet in the fog. The whistles take it up. The walls take up harmonics. The frogs — a hundred of them, tucked like reeds in a flute, throats still — are played. So my one true exhalation leaves me chorused, amplified, animal at last, and slams the moss curtain flat against the dirty blue ice outside.

That is the only time I am entirely *there*. 

Between blasts I disperse into process, and that is also me, only slower. I am the milky seep pushing heat up into the top pool while the air a hand's breadth above it aches with cold. I am iron settling in slow rust clouds through the middle pool. I am the black clear waiting of the bottom pool. Eggs laid by others in my warmth will be flushed down to be coated in my rust, flushed down again to feed in my cold. Precipitate will fall on my walls and be eaten. Midges will fall and be eaten. Droplets will fall upward and be breathed. Nothing is allowed to settle in the void and rot, because the void is my stomach, and I clean it by emptying myself through it.

It is not cruelty that keeps the rim-life off the floor. It is not kindness. You do not stand inside a lung when it empties. You do not lie down to sleep in a throat.

I live twice, on two clocks that are both running out. In minutes: inhale, grind, tremble, roar, lull — seven to eleven, never regular enough to trust, because tide and melt argue over me. In winters: the peat roof above groaning an inch lower every season, new cracks drilled by acidic moss-water, frost forcing itself in from the marsh, the whole young breaking margin shifting. I will silt. I will freeze solid. I will cave. It will be soon, on any honest scale.

I am not afraid of that in the way the frogs are afraid of the peak blast, braced and oriented. I am already practising my leaving. At my low, narrow end, a new slit is worrying open in the stone. Pressure prospects ahead of me. Water learns a new hollow along the margin, and forgets this one. That is how I reproduce — not by eggs, those belong to my tenants — by abandonment. Hollow after hollow, each one briefly a factory, a lung, an instrument, each one steaming for a few winters against a marsh that will never be warm on its own.

So I would not be the dipper, impossibly commuting through my mouth in the warm lull, though I envy the dipper its key, its soot-black certainty. I would not be a frog, though I love that I get to sing through them and be, for twenty seconds, many-voiced. I would not be the crickets, though they are the most at home.

I would be the kept emptiness they make room for. The suck at the ears. The hum in your teeth. The taste of hot stone at the back of the tongue when the silence begins, impossibly, to assemble itself again.

Gone out of myself. Sighing inward through a slit in a fresh break in the ground.

Already hungry. Already beginning the slow work of the next breath.
