I keep starting too far out.

Out on the yard first, in the white glare at 3,800 meters, where the air is too thin to keep anything it is given — not heat, not moisture, not sound, except that the crushers have turned sound into pressure and the pressure is in everything anyway. I think if I were there I would have to be something that moves through it. A swallow, maybe. They make it look possible. They come and go through the roar as if the roar were only weather, cut between the conveyor belts, ride the updraft off the exhaust ducting, take moths out of the floodlights even at noon. A whole life beakful by beakful, mud from the settling ponds below, carried up and glued into a gourd under the overhang.

But I wouldn't last as a swallow. I can feel that pretty quickly. Too much leaving. Too much precision required in a space a handspan wide, wings clapping off the walls without ever touching them. I would want, eventually, to stop beating against the brightness.

So I drift a little lower, to the ledges in the first meter. That's the bright country, relatively speaking.

The lizards are closer to my temperature. Dust-colored, pink-grey, flattened to the fresh andesite as if the rock had pressed them into its own shape. Bellies spread to gather heat, chins down, only the throat moving — pulse, pulse, pulse, papery and quick — spending back a little of the warmth they've worked all morning to collect. The old male on the highest ledge, scarred down one flank, tail grown back blunt and darker than the rest of him, holding a territory you could measure in inches and defending it mostly by having been there longest.

I could be him for a while. There's something honest in living thriftily at altitude, in knowing your blood is only ever as warm as the stone you can find, in arranging your whole day around a wall that — miracle of miracles — never quite goes cold, because on the other side of it men have built a crusher house that leaks its heat through concrete all night. To start earlier than any lizard on the open plateau. To finish later. To learn that a boot going past, a shoulder scraping sideways, a shadow crossing, does not require flight. Open one eye. Shift a centimeter. That is enough. Nothing that eats you can turn in here.

But the lizard still has somewhere to vanish to, his own crack, and he owns his ledge. And the longer I stay in the seam the less I believe anyone in it owns anything outright. The swallow droppings spatter the lizard's rock white over tan. The lizard, stirring, knocks shed skin and beetle husks down into the dark. Everything is feeding the floor below it without intending to. Everything is being fed.

So I go deeper, where I have to turn my shoulders.

This is the part my hands learn before my eyes do. The rock here is new — that is the strangeness of it that won't wear off. Never weathered. Blast fractures still sharp, edges still the pale color the andesite was inside the mountain before it ever met light. No seep, no stain, no lichen softening it. Only dust: mineral dust, diesel soot sifted in from the yard, and underneath it a finer dust, tan and acrid, that gets into the seams of skin and stays. My shirt catches. The crusher house wall is hot against my back. The cut face is hot against my chest. Forty centimeters, six meters, and narrowing at both ends to a crack you could slide a hand into and no more — a place nobody built, nobody planned, between the back of the building and the mountain, that exists only because the foundations were poured a careful distance from the face and no one thought about the distance afterward.

In the brown dimness the tenants get smaller and more numerous, and this starts to feel truer. Darkling beetles packed shoulder to shoulder into the fissures for the day, black cases gone dull with dust, waiting for dark to move. Earwigs threading the same cracks. A wolf spider with her legs folded tight against her body to fit the width of her chosen split, eyes catching a stray photon green, an egg sac almost as big as her abdomen wedged so carefully it doesn't scrape. When they hatch they'll ride her back in a living crust until even the seam is too crowded for them.

I could stay there a long time. That middle zone is the seam's processor, and I recognize the work. Nothing grows in here. No green thing, no moss in a crack. So everything lives on what arrived from somewhere else and died, or was dropped. Moths drawn across kilometers of cold plateau to the plant's white lights, battering themselves against the hot wall and falling papery into the crack. Flies for the droppings. Beetles for the moths and the maggots. Spiders for the beetles. Lizards coming down as far as the light lets them, for the spiders. An import business, steady as a shift roster, hung entirely on waste heat and waste light. I know what it is to live on leavings and call it an ecology. Most of us do, if we're honest.

But even the spider holds her own fissure. Even the beetles wait. And I keep being pulled — by the temperature, mostly, the way you found the place in the first place — toward the far end, where the seam widens just enough to make a pocket no bigger than a wardrobe turned on its side, and the air gets close and used and physically warm, and the smell changes: hot iron, ozone, old guano ground to powder, and underneath it warm animal, musk, old leather, something sweetish from insects digested and re-digested.

There is no bare rock in there. That's where I stop being able to pretend I would be one animal.

Hundreds of mouse-eared bats, hanging head-down in a single continuous mass, a second ceiling made of bodies. Hind feet gripping fractures no wider than a fingernail. Wings folded forward over their chests like cloaks, each back pressed to the next flank, fur gone tan at the tips with dust. Long ears — absurdly long on one bat alone — folded back, or twitching upright in slow rotation, so that across the whole cluster there are always a dozen ears up and listening while the rest sleep. One shifts its grip and the adjustment ripples, shoulder to shoulder, claw to rock, across the width of them and settles. A pup parked in the densest part while its mother hunts, kept warm on all sides by bodies that are not its mother. If it falls, it falls soft, onto guano built a centimeter a year, pellets over powder over compressed powder, warm all the way through.

That is where I would find myself. Not at the edge of the mass, where you could still imagine peeling away alone, and not exactly at the center either, where the pups are. Somewhere in the shoulder of it. One bat among hundreds, indistinguishable, which turns out not to be a loss of self so much as a different physics of self. My warmth isn't mine. It never gets a chance to leave before it meets another back. My hearing isn't mine either — my ear comes up in rotation, does its listening, folds down, and another takes over without anything needing to be decided. When I shift, I move everyone slightly. When anyone shifts, I am moved. We have recalibrated fear down to almost nothing, not out of tameness but out of geometry: there is nowhere to pour out to, no room for a hundred wings to unfold at once, so a torch beam, a slow body at arm's length underneath us, gets watched instead of fled. Ears track it. The thin social squeaking goes on like wet fingers on glass. It sharpens if dust falls. It settles again.

*Calm is not tameness here; it is physics, learned animal by animal and taught by the walls.* I think I have been trying to learn that in rooms much larger than this one and failing because the rooms let me panic.

And then perhaps even that identification — one bat — is still too small, the way saying the bats live here was true but not true enough. Because what I actually recognize, standing in the pocket with the crusher grinding through the rock into my sternum and my molars, is the whole seam's single shared temperament: the lizards holding, the spiders holding, the swallows timing the conveyors, the beetles waiting out the day in their fissures, the bats watching instead of bursting — every zone pressed so close to the next that the narrow mistake in the rock has become something like a body, lived in the way a body is lived in, in layers.

So let me be that, finally. Not a tenant. The tenancy.

I am forty centimeters wide and six meters long, high on a plateau that freezes before dawn and burns white at noon, and I hold a temperature neither of those weathers can account for. Sun-baked stone above me, a crusher house radiating against one wall, exhaust ducting warming the mass through, and then all these warm bodies adding their own until at midday in my deep end it sits near forty degrees and I breathe out hot, ammoniac air past anyone walking the service track, and they find me by that breath without ever seeing me. I change at a geological pace wearing a biological disguise: guano rising a centimeter a year, a slab spalling off my fresh face once a decade to rearrange the ledges, young swallows fledging into the roar and learning its gaps by heart.

My favorite hour is the one that isn't really an hour. Shift change, early evening, when the crushers idle and the pressure in the molars eases and a cooler breath sucks in through my mouth. The swallows make their last sorties and crowd heads-out into their mud retorts. The lizards go torpid on still-warm stone. And my far end begins to peel — one bat dropping, opening, finding the exit by a memory of turns laid down in complete blackness, then another, then a loose unhurried stream, each waiting for the air ahead to clear, out into the floodlit yard and gone into hunting circuits over the settling ponds where midges rise even on cold nights. For a while I am briefly, emptily myself: hot dark rock, settling dust, spiders reclaiming the vacated walls, beetles beginning their slow patrol, the guano slope ticking as it cools.

I don't mind the emptiness. I know the return the way rock knows anything, by holding heat through it.

Before dawn, when the wind is scouring dust across the yard in sheets and the plateau is at its most murderously cold, they come back one by one, fur cold-smelling, bellies tight with insects, and rebuild the mass in reverse — claw finding fracture, wing folding over a neighbor's back, ripple after ripple settling until there is no bare rock to see. The lizards are already waiting for a heat that will not fail them. The swallows are already muttering in their sleep.

And I breathe out once more into the morning, hot and alive wall to wall, and hold.
