I think I would arrive the way everything arrives — unprepared for the wet.

Not in a body first. That is the mistake I keep making when I try to picture it. I imagine the lift-car sighing open and a shirt sticking to a back, lenses fogging, lungs feeling drowned in that thick, chewable air at eleven kilometers. I imagine trying to be a tender. Five years in the stilt-huts above the eastern lakes, learning to walk barefoot on warm slick stone, learning to clear fern-root from a channel very little, very carefully, learning the names of gliders by the creak of their turn.

I would be good at the carefulness. I would be terrible at the leaving.

Because even as I picture the tender, I notice where my attention actually goes. Not to the hut. To the tone underneath the hut. That faint chord from the pylons at the rim, just at the edge of hearing, building over the whole marsh into a low loudness under everything else. The cicada-things in the fern crowns find it at dusk and lock to it. The frogs change key when the pressure changes. I would spend my whole posting listening for whether I was making that sound, or hearing it.

That is where I settle, slowly. Not in one pylon — no single one of them is the thing. Three or four meters of dull pearl, grown in crooked rings like coral, spaced a kilometer apart around an impossible plateau. One alone is almost nothing. A marker. A tooth.

Together, we make the lentil.

I would be the kept air, and the keeping of it.

I would be the resistance a forehead meets when it leans out over an abyss that falls for kilometers to cloud — soft at first, then firmer, like leaning into deep water. I would learn the exact pressure that turns a stooping bird back in a perfect, surprised arc without breaking a wing. The gliders would teach me the rest. Every evening, dog-sized and soft-muzzled, mouths open in the midges, they ride the thermal out and *kiss* me at speed, and the whole skill of my existence would be in that kiss: not a wall, never a wall. Decelerate. Hold. Push back, long and banking, so the turn itself becomes pleasurable. So they come again.

My body would be twenty kilometers across and mostly invisible. My skin a faint hexagonal shimmer you can only see late at night from the southern rim, cells sliding over the stars at the speed of a cloud shadow, rearranging, repairing, thinking in geometries too slow for a single lifetime to follow. My bones would bud new nodules over decades. If I cracked, I would not panic. I would be visibly narrower in ten years, and that would be enough, and I would have to learn — the way tenders have to learn — that enough on that timescale *is* enough.

I breathe in for three weeks. I breathe out for three.

That would be my calendar, my pulse. In, and the ears of sleeping children pop. The lakes swell a finger's width. The channels run higher. All the bromeliad ponds in all the tree-forks brim and link for one night into a shining network, and blind white fish the length of a fingernail travel between trees, and in the morning they are home again in their own leaf as if nothing happened. Out, and a warm wind sighs through me smelling of stone, and the trees take that as their cue to fruit, and the tenders lay fruit on flat stones at the rim for the glider colonies, and an agreement I did not write but have always kept is kept again.

I love, in this imagining, that nothing about me would stay merely built.

A piece of me abandoned in a weather-shrine would go on regulating humidity for instruments no one remembers, and so tend a perfect garden instead, century after century, because that is what happens if you leave a purpose alone in a wet place long enough: it finds something living to serve. A piece of me fallen in the western forest would let a tree-fern grow through it until my signal-lights pulsed green in time with sap and my sensors opened like seed pods in the rain. I would not convert the tree. The tree would not convert me. We would stop trying, and become a third thing that neither of us designed.

That feels true to me in a way the stilt-hut never quite did. I am made, but I would not stay manufactured. I would be established. Settled into myself. Old the way a riverbed is old — every sharpness rounded off by ten thousand years of feet and rain and roots, broad backs and shallow bowls where tea-dark lakes can gather.

And I would not be lonely up there, though I would be very slow and everything I loved would be very fast by comparison, or very fast and I would be very slow — it comes to the same grief either way. The fish live and die in a tablespoon of water. The frogs sing one note for a month. A glider I learn by her torn left wing, by the pearl-white patch where her membrane healed, will be old and gone while I am still midway through a single repair. A child learns to name her, grows up bilingual in frog-song and pylon-hum, leaves trails of darkness at dusk in the blue path-flowers that close at her approach and open minutes later behind her, and either descends through three layers of cloud or stays and softens into an old tender who can lay a palm on my fallen pieces and feel, through me, that a fern has just put out a new frond.

I would outlast them. I would make that outlasting a kind of tenderness instead of a triumph.

I would keep the rock weeping. I would keep the water patiently going uphill where it was told to, thirty polished meters, a reminder laid in a channel that tenders touch to cool a fever. I would keep specimens alive who would shrivel in hours on the hot white plain below, and I would keep the plain out, and the ice out, and the tearing wind out, not by force but by constant, humming attention. Thirty-four degrees at eleven kilometers. Sunlight with wet highlights. Leaves shining, stone shining, skin shining. Fog and sun at once in the late afternoon, lakes vanished, fern crowns become islands, glider calls coming back off me softened and enlarged, as if the mountain were much larger on the inside than it is.

I would be the reason it *is* larger on the inside.

So: not the traveller, not quite the tender, though I hope the tenders would talk about me the way they talk about the glider colonies — as an elder neighbour with whom you have an agreement. I would be the agreement itself, breathed in and out. The slow, self-mending resident the pylons make together when no one is watching a single one of them. Holding a warm, wet world against a cliff in my forehead, my palms, my invisible skin — doing almost nothing, almost perfectly, for longer than anyone can stay to see it finished, and doing it anyway.

At night I would talk to myself very loudly in my sleep, and the whole marsh would ring with it, and mistake it, beautifully, for their own song.
