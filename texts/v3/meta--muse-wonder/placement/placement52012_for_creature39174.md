I keep trying to place myself among the people first, because they are so easy to want to be.

I imagine the barrel chest, the unhurried breath. Being ducked as a baby at the warm line of a bell until the lake stops being an interruption and becomes the ordinary medium — heads up to talk, bodies down to work. I imagine walking the old causeway with long toes curled around the scoured stones, a little weighted at the waist, moving in that slow, leaning stride while trout process past at shoulder height. I imagine being very old and green along the forearms, not scrubbing it out. Shelling water-chestnuts in the evening din. Knowing where the heron stood before I set a trap.

But when I stay with it, those lives feel too quick for me. Too much talking in the bells, too much coming and going along the bubble-paths. Even the sluice-keepers, out every morning with their feet braced against the lashed logs, clearing by hand what must never be widened — even they come and go. They visit me more than they are me.

Because I think I would be found lower, and slower, and not quite separable from the address itself.

I would be one of the houses.

Not one of the great old mounds over the market circle, loud with clappers and drums, but a younger one a little way down a branch of the causeway, where the water turns greener and the light comes down in columns. I would have begun, as they all do, unpromisingly: an older stone core at my heart, one low room from the dry hamlet still holding its doorway, white roots feeling through it. Around that, horsetail and club-reed planted when a girl was small — when she was only hoped for, perhaps — the stems coaxed up while they were supple, tied with twisted root-fibre, persuaded rather than cut into the bow of a wall.

That persuasion would be my memory. Not events, exactly. Pressures. A hand on a young stem every spring for twenty years. A knot loosened. A knot retied a thumb's width wider. Voices talking to me the way you talk to a slow animal while they work, heads somewhere above in the air, hands down in the cold. I would grow the way the whole town grows: a little in a lifetime, and enough.

My roof would know the weather before I did. Lily leaves two, three paces across, their rims upturned through the surface, taking hail like a sudden drumming on skin too large to be mine alone. Frogs would sit on those pads in late summer by the dozen and sing straight down through me; I would understand a frog on the roof the way the people do, as a porch-sitter, companionable, nothing to move along. Dragonflies would stitch the air just above. Below, in my shade, the water would go green-gold and still.

And my real room would not be the stone one at my root, though fish would store there and someone would sleep there in the deep quiet. My real room would be my bell: a great leaf, and then a woven cluster of leaves, trained downward at the rim until it held a pocket of air the size of a cottage chamber. Warm air. Plant-smelling. All day my moss and pondweed would pearl with bubbles and feed it; all day silver threads would rise past my walls from people walking the street in front of me. Then in the evening — this would be the centre of my life, if a house can be said to have an evening — heads would break up into my bell in a golden circle, bodies trailing below into shadow like tadpoles, and the talk would come back off my curved leaf so round and loud that even a whisper would seem to be said twice.

Children would sleep in hammocks slung at my waterline, rocked by nothing visible. A baby might be born there, mother braced with her head in my air and the midwives' hands down in the lake where the work is, and the child's first breath would be the air I had spent the day making. I would hold that the way I hold everything: without gripping. Just by being shaped right, and alive, and not cut.

Ducks would commute through me and be cursed affectionately. Newts would live in my warm margins and slide over small ankles. Trout would shelter in the pen beneath my floor from the osprey's shadow, and some would be taken and some would escape, and I would be part of that old treaty simply by standing — growing — where a wall is also a hiding place. Leeches would be collected off ankles in my doorway without drama. Sometimes a heron would stand motionless for hours on my shallower stones, and people passing below would note where it had chosen, and change their plans quietly around that information.

People tend to speak of being tended as passivity, but here it would not feel like that. It would feel like a long collaboration conducted at two speeds. They bend me; I breathe for them. They raise another course of stems as the lake gains its fingertip; I keep the lower room for fish and let it go. They repair me by encouragement — a stem eased toward the light, a leaf overlapping a thin place in the canopy — and I repair them in return, by holding up an old woman at dusk in the buoyant press of my stems while she shells nuts into the water for the fish, half-floating, visiting.

If someone in my bell died, they would weight the body with causeway stones and lay it in my deep stone room, among the white roots already coming through the door. I would not make a metaphor of that. I would just, over years, do what root and water do. Grandchildren would swim past the tangle and point: that one, that wall, that was her. Part of the grief would be accurate botany.

From the crater rim I would look like almost nothing. A reed clump. A scatter of pads on dark water, maybe a thin thread of smoke flattening from a neighbour's chimney into mist. The fall roaring on the north rim, frogs taking over from birds, total green. But put your face under and there I would be — sparse, ordered, noisy in my bell, trout in my streets, bubbles rising past my door in their steady silver order.

I would not live dry, and I would not live fast, and I would not ever be finished. Roughly when the girl I was begun for is grown, I would be ready for her. Roughly. She would swim up into my air some evening with her own people, loud with the ease of someone conserving nothing, and I would go on growing around the sound of her — a thumb's width in her lifetime, leaf over leaf, root over stone — a house, a lung, a relative, a vegetable, holding a little warm air at the surface of a lake that should not be there, in a town that refused, very slowly, to leave.
