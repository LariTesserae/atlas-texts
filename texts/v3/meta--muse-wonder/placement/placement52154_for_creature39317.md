You would find me first where everyone is found first: coming up out of the cloud on the gondola, pale and too loud.

I would try to shout over the spooling drums when I arrived. Not from rudeness — from habit, from lowland air, thick air, air that can be spent. Someone would have to walk all the way to me, clip themselves to the rail beside me so the swinging car wouldn't take either of us, put a hand lightly on my forearm to keep me there, and say the correction into my ear on one exhale.

*Low. Brief. Close.*

And I would be embarrassed by how much breath it had cost me just to listen.

At first I would think I belonged to the more obvious places. To the rime-crews, surely — the ones who go out before dawn in masks, on tethers, to scrape the white fur off the windward rock while it is still there to be taken. I would romanticize them the way newcomers do: the insulated boxes heavy on their backs, frost in their eyebrows, that moment in the Lips when everyone stares because they have been *outside* and seen the sun come up thin and hard over a curved horizon and a sea of cloud four thousand meters down.

Or to the forge pit, perhaps. Down through the floor grate where the orange breath comes up. Casting anchor bolts and pitons and the teeth for ice-engines, learning the exact bodily flinch before the soft, furious *whump* of a quench in oil. Work you could point to. Work that holds the line.

But the forge hands would know my hands too quickly, and the rime-crews would know my lungs. Altitude would take me the way it takes everyone, in small debits rather than a drama — the headache settled patient behind the eyes, the thought arriving a second late, sleep snapping open in the tier because my body had remembered, suddenly, how thin the world was. I would learn to work in bursts. I would learn to rest without apology when the gates seal and the roof drums and the whole city draws inward.

And slowly — this is how it would actually happen — I would drift upward and sideways from the grand jobs, into the warm junctions.

You would find me on the catwalks.

Not the highest ones, not at first. On the narrow ways that run past the workshops cantilevered over the Yard, past the sleeping cages climbing the east wall three and four high. Felt soles. Waist clipped even indoors, especially indoors, especially when the membrane sucks in above us and the lamp strings begin their slow swing and everyone on a ladder leans, instinctively, a fraction of a second before they need to. My gloves would have the fingertips cut out. Not as a uniform. As a necessity that became an identity: tape-wrapped tools, splicing work, knots and fine bolts and frayed canvas to be felt properly. Oil worked permanently into the knuckles. Nails kept brutally short. Fingertips callused, a little grey from pumice, astonishingly sure and always a touch cold.

I would wash in hot dust like everyone else. My hair would be bound flat. I would smell of wool and ammonia vapor and lamp oil and other people, close-lived. My throat would learn its economy — wet the mouth first, then the sentence, and only if the sentence is worth the water it will cost to make it. I would sign for that water by weight. Never pour it. Sip it as if the flask were listening.

And speaking that way for a season changes what kind of creature you are. You stop broadcasting. You go to people. Foreheads nearly touching over a bench while a conveyor, padded in felt, carries food bricks and mail and machine parts behind you. A tap on a tether meaning *check yours*. A glance upward meaning more than a paragraph.

Because I would be watching upward. That is the work that would finally claim me.

Not quite electrician, not quite engineer. The keeper — or the keeper's second, learning it hand over hand — of the chalk nest-map.

I would know it the way others know wiring. Which truss the glossy black choughs favor for the warmth in the steel junctions, red feet shifting above the Yard. Which vent cowl the yellow-billed ones crowd, and which therefore we do not clear this month, whatever the schedule says. Which crane sits idle, patiently useless, because something has built in its sheave housing and will not be netted, will not be moved, until the fledglings are gone. Where the swifts — who were here in the bare cliff before there was a city to be in — stitch the dim air from dawn curfew to lamp-out, so close their wings tick the space where your face was. Where the indoor sparrows, fat and dust-brown and generations from any tree, wait under the mess benches. Where the mice go, solemn and unafraid, through the duct access, looking at you like older residents evaluating a tenant.

That last part would stop feeling like a metaphor. They *are* older residents. I would step around them.

My job would be to read the ventilation made visible. Where the birds will not fly, I do not send a person to breathe. Where the moths thicken too strangely around a lamp, where a pocket of air goes still and unvisited — fans, ducts, a panel breathing wrong. I would learn the roof by ear while I learned the roost by eye: this creak in a rib is weather, this one is a panel wanting re-tensioning, this sigh in the membrane means the gust outside has eased just enough that the roof-menders can crack the gates and let the star-dry cold knife in, just enough that the crews can spill out to repair cable and clear traps and stand, squinting and watering-eyed, in the blinding noon that up here is not blue at all but bruised indigo.

I would be, in the end, one of the ones who stays.

A rotation becomes a year because the descent starts to sound improbable. Too-thick air. Too much water, everywhere, unweighed, falling out of the sky and running in pipes as if it were free. Trees — I would still know the word, the way the sparrows presumably carry the shape of it somewhere in their blood without ever having used it. Theoretical green. My world would have narrowed honestly to two hundred meters long and thirty across: a roofed mouth in black basalt, lit amber, breathing overhead, crowded shoulder to shoulder at shift change and yet so quiet, so muffled, because nothing here can afford to be loud.

If you came up now, newcomer-loud on the gondola, you would find me at lamp-change.

I would be up on a ladder leaning with the swing of the lamp string, clipped at the waist, chalk on my cut fingertips. A chough shifting its weight on the warm truss a forearm's length above me, unconcerned. Sparrows bursting out from under a passing crate. A mouse withdrawing, unhurried, into shadow by my boot. The forge sighing orange up through the grate. The great drum turning somewhere in the dim, winding the bright line that holds us to everything underneath.

And I would not call down to you. The air won't spare it, and besides, that's not how we live here.

I would climb down. I would come close. I would put a hand on your forearm to keep you for the whole sentence, and I would say it low, on the exhale, into your ear —

*Check your tether. Then check mine.*
