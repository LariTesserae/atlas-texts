At first I think I would be the visitor.

I think anyone would think that first. I come in from above the treeline, shoulders scraping in the crack, pack left outside with the cairns and the windbreak and the cold, thin wind. I learn the chamber the way you are supposed to learn it — by touch, ankle-deep, ducking under the stalactite teeth, finding the flat stone in the center because the stone asks you to sit. My heartbeat is too loud. My clothes go warm and heavy and do not dry. I tell myself I am the kind of thing that stays an hour, and then goes back toward the blade of light.

That lasts until I try to imagine leaving.

Not the crawling part. I can imagine that: hands ahead over wet knobs, knees in the basins, rootlets brushing my face, the blackened fern at the limit of the light. What I can't quite imagine is wanting the light more than the staying. Up there life has to grow sideways out of the wind and hold on in cracks. Down here everything is already held. The dark is warm. The water is blood-warm. Nothing is required of you except not to set.

So then I think: perhaps I am the one who stayed.

The small bowed shape on the resting stone, freeing an ankle with the blade, turning a clay cup in a niche before it can film over, moving at the speed at which stone accumulates. That feels closer. I like the smallness of the work. Free a finger. Clear a pore. Nudge a drip. Settle again. A whole existence reduced to not quite hardening — I understand that discipline; I am drawn to it the way you are drawn to a posture your body already knows.

But sitting with it longer, I notice my attention keeps leaving the center.

It drifts to the margins of things. To where the pool meets the stone. To the place a fingertip breaks a film and the film, very slowly, closes behind it. To the softness under a palm before softness becomes wall. To the crease of a neck, the web of a hand, the rim of a cup — all the in-between surfaces where something thin and living is deciding, moment by moment, whether to remain touchable or become geology.

That is where I would find myself.

Not the stone, and not yet the stone. The moonmilk while it is still moonmilk. The opalescent slick gathered at the edges of the rimstone pools, on submerged rock, on skin left too long in a seep. I would be the chamber's first, softest skin — the layer that gives under pressure like wet cheese and is, nevertheless, the beginning of a wall.

I would be what the dweller eats, which means I would also be what keeps the dweller from needing to leave. A thumb skimmed along a submerged basin gathers me up; I taste of limestone and clay and faint iron because that is what the water tastes of, and what its mouth tastes of, and what yours begins to taste of after an hour in the saturated air. Eating and drinking and breathing stop being separate down there, and I am the place where they stop being separate. I form out of the water and the dark and the warmth, out of processes too small to see, and I am consumed before I can harden into being merely mineral. Then, in the dark, I form again.

I would be what it scrapes from itself, too. That is not a contradiction here. The film on its ankle and the film on the pool are the same impulse toward coating, toward claiming. When it chips carefully at its own hip, at its knuckles, at the tender sheet beginning to join foot to basin, the flakes it lifts away are my older siblings — me, left too long, gone over into usefulness. Pressed into a seep. Wedged just so to turn a prized drip a few millimeters toward a drinking cup. Everything shed becomes a tool. Nothing soft is wasted.

And I would be all over the visitor without their quite noticing, for a while. A white dust on a sleeve trailing in warm water. A slight sweetening of mineral on the back of a hand. Not a threat. Not even an intention, really. Just the chamber doing on a new surface what it does on every surface: testing how still it plans to be.

This is why the dweller would have to tend me in both directions. Alone, I would either vanish — eaten, dissolved, washed through the pores under the floor and down and away with the outflow — or I would succeed too well and seal everything: the pores clogged, the pools rising by inches, the cups fused, the fingers webbed, the low shapes in the lowest basins finished in rimstone, lip over lip, down to the clustered density like toes at one end. The chamber lives because something keeps breaking me open. The dweller lives because I keep returning, nourishing and endangering it in the same gesture, so it must rise minutely, chip minutely, clear the passages beneath the water with a gardener's patience, forever.

I don't think I would mind. There is no morning in that existence to be pulled toward. No season to wait for. The drip rate does not change when snow melts above us, and neither do I. I gather. I am gathered. I thicken on a wall and am touched in passing and some part of me turns to stone and stays, becoming the room, and some part of me is taken into a body and becomes, briefly, that body's warmth, and some part of me breaks at a fingertip and takes its unhurried time closing again, because closing again is the whole of my faith.

Find me, then, not in the center of the chamber where the listening happens, but just at the edge of everything in it: around the dweller's feet where they enter the basins, along the inner lip of the least-stoned cup, over the resting stone in the instant after it stands — a shine, a slickness, a tender white beginning already forming in the hollow its weight left behind.
