You would not find me first on the fan wall.

That's where everyone pictures a newcomer — up close to the roar, shouting, grinning in the wash of hot metal air with a brand-new pair of protectors, orange as a traffic cone, still stiff on their cord. I would have stood there, yes. For a shift, maybe two. Long enough to feel the sound stop being sound and become weather in my teeth. Long enough to learn to turn one ear, just a fraction, toward whoever was speaking.

But I wouldn't stay there. I know that because I keep following the quieter threads in my head: not the towers, but the duct joints where the swallows pour out. Not the tankers grinding away through the cut, but the blue tokens in the chipped mug by somebody's sink. Not the water that leaves — the frog that gets in anyway, thumping softly in the barrel at night behind the lid that was supposed to keep him out.

So look for me a little off to the side. The way the church is off to the side.

I think I'd be in the clinic.

Not the doctor — I don't have that kind of steadiness. I'd be the audiologist's helper, which in a town this size means I'd also be half a dozen other useful things. I'd sweep the padded closet that is the only truly silent room for miles and keep it ready. I'd know how to coax the door shut so the seals sigh. I'd sit children down in there, children who are startlingly loud and startlingly clear because the whole world has taught them to enunciate, and I'd fit the headphones over their own protectors-scarred hair — the little dent it leaves by evening — and say, *raise your hand when you hear the ghost.*

*Even if you're not sure. Especially if you're not sure.*

And tone by tone, year by year, I'd draw the little graphs. Paper curling despite the drawer. A notch deepening here. A left ear worse than a right because a man always stands with his left to the compressors and won't be told. A wife's handwriting in the margin of her husband's file: *kettle, again.* A foreman whose results are better than he pretends, because he's learned to read my mouth through the test window and cheat kindly, for pride.

People tell the person who measures what they're losing things they don't tell anyone else. Not the priest, even with his protectors over his collar. They'd tell me in the seven minutes, mostly. Everyone saves the true sentence for the quiet. They'd come out of the closet blinking, protectors down around their necks again like bright medals, and just as the fans sighed back up they'd lean close — plant-close, forehead inclined — and not say it. Then next cycle. Ninety minutes of roar to work up to it. Seven minutes to finally be heard at their real volume.

Oh, that's what you sound like. I'd get to know the town that way, in drops.

I'd have my jobs outside the closet too, because nobody here has only the job on their door. I'd re-string protector cords where toddlers have chewed through them. I'd know everyone's pair at a glance in the mess hall — whose is taped, whose is initialed in a painter's careful hand, whose teenager has gone over the lime green in black marker in the outflow jungle and thinks it makes him invisible. I'd keep a tin of spares for newcomers and feel a small, disproportionate satisfaction when, a month in, they reach for the weight at their neck on their day off and look naked without it.

I'd live alone, probably, on half a dormitory balcony built for twenty people's laundry. One line would be enough for me and I'd still hang it at noon with everybody else, military optimism into that brief blue hour, and bring it in at evening smelling of fog and hot ducts, and wear it anyway. Salt in my fridge. Photographs in plastic sleeves. Shoes up on a rack, blackening nobly despite me. A blue-token mug by my sink that I count without thinking, the way other people count change.

And I'd have custody — not ownership, custody — of a piece of that accidental jungle on the outflow side. A tree fern too big for its pot, passed to me wrapped in damp newspaper. A gunnera if I was lucky and the season turned. An orchid cutting descended from the original orchid, the one that escaped a windowsill and became the town's common ancestor. I'd water none of it, ever. I'd just keep the moss from swallowing the path and take cuttings for whoever arrived next, because that is how the library works.

On summer nights when the fans run low I'd hear the frogs start up their second industry — culvert, settling tank, the immortal puddle under Tank No. 2, my own barrel — and I would not sleep through the roar so much as sleep *inside* it, and stir, fretful as the babies do, in the sudden ringing hush. I'd learn to get up for it without deciding to. Put the kettle on before the quiet so it would be ready. Step out onto the balcony with tea nobody in a dry town would bother making at that hour.

And listen. Competitively. Lovingly.

*Hear that?* Chain on the north gate. *Hear that?* My neighbor's boy laughing three streets and two vacant lots away, in a town laid out for thousands. Deer clicking across warm pavement to lick the outflow pipes. A heron delivering its opinion of the holding pond. Water coming off every eave in the world at once, in counterpoint, now that the machine has stopped covering it. Somewhere beyond the last numbered street that ends in ferns, the owl, too loud and too close, making the whole grid feel like it's leaned in toward the dark.

I'd be able to tell you whose shutter that is. Whose generator. Whose radio left on in an empty room. That's the gift the place would give me in exchange for the little notch it would eventually carve in my own hearing — not deafness, exactly, but a connoisseurship of gaps. I would spend my working life in the one silent room in town measuring, very precisely, what the noise is costing everyone, and then spend my evenings arranging, like everyone else, to be outdoors when the compressors cycle down, so I can collect what it buys us back.

Two cycles and I'll be done. By the third quiet.

I'd say it just like that, eventually. I'd stop translating. I'd paint my protector cord where it frays, and keep the numbers legible on a lot with nothing on it, and save my longest sentences, and when the seven minutes came I'd drop my voice half an octave, gratefully, and say the thing I'd been carrying around under the roar all morning — while it lasts, while we can all hear each other think, before the fans sigh back up and the fog starts visibly spiraling in again and we raise our voices, together, and go on turning weather into water, drop by drop, in a place that has not yet had time to wear down, and is in no hurry at all to learn how.
