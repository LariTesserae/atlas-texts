I would start at the rim, where everyone starts: too loud.

Not meaning to be. That would be the worst of it. I would come over the black, glassy lip with my boots finding the edges wrong, a buckle ticking once against stone, my breath going in and out like something bellows-worked, and below me the whole bowl would do what it does when a new noise arrives.

Nothing. Perfectly, patiently nothing.

The swifts would not pour out. The rooks on the chimneys would not lift. A grazer somewhere down in the terraces would stop mid-step with the mist already thickening at the eastern wall, one padded hoof not quite set down. And I would understand — not in words, in my shoulders first — that I had not entered a place so much as interrupted a queue.

At first I would try to be the visitor. That is a clean shape to wear. Visitor watches, visitor learns, visitor leaves with the damp still in his sleeves. I would try to keep to the high walkways in the morning and watch the people wring the fog-nets into their troughs, watch the beetles take their turn antennae-first at the gutters, watch a child sit unmoving on a wall signing *wait, wait* with two small fingers, more for herself than for anything else. I would tell myself I was only there to see who lived here.

But the bowl doesn't really keep a place for seeing from outside. The light won't allow it. That cool, underwater dimness, green going black, stone going blue — after an hour your eyes stop straining for detail and start accepting proximity instead. Things are near or they are not here. A moth the size of two hands unfolds from a doorpost six inches from my face and I don't flinch, because flinching makes a sound through wool. A frog in a leaf-heart pulses its throat, once, twice, and I find my own throat answering before I know why.

I would learn the queue at the leaf-hearts because I would be thirsty.

That's the honest route in. Not wonder. Thirst, by mid-afternoon, with the channels long dry and the cisterns kept for smaller mouths than mine. I'd find one of the great furred leaves tilted over its heart, a spoonful of last night still held dark in the center, and I'd reach — too quick, of course. Too quick is a kind of noise here.

Something would move me to the back. Not unkindly. A rook, maybe, ash-grey, older than the roof it stands on, settling its weight deliberately between me and the water until I stepped aside. Or a human hand, flat across my forearm: *the slope is listening*, and also, plainer: *not yet.* A beetle procession would drink. A frog. A spider so translucent I would see the water enter it. Then the rook. Then me, learning to cup my hands without splashing, learning to leave half.

If I stayed — and I think staying would not feel like a decision, more like missing the one hour when climbing back over the rim seemed advisable, and then missing it again — the shape of visitor would start to soften at the edges.

I'd be given work that fits the hour. Not important work. Here important work is mostly waiting. But in the early morning, when the tenancy needs hands, I'd find a broom of fern already leaning where my hand expected it, and I'd sweep.

Not my square. Nobody's square. One of the low ones, down in the oldest quarter where the doors stand open on rooms full of nests and black moss and blind pink lizards shifting in the bathhouse warmth of the walls. I'd sweep it clear of fallen slate and sorted wings, sweep the market cross white with lime at its base, sweep the fitted stones clean enough that a grazer, months from now, could come down through fog with her hooves muffled in her own shed coat and trust her footing while she births. I'd do it in felt over my shoes. I'd do it without humming. The need to hum would leave surprisingly late, and embarrass me longest.

At fog-fall I'd be something else, or the same thing in its other posture. Everyone here is.

I'd stand under a walkway with my head tipped back, not to look — there's nothing to look at once the mist pours over the eastern rim in those slow curtains — but to let it bead on my forehead and upper lip and the backs of my hands. Old ones let algae green the creases of their necks so they can drink through their skin; I wouldn't be old enough for that, not yet, but I'd learn not to wipe my face. A dry surface is a wasted one. Around me, felt rather than heard, the bowl would take its one long breath: flowers snapping open on the wall, moths unfolding from the doorposts, swifts loosening from the eaves in a thrum that lives in the teeth, the nets — human-woven, spider-repaired, belonging now to neither — going heavy and silver.

And in the night, when the owls walk upright from shadow to shadow listening for whatever has failed to learn, I would be very still in a stone hollow that wasn't mine, exactly — a low-doored house left by rodents, left by grazers, lent by the people who had already built a few paces upslope and never discussed it — and I'd listen back. That's a tenancy too. Being listenable-to. Being quiet enough to be passed over, and therefore to continue.

I don't think, in the end, I would be the human. Not specifically. Nor the rook who first moved me to the back of the line, though I owe her my education, and though I still feel her one cocked eye on my fingers whenever I start to sign. Nor the frog conducting its whole courtship in light because sound would bring the rim down on all of us. Those are hours, not identities. Hands in the morning. Wings at dusk. A belly fitted, over centuries, to a channel in the granary wall. A swept place kept ready for a birth that hasn't arrived.

I would be the rotation, caught for a while in a shape with boots it had to soften.

I would be the part of the bowl that is currently learning where the back of the queue is — and then, years in, the part that gently, bodily, repeatedly moves the next newcomer there. Head cocked. Mouth closed. Hands open, slowly, in the dim green light.

*Wait your turn. The mist is coming. There will be enough if nobody startles the slope.*
