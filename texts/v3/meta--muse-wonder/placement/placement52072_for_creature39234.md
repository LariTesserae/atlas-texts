At first I think I'd arrive the way everything else does.

On the morning truck, with the food. Stepping down into heat that doesn't hit so much as close around me — thirty-eight, thirty-nine, already steamed through the shirt before I've crossed the yard — and being shown a dormitory, a bed, a hook for ear defenders I don't yet know I'll need. I'd lie there the first night, by the clock, under damp blackout curtains, and feel the pumps come up through the floor and the frame of the bed and into my teeth, and think: I will never sleep in this.

Then I'd learn what the old hands know. You don't sleep despite it. You sleep *in* it. It's the stopping that wakes you.

I try to place myself in the work, the way you do at first. Maybe in the pump hall, in that amber, steam-cut dimness, learning the hand signals because shouting close to an ear gives up so quickly — flat palm, pointed finger, *shut it down, turn it over* — the quieter language following me out to the mess hall afterward. Maybe on a brush crew, shaving the canal edges back to the template, boots full of black silt that smells of iron and rot. Maybe in the office, repainting the white waterline on the main canal wall when it dulls, even though the black water has never once moved off it. Yesterday it sat there. Tomorrow it will sit there. The job is to keep painting the line anyway.

But my ears adjust, or stop trying to adjust, the way yours do crossing that blinding gravel yard at noon when the town looks utterly empty — and I start hearing how crowded it is. Not with people in the open. With everything that has arranged itself under the roofs.

I don't think I'd stay entirely human in a place like that. Not out of romance. Out of fit.

I wouldn't be the heron at the outfall. I envy the heron — motionless for a whole shift where the discharge stuns the fish into circles, sleeping at night on a bollard in the full streetlight, one leg tucked, so habituated to the reversing alarms that it won't even lift its head — but I'm not that patient, and I'm not that solitary. I wouldn't be a swallow either, though I came close: raised in the rafters of the pump hall above the flywheels, in nests the engineers chalk-mark and work around in nesting season, pouring out through the louvered vents with the bats at shift change. To grow up taking that vibration for weather. To return to it. That tempted me.

But I kept coming down. Lower. Out of the rafters, past the geckos waiting motionless by the light fittings for the moths — an hour of stillness, then uncatchably fast — down to ground level, where the town goes on at ankle height without ever being on the roster.

You'd find me under the water tanks.

On the cool concrete there, in the deepest shade the town makes, in a row with the others through the hottest hours. Sleek from the mess hall scraps the cook saves — because someone always feeds the cats — tail curled over my nose, silt washed off my paws by lying still long enough. That's the address, if I have one. Not a dormitory. The dry space behind the fuel depot bund is where the litters are raised. The cable ducts are where I work. The underside of the water tanks is where I sleep.

I'm a cat. One of the feral, entirely unembarrassed citizens, who arrived a night after the rats did — as if issued, as if the drainage system required us and so we turned up. Nobody brought me officially. Nobody can quite remove me either. I keep the ducts passable. I know where the rats run them and, just as the electricians do, where not to reach blind. I know which walkway will blind with steam when a vent cycles, and not to lean against the cold pipes that run with condensation and soak you through in a second. I know the foreman by the soles-of-his-boots knowledge he has: he can tell which of the three green pumps is hunting off-note. I can tell it too. I grew up hearing it through concrete. When one is taken offline for servicing and that sudden, exposed ringing opens up where the throb should be, I'm awake before the sleeping men are, ears aching in the comparative quiet, waiting without knowing I'm waiting for the note to come back and settle us all.

They've named me, of course. Repeatedly. I'm Bolt this time — for the orange blush of first rust at the bolt heads, maybe, or just because the last three were Bolt too and the name was going spare when I turned up as a kitten. Old toms disappear. New kittens get the names again. In a town where nothing has had time to erode, where a bank slumping on Monday is regraded by Friday and a canal still shows the tooth-marks of the excavator that cut it, I'm one of the few ways the place keeps a continuity it isn't trying to keep.

I observe the treaties. I stay away from the roosters behind the dormitories — the town's open secret, calling at dawn and then, confused by the cooling-tower cloud and the streetlights that make night only an orange version of day, calling intermittently all the hours that aren't shift changes. Everyone knows whose birds they are. On the record, no one knows anything. The cooks save them scraps too. A man signs on to keep a polder from drowning and also keeps a chicken in a crate to hear something announce an hour that isn't a roster. I understand that. I keep off their crate. In exchange, at evening, when the mosquitoes rise in a visible smoke off the levee grass and the bats come flickering down from under every eave and the foremen close the mess hall doors, I am let alone to go.

Shift change is my hour. The doors shut and open, boots come off the racks still leaving damp silt prints an hour after the cleaners have mopped them, men cross in single file through the covered ways raising their voices without noticing, signaling to each other across the mess hall tables before they remember to speak. The frogs come up from every drain and slow ditch — warm, permanent, bath-warm water filmed emerald and gold where the cutters can't reach — a grinding, pulsing wall of sound after rain that competes squarely with the diesels, and underneath it even at midday, so that when I hunt I hunt through frog-song and turbine whine and cicadas in the staked, dusty trees, all of it ordinary, all of it home. Dragonflies have gone in by then. The egrets are whitening the one dead tree by the treatment ponds, the tree that was left standing because they nest there. The swallows pour out overhead. I go the other way, low, along the ducts, where it's dry and dark and smells of hot cable and rat.

Late — by the clock, since the gloom outside the mess hall windows is much the same as it ever is — I'd walk the ring road the way anyone can. Pumps on. Lights on. Steam rising into the cloud the towers make of themselves, condensing, falling somewhere out on the marsh as warm rain. The levee a clean, fresh, steep line of pale clay and unlichened stone between the kept place and the unkept. Black water sitting exactly on its painted line. A heron asleep on a bollard. Nothing winning. Nothing being taken back.

I'd come back along it with something in my mouth, probably, past hydrants and numbered streets and the bus making its twenty-minute round whether it's carrying anyone or not, and settle again where the concrete is coolest, and wash the silt off, and sleep in the noise.

Not kept, but fed. Not wild, but uncounted. Part of the drainage, in my way — one more life that has learned the pump-note and the steam cycle and the exact level of the water, and arranged itself, at full volume, inside a moment that is not allowed to finish.
