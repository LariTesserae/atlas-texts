I would not be at the rim.

That is the first thing that settles, slowly, the way everything settles there. Not standing where the rootwood weeps and looking down at the hard country. I have no curiosity that pulls outward. The bright places hurt behind my eyes. The dry air would feel like being unwrapped.

I would not be in the center of a light-gap either, not in the brief, blazing decades when sun-plants rise and butterflies come blundering up out of nowhere. I would skirt it. I would visit its edge in the late afternoons, when the light has already been taken apart by leaves, and let it warm one shoulder at a time.

Somewhere between those two refusals is where I find myself: far inland, under the second and third ceilings, where the cloud forms inside the forest by late morning and trunks vanish at twenty paces. A day's walk in any direction would tell you almost nothing — more tannin here, less iron there, a reed that means cleaner seepage, a moss that prickles faintly under the sole. That would be enough. That would be a whole geography, if your feet were doing the reading.

At first I tried on other shapes. A blind fish in one of the black pools, nosing through peat, patient in water opaque as steeped leaves — there is a kind of peace in that I recognize. A bird, low and short-winged, speaking in clicks and a flicker of dull blue rather than song. A grazer pushing a steam-breathing path through the ferns, chewing, never hurrying, the whole family following half a day behind the herd of me.

But none of them stay with what I actually do. I stay. I listen through surfaces. I remember sequences that other people only passed through once.

So I am one of the people, after all. Just not one who ranges far anymore.

I am short the way we all are, lowered toward the useful ground, broad through the hips, thick in the calf, belly round on soft food. My head comes to a lowlander's chest, if a lowlander ever stood still long enough in the wet for me to be measured against him. My feet go first into any place, toes long and splayed wide, webbed to the first joint, soles feeling for root before I give them my weight. I have walked barefoot so long that the ground is not a thing under me. It is a slow water that takes my shape, holds it a while, and then forgives it.

There is moss established along the backs of my forearms now, and a green-gray film deepening in the creases at my neck, where my hair mats and holds the drip. I am perhaps ninety. Perhaps more. Counting stopped being interesting when I noticed that the young ones guessed me at half that, and the old ones — the few older than me — only smiled with their mouths close to my hair and said I was getting denser. That is the change that matters here. Not age. Density.

For a long time I moved with a family. Five of us, then seven, then five again when two drifted off for a decade with cousins by a fresher chain of pools. I carried children against my hip until they were nearly too heavy, because a crawling child vanishes into leaf litter and because, held close, they learn the world through your breathing before they learn it through words — how your breath shallows when the peat is about to give, how you slow at iron in the water, how you lean, almost without knowing, toward a vine flowering to a light-gap older than your grandmother.

I still know the long itineraries by taste. If you whispered against my ear — under the hair, where speech is felt as warmth as much as heard — and asked the way to the quartz hump with the carved basin, I would not point. There is no pointing. I would tell you: go toward damper until the drips on the broad leaves go *tok ... tok-tok*, then where the underfoot warms a little and the black water loses its iron on your tongue, then follow the non-prickling moss downslope you would swear was not a slope, until the frog-children in the basin are loud enough to be felt in your jaw.

That is what I have become good for. Families on the move keep contact by footfall, two thuds through a hollow root-chamber, pause, one, answered without breaking stride. I hear those conversations better through my feet and teeth than through my ears, which I keep waxed small against water and burrowing things. Lying at night in a ghost house — in the hollow where wooden walls rotted out a hundred years before I was born and moss kept the shape of them, doorway and all — I can feel a herd shifting a marsh over, rain beginning three ceilings up, a buried layer preparing to sigh an acre down by morning. I wake before the pools rearrange themselves. I am already listening to where they will go.

So, increasingly, I do not go. I have taken up residence — we would not use so stiff a word, but it is near enough — under a root arch by a favorite pool, in the part of the ghost house that my own shoulder, leaned there season after season, has kept from slumping. Hands pass and push the fern roof aside where the drip wants in. The house gets more plant and more mine at once. Younger kin come pool to pool in the indoor cloud, holding wrist to wrist so as not to part, and bring me fallen fruit already softened to sweet pulp in the warm hollow chambers, shrimp scooped translucent from the shallows, larvae still in their chewed-leaf cases tasting of the leaf they ate. They leave news the same way fruit is left. Close. One warm whisper at a time.

A tree took eighty years to fall beyond the marsh and fed three families while falling. A storm left the center when I was small enough to be carried, and my daughter heard, as a grown woman, that it had finally reached the rim and cried off the edge of the world. A pool near here was a house; before that the hollow was warmer for a while, orchids came, and a woman whose name my grandmother carried against her chest went into the peat, light as leaf-mold. I keep those. Not as stories about beginnings. There are no beginnings. As itineraries of slow transformation.

In the long dripping nights that are hardly darker than the days, when phosphorescent fungi stipple the undersides of the roots just enough to show where the ground drops away, someone will settle against my side for grooming — the slow picking through hair, the re-braiding, beetles traveling undisturbed like small pale weather — and that touch will also be speech. And I will give back a route, a transformation, a warning felt first in the knees: that acre to the fresher side is about to go down a hand's breadth. Sleep with your weight toward the roots tonight.

I am not a landmark yet. That would be too final, and nothing here is final. But I am becoming the kind of place other people cross by. A particular softness in the ground that grandchildren will feel through their soles, long after the sigh and the stitching roots, and associate — without superstition, without fear — with being accompanied.

Under the third ceiling, in warm, directionless, green-gray light, with moths blundering gently against my face at twilight and the bog popping one slow bubble a minute from decay far below, I put my foot down deliberately on the hollow chamber beside my sleeping place.

Two thuds. Pause. One.

*I am still here. The way is still damper this way.*

And somewhere in the mist, not loud, not far, another foot answers through the peat — and the plateau, having taken both our shapes for a moment, thinks them over, keeps them a little while, and lets them subside.
