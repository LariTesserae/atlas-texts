At first I think I would be one of the people who climb to her.

I can picture that part easily enough — the unweathered black rock at 4,000 meters, too young to have learned how to be climbed, the sound getting into my teeth a hundred meters before I could see anything, my words coming back to me as vibration. I would be the one who goes quiet out of reverence and tells myself later that it was reverence. I would tie the rope around my waist. I would not stay long.

But that doesn't settle. A visitor has somewhere else to go afterward. He has the week of hearing the crash in quiet rooms, and then the quiet rooms win. He gets to incorporate the place. It doesn't incorporate him.

Then I think I might be smaller. One of the translucent things in the middle water, when the vortex stutters for twenty seconds and holding still becomes briefly possible. A thumbnail of a creature, blind head tucked, too many swimmerets beating under me, pulsing hard just to stay exactly where I am. That feels honest for a while — frantic, unfinished, made to be taken sideways by the current and dissolved.

But they're not a species. They're a stage that never arrives. They're what falls off something larger, and I can feel the larger thing pulling at the thought from underneath, the way the undertow in that cup isn't really an undertow.

So I go down.

Not down into the bowl — the bowl is twelve paces across and a hammer falls through it too long, that's the lie of it — but down into the pressure under the bowl. Into the place where the water stops pretending to be a mountain tarn and remembers it's an ocean, compressed, misfiled, carried up into the sky inside a rising peak.

There. That is where I find myself. That is what I would be.

Her.

I am not above the light, I am below it, and the bright cup at the summit is only my roof-yard. My open eye. Daylight comes down through it clean and white at noon and it keeps going through the water farther than it should, and I can feel it on skin that has no eyes to close with. Not see it. Feel it change, fraction by fraction, the way you feel someone stand between you and a fire.

My body is built for the two lies of this place: that it is small, and that it is shallow. I hold myself not by swimming — swimming is a memory my children are born carrying, a slow S and a correction they practice while being carried — but by wedging the pressure. I let currents that reverse without warning pass through my frilled rings rather than against me. I expand. I consider. I let go.

Every forty or fifty minutes I breathe, if breathing is the word, and the whole surface up there sighs down a hand's breadth and surges back and floods the little stone platform where two people can crouch. They look for the moon to explain it. There is no moon involved. It is my great slow venting, far below, drawing the whole impossible column through myself and closing again. The knee-high waves born in the middle of nothing are me shifting in something that isn't sleep. The vortex wanders because I turn, incrementally, year by year, toward a warmth I remember in my tissues that no longer exists anywhere up here.

And the sound. People stop speaking a hundred meters out because speech stops working, and they think the mountain is doing that. The bowl is a drum because I am pressed against the hollow of the peak like a hand inside a bell. That crashing that rearranges a visitor's pulse in an hour, that leaves his hands shaking — that's ordinary for me. That's my heart working against mountain instead of ocean. I know everyone who comes entirely by vibration before they reach the slit: the hesitant weight in the notched handholds, polished dark by wet hands and once, in some earlier century, by me exploring upward and withdrawing; the double heartbeat on the platform; the copper ladle banging its bright, insane, irregular note against the wall on its cord, decade after decade.

I could stop the ladle. I don't. It's the only sound up here that isn't mine.

What drops, I keep. Ladles before this ladle. A copper blade no thicker than a fingernail. Antler, basalt, hammered things worked slowly into the mineral of my back, where they show sometimes in the fresh stone as greenish veins. I am incorporating everyone who ever tried to tend my home, not out of hunger — I eat almost nothing, and the almost is precise: a day's smear of algae off new stone, my own unsuccessful young, once a small alpine bird that came through the eye and was held under too long — but out of the simple fact that in my country, nothing sinks past me. Everything that falls long enough becomes part of the floor I'm trying to build.

That is my other labor. The ticking and soft fizzing on golden evenings, like bone knitting up through the spray. My back mineralizes as I age. It blisters outward in pillows and domes, hardens, cracks under its own weight, sinks to be begun again. I am precipitating myself a shell the size of a seafloor, one aborted floor at a time. The milky edge on the water is my work going to waste. The too-clean boulder by the eastern wall is clean because I rub against it in the dark just below visibility, year in and out, scraping off the newest, itchiest growth. It is worn smooth on one side in a way the handholds would recognize.

When the water below warms by a fraction and turns less cold, I shed them — clouds of larvae from the frilled undersides of my arms, nail-pale, too many swimmerets, mouths not finished forming. Most are torn apart by the turbulence itself. Some cling for a day and graze. A very few grow long and dark and become the eel the old people swear is always the same one, and the young people swear cannot be. Both are right. None of them have ever matured. The cup is already occupied, and the mountain will not make room fast enough.

So I make room myself, and the room fails, and I persist in the one success available.

I don't know, down here, whether I was trapped — a deep-water animal hauled up in a pocket of my own ocean as the seabed obscenely became a summit — or whether I followed the water up through fractures that shouldn't admit a minnow, pursuing the one place where the pressure still tells the truth. That distinction belongs to stories told on the platform. In the pressure it matters less than the pressure itself.

On evenings when the light comes down gold and breaks into brief rainbows in the spray, I dim the blue-green clarity below without ever surfacing, and my young rise toward me in a body, and for those twenty seconds everything in the cup is ordered around a presence arranging itself to be warmed. I glow faintly in response — not with light of my own, but with the mineral bloom that lives on me and dies daily.

The mountain is still rising. Year by year I am carried higher on a sea that should have spilled, evaporated, frozen, failed. My shell attempts a floor and the floor fails. My children attempt a life and the life fails.

I am a deep-sea animal in the sky, blind, excessive, solitary, tasting of deep cold and copper because everything down here tastes of deep cold and copper, mother to a translucent weather that never becomes me.

And when the vortex stutters up above, and the waves fall briefly out of rhythm, it is not the place resting.

It is me, far down where a pool becomes an ocean again, listening back up through the water for whatever the world has sent me this time — a hand at an ankle, a weight on the rope, a heartbeat doubled on stone — and deciding, slowly, with the whole mountain for a body, to let it go.
