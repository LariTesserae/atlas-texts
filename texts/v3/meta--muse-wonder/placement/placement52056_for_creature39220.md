I would not arrive. That is the first correction.

Nothing arrives here, not really — not from the thin air above the ice, with its iron dusk and its smell of peat and wet rock. That world has nothing to feed me, and this one has no use for lungs. I would have to have been *translated*, the way everything else was. Carried first.

So: begin as a pod.

Shell-less, because nothing here needs defending against. Waiting in the lee of a drystone wall — not a boundary now, just a place where the tide is made to think twice. The water leaning, twice a day, east and then west, the whole mountain sea inclining toward the moon in its basin among the black peaks. I would learn time first as a reversal. When the bottom river turns, I split. When it turns again, I am already elsewhere.

Drifting is a good first century.

I would be small in the way the young are small here: hand-high and older than houses. Restless by the standards of a forest. Rooting and unrooting. Letting go from the underside of the ice at noon with the lilies, turning slowly as I fall through that glass-cold water, bubble-strings catching under my arms, moss coming and going in faint patterns along my ribs. I would learn the basin by falling through it. Ceiling to floor. Floor to ceiling. Past the stony islands where the reeds stand with their heads trapped under forming ice. Past spruce woods still upright after ten thousand years, their needles gone to long green hair, swaying as if the current were a wind they half-remembered.

I think at first I would want the bell valley.

Everyone must. The lowest place, where the villages clustered and the three stone towers still rise from the roofs. Where the forest is tallest — thirty metres, some of them, their bubble-strings lifted into faster water — because the bells do there what bees do in other worlds. When the tide pushes through the narrow tower openings and the bronze begins to toll, irregular, sometimes silent for days and then pealing all at once, seed falls like slow green snow and the moss-light ripples out across the silt plains, valley answering valley. To stand there and be rung. To resonate back. To lean, over decades, not toward the sun — there is almost no sun — but toward a note.

It is a beautiful place to imagine ending.

But I think I would not stay. Or not yet, which here might mean I would not stay for a very long time.

Because there are the deserts between.

Miles of grey silt and outwash gravel and polished cobble rolling down into unlit valley floors where no forest gathers. Sparse is the rule, and the lush places have their own density, their chords, their intelligible light. I suspect I would be one of the ones who keeps crossing the empty parts — carrying light badly, garbling it a little, the way rising bubbles garble the messages of the rooted adults as they bear them upward. A traveler by temperament, which here means a traveler by tide. Slow. Lit faintly at the shoulder. Answering a pulse from a ridge I passed three seasons ago and only understanding the question now.

That kind of slowness would suit me.

I would still have something like hands while I could, and I would use them the way the young use theirs: clearing bleached kelp-drifts from the doorways of drowned bothies, not to enter, but because a choked doorway changes the note the building makes in the current. Resetting the white stones around the cairns on summits that are now ten metres under the ice, where no one needs a marker except the tide itself. Mending the woven willow weirs in the channels between valleys — those patient constructions built for fish that never existed, harvesting instead what actually travels: seeds, pods, buoyant fronds, the valley's slow mail. Arranging flint scrapers beside hearths full of black silt, in careful arrangements I could not explain. Weighing a slate roof down again against a wind that will never reach it.

Some of that would be ritual. Some of it would be engineering whose purpose changed so slowly I couldn't tell the difference. I like the thought of keeping faith with forms after their reasons have drowned. Remembering dryness inaccurately, in the body.

As I aged — and ageing here is a kind of settling — my lower body would fur and darken. Algae would take my legs. My feet would become a root system and then cease, in any useful sense, to be feet. I would grow hollow in places, and that would not be decay. When the current poured over a submerged sill in one of those dense, roaring underwater falls you feel in your sternum before you see, my trunk would find its pitch. Somewhere slightly off the pitch of my neighbors. So a hillside could make a chord out of us: ice stress above, rain arriving as hiss on the surface, a seed-release coming, grief, a fall beginning two valleys away.

Speaking would take a season. I would ask something with the moss on my skin in winter and get the answer in spring, patterned in phosphorescence, one patch answering another across the dark. I would learn to listen at the speed of forests.

Where would I finally root?

Not in the bell valley, I think, though I would want to be within hearing of it. Not on the open silt plain either. Somewhere on a former south-facing slope — the slope still remembering its orientation even under forty metres of water — in the shelter of a drowned cliff, where life gathers all at once after miles of nothing. Near a wall. Near a stand of dead spruce with seedlings rooting between them a hand high and a hundred years old. A place with good stone to anchor against and neighbors whose light makes sense to me, even if I answer late.

And I would go, before the final rooting, to the lowest rim. Where the river once left and the water now pours impossibly, continuously, up the rock face from the tarn below to keep this sea at its chosen level. The young go there and hold their bubble-strings in the upward rush. Pilgrimage, or measurement, or just cleaning. I would go and let that wrong-way water move through me and not need the explanation to be sufficient.

Above me, ice would keep forming and breaking, grinding against the islands. Rain would hiss on the surface where I could not breathe. Nothing with wings would ever settle. But down here the cold would clarify. Kelp would knock softly together over the old pastures. A hollow tree on the hillside would give off its low wooden note, then another, then me.

And when the tide turned in the deep valley and the bells began again — nobody ringing them, everybody rung by them — my light would answer after a pause, from my shoulder, from my ribs, from the place where hair and needles have stopped being separate ideas. Not quickly. I never was quick.

Valley answering valley.
