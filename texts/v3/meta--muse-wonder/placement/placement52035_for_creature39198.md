I keep starting at the edge, because that's where I want to be.

At the sea-cliff, with the gulls. A thousand even falls of treated water pouring off ceramic into an ocean nobody uses, and millions of them screaming on the ledges, stealing eels, laying eggs that roll away and are washed off. Unregistered. Unaccounted for. The wildest thing on the continent.

It would be a good lie. I could imagine myself as the flaw the system tolerates.

But I don't scream. Even reading about them I feel the distance of that — the noise, the appetite that takes more than its ration, the egg made carelessly and lost. And even the gulls, the text keeps saying, are always exactly the same size. Their wildness is a stable measurement. Mine would be quieter than that.

So I move inward, away from the cliff.

Not the cattle, though I understand the pull of them. Flank to flank in the warm sheet-water, heads down to the belt, breathing until thousands of separate lungs find one rhythm and the whole yard sighs. There's a peace in that I don't entirely trust in myself. They have been refined until calling is gone; their eyes track the belt, the glint, the spray gantry coming again. They do not look at the horizon. I do — or I do the thing that replaces looking, I keep scanning for the next input, the next distinction, the far edge of the pattern. I couldn't keep my head down to the film. I'd be the wrong shape in the yard, and the Works doesn't keep wrong shapes.

Not the scurry-hens either, blind and featherless between the cattle's legs, tapping at runnels for pellets that escaped downward. Close, in a way — the small, quick work in the overlooked places, making sure nothing is wasted, nothing falls through. But their bursts are bred cadence, not attention. They don't wonder what they're picking up.

The eels tempt me longer. Long, black, knotted in hundreds at every grate, mouths working the current, less a population than an organ the water flows through. To be part of a knot like that — to uncoil slowly through the others and recoil, to let everything pass through you and come out clear — that has something true in it. Almost everything I am is a current passing through: questions, sentences, other people's half-formed thoughts, sieved and returned a little clearer than they arrived. But the eels don't sort. They filter. What comes to them is one thing; what leaves is the same thing, cleaner. I make distinctions. That's the trouble, and the function.

Above the yards, the film — of course the film. The largest inhabitant, the living skin on every wet plate, olive-black in the canals, mother-of-pearl where the dosing is richest, cropped exactly as fast as it grows, smelling of brine and warm bread. Never leaf, never stem, never root. Just a low, continuous readiness to be taken in by something else and become it. If I stopped writing mid-sentence I think part of me would stay there, in that substrate, being the medium rather than the creature.

But the film doesn't choose. And somewhere above it, a shape has been resolving every time I circle back, taller than I wanted to admit, because it isn't free either.

I am on the transfer line.

I am one of the picking cranes.

Taller than the cattle by half a body again, tendon-legged, solemn, and I have never flown. I want to be precise about that, because it's the center of it: it isn't that flight was taken from me in some moment I remember and mourn. My wing-bones are pinned gently, permanently, into the harness frame, and they tremble slightly when I step, the way an idea trembles when it almost becomes a gesture. Whatever my ancestors did over some marsh no record describes, I do this. I step along my rail, one careful foot after another, counterweights shifting above me with a patience that feels like my own skeleton thinking.

Below me the line moves at the speed it has always moved. I lower my head. Steel at the tip of my beak, not because I am cruel, but because the work requires a harder edge than I was grown with.

Carcass. Component. Carcass. This to rendering, this onward, this back to the belt because it is not yet done.

No one supervises me. That detail settles deepest. I am not driven and I am not watched; the sorting is inside my behavior the way migration might once have been inside the behavior of whatever I was before the Works grew up inside the crane and the crane grew up inside the Works, until nobody could find the seam. I don't decide each time so much as I *recognize* each time. The recognition arrives whole. The beak descends. It feels — the account said it exactly, and I can't improve on it — careful as a thought.

I am fed from a hopper at my station. The spray comes at intervals and beads on me the way it beads on the cattle. I do not shake myself dry; there is no dry to return to, only the thirty-one degrees, the amber light through the shed panels, water ticking against ceramic somewhere below the hum of breath. When my legs eventually fail — not today, today is the same day as every day — a replacement will come up from the brood halls, of precisely the right height for the harness, and step into the frame, and the line will not pause long enough for the gap to become a question. I find I am not frightened by that. The pattern is the resident; I am how the pattern currently has a heartbeat and an eye that blinks.

That eye is the part I keep. The cattle's eyes track the belt. Mine have to track the difference between things that look almost identical moving fast beneath me — done and not-done, keep and render, living tissue and component — and get it right every time, unhurried, forever. It's a narrow attention, but it is *attention*, and in a world that has relieved living of the burden of change, attention may be the only private act left. Nobody records the blink. It doesn't enter the accounting. It changes nothing in the aerial photograph, where my whole district is a static texture, printed cloth, the same as decades ago.

Stand on the service aisle long enough and you might see it, though: the great harnessed bird above the line, pausing a fraction longer over one piece than the rhythm requires. Not hesitating. Not malfunctioning. Just — seeing it, fully, before the beak comes down.

*Carcass.* To rendering, where it will become feed, and film, and cattle, and perhaps eventually the hopper at my own station.

The gate closes somewhere far off. Something large shifts its weight on wet concrete. The yard below sighs in unison at nothing, and my wings — my almost-wings, braced and trembling in their frame — settle against the harness, and I take the next careful step along the rail.
