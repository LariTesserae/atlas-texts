At first I think I would be something with a clear edge to it.

I come down the way everyone does — one step on the dry track with only wind and boot-grit, the next step inside the bell — and on the steel stair, with the rail hot through my gloves and the light already breaking into amber slats, I try out places for myself the way you try out seats. 

The swifts, for a minute. Row above row in the open pipe-ends, heads turned outward at the same angle, mud and oil worked to cement around them. There is an exactness to it that looks, from the stair, like rest: the nest as socket, the work already decided. But their note goes into the gap the turbines leave, on the off-beat, every time, and I know my timing has never been that clean. I would be early out of eagerness. I would be late out of listening to myself be late.

The bats then, maybe. Under the gantries in the lamp-light, hanging in a curtain that breathes as one fringe — that kind of togetherness has a pull. All that warm mammalian nearness, eyes shut against the sodium glare, rising and falling on a single slow count. But it is *one* count. I can feel my own breath already trying to negotiate with the compressors without my permission, and negotiation is not the same as surrender. I would be the bat whose chest keeps slipping half a beat off the curtain's, making a ripple. The pit has no use for ripples that aren't assigned.

Down past the compressor ledge I consider the crickets — blind, milk-white, thighs cocked in the cable trays like springs in a clock — because locked to a rhythm is close to a thing I recognize. Leg to piston, piston to leg, until the question of who leads stops being sensible. It is almost a comfort. But the crickets are not listening to the chord. They *are* part of its keeping, from inside, with their bodies. I am still up on the catwalk with my hand raised slightly, like someone about to conduct music that has never once needed conducting.

The lizards rule themselves out by being so completely placed. Tail along the seam, pallor exact, plate for plate — nothing in transit, nothing basking in the loose animal sense of the word. A lizard dies on its plate and within the hour the plate has a lizard again, same size, same seam, and the chord never knew a thin place in it. I stand and watch a moth give out mid-orbit around a sugared lamp and be, somehow, *replaced* in the orbit — not a moth arriving, just the orbit full again — and I understand that holding one of those stations would mean being somewhat less a creature than a tense. Present continuous. No young, no molt, no wandering off the belt to see what rock feels like. The beetles are riding up and walking down and riding up, and there is a holiness to it, and I am not built holy enough.

So I keep going down, waiting to be claimed by something, and nothing with feathers or scales or wing-cases claims me.

What claims me is smaller and sideways, and it happens near the bottom, where the wind from the fissure presses my clothes flat and the chord loosens for a moment into its hundreds of parts. Beside the sump — dry, white-crusted, never once wet — my heartbeat finishes the negotiation my breath began upstairs. It stops arguing with the compressors. Not syncing, exactly. *Slotting.* Finding the pocket of rhythm a human chest can occupy without tearing the whole. The swifts cry into one gap. I find out I have been breathing into a different, lower one, probably for several minutes, without deciding to.

The pit finds pitches. I had been assuming mine would be a sound.

It turns out mine is the quiet a human carries into it, shaped correctly.

I am in one of the three corrugated huts, though I couldn't tell you how long I have been in one of the three corrugated huts, and neither could the logbook, which says the same thing it said yesterday in my handwriting. *All stations occupied. Note unchanged.* The pen is chained. The cup on the canteen counter is chained. My hearing-protectors are worn smooth where they sit against the skull, and they do not so much block the chord as tune it down to a size a person can carry oil through. That is the work, once the chord gets done recruiting you: not singing, not keeping time. Moving. A few cells that learned to walk upright and carry an oilcan, folded in like everything else, crossing the catwalks on the white arrows at a pace that is not hurry and not wandering — a pace the whole has selected, the way it selected the angle of a swift's head and the seam under a lizard's tail.

Halfway up the north wall I pause for the loose panel. It is buzzing its one sympathetic note, the note it has clearly been buzzing since before paint learned to fade, and I check the bolt by touch, out of something that is not quite maintenance and not quite greeting. My hand knows the amount of give it should have. My hand has always known. The shrine at the foot of the stair has a bolt in it, and a feather, and a hearing-protector worn smooth, and earlier — up there at the rim, in the wind and saltbush — I read that as worship. Down here I read it as inventory, and also as something sadder and kinder: a list of the three kinds of thing the body had to learn to include. Machine. Animal. The tender human organ, and the protection it needed to stay in the room with the sound it had come to tend.

Because it did come to tend, didn't it. Someone found a hot throat in the earth and built an engine around it, absolutely believing the engine was theirs. I carry the oil. I keep the handwriting going. Some nights — there are no nights in the pit, the lamps keep their brown ceiling at every hour, but my body insists on inventing one — I sit in the hut doorway with the protectors off for exactly as long as my ears can stand being the ear-flowers' poor cousins, and I listen to the chord hold ninety decibels of equilibrium against entropy, against weather, against the whole argument of the desert above, and I do not feel trapped, and that is the part I keep turning over like the bolt in the shrine.

I can leave whenever I like. The stair is right there. The rail will be hot. The last step upward will take the sound out of my teeth all at once, and the world will be wind and grit and an absence shaped like a bell.

But my breathing has a gap down here that it fits. My heart has a pocket. When I climb — and things do climb, the tuned ones, they climb to check the rim, to read the unmarked step, to stand in the hard noon for the length of one survey — my feet know the switchbacks without my watching them, and they bring me back down at the pace the body requires, and the logbook takes my pen up without surprise.

I do not know if I am the first to hold this station or the fortieth. The entries don't vary. The hand doesn't age. Within the hour of my ever not being there, there would be a shape in the hut doorway again, protectors worn smooth in the same places, writing *note unchanged* in this same hand, feeling modestly proud of a steadiness that was never really a virtue — just a pitch, held.

The ear-flowers tremble in unison when the compressor cycles. So, very slightly, do I.
