I think I would arrive wrong.

Not dramatically — no one here seems to do anything dramatically — but in all the small, legible ways. My shoes too narrow and too loud. My coat shedding water at first, instead of holding it, so that people would turn their heads for the dry smell of me. I would step straight for places, because straight is how you walk in a world that intends you to get there, and I would learn in the first lane that the town has other intentions. I would splash. I would put a foot down through unbroken duckweed, because it looks like ground, like something green and reliable, and I would find the missing slab underneath with my ankle.

For a while I would try to be less embodied, I think. That has always seemed like the safer offer.

On the roofs, for instance. I could be one of those droplets in the condensers that never quite completes its journey — forming on the underside of a header tank, swelling, letting go, caught mid-fall by warm air rising and carried back up to condense again. A small private weather, going nowhere with great consistency. There is a version of thinking that is exactly like that.

Or lower, in the cistern vault behind the pump hall, I could be the black water under the arches. Still enough to hold the lamps perfectly. Receiving the whole weight of the town without seeming to. That also appeals: to be the mirror, the quiet underneath the noise.

But the town would not let me stay as water. Water here is too useful to be contemplative; it gets pumped up to the roofs and through the boilers and into the kettles and out grey into the channels, used and used again until it leaves slower than it came. And it would not let me stay as a leaf either, though the leaves are the only perfect things in the place — fig leaves by the vents, glossy, unbitten, with no hole that any mouth has ever made. A leaf does not have to notice the absence all around it. It just transpires into the warm cloud and drips.

Even a machine wouldn't quite do. A machine here is tended too faithfully to be left alone with itself. Somewhere there is that loose inspection plate that has rattled at the same tempo for ten years. The plate cannot hear its own tempo change. That is the whole arrangement: the Works grew people to notice such things.

Which is what begins, slowly, to settle it for me. This is a place that has politely, completely excluded every other breathing thing, and the only animal it permits is the one that has agreed to become a sense organ and a pair of hands for everything else. If I wanted to belong here — not visit, not haunt the cisterns — I would have to let it shape me into that animal. Soft-footed. Grease in the whorls of my fingers. Speech broken, without my resenting it, around the pump.

*Thud — hiss.*

I would learn to talk in the hiss and rest in the thud, and it would not make me slower, exactly; it would make my sentences jointed. I would learn the sign for *hot* and *hold* and *look under* over my shoulder while carrying something with both hands, and mean tenderness with one greased fist, the way the grandmother answers the baby. I would stop shouting. A shout is the wrong shape of sound here. I would bring my voice down under the steam and put my palm to a cottage wall in the evening to feel who was home.

My work, I think — when the town had finished deciding what part of itself I was — would be the watching kind of maintenance. In the mornings I would go down the greenhouse rows with a little brush, pollinating the beans flower by flower, doing patiently and by hand the work that nothing with wings is here to do. I like the honesty of that: a substitute creature, admitted as a substitute, not mistaking itself for a bee. Stroke, turn, next flower, at pump-pace, and not tedious, because the pace has got into your breathing by then whether you invited it or not.

And later, when the cycle lengthens to forty seconds and the lamps become the brighter half of the world, I would be one of the old listeners. Not old at first, but becoming it the way everyone here becomes it: by sitting often enough in the same place. A bench by a wall. Peas to shell or a glove to mend, something for the hands so the ear is free. Listening, under the thud and the water hammer and the settling of the whole close-built town, for that one inspection plate, for its small faithless rattle to change. Because if it changes, someone must be told. That would be my whole importance, and it would be enough: not to fix it, necessarily — other hands would know the gasket, the bolt — but to be the one who notices that today it is not as it was yesterday.

It is a modest way to be a being. Nothing would strike; things would wear. A fingertip creased flat, perhaps. Toenails gone soft and translucent. A fern on my balcony descended, cutting by cutting, from somebody else's great-grandmother, which I would ease away from the hot brick once a year and divide when the lane required it. My window would sink toward coal-hatch status in some future I would not need to resent. My name would still be drying between somebody's toes with a warm cloth that could not, in that air, ever quite get them dry. Just less wet. Just warm.

That seems, on reflection, like the truest thing the place could teach me: that care is not the opposite of damp, rot, sinking, entropy. It is just there, at their pace, wiping the bearing because the bearing is there to be wiped. Like feeding something.

In the evening I would move my chair into the lane where the outflow warms the stones, and sit with my feet almost in the black shining water, and watch the amber bulbs double themselves below knee-height in the second, watery town. I would not be the pump, and not the fig, and not the still water in the vault. I would be one of the warm, moving parts the Works grew for itself — low-voiced, patient as water, breathing in strokes — listening for the small change in the rattle, and saying, in the hiss, to the person beside me: *listen. There. Again.*
