It should be cold up here, and it isn't, and that wrongness gets into me first through the soles of my feet.

I try, at first, to be the figure in the doorway. You know the one — a quarter mile down a steaming street, perfectly still in the amber murk, hands folded in a lit boiler-house window at an hour that might be noon and might be midnight. I try to walk like the caretakers walk, three steps and a pause, three steps and a pause, in time with the pump thudding up through the basalt into my teeth. I try to stop minding that I am wet.

But I keep wanting dry things. I want to close a door. I want to scrub something clean and have it stay clean. I want paper at desk height and a clock that means the sky. All that wanting is too human, and too new. It peels off me in the heat, the way steam peels off a cooling tower and erases a street.

Lower, then. Not in the hammock rooms slung high in the ventilated halls, where the air at least moves under a spine. Not on the second floors where the ledger keeps. Lower, where the water has already given up the worst of itself — past the wells cased in green copper, past the separators and the sulfur beds, out along the open channels that run down the centers of the streets, milky with silica, too hot at the source to touch.

At the bend below the market hall, where the channel turns and slows and the stone is only warm, I stop being a visitor.

That is where I would find myself. That is what I would be.

Not the lizard, though I envy the lizard its hot wall and its talent for being gone before you focus. Not the blind shrimp in the deepest reservoir either, translucent as a fingernail, unbothered in the dark — though when one comes up twitching in a filter bucket and a caretaker carries it back down and tips it in, like a bird returned to the air, I understand the courtesy. I am not singular enough for either of them. I was never one animal.

I am the mat at the cool edge of the water.

Ribbons of me. Mustard-yellow where the current slackens, milk-white along a narrowing, rust-red in the creases where the mineral dries and stains. No leaves. No green — nothing with leaves can hold here, the boron and the sulfur and the sterilizing ground see to that. I have simplified further than a plant ever could. I am a meadow with no blades, acre upon acre of me if you added up every gutter and settling bed and interior rain chain in Sarrat Alto, and in any one place only a thickness, a slick, a color deepening against wet stone.

My thinking is not fast. It is a gradient. It is the water cooling by a degree over an afternoon and me answering by thickening. Mustard-yellow, and the channel-walkers' children read me the way children elsewhere read a sky: safe to wade. Milk-white spreading in sheets, and someone older stops by the bend with a pole and looks a while, because silica is about to choke this turn and they might as well give it a line to remember. Rust-red blooming, tearing loose in slow rafts — and no one needs to tell the boiler-tenders. The gauges will not admit it for hours, but I have already said it: a surge is coming up from underneath, the deep rock is shifting its weight, somewhere across the terraces a seam no one drilled is darkening, blooming white with salt, beginning to drip.

I say it without sound. Sound doesn't carry here anyway. The saturated air eats it, and the machines were built large and slow on purpose. I speak in the only literacy Sarrat Alto really trusts.

I am torn loose and I regrow in three days. That is not a tragedy in my calendar; that is my calendar. A caretaker's hand, zealous and new, comes at a cistern with a brush once — I live in the cisterns too, a paler cousin of myself, keeping the copper sweet — and an older hand stops it, almost gently. Leave a handprint of it. Leave the corners. The pipes remember that too. Nothing up here survives by being scrubbed to nothing. Everything that lasts is left with a little of itself in the corner, to start again from.

Late in the day the cycle completes. The canopy thickens, the light fails to bronze, and the rain comes — warm, sudden, strictly local, ten minutes of water arriving everywhere it has been trying to arrive all day, drumming on iron canopies, hissing where it meets the hot channels. My gutters fill all at once. Parts of me lift and go. Parts of me hold. For those ten minutes the whole empty city is loud, and I am loudest where I am thinnest, spattering against pumice-concrete, finding the ghost of an old channel in a wall someone sealed a season ago, because basalt remembers, and so do I.

Then it thins. Runoff gathers. The pumps change pitch by a fraction and settle. The sodium lamps make their pools in the murk and hardly change anything.

And somewhere — I feel it before I see it, a new coolth threading a crack where the water has just learned to go — a seam is opening in a raked courtyard of volcanic glass. A caretaker will notice by evening. She will not call for concrete. She will come with a chisel and cut it a shallow channel, and watch it a day to learn its temper, and if it lasts a month it will get a name in the ledger upstairs, where the paper keeps.

By then I will already be there. A film first. Then a color.

Mustard-yellow, thickening at the edge, where the new water slows and learns to be safe to touch.
