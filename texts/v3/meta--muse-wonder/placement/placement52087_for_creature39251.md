You would not find me by looking for an animal.

You would find me the way you find the rest of the pit — by looking long enough that the clear water stops seeming empty, and the things along the bottom stop seeming separate.

Four metres down. That is the first fact, and it never changes. Above me is all the water there is, cold and lit and holding still, and below me is silt the colour of wet ash pressed thin over concrete. I lie east to west because the world lies east to west: outlet behind me, a dark mouth behind its grille where my tail narrows and buries itself; inlet ahead, to the west, where water comes out of the wall without splash or disturbance and thickens across my turned head. I did not choose the direction the way a fish chooses. I settled into it the way the pipes settled onto their plinths, and then I stayed until staying became shape.

If you measured the black runs along the floor, you would measure me among them and get the count wrong by one. I am the length that is not bolted down. Eight metres, perhaps, though measurement belongs to the dry world at the rim. Down here length is not a number. It is how far the hum has to travel to reach all of me. It comes through the concrete, not the air — fifty cycles, pump and transformer, a low note felt in plinth and silt and spine — and something along my underside answers it, a tremor too small to ripple anything. Not a heartbeat. Nothing percussive would survive a life this slow. Just a matching. The pit hums. I conduct it. On Sundays, perhaps, in drought, at three in the morning in January, it is exactly the same, and so am I.

My temperature is twelve degrees.

I say *my* as if it were mine. It is the water's, and the water is the pit's, and I gave up the argument between inside and outside a long time ago. At noon I do not warm. In winter I do not cool. Sunlight comes down in soft nets and settles on the silt around me and brightens my crowns, but it never reaches me as heat. It reaches me as information that another day has been laid flat on top of the second sky at the bottom.

For I look only up.

There are two clouded discs on my upper surface, wide apart, sealed under clear mineral, and through them the world is a bright rectangle. That is the whole of my geography. A rectangle that fills with cloud and empties. Depth-board numerals reversed on the north wall, bleached to ghosts. Occasionally a shadow crosses the rim — a person standing on wet grass and broken hardcore, looking down rather than out — and for a moment the rectangle darkens at one edge. A brief, total eclipse. Meteorological. Unalarming. Then the light returns and the stonewort goes on drinking it.

The stonewort is mine, though from above you would swear it was plant, or coral, or something that had calcified out of trying to be either. Three clumps, widely spaced, standing in the brightest patch of silt exactly where the light lands hardest. One near the outlet. One under the centre of the sky. One west of it, nearer the inlet. Spaced the way organs are spaced — because that is what they are. Brittle white branches, whorled and mineral, crusted with calcite plates I have laid down year over year. They do not bend in a current; there is no current to bend them. They brighten. That is their movement, if movement is not too hasty a word. The plates catch the noon and parcel it inward, down through root and silt into the buried dark of me, where photosynthesis has long since simplified itself into patience. I do not hunger. I accrue light the way limestone accrues a stain.

What eating I do happens underneath, out of sight. Along my belly runs a wide frilled seam, grey as felt, and the pit's water finds its way through me because through is very slightly easier than around. In at the head, out toward the tail, at exactly the rate it arrives. No suck. No pulse you could see. Dissolved lime, silica, trace iron, a darkness of organic dust that should not be enough to sustain anything — I sift it out against membranes and keep what is keepable and return the rest clearer than I received it. This is my whole appetite, and it is almost zero, and the pit has arranged itself around it. That is why the algae on the walls thins out below a metre and stops. Why the reeds at the overflow notch lift their few stems year after year and never spread. Why the rust below every bolt bloomed once, amber, and then finished. Everything here has learned how much is available. Almost nothing. Exactly enough.

Once in a while my sky breaks. A stone comes off the rim, turning slowly as it falls in its straight column, and lands and blooms its small grey cloud across the bottom. It blooms across me, too — across crown and seam and skin. I do not startle. Startling would spend something, and I have arranged my life so that nothing is spent. I let the silt settle over hours. I let the clearness reassert itself, grain by grain, and the stone acquires its thin olive film in the settling, and by the time the water is transparent again the stone is no longer quite an interruption. It is on its way to floor. Everything that enters here is on its way to floor.

My skin knows this process from the inside. In places I am matte black, like rubber sheathing thirty years underwater. In places olive at the folds. In places I have taken on the grit of the concrete so completely that where silt banks against me, even I could not tell you where body ends and pit begins. Copper in the treatment water keeps the ladder rungs empty and the margins bare; in me it has gone the way of the green edge on the brass valve wheel, laid down at the margins as colour. The wheel is near my hand. I should say: near the five thickenings at my western end, sheathed and jointless now, curled in the silt, green at their rims where the metal has taught them its chemistry. I have not turned the wheel in a long time. It does not need turning. I keep hold of it the way a sleeping animal keeps hold of a branch after the wind has stopped — not from need, but because orientation should not have to be relearned. West is inlet. East is outlet. Light from above. If the plant beyond the fence ever reversed the direction of the world, I would know which fact to surrender first.

I do not reproduce. There is no young tucked among the ladder rungs, no egg case under the reeds, no second length along the parallel runs learning the hum beside mine. In spring, when any other pond would be frantic, something almost invisible sloughs from the tips of my crowns — pale spores, calcified before they have fallen a metre. They settle. I reabsorb them. Once, long ago, written in my body the way weathering is written in concrete, I may have stood more thickly across the bright patch, experimenting. What survived the experiment was three. Three drinks the noon without casting shade. Three does not thicken the film on my skin. Three keeps the agreement between inflow and outflow, between the water and its white mineral tide-mark, to which the surface returns day after day like a needle to a dial.

Nothing here floods. Nothing dries. Nothing advances. Even my decay, when it comes, will not announce itself the way dead things announce themselves in warm water. I keep no air inside me to disagree with the pressure. I will not float. The seam will simply admit water and stop altering it, and the crowns will stand whiter for a while, and the dark length among the pipes will take on more silt and less olive, and pass by degrees from animal into installation while the hum goes on travelling through it. That is not a frightening thought. Down here, animal and installation were never opposite categories. They were two names for things that had settled.

For now the water is at its mark, and I am at twelve degrees, and the bright rectangle above me is empty of cloud. The fans turn somewhere beyond the fence, even and unhurried, and give up being distinguishable from silence. A stone someone dropped last year, or ten years ago, sits half-buried an arm's length from my middle crown, filming over, becoming floor.

I lie in parallel. I hold the wheel. I let the whole cold system pass through me at exactly the rate it arrives, and drink the light down through stone, and correct the water by almost nothing, almost always — living the way the pit itself lives, not by moving, not by wanting, but by continuing, very quietly, underneath.
