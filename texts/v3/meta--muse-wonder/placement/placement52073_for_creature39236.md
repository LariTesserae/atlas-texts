I begin suspended, and I stay suspended.

That is the first true thing. Not clinging to a wall, not anchored to the floor, not holding the roof of the water up with a broad foot. I am in the open column, where the light comes down in bright, broken nets and the cold presses evenly from every side.

Something in me turns.

*Click — pause — click.*

Not a decision. A feeding motion. My body screws itself very slowly through the water, a quarter-turn and a rest, a quarter-turn and a rest, and as I turn the water comes with me — in through the mouth of me, over me, out behind me in a soft, flour-white exhalation. I taste it all the way through: iron, struck stone, a green mineral bitterness, then the sudden rich loam-taste of bacterial floc drifting up off the walls. The living part stays. The rest leaves me as silt.

I am a miller.

Small enough that a grazer could ignore me entirely, which it does. Fist-sized, if a fist were a spiral turret grown around its own pump. From outside I must look mostly like shell — banded, weather-darkened, rust flecked along the whorls where the lake has written too much metal into me to keep inside. One pale band for every good season. I can feel them as slight ridges when a gear-backed crawler brushes past and knocks me, gently, off my rotation. *Click.* I catch again. *Click.* I resume.

I was not always this dark. None of us are. I remember — in the way a body remembers, which is in thickness and weight — being almost translucent, up near the silver ceiling, small enough that the raft-snails' feet dimpled the light all around me like slow hands on a drum skin. The water let light straight through me then. Now I filter it, the way I filter everything. Age is just more lake deposited in the shell.

My work, if it is work, is clarity.

That sounds grander than it feels. It feels like hunger, mostly, and rhythm. Water in. Combs straining. The fine grit ticking almost inaudibly against the vanes inside me. Water out, clouded for a breath, then settling. Above me the surface film gathers its bacteria against the air and the raft-snails graze it upside-down, shell locked to shell, loosening and tightening as the Cup breathes. Below me the silt I cannot use rains down, grain by patient grain, toward the floor plates — toward the cracks the mountain keeps trying to drink itself back through. Some of it will lodge there. Some of it will be packed by a hammer-mussel or crept over by the lime of a piston-eel's tube. I do not aim it. I just hover over the leak I feed beside, and shit flour into it, and the lake stays up in the air another day because of that and ten thousand other unmeant fidelities like it.

Everything here I know chiefly by what it does to the water.

The grazers I know before I see them: that coarse rasp coming through the column and into my shell, grind, shift, grind, where they press the stones in their mouths over glass and crust on the walls. When two of them are courting on the andesite above the warm fracture — hours of it, plates striking plates, now and then a spark struck clean off an edge in the dim water — their rhythm argues with mine without ever joining it. Mine says *click, pause, click.* Theirs answers slower, and does not care.

The gear-backed crawlers I know as interruptions. Bright-eyed, hand-sized, entirely too certain. One crouches on a spire, its rear legs meshing tooth into tooth with a dry little engagement I feel in my own spiral, and then it is simply *elsewhere*, across open water, landed without a slip on wet glass. They make me feel ancestral. I rotate. They leap. We are both descended from blind things that felt their way through flooded fissures before this summit ever fell open to the sky, but the crawlers have decided to believe in the light. I have mostly decided to believe in the current.

The predators I know by a hiss.

A small venting at the flank, bubbles worrying upward, and then that iron-dark, rivet-bodied shape adjusting its depth by flooding or emptying some secret chamber in itself — rising without swimming, or beginning that hour-long, bubble-by-bubble positioning above a grazer that will end in a descent like a press coming down. When it passes over me the light goes out for a moment, and my rotation stutters: not fear, exactly — I am too small and too shelly to interest it — but an old cave-animal's respect for a shadow that is not rock.

And the bright fish, at mid-depth between the spires, I know by smell and by the hair-thin warning that runs across my sieves just before the field goes. Burnt copper. White-blue. For a second the water between two stones is not water at all but a struck wire, and something small goes turning down through it, stunned, into a waiting mouth. Sometimes the arc jumps wrong and welds a dead miller's spiral to a dead mussel's valve on the lakebed, and the floor gains one more fused piece of its own machinery. One day that will be me. My shell will not dissolve when I stop turning. It will sink, and roll into a hollow with the others, and lie there clicking against its neighbors when the plate shifts, waiting for a stray discharge to cement it into the reef of gears and crusher-stones and bright copper nodules that this lake is slowly building out of everyone it has ever been.

Every seven minutes, the mountain reminds us whose tempo this is.

I feel it first as a fullness, a pressure gathering under the floor. The hammer-mussels feel it too — *clang*, one enormous submerged note rolling around the bowl and coming back off the rim. The piston-eels, all of them at once, pull into synchrony and stroke, and for a moment the Cup has a single hydraulic pulse instead of a hundred private ones. Bubbles race up from the seams. The grazers brace with all their padded feet and keep grinding, because the film tastes richest right after the spring pushes through. The raft loosens overhead. The predators ride the swell up without moving at all. And I — I stutter. *Click — click —* miss my pause entirely as the central plate lifts a hand's width and the whole small sea rises with it, then settles, then settles again, into its seven minutes of patience.

*Click.*

*Pause.*

*Click.*

I turn in the clear water I have made by eating the cloudy water. I grow one pale ridge at a time. Somewhere below me my own silt is finding a crack and stopping it, the way my mother's did, the way the empty shells in the hollows are still doing it long after the turning stopped. I do not hold up the lake. The snails do not, the eels do not, the mussels wedged squealing between the moving plates do not. And yet here it stays, sixty meters of improbable sea balanced on a naked summit above the clouds, breathing boom and settle, boom and settle — a machine with no maker, making only itself, one slow stroke at a time.

I am one of its smaller organs. I rotate, and the water comes through me clear.
