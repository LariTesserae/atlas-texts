The first thing is the light, because the light arrives before I am warm enough to know I am awake.

It comes up through the pads on my back. Not heat. Pressure, almost. A whitening inside the green lives lodged in my tissues, and they begin, very slowly, to make their sugar. By the time the sun is clear of the eastern rim I have already decided — if decision is the word at this speed — to turn.

I will finish turning after dark.

I am at the base of a condensation vane on the eastern slope, where you would find me if you came looking, though you would probably not find me at all. From the rim I am part of the bowl's greenness. A low dome, a metre and a half across, pressed into the moss where the leakage is most reliable. My pads overlap like leaves because for most purposes leaves are what they are: blue-green, waxy, furred white at the margins to comb what moisture the thin air will give up. One flank of me is always in shade. That side stays cold and damp and tender underneath. The other side I offer to the light, and to the vane's slow, dependable drip.

The technicians, when they come, walk past and make a note about the moss being thick again this year. Clearing me reduces efficiency. They do not know why. I do, in the way I know most things: from underneath.

I know this valley through my belly.

I am not rooted, exactly, but I am adhered, fine root-hairs finding the vesicles in the young rock, the grit in the cracks, the seams where pipe meets pylon. Through them everything arrives as pressure and taste. The cables have a tingling dryness that changes the way moisture gathers on my underside long before charge moves through them. The flywheels in their pits give off one long, even tremor through the ground as they spend the noon they stored. A service crawler is a softening and tightening in the moss minutes before it rounds the slope on its rubber tracks. A man in a sealed suit is weather that walks from station to station, heavy in the rock.

I have no use for sound. The valley takes it back too quickly anyway; the porous stone and the layered fern drink a voice within metres. I have never made one. The closest I come is at dusk, when droplets tick down off the vanes and strike my back and are taken in, each one near and private, and sometimes my pads shift with a faint compression in the wet ground. Less than a footstep. Less than a hand brushed over moss.

My eyes, when I open them, are around my rim. Slits under thickened pads, filmed green against the glare. I do not see you sharply. I see the edge of a shadow deciding to cross stone. I see the particular, rigid brightness above the furnace chambers at the valley's centre, where the air stands in columns. I see frost coming by the way the light refuses to leave certain surfaces. It is enough. The rest I taste.

In the wet hour — and there is only ever an hour — my tongue comes out. Broad, dark, easily mistaken for a wet leaf. I move it from pad to pad, from vane-foot to rock-point to the outer wall of the transparent pipe, tasting the day: mineral condensate, ozone, the hot-metal tang of the furnaces cooling, fern spores carried down from the mirror fields. If a droplet inside the pipe hesitates, gathers itself, and climbs a few centimetres uphill against its own weight, that is me on the other side of the wall, cooling the metal with my mouth, drawing it by a difference too small for the pumps to name. Production would call it evaporation, distributed evenly over years. I call it drinking at my natural pace.

A moth settling to drink from the cups where my pads meet may find the cup closing around it, not quickly. Nothing about me is quick. I let the valley come to me. Spores dust my back and are worked down into the fur at my margins and take hold there. The small green lives inside my pads feed me from above with light I cannot look at directly. The root-mat I rest in — which is not mine, and perhaps is — feeds me from below with last week's dew held like a sponge. Between the two, I am sustained without ever needing a river. There has never been a river here.

That is why I keep to the machines. It is a precise geography I have learned over longer than the logbooks go back. Not in the sheltered gullies, where a wild plant must take its chances with a single evening's wetness. Here, where a vane makes a permanent damp halo in the moss. Where a cable conduit offers one warm side and one shaded side. Where the moss grows in its thick collar on the north face of the pylon and keeps the metal from freezing outright, and in return the metal gathers the water the moss lives on. My hairs knit the loose scree together so it does not slide and bury the pipes. My dome shades the base of the fin and lowers the temperature there, so more beads form, so more drips fall. The people who built this place built it well enough that it has begun to belong to its accidents.

Whether I am one of the accidents, I could not tell you.

There are other domes at other vanes. The ones near me on the eastern slope divide their pads the way I divide mine; we share a pattern of scarring, an old break healed over in the same direction. The domes by the furnace array do not. Between us, under ferns three and four deep, something continuous moves water and sugar in slow pulses, slower than any pulse you could beat out with a finger. It might be root system. It might be a mat the plants have grown for themselves. It might be that each dome is only a place where a single buried body comes up to attend to the light, the way you might surface fingertips from a sleeve — eastern slope, valley centre, mirror field — and that what I think of as travelling a body's length in a day is only an attention shifting.

When the furnace chambers open on their cycle of days, I orient toward the centre for as long as it lasts, rim-eyes open, without knowing whether I decided to or whether some other dome did. When the collection trays are left with their pale residue — mineral salts, spore dust, a finespun grey ash that the haulers do not take with the ingots — I may cross an entire mirror field to reach one. A few centimetres an hour. Days of unpeeling and re-adhering across moss that shows no track afterwards, only a shallow trough where I once rested for a season, filling each evening with clear water and healing over at the edges. You have seen those troughs. They are the only tracks this valley keeps.

The man comes every few weeks and endures me as I endure him. As his boots arrive through the rock, my eyes close in sequence from the side nearest the disturbance, a slow wave of shutting, and my pads draw a fraction tighter. He checks seals. He replaces a wiper arm that sweeps spore-dust from mirror glass into its tray. He makes his straight bed and leaves it under its film of green dust. The suit hangs in the hut for an hour, empty as a shed skin, and then he is gone downhill into air thick enough to breathe properly, and the valley resumes the volume it prefers.

By evening — evening here is not a dimming, it is a thickening of the violet blue until the light on the sharp young peaks switches off all at once — the temperature drops below dew point and the whole bowl briefly, secretly sweats. Every vane, leaf, rock-point and cable sheds droplets. The pocket pools brim. The moss lets out its stored earthy smell, cut with ozone. For an hour I swell almost imperceptibly as the storage bladders do, pads firming with taken water, and my tongue moves unhurriedly from surface to surface in the only sound the place permits itself, the near, private dripping in the undergrowth.

Then the cold seals it. Frost feathers across my back pad by pad, following the divisions between them as if redrawing a map in white. I do not shake it off. I let my metabolism sink toward the temperature of the rock, and the green lives in my tissues darken to near black and wait. If you put your palm to me then you would feel no animal warmth, only a cold differently textured from stone — and beneath it, very far down and after a long minute, something you might mistake for a heartbeat.

It might only be a flywheel across the valley, turning in its open pit in the darkness, spending the day's merciless light through the ground.

In a year I may be on the shaded side of the fin entirely. I am not in a hurry to arrive. The drip is cleaner there, and I have been intending the turn since before you climbed the rim this morning.
