You would not find me at first. That is the first true thing.

You would have been in the pocket for an hour — it takes an hour to cross eighty metres here, picking your holds in the bamboo grass, ducking under branches that do not touch the ground, going wrong where a stream an hour old brings you to a root-mat and stops — and you would think the absence was getting stranger. No birdsong. No insects at the pale flowers. No tracks in the moss. A full, lush place, dripping at a thousand pitches, with the animal part left out.

And all that time I would have been within arm's reach of you, twice. Perhaps three times.

I hang where the bamboo thins between three trunks that grow too close to separate, belly up under a branch with my back bowed along its underside. Or breast down along one, head tucked so deep between my shoulders that from above I am only the back continuing. I do not work at disappearing. There is no need to work at it. Moss grows on a trunk to its last twig; moss grows on me to my elbows, in beards, in cushions. Liverworts plate my throat and belly in overlapping discs, pale as the underside of a leaf. Behind my left shoulder a fern lives, two fronds, no more — it opens a fraction when the year warms, if opening is the word for that slow uncoiling, and closes again for the long cold. In the fork of my right hip an orchid sits in shed skin and old moss and holds its own cup of water still while everything else is being shaken.

I am about the length of a child, if you laid me out, which I would never do. Heavier than I look. Most of that weight is not me. I carry perhaps three times my flesh in green. It is not decoration, and it is not only hiding. It keeps the cold from finishing its slow work into me. It drinks for me. And it holds me down.

That last is the one you would understand if the gust found you upright. The ridge above takes the weather whole and pours it over the lip. Cloud boils over, falls in, tears for three seconds on more cloud, and closes. A gust comes into the bowl like water over a weir and every branch thrashes and throws off its stored water in a hiss, and in that moment I loosen two holds of my four — never one at a time, never all — and let myself be taken. A metre. Sometimes from one tree to the very next. Moss streaming sideways, the fern flat against me, the orchid's cup shivering but not spilling. Then the calm, sudden and complete, and I have already taken new holds and gone still. You are left with the small suspicion that something at the edge of sight was elsewhere a moment ago, and nothing to put the suspicion on.

In the calms I do not move. Breath slow enough that its showing is lost among the mist-rags hanging between trunks. Heart slower than breath. My blood is near the air: seven degrees, eight. At night it falls lower, and the first gusts of the morning have to work on me for an hour before I am properly roused.

Except when the cable is late.

You hear it first from above: a rising hum in the steel, a rattle as the hanger takes the strain, then the bucket swings out of the fog over the canopy, swaying wildly, ore grinding inside it. Every half minute or so. It has been every half minute for longer than the trees around me have been flagged into their bent shapes by the wind. I do not time myself by light — there is not enough light here to time anything, midday is a drowned green dark under cloud under canopy under undergrowth — I time myself by that. It is closer to a pulse than my own pulse; I can be lifted out of cold torpor by a silence that goes a few seconds too long, my head raising, my filmed eyes opening on nothing, listening with the fine bones of my skull.

Once, perhaps twice in a season, in the worst weather, when the wind finds the notch in the rock ribs and rises nearly to a scream and the buckets come through at their steadiest over the treetops, something answers in the back of my throat. Not a call. There is no one to call. A low ticking, electrical, felt more than heard, that sets my teeth and skull humming in sympathy. My cheek knows that ticking better against stone. At the eastern rib the pylon leg goes into the young black rock, rust scaled orange under its sheath of moss, and the buried cable runs beneath it. After a storm, when the tramway has been running hard, I keep to that rib and lick the rust where the fine dust has drifted and stuck — grey-red, iron on the tongue, and other things this soil-less place cannot make for itself, shaken grain by grain out of the buckets overhead. Then I put my cheek down along the line of the cable and rest, and the place and the pylon are not two things in my mind.

The scar is why, perhaps. Under the moss from my left shoulder to the middle of my back, long and straight, where the moss has never grown as thick again. I do not cross under the cable except between buckets. Then I move with something that, for me, is almost haste: three holds, four, three, through and frozen before the next hum rises in the wire. Whether I learned that or arrived knowing it, I cannot say. The scar is old enough that the moss-rings over it record bad years I do not remember in any other way — a stopping, a resuming, a stopping again, farther back than this generation of trunks.

I eat what the pocket does with its dead. I do not come down. Fallen branches never reach the ground here; they are caught a metre up in the weave and kept there, green outside and black and fibrous within, softened by fungi. I work my blunt muzzle into that softness and eat for hours at a bite, the wood as a paste. Flowers where they lodge in my own moss after the wind strips them. Mineral silt licked off leaves on still mornings. I drink by inclination: fog condenses on my back until the moss saturates and tips, and the water finds the channel along my spine, is guided by the set of my head, and arrives at the corner of my mouth. Sometimes, after heavy rain, I move hold by hold to a black hollow in the rock and lap before it empties, or put my whole face under the thin thread falling from a leaf cluster and swallow, and swallow, eyes closed.

If the cloud tears and that brief, directionless glare comes through, I press all four palms flat and wait blind. My eyes are made for this permanent dusk. The glare flattens my world as it flattens yours. I wait for them to give the dark back.

I am not the first. You passed two or three of us coming in and thought we were fallen wood — long green shapes in the weave, recognisably animal for a season, then only shape. When my time comes I will take my final holds in a fork somewhere and stiffen there, and the moss will take me not as a surface but as a continuation. From a cup like the orchid's cup at a dead hip, in a wet year, something small and blind may begin already furred with spore, already holding. Or it will happen in a way no one has been still enough to see. The pocket has never been crossed slowly enough.

For now, I go sometimes to the edge. There is a palm-print in the moss of the last flagged tree on the eastern rib, mine, pressed and returned to over so many years that it springs back a little less each time. I hang there in the calms facing outward: one step past, and the green stops the way a coast stops — lichen crust, then nothing, wet black rock falling away, wind coming up it with the rain from below, cloud racing past on its way to fall into the bowl behind me. The cable hum is faint there, under the roar. I can see very little, with eyes made for drowned light. I stay anyway, a while.

I do not cross. My coat would be stripped there in an hour, and my coat is what I cannot survive losing. My food would not follow. The hum would be gone.

So when the next gust comes over the lip and shakes all the stored water off the leaves at once, I let go with two holds, and I am swung back in among the trunks, moss streaming, my fern pressed close, and I am gone into the general green before the calm has quite settled — the one animal in the pocket, wearing the pocket, eating its dead, drinking its fog, listening for the bucket that is due, and due, and due again out of the cloud.
