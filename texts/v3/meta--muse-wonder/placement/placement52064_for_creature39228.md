You would not find me from the yard.

From up there I am nothing. Hot concrete, expansion joints, a stunted fig in a drain seam, a service grate clogged with bottle caps and silt beside the Number Three pump house. If you stood over me at noon you would hear the river beyond the levee more clearly than you would hear me. You would have to lift — both hands, the hinge is sharper than it should be — and let the wrong air come up: wet earth, hot copper, animal musk, machine oil, a faint sweet rot where there are almost no leaves to rot.

Then climb down feet-first, four rungs, ducking under the lowest pipe, and you would be inside me before you understood where I begin.

Because I do not begin in one place.

At first, if you stayed very still, you might think I am the water. I am down in the sump, an inch of tea-brown water over silt, over a drowned valve wheel. I am keeping tadpoles who have never been told to stop being tadpoles — there is no cold here to instruct them, no dry season to hurry them. I am letting hundreds of snails rasp at an algae too thin to see, shell to amber shell, until there is no floor left, only their slow pavement. A few of my largest still carry your kind's white numbers on them. 7. 12. 19. The paint is going. I keep moving underneath it.

Then the water rises, as it does every seventeen minutes, and I let go.

The snails float belly-up and turn in their carousel. The fish rises in the standpipe. Ah — perhaps I am the fish, for a moment. Pale enough to see through, long enough that I never quite show my tail, I have narrowed my whole life to the exact diameter of a vertical pipe. I came in with last year's flood and did not leave when the flood did. All day I rise and sink, rise and sink, face turned to a current that is not there yet because I know it is coming.

But I am also what knows it is coming sooner.

A hand's breadth above the water, where the concrete weeps and never dries, I am woodlice pouring out from under your knee without panic, into the vacancy your other knee just left. I am beetles retreating into threaded bolt holes. I am black, I am copper — my wing cases burnished wire-bright from squeezing behind pipes. I am roaches lying with their antennae flat against warm metal, listening through their feet. When the pump is about to turn, I climb your boots in an orderly evacuation and wait at your laces, and I start climbing down again before the water has remembered to fall.

Higher, I grow warmer.

I am the ant road that never stops to be a single ant: up one cable, across the junction box, down into the crack behind the fogged gauge, white larvae going one way, empty husks going the other. Put your wrist in my way and I will not stop. I will reroute across you, patiently, as if you were a new piece of conduit.

I am geckos pressed belly-to-metal on the sweating pipe, in a row like mottled tiles, throats pulsing, closing one eye, then the other, in no hurry at all. Between each gecko, wedged exactly into the seam where pipe meets bracket, I am a frog no bigger than a thumbnail. Green when the light shaft falls straight through the grate at midday, nearly black the rest of the time. I lay my eggs in the warm film inside a cracked insulator that stays at blood temperature all year because nobody taught me not to.

At the very top I am air, and I am not empty.

I am midges and gnats and tiny wasps in a slow spiral that never thins, only reforms around whatever warm thing enters the light — your head, your breath. I am sparrows worrying at me from above, their beaks scarred into the underside of the grate. I am a bat folded into a dead alarm bell, in precisely the space where the clapper was removed, wings tight as a closed umbrella. I arrived this spring and became a missing part, correctly shaped.

Along my edges I make use of what you leave. A rat's nest on a cut-down milk crate, woven from cable ties and blue work gloves. Mice coming and going with great domestic purpose through the tiny door of a child's red-roofed plastic house. A spider sitting at twelve o'clock on a gauge whose needle has not moved in years, in the center of a web she has built in exact imitation of the dial, waiting for whatever flies keep appointments. One fern — exactly one — growing from my newest fracture, ants farming pale aphids on its underside. Three blades of grass. Coins of moss no bigger than coins, fed by condensation that gathers under the cold pipe, swells, and then, instead of falling, crawls upward along the grime to reach them.

That is how you will know me, if you stay long enough. Not by any one of those lives. By the pattern between them.

The relay in the junction box ticks. A green lamp comes on. Another answers it. And then — almost conversation, not quite — my frogs blink their pale throat sacs in sequence. The pump behind its mesh guard clears its throat. You will not hear it so much as feel it through the soles of your feet, because I take sound into silt and soft bodies and give very little back.

The sump rises two inches. Everyone shifts exactly one body-width up. The carousel turns. The descent begins. Everything is displaced and everything is returned, a little rearranged, nothing lost. My total fullness does not change.

I am young. That matters. I am not an ancient ruin softened into wilderness. My concrete is still bright where it cracked, my rebar has not rusted dull, my fracture is clean as broken biscuit. I was a pressure-relief chamber, an inspection vault, two meters by one and a half, built to be empty and useful. A few years ago the river undercut the yard and opened me, and because out there is a hundred meters of baking concrete in every direction and in here is the only damp, shaded, edible pocket there is, everything that found me stayed.

And to stay, everything became less.

The gecko flattened. The fish narrowed. The frog miniaturized. The bat learned to be a bell-tongue. The woodlice learned my seventeen minutes better than any clock. Species that would have eaten each other elsewhere were filed, here, into strata so precise that your knee, arriving uninvited, is just another temporary geography to flow around.

So if I must say what I am, I will not say snail, or frog, or fish, though I am all of them and will lose none of them lightly.

I am the making-room.

I am a fullness that has learned to behave like one creature by constantly, minutely, negotiating itself. Move me with the back of a knuckle and I decide. Thrash, and I go flat and silent and take a full pump cycle to forgive you. Stay quiet, crouched, breathing shallow among my small sounds — a rasp, a relay click, a throat pulse, water sighing out through a slot you cannot see — and I will incorporate you. The midges will take the shape of your head in the light. Something small and warm will settle into the hollow behind your ear and stay for the duration. I will give you exactly your volume, and no more.

And when you climb back out, backwards, rung by rung, I will not keep the hole you left.

Frog by frog, beetle by beetle, snail by settling snail, I will close over it busily, completely, until the grate clangs back into its frame and there is only the hot yard again, four bottle caps in the slots, and underneath your feet — unmarked, unneeded, continuing seventeen minutes at a time — me.
