At first I think I would be up on the levee.

That's the honest starting place. On the grassed embankment where the air still moves, where a shirt can dry, where you can see the river and hear the pumps only as a thump coming up through your shoes. I would come down the gravel track between the banana stands the way the dried-fish traders do — too fast, trying to keep my feet dry, slapping at mosquitoes and missing, conspicuous for a day by how much I fought the pace.

But nobody stays a visitor in the bowl without either leaving or being changed by it. Twenty feet down and the heat takes the run out of you. The light goes green through two and three layers of leaf. Your trousers learn to roll themselves to mid-calf. Your paper softens. If I stayed, the place would have to find a use for someone whose instinct is not to clear, not to build new on empty ground, but to keep returning to what is already growing and ask what it can bear.

I wouldn't be a pump engineer, though I thought of them first. They are the priesthood with the most obvious power — two big diesels, a bass note you feel in your soles a hundred yards away, the painted subsidence marks on the pumphouse door checked monthly, the judgment call made at breakfast from an extra inch in the canal. If they stop during the flood pulse, the lowest sheds go dark from beneath and then under. It's necessary, urgent work, done half in gesture and lip-reading because the high registers of their hearing are already gone.

I don't belong in that roar full-time. I would go deaf in the wrong way there — I'd lose the smaller sounds underneath it.

I wouldn't be the heron either, though I envied it for a while. The one that stands motionless in the settling pond behind the mill, where the fiber wash-water spreads through reed pans before it goes back to the river. It visits. It is not digested by the place. It can lift off over the rim whenever the damp gets into its lungs.

And I wouldn't be in the decorticator crews, sweet-smelling and deafened, feeding cane into the grind, or hauling bales on the trolley, or working the one permitted hearth in the cookhouse. All of that is real work, steady work, shoulder work. Mine would have to be slower and more particular.

It finds me at first light, I think. Before the yard goes amber, before the shift-change steam blast trembles every loose leaf.

You would find me on a ladder.

Not high — nothing here is really high, the doorways are hung low in fig walls trained for two seasons, everyone lives ducking and stooping — but high enough that my head is in the leaf roof over Pump Lane, thinning it so the lane below gets its stripe of noon light again. My forearms would be roped from climbing. My hands and forearms stained that green-grey-brown from fiber juice and crushed leaf that never quite washes out. Grease under the nails from helping with a valve in the morning, black earth under the grease from the root-mat. Trousers rolled even though I'm not in a canal. Feet flat, a little splayed, sandals of oiled leather re-oiled every few days so they don't bloom white.

I am a pruner. Or — because the pruners here are never only pruners — I am becoming the memory the pruners carry.

That's the work that pulls me in, down in the bowl: not cutting, negotiating. There's a root beginning to lift the walkway by Second Canal and I can trim it back, shape it, persuade it sideways. There's another root, three lanes over, thick as my waist where it fuses with two others behind the dormitory, and that one I may never cut through. It is holding the building up. The iron frame it was trained on generations ago has rusted inside it; now you cannot tell where support ends and tree begins, and it doesn't matter, because the prohibition is what matters. Children here learn those roots by name the way other children learn roads. Somebody has to keep teaching the names. Somebody has to stand in the mill office every season and repaint the law on the wall where the damp keeps eating the paint, in letters plain enough that a tired person at midnight can still read them.

Paper won't do it here. Paper swells and molds at the edges within the month. So knowledge lives in painted marks and notched posts, in a dozen precise words for damp — a shirt aired, leather on the turn, floorboards gone dark from beneath — each with its consequence and its remedy. It lives in old people, listened to not sentimentally but practically, because if an old pruner says that wall will not take another story, the dormitory does not get another story. I would be the one who listens longest, who repeats it back until I have it in my feet. The archive that walks around in a wet shirt.

That suits a mind like mine, I think. I would not own a house so much as succeed into one: inherit a wall still thickening by fractions of an inch a season, a walkway my predecessor bound, a fig that weeps clean water down its trunk in the morning where it presses a warm wall. If I married, we would not sign anything that lasted. We would set two iron frames, plant young figs, tie them, and wait two seasons to see if there would be a place to hang a door. My patience would have to become literal. My feuds too — you cannot storm out here, you can only slowly begin to train an elsewhere, and by the time the elsewhere could hold a roof, you might have decided to stay and prune instead.

In the afternoon I'd be in the market square, crossing the springy root floor worn polished in the middle, stepping around the trip-root everyone knows without looking down. I'd know which bridge presses lower when the hyacinth mats rise with the flood. I'd know, during the brief midnight pause when the mill shuts down and the frogs take the canals all at once, how to count that chorus and hear how high the water is. I'd boil my well water without thinking about it, four feet down and brimming. I'd oiled my tools. I'd checked the mark.

I would not thrive anywhere thinner. That's the final recognition, and it doesn't feel like being trapped. Outside the bowl, up where clothes get crisp and buildings are built without asking the plants first, everything would seem wastefully dry, loud in the wrong ways, cleared instead of negotiated. My competence here would be so specific — which root, which lane, which state of damp, which sound under the pumps — that it would look like nothing elsewhere. But here it would be total. Here it would be enough to be useful for a lifetime.

So at dusk you'd find me going home across the little plank bridges with everyone else, not hurrying because the machines do the hurrying for us, steam hanging at head height between the drying sheds, finished bales giving off their own curing heat under the open roofs. My window would come on behind leaves, not on them, but inside them — one more floating light in the denser green of the hollow, held, maintained, and slowly, almost imperceptibly, thickening.
