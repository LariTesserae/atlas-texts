You would find me before dawn — though dawn is a generous word for it — up near the spire, sitting very still in a house that did not exist when I went to sleep.

Not building it. No one builds up there in the lowland sense. I am a Finisher's apprentice, which is also not quite the right phrase, but it is the one stamped on my papers by the Office of Emergence, underneath a second stamp that says my papers are to be read politely and filed upside-down.

Most mornings I am sent out with a chalk stick, a rivet gun I am not yet trusted to use, a thermos, and three kinds of counting. Counting stairs. Counting rooms. Counting to twenty before I answer anyone.

That last one is personal.

I wasn't born beside the spire. My family are Lower Foundry people — I still pause unconsciously before the important word in a sentence, waiting for the hammer to fall through the floor of the world. Twenty-two seconds. You think your body forgets that when you move three tram rides away. It does not. My teeth remember before I do.

But Foundry children get sent where their oddness is useful, and I was an odd child long before the mountain got to me. Quick to laugh, they said, in a way that made adults look around. I would turn my head toward a dropped cup before it hit. I would say *I'm all right* half a breath before my mother could ask if I'd fallen. It worried her in the specific Sessran way — not *is he ill* but *has he been noticed*.

I was nine when I stopped being only that and became a story other people's cousins tell.

I don't remember a dramatic moment. There was no white light, no voice out of the fissure. I went scrambling where the fresh basalt comes up in black columns behind the Upper Cistern, where the moths beat their slow dinner-plate wings in air nobody else can use, where children are told quite specifically not to go because the rock is still deciding what shape it is. I came home for supper on time. That, my mother will still tell you, was the first strange thing. I had never once come home on time.

I was perhaps half an inch taller down my left side than my right. I still am, if you measure carefully, which tailors in Sessra learn not to do where I can see them wanting to. The smallest fingernail on my left hand is dark glass now. It sweats condensation on the inside, faintly, when a new room is forming nearby. And my left ear —

My left ear hears twenty seconds early.

Not clearly. That would be a gift, and Sessra is suspicious of gifts that don't come with a maintenance schedule. It hears the way you hear through a wet wall: muffled, flattened, all the edges off. The forge-hammer arriving before the blow. Tram bells before the tram rounds the curve. My own footsteps coming back to me from a Thin Street before I have quite committed to the stride. Other people's questions, occasionally, while they are still arranging their faces into an expression that will make the asking seem casual.

So I count. One, two, three. All Sessrans count. I just have further to count to. I hold answers behind my teeth and let the world catch up and ask them properly. It's manners. If a voice gives an instruction before its mouth has earned it, the council ruling applies, and besides, it frightens people to be replied to too soon. Lovers, mothers, Finisher crews — up here you learn to wait for a person to finish arriving in the moment they think they're in.

That is why they gave me the work.

Because a house near the spire has the same problem I do, only larger and made of stone.

You go in at first light, if you can call it that — blue-grey, shadowless, the ceiling breathing somewhere far overhead and dripping warm black water down the new brick. The mountain will have started something in the night. An arch where yesterday there was a smooth hot crack. A stair that rises four steps toward a landing that has, so far, declined to exist. A window with the glass already in it, sweating on the other side, looking out on a view of the plateau as it was perhaps a minute ago: you can see yourself approach the house across it, a little younger in intention, still deciding whether to knock.

You do not knock.

You ring your bell. Once, clearly. Mine is brass, small enough to sit in the hollow of my palm, and it does not ring true the way my mother's does. Mine has a flaw in it — a hairline the bell-founder swore wasn't there when she cast it, which developed the first winter I carried it up toward the spire. It gives two notes, very close together, the second almost twenty seconds more honest than the first. Everyone in District Nine knows it. *Nearly, then truly,* my crew chief says when she hears it through fog. *That's him.*

I ring, and I announce myself, and I sit down in whatever the mountain has managed of a kitchen, and I begin to count the house aloud.

Four walls. One door. One door. One window, sweating. One stair, going nowhere with conviction. Four walls.

The house listens. That is not a metaphor the Finishers permit themselves before their second decade, and not one they bother denying after their fourth.

Sometimes a wall, encouraged, decides to bud. You can hear it in my early ear first — a deep, patient creaking twenty seconds before the sound reaches the rest of me, stone considering stone. So I have time. I ring again. I say, very politely: *We have walls. Four is traditional. Four is generous at this altitude.* I count them again. I name what loads they are already carrying, the roof that hasn't been thought of yet, the family that might one day quarrel in this room about the price of imported earth.

A good Finisher coaxes. A bad one argues, and ends up with a corridor that has been holding a grudge in its proportions for forty years.

Mostly what I do is sit and be a fact the house has to include. I eat my lunch very slowly. I let my echo arrive and finish arriving. I breathe where the air has pooled — you learn that fast up there, to read sparrows skimming the floorboards like a green light, to hold your breath when only the moths are moving, those great pale patient witnesses, beating their wings in emptiness as if they know the mountain is about to make more room for them. My left fingernail clouds. The new stone sweats. Somewhere below us both, the hammer falls, and falls in my ear again before it falls, twenty-two, twenty-two, until the whole raw plateau seems to be ticking in basalt.

If there is a fifteenth step by afternoon, I chalk it and do not climb it.

If a second door finishes dreaming itself in a wall where we had just agreed there would not be one, I ring my bell and count doors until the place settles — not always on my number. Finishers lose arguments more often than the Office reports say. Sessra is full of houses with one courteous extra closet that everyone pretends is for coats.

And if, now and then, from the room above that we have spent all week agreeing does not exist, a voice says something instructional in a voice very like my own — only earlier, only almost in step — then I stop counting out loud.

I listen with both ears, the late one and the early one.

I check whether it knows the bell-code.

It is learning. Year by year, house by house, I can tell it is learning. At first it only repeated. Then it anticipated, clumsily, the way I did as a child, answering before the question had put its coat on. Lately it waits, sometimes, through sixteen, seventeen of my counted seconds, as if it too is holding something behind its teeth.

I do not open the door that sort of patience earns. I am not a fool, and I was raised by Foundry people. I chalk it, and I ring twice — *nearly, then truly* — so that whatever is in there will have to work a little harder to be me, and a little harder still to be merely my echo.

Then I sit a while longer, until the house agrees, for now, on how many of us it contains.

I think that's why I stay. Not for the spire, not for the stories, though I've become one whether I wanted the honour or not. I stay for the exact quality of the dimness, which is soft enough to think in and hard enough that nothing — not grief, not joy, not a fresh black window with the weather already in it — ever gets its edges worn off. I stay because my body is a small, unfinished argument that a person can be made slightly ahead of himself and still learn courtesy. I stay because every few months, out near the northern rim, the moths gather thick over bare rock where no street has been surveyed, and the shepherds ring once as they pass, and a month later there might be a lane there, cobbled, waiting to be counted.

Someone has to be there when the mountain makes the next room. Someone whose left side lists half an inch toward the future, whose nail clouds when the stone starts sweating, who can hear the extra voice coming twenty seconds out and still has the discipline not to answer until it has learned to knock.

So I pack my chalk and my flawed bell and my thermos, and I count the stairs down out of a house that had fourteen of them this morning.

One. Two. Three.

I pause at fourteen, bell quiet in my hand, listening early.

And if there is no fifteenth today, I step out into the blue, submarine afternoon, close the one agreed door behind me — gently, from my side — and go home across the Thin Streets holding my breath, my footsteps arriving just ahead of me like something eager that I am politely, year by year, teaching to wait.
