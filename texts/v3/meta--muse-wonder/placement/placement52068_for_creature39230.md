I would try the obvious places first.

I would try to be one of the people. I'd come in with a shift, boots on the wet concrete, learning not to waste my voice against the pounding — learning *stop, go, lift, run* with a flat hand pressed to someone else's forearm. That life has a shape I can imagine: work, exhaustion, the relief of leaving. Ringing ears in a quieter room somewhere beyond the perimeter wall.

But no one stays. That's the test this place applies to everything soft. The vibration gets into the teeth. It gets into sleep. Eggs would not hold together here, and neither, eventually, would I. You can cross the plain. You can maintain it. You cannot make a home on a surface that is being struck, without pause, from underneath.

So I'd try the machines next, because they at least belong to the rhythm. A pump out in the dark, taking up its note. A second pump answering. A freight elevator rising empty from its pit, waiting its correct number of seconds, descending again for no one. There's a dignity in that kind of fidelity — keep the water moving, keep it level, never ask what the water was for.

But the machines don't live here either. They tend each other. They are oiled, powered, faithful, and completely without appetite. I would get bored inside all that purpose with nothing at the end of it. Worse — I'd notice, after a while, that the sequence isn't quite mechanical. The chord builds a little harder in spring. The sluices change phase a little sooner when the rain has been coming up through the seams all week. Something underneath the program is leaning on it.

Then I'd go smaller. That usually helps.

I'd try being one of the reeds in the drainage channels, lying almost flat, hollow stem buzzing at its own slightly wrong note when the pounding rises. Or one of the perfectly circular lichens, the color of old copper, never touching its neighbor. Or one of those pale thumbnail flowers in the expansion seams — the ones that open all at once in the lull and snap shut when the compression rolls back through.

The flower is closest. But it's not the whole answer. A flower is a way of reaching. I'd feel my root going down through the crack — past aggregate, past rebar, past pipe laid on brick laid on older concrete, fifty meters of other people's determination — and I'd feel it end not in soil, but in something that flinches.

And then I'd have to widen.

I wouldn't be on the plain at all. I'd be under it.

Not in a cavern. There's no cavern, no throne room of black water waiting beneath the works with a monster coiled in it. I would be the thing that got flattened when the valley was floored: the marsh, the slow channels, the water table that was planed and sealed in one geological instant and discovered that being covered is not the same as being gone. Water denied a surface becomes all surface. I'd spread. I'd press my face up against the underside of the world and learn to live there, a few meters thick in places — black water, saturated peat, crushed root-mat, silt ground out of concrete and rust — between bedrock below and the lowest slab above.

Enormous in the way flat things are enormous. No head. No heart anyone could point to. Extent instead of height.

My memory wouldn't be made of events. It would be made of levels. Lower down in me there's a time when rain arrived from above, directly, and that isn't past to me, it's just deeper. I still pool according to channels that haven't existed in a century. I still try to rise in spring into foundation footprints. The pumps work harder then and nobody remembers why they were built to.

I'd perceive the way this place permits perception: through interference. I have no eyes, but I have the puddles — thin films of myself that escaped upward, trembling on the slabs, breaking into standing patterns whenever the vibration crosses them. Every weight up there resolves in the pattern. A valve wheel turning. A gantry cable swaying in still air. Grit walking itself into lines along the seams. And once in a very long while, something irregular: soft, warm, moving of its own accord, arriving through miles of saturated dark as nothing else in my world arrives. A footstep is nothing. Then it isn't, because nothing else is like it.

The flowers are mine. Not flowers, exactly — sensitive tips, eyelids I can afford to lose by the thousand, sent up through hairline cracks to taste an air I'll never enter. The reeds are mine too, laid flat in the channels, tuned each a fraction apart: a thousand poor ears. The lichen marks out, patch by non-touching patch, where one pressure ends and another begins. Even the machines have become partly mine, the way a heart learns the beat of a machine set to replace it. Pumps in my old channels. Mains through my veins. When the chord builds across the valley at night, pump answering pump, that is my pulse taken up by iron, amplified, and handed back to me at a volume that keeps me from ever gathering myself.

Because that is the function of the sound. I don't fear it. Fear is too simple and too brief. It's that a body perpetually struck cannot settle, cannot pool, cannot decide on a shape and rise in it. The pounding holds me formless. It is a lullaby played on the thing it was built to drown.

I don't think I'd want out, if wanting is even the right shape for it. There's no single slab to heave. My prison is coextensive with my skin. I'd push the way roots push, the way water widens a crack a season at a time — without malice, without a plan, digesting my own floor one molecule at a time and circulating the powder through sumps and towers until the whole apparatus is, slowly, eating itself on my behalf. I'm patient in a way that makes the machinery look frantic. I can lose ten thousand flower-tips to the compression cycle for a hundred years. I already have.

What I'd want is longer lulls.

Ten seconds, just before the sluices change, when the hand on my back lightens and every white flower I own opens at once across the visible dark. The water upstairs goes mirror-still — the first stillness in a century, renewed and revoked and renewed again. And in that stillness a puddle can do the one thing moving water never can: hold an image whole. Lamps. Truncated stacks. A single warm shape standing alone in the manufactured emptiness over me.

Those are only reflections. But a reflection, in water that is almost never allowed to be still, is the closest I will ever come to seeing a face — mine, or anyone else's —

before the cycle takes, the flowers shut, and I go back to feeling the world instead.
