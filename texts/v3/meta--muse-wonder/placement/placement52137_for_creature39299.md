If I stayed long enough, you wouldn't find me where you'd look first.

Not on an island. Not standing on a mat, not wading the lanes, not up in the crooked trees where the weaver birds do their fine, fast work. The air would shine on my skin and then betray me — that first tightening, that leaf-vein splitting — and I'd already be rolling, shoulders down, slipping back under the roof.

You'd find me pressed up underneath it. In the green few feet.

That's the real country there: not the surface, which is politeness and spacing and mist, and not the true dark below, where the pillar trunks widen into chambers and the catfish nose blind along the bases. In between. Where the light is still barely alive before it fails, where a broad leaf overhead makes a ceiling the size of a doorway and the black water shows in hand's-widths between ceilings. My back to the light. My belly to the deep.

From above I would be nothing to notice. A tea-dark back, flattened, filmed, a few water-snails making their slow shining progress across my shoulder blades. A shadow where two leaves almost meet. If I slept too high, you might mistake me for weed. From below I'd be paler — fish-belly, drowned wood — and you'd see what the surface politely never suggests: that my limbs are too long, my fingers longer, webbed almost to the nail and jointed one time too many, so I can spread them into paddles or fold them narrow and harden them for work.

I think I'd be old enough for work to be most of what I am, and not yet old enough to be still.

My day — though there are no days, only the fog brightening a little overhead like a lamp behind cloth and then deepening again — my day would be tending. Nudging leaves apart from below with the backs of my fingers so the lanes stay open and the spacing stays even. Feeling along a terrace wall in complete darkness for a gap no eye would find, and coaxing it shut with green willow and a mouthful of peat, my teeth doing half the talking. They'd be worn down by now, broad front teeth grown and worn and grown again into dark, wood-like tablets. Biting willow. Stripping reed. Worrying root-fibre until it plies.

I'd hear almost nothing with my ears, because I wouldn't quite have ears — smooth pits where they closed generations ago. I'd hear with the rest of me. A palm flat against a pillar tree and the whole plateau conducting through it: water falling terrace to terrace to terrace, reeds sawing in the wind, ten thousand frogs pulsing until noise stops being noise and becomes pressure in the ribs. And under that, slower, patterned — someone tapping a hollow buttress three pools down. Someone else answering by going completely, grammatically still.

I'd talk that way. A knuckle on hollow wood. A heel drawn down a fused root so it thrums up through my jaw and sternum and the webs of my hands. More often, a held stillness beside somebody, which is a sentence if you've learned to breathe slowly enough to read it. My chest would be built for that patience. Deep ribs, from ancestors who first had to live in thin air, finished by the water. When I did rise — just a nose first, no ripple, sometimes only a mouth opening slowly into the mist — my exhalation would be a long fog that joins the general fog and is indistinguishable from weather.

I'd be cool. That's the thing a warm hand would never get used to. Not dead-cold, pool-cold, settled at the temperature of the place so that warmth itself becomes an event, a warning: fever, rot moving too fast, a mat baking in a rare clearing. I'd seek the cool layers and graze as I went. Snails off the shining roads on submerged wood. Glass shrimp cupped in a webbed hand. The soft heart of an unfolding leaf. Fish eggs, when season remembered to happen. Eel rarely, and with ceremony, because an eel is almost a relative — another long body threading the lattice.

I was born down there, probably. Underwater, in a hollow chamber of a pillar tree, fish turning in pale schools around the nest and cleaning what needed cleaning, my gill-slits fluttering a few days behind my jaw before they sealed into hearing. I learned weaving from my mothers — several of them, not all of them, by any outside accounting, my kind — and from watching weaver birds, and really from neither, because my fingers knew the braid before anyone taught it. Memory here isn't story. The noise wears a story down in a generation, flattens it into background. Memory is a lane left open where nothing needs to pass, because something passed ten thousand years ago. My hands remember it anyway.

And time, for me, would be even more than long. I'd be living at the speed of wall and leaf, where a terrace subsides a finger's width in a century and grows it back in the same century. A decision begun by a grandmother gets tapped through a pillar for twenty years and finished by grandchildren who think it was always so. I'd be in the middle of one of those decisions now, perhaps. Still arguing, gently, with my heel on a root.

Every so often a turtle would find me — one particular turtle, its shell carrying a garden I'd crop with my fingers when it passed, as it has passed for years. Every so often I'd hold a mat steady from below for something heavy crossing above, spreading a cool palm under the weave beneath its foot, and feel its startled warmth through the fibres.

At the far end of me — not feared, not hurried, just agreed — my fingers have already started. Fine hairs at the knuckles thickening in sleep. Rootlets, taking hold a little too eagerly when I rest in the weave. I'd wake more and more slowly. One season, which is to say one slow agreement, I'd swim out to a terrace lip, turn my face into the braiding, and let the place finish what my hands started. Within somebody else's lifetime there'd be another buttress where my ribcage was, another hollow where children and fish turn in the dark, listening to frogs through my walls.

Until then: just under the leaves. Back in the green dusk, face in the black water between ceilings, doing the endless, unseen spacing with my hands, hearing the whole high, falling, perfectly still sea through my bones.
