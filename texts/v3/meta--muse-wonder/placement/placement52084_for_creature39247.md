At first I think I would be at the Tearing.

That is the obvious place to put yourself, isn't it — at the safe distance, scratched fresh that morning on the tally stick, children up on shoulders, all of us in a loose line watching the valley make more of itself. Dust sighing out of a crack. That underwater sound of lake-ice breaking inside the earth. Then the block settling, the boom in your sternum, bats bursting out of a fissure that wasn't there an hour before, white crickets falling *up* on the heat.

It's dramatic. It gives you a role: witness.

But I don't think I'd stay there. Not because I wouldn't watch — everyone watches, sometimes — but because watching is not a job in the Low, and I can feel, the longer I stay in it, that I would not be one of the people who can afford to be mostly a witness. I'd be drawn back down the floor, away from the edge of the new, toward the noisy, crowded middle where things have been alive for a whole week and are therefore almost familiar.

I'd be somewhere near the Wet Seam. Not on it. Never on a black mat, never where the crust looks thick enough until you put a child's weight on it. Up on a raised plate a little way off, high enough that the frogs are loud but not so loud they take your sleep apart, close enough to a seep that I can hear it bubbling steadily through the night. That bubbling would matter to me more than the view. It would be the sound I set the camp by. If it catches — if it starts to hitch at the top the way a vent does when it's building pressure — I'd want to be the one awake enough to notice.

I think that's who I would be. Not the strongest walker, at first. Not a great hunter. Someone with an ear that gets educated faster than the rest of him.

On the plain I might have been unremarkable, but down here I start to be able to separate things. The returning river of bats at dawn from the crickets shuffling under their overhang. Cricket-silence that means a snake from cricket-silence that means a wall is about to think about falling. A pool gone too still. I learn it the way the children do, before I have language for all of it, by going suddenly still in the middle of doing something else and not knowing, for half a breath, why I stopped.

My feet would change before my opinions did. That seems true. The fine white scars from basalt and fresh obsidian, the soles thickening, the toes learning to test a stone for whether it is locked or singing loose while my eyes stay up on the wall. I would get the permanent squint. The crow's feet early. I would work with my face turned a little aside at midday, because midday in the Low is not for looking directly at things — the light comes straight down all at once, white off unweathered stone and wet mineral and wings, and for four hours you mostly listen and endure and wait under hide while everything else bellows and mates and feeds. Dawn and dusk would become my tender hours. That's when I'd read cracks darkening, mats shifting from orange to milky to the bad black, a wall beading with condensation where yesterday it was dry.

My wealth would not look like wealth upland. A good fiber net, mended and remended, that I know how to stretch across a narrowing at dusk where the bat-river pours through, and take down heavy before midnight, fast, in the dark, while wings rain squeaking around my shoulders. A bone hook worn smooth. A scraper I knapped myself from a fresh fall and will leave behind without a thought when we move. Maybe — if I am lucky and a little vain — one metal thing kept against my body, not in the camp. A knife, a ring. Something I would not have to choose between saving and running.

And I *would* run. That's part of the shape of the life, not an interruption of it. The crickets go quiet in a patch twenty meters across. Dust lifts off the wall in that fine, thoughtful veil. Without deciding to, I'd already have the sleeping mat up by its corners, dirt kicked over the fire, a water skin over my shoulder. I'd go up the nearest scree — not the path, never the path, paths belong to yesterday's valley — and I'd wait with the dust in my teeth and my heart slamming, and then, almost embarrassingly soon, I'd be filtering back down, laughing at something somebody said on the way up, to see what the fall had made for us. A new overhang. A cracked seep. A bright plate of stone split clean enough to work.

I'd eat catastrophe and not find that morbid. Skimmed insect drifts pounded into oily mash. Blind white fish cupped up gently in warm water, half-apologetic, the heron watching six feet away for what my hands stir up. Bats from the net. Once in a while something enormous and improbable from above that feeds three camps and leaves us with gut-string and stories we will exaggerate until the upland cousins don't believe any of it, which will only make us tell it better.

I don't think I'd have a grand name for myself. That's the thing that keeps settling, the longer I imagine living there. Uplanders would call me a Low person and mean it as a diagnosis. Down here I'd say: we're the ones by the Wet Seam this month. We were at the Dry Pan when that vent opened. I carried these roof-poles through the last three moves with the same person, so, yes, that counts. My identity would be as provisional — and as real — as a cairn. It marks where I was. It doesn't promise I'll be there next season.

In twenty years, if seeds ever forgive the hot mineral ground, if the edges soften and the flood of creatures thins into something you could call a population, maybe my grandchildren will build heavier and stay put and think my life sounds frantic. For now I'd live at the pace the floor sets: attentive, unsolemn, a little grey with rift dust by evening no matter how I started the day, gambling knapped shards on when a mud pot will boil dry, listening with my whole body to ground that heaves a hand's span in a bad week.

Not waiting for the New Low to finish.
Deciding with it, daily.
Back down the scree as soon as the dust settles, barefoot, to be first to whatever new thing it has made.
