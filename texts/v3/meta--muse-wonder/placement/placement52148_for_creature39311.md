I would come down the steps the way anyone does — sideways where I have to, a palm out for balance — and at first I would mistake the problem. I'd think I needed to find a place to *stand*.

I try the flat-topped stones above the deepest water. My feet find the ledges worn glassy under the surface, and for a while that's enough. Warm up to the ankles, then to the calves. Mist at my shoulders. A drip *tick*ing behind me into a pool I can't see without turning, and turning means negotiating again: hip against an ochre column, shoulder eased past another, the mineral skin slick and faintly warm.

But those standing places aren't mine. Someone squared them off with hammerstones, someone whose body already knows this bowl. And the keeper is already doing the standing — upright in the waist-deep basin near the centre, wedged gently between two pillars, becoming indistinguishable from them. One warmer pillar among hundreds is enough. If I stayed there long enough beside him, I'd learn the same slow arithmetic: wrinkle, seal, crust, stillen. I can feel the temptation of it. Nothing here rushes toward decay. Nothing here asks you to go anywhere else.

So I don't stay upright.

I'm not the heron, either. I lingered for a while, in my mind, at the northern pool where the light falls best — the widest water, the only place you can almost see across. But that's a visitor's place, and a bewildered one. Too tall for the gaps, knees all wrong, finding almost nothing to eat. I'd stand there conspicuously, hoping the bowl was a pond, and it isn't a pond. It's a balance.

I'm not the snail, though I envy it a little: the silver track below a waterline, weeks spent crossing one column's submerged flank, certain of its surface in a way no wading thing can be. But a snail leaves a legible line, and gets eaten whole now and then for its trouble. I don't think I'd leave a track.

It's when I stop trying to stand that I find where I actually am. Lower. The water gets more honest as it gets deeper — ankle, waist, and then the basins, hollowed out, where the current-that-is-not-a-current barely moves at all. Under the central overhang, where the perpetual condensation rains back in its slow circle and the light comes down in one dim shaft and dissolves fifteen feet out. The deepest basin. The stillest.

Down there, almost at the bottom of noticing, is a crack in the stone.

That's me.

One of the tiny pale fish, no longer than a fingernail, that flicker there and vanish. Less an inhabitant, the place would tell you, than an exception the mineral water tolerates. I think that wording is exactly right. I wouldn't belong to the bowl the way the columns do, or the way he increasingly does. I would be tolerated by it — which, in a place that admits almost nothing living, is a kind of belonging all its own.

I would live mostly in the crack, where he cannot follow. That's not a small thing. His whole life is measured in fifteen visible feet, in gaps just wide enough to turn sideways through; the crack is a country past the limit of his turning. In there it's darker than the gloom, narrower than any passage above, and the stone is unencrusted in a way the open pillars never are — because I move in it, because other small bodies have moved in it, because a flicker now and then keeps the deposit from completing itself. He will finish by stopping. I persist by never quite being still in the one place stillness can't reach.

My life would be brief motions between long hidings. Out along the basin floor when the film drifts down from the warmest pools in broken flakes. Out when an insect from the plain above blunders through the mist and drowns in the central pool and the disturbance reaches me as a thickening in the water — his meal, mostly, but the rumour of it is mine. I eat what settles. I leave the surface to him.

We would know each other the way everything down here knows anything: not by sight. His eyes are filmed like glass left in steam; mine, in the permanent grey-green, would be mostly ornamental. But when he lays his cheek to a column and listens, he'd feel my movement as a pressure through stone and pool. And when his fingertip taps a wet pillar — that soft *tick* answered by dripping — I'd feel it in the crack as a small weather. He leaves me alone, whether from indifference or some older knowledge. I'd return the courtesy. When he's still long enough overhead, a shape indistinguishable from the ring of tallest columns, I might cross the open basin in one pale flicker, just at the edge of the shaft of light, and be gone before the mist finished deciding it had seen something.

Because that's the other part of it: he watches the fish the way he watches the drips fall. Not as food. As proof that something else can still move in cracks he cannot enter. I would be that proof, and I would understand the job. In a place settled so completely into its account — springs giving, the throat under the southern wall taking precisely as much, every drip grooved into its lip and dimpled into its stone — I would be the tiny unsettled remainder. The thing that doesn't add up to stone.

I'd sleep wedged where the water is warmest and the crack holds a faint pull toward the deep. I'd wake already wet, already at the temperature of everything around me, no edge to waking — in that, at least, we'd be alike. Rain on the plain above would reach me as a muffled rushing and then as nothing; the level wouldn't rise. A year could pass. The same drip would fall into the same dimple between the same two columns, and below it, in the deepest basin, something fingernail-small and paler than the film on the pools would stir, catch what light there is, and slip back into the dark it fits.

Not a pillar. Not becoming one. Just the quick, tolerated flicker at the bottom of the bowl — alive precisely because there's so little room for it, and persisting in exactly that little.
