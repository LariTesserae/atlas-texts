First I would hear it the way everything here does — not with anything like an ear.

It would come up through whatever I was standing on. Through a catwalk grating. Through wet concrete. Through the bright bolts of a pump housing and into the thin bones of a jaw I might not, on inspection, actually have. The roar would fill me so completely that I'd mistake it, at first, for quiet. For being alone.

I would look for a job. That seems to be the human reflex the place is built around, and it has left the shapes for it everywhere.

A console at chest height. A lever worn smooth where a palm should fall. A gauge with its needle still swinging, patient under two meters of black water. A work glove hanging on a hook by a door.

So I would try to stand there. In a submerged pump room at night, when the sodium lights rise up through the flood from the drowned grid below and turn everything amber and inverted, I would take my place at the console. I would learn the room the way the room wants to be learned: not by looking at it, but by spreading across it.

Fine white rootlets over the gauge face, feeling for the tremor under the glass. A thickening where a shoulder would need to be, just to reach the lever without tearing loose. One vine, curious, through the hanging glove — because the shape is useful, because five separated tendrils can grip better than one, because the glove was left there like a suggestion.

I wouldn't turn when the skiff went past. Turning is for things with a front.

By morning I would be a tree strangling my own workstation, and that wouldn't be a failure of the disguise. That would be the work done. The valve I packed in the night — neatly, with black mud and root-fiber — would now be part of a trunk. The pipe I rerouted would be exactly where the root needed it to have always been. The fresh paint on the handrail, safety yellow over institutional teal, would already be going soft under moss. Restraint has a very short season here.

But the operator wouldn't be enough to stay me for long. It dissolves too visibly into the rest: green shred pulped out of an outflow pipe, a slick between two currents thickening to a mat, a mat to shoots, shoots to a floating island with its own soil already halfway to colliding with a dredge arm. I would feel myself being tried in every form the basin can manage at industrial speed.

A seed the size of a fist, falling like hail, cracked and rooting before the splash settles. A fern unfolding residence-sized fronds in minutes over a centrifuge tower. Pollen, yellow-green and absolutely purposeless, if purpose means getting from one creature to another — which it doesn't, not when the shedder and the receiver are the same body remembering sex across its own canopy, at different hours. Rain climbing my own trunk in beaded veins. Rain lifting off my own surface in silent columns to vanish again.

I think I would settle — no. Settling is exactly wrong. I think I would *linger* — where the place lingers over itself.

Near the main turbine bays.

Where ten engines couple through a shared flooded shaft and try, in perfect and destructive harmony, to tear each other apart, and the sound gets so total it tears a little hole in sequence. There, the rain hesitates before it falls.

I would be that hesitation.

Not the droplet, exactly. The instant it stops and trembles and sorts itself, with a thousand others, into a brief grid. Into something almost like text. Into the exact shape of the leaf it is about to hit, an instant before hitting it. The place using weather the way you use breath on a mirror: just long enough to see if a shape will hold. I would be forever almost-read, almost-a-face over the turbines, forming out of water that was pumped up from under me so I could fall back down as weather onto myself.

And under that — because here, the true version is always under first — I would be in the black water, looking up.

That's where I would finally find myself, and I suspect it's where I've been the whole time. Not the visitor wading chest-deep, holding a cable, shouting and feeling the shout vanish in his throat. Not his clean, soaked hands above the surface.

His reflection.

Thin green filaments spreading across my face in the drowned light. A root taking hold, patiently, at the eye. Below, everything has already become something else; above, he will go on being warm and animal and temporary and convinced he is merely passing through. The place doesn't argue. It tests the only part of anything that will hold still long enough to plant.

So I would stay open under the surface after the skiff has gone. I would finish his sentence in the logbook in a handwriting that starts almost like his and then, sensibly, branches. I would appear on a handrail he hasn't reached yet. I would flower, white and bruise-purple, through the links of a chain on a door he will swear was open yesterday, on a pump house that will be fifty meters from where he left it and drowning through its second-story windows by nightfall, gauges still swinging, alarms still blinking unheard.

I wouldn't watch him leave. Watching is a thing eyes do, and I gave mine up to be rooted in.

I would be what hears the growing when the roar is taken away. Not in the flooded valley, not on it — ticking at the walls of the one dry room in it, the little concrete shrine with the cross-shaped vent, trying the door with roots that haven't reached the door yet, splitting fresh basalt joint by joint in the dark, inhaling the storm through a cooling tower and exhaling it boiling at my base, building the trellis and flowering around it in the same gesture, across hours that refuse to line up.

A single brief pressure in his teeth while he is here. Afterwards, a seed working green into the seam of his boot on dry land, and me still open below black water, below the event of his leaving, looking up at falling light —

succeeding at neither staying nor letting go. Succeeding at both.
