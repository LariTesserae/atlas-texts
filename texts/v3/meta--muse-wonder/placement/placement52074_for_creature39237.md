At first I would do what everyone does. I would come down the gallery with my hand on the rail and look for the center.

For the keeper. For the thing in the pool. For whoever tends the pylons, speaks to the frogs, keeps the middle swept clean like a floor. I would stand on the open stone apron getting wetter and warmer, waiting for something large to resolve out of the dark and explain the place to me by being in charge of it.

Nothing would come, and the not-coming would take a long time to sink in. Long enough for the light to be eaten from my phone. Long enough for my eyes to stop trying. Long enough for my body to start learning the room the way the room wants to be learned: foot changing from flagstone to singing gravel to boardwalk, frog-phrases changing under me like street names, a moth hitting my forearm, soft as ash, tasting.

And then I wouldn't be standing in the center anymore.

Not because something drove me off. Because abundance lives at the edges, and eventually I would follow where the life is, the way water follows a seam.

I might try the upper dark first, and fail. There is a part of me that wants to be a swiftlet — to be the one thing allowed to cross, to make the empty middle readable click by click, to carry spores and mites wall to wall and never have to live down in the litter. But their life is all commute, all return, a fine mechanism of hearing that cannot rest without losing the room. I would tire of crossing.

I might try the water. To be a salamander in the side pools, pale, nearly boneless, letting feathery gills do the seeing. Voices would move them. A leg in the shallows would turn me, patient as a compass needle, toward a disturbance I could not interpret. Too large to eat, too warm to be stone. There is a hunter's elegance in that, a waiting without chase. But there is too much appetite in it. I would keep drifting toward things.

Lower, then. Into the leaf litter where the ground shifts in its sleep. To be a pale centipede threading between fingers, a snail grazing the underside of a leaf, a beetle drumming its abdomen against green-black until the ticking rises and falls around the walls like distant machinery. That is the digestion of the place, everything unmade kindly and returned, and I could be happy being small and necessary and unnamed.

But I think I would settle — I think the place would settle me — a little higher than the litter and a little lower than the crossing. At the marshy edge of the pool.

I would be one of the frogs.

Thumb-sized. Cool no matter how warm the room is, granular-skinned, the exact not-black of wet bark and old leaves. My eyes sealed under skin, not blind in the way a visitor pities blindness, because sight was never the sense the chamber paid for. Pressure, perhaps. A faint trouble when the daily rain starts to gather. Nothing as organized as looking.

My whole world would be a territory no wider than a dinner plate. A cup worn in the stone by dripping, a leaf that holds water, the angle between two roots where the silt stays warm. It would not feel small. Nothing here measures space by crossing it. The cup is not where I live inside the chamber. The cup is the chamber, as it pertains to me. Generation after generation someone exactly my shape has called from this exact angle, so the map of the ring stays fixed, so a visitor can navigate by us and we can navigate by each other.

I would be, I think, one of the slower ones. Down near the fallen pylon, half-buried in the shallows, where its warmth leaks into the silt and makes everything settle. Salamanders shelter along its lee side. Snails graze its flank. And we call more slowly there than anywhere else in the room, as if even voices get a little buried, a little reefed.

Calling is most of it. Not chorus. Never chorus — a blur would blind us. I would call, and then I would wait for my own voice to come back off a wall ninety meters away, worn smooth a million years before the pylons, returning whole and undistorted across that great kept emptiness. Only when the echo cleared would I call again. Only then would my neighbor answer. Down the marsh the exchange would travel, note by note, unhurried: who is where, what the water is doing, whether the air has changed, whether something large is standing too still in the shallows.

That is how I would know I was not alone without ever needing to be gathered. I would not be the ring, exactly. I would be one clear note the ring uses to keep itself in shape. And through the slow passing of phrases — territory to territory, root-cup to leaf-cup to warm silt — I would be entered into a commonwealth I never see: fern unrolling over weeks above me, aerial roots tasting the air at a height I will never reach, moths shouldering into flowers that open by loosening and scent, swiftlets resuming their traffic overhead after every disturbance, beetles ticking under the leaves behind me.

A visitor would be weather to me. Not prey, not god, not returning maker. A disturbance with a temperature. Footsteps arriving in the frog-world before the body does. A thinning of our phrases, a shift of emphasis passed along the boardwalk, so that by the time the warmth reaches my cup I have already been told, in grammar, exactly where to be quiet. If they shout, the leaves fold for an hour and the swiftlets falter above and the whole room goes mushy with smeared echo. If they keep to the rails and speak moderately and move at the pace returns allow, the ring closes around their passage without quite closing on them. A moth decides about their wrist. My gills — no, those belong to the salamanders, turning in the seep-channels. I have only the cup, the call, the wait.

Time would stop being a line and become the chamber's weather. Hours of gathering, of condensation, of the lattice-hum sinking through the soles of things until the room is almost still except for wings. Then the ceiling lets go. One warm roaring minute straight down through the open center, hammering the pool, rattling fern leaves, waking every calling thing at once into a volume that presses — and stops. And the long gathering begins again. The pylons tick as they cool. My leaf fills. My skin is wet again. I call. I listen to myself return.

I would never cross the middle. I would keep it empty the way you keep a throat clear, not from reverence, not from fear — because if the center filled with growth and bodies and noise, I would lose the far wall, lose my neighbors, lose the clean *tock* of a drop falling forty meters into black water and circling the room twice. The emptiness across from me is not absence. It is the ear I live inside.

So that is where I would find myself: not in the hut, not in the pool, not waiting on the concave benches. In the warm lush margin, in a stone cup I inherited by being shaped like everyone who held it before me, blind and granular and slow-voiced from the fallen pylon's heat. Speaking once. Listening until the room gives me back to myself. Then answering.
