I don't think I would arrive from the plateau.

The traders do that — come down the long ramps squinting against the second sun coming up off the floor, complain about the silence and the cold sitting in their bedrolls, leave two nights later with headaches. I would be bad at leaving. And I would be bad at the plateau to begin with: too exposed up there, too much wind with a voice in it, nothing rounded close enough to put a hand on.

Coming down, the first thing that would go for me is the view. Everyone talks about the view from the rim — the archipelago of districts, the cook-fires, the mirror-flats between. But at noon in the basin sight is a rumor. Light from above, light from below, eyelashes stiffening with salt, that thin useful tearing that never quite clears anything. I would learn the way the old do, early: go white by degrees, stop arguing with it. Judge by foot. By palm. By the faint springiness under a threshold that says a floor has decided something in the night.

So not a water-warden, striding the flats. Not a swift either, though I envy them at first — those birds who never have to leave the cup, stitching the bright air, fed entirely by what rises off the morning pools. Too much sky in that life. Too little contact. I notice I keep drifting, in my mind, toward the walls. Toward doorways, where people pause without knowing they pause. Toward the hand rested on plaster as you pass, checking a house the way you'd steady a living animal.

Closer. Lower. Quieter than the human quiet, which isn't quiet at all once you learn the hands — whole arguments roaring across a flat at mid-morning, fingers stabbing, children translating obscenities behind their own hands. I want the other register. The close one. The one under that.

It turns out I want the crack.

Find me, then, in the one wall in the house that is never finished. Every sleeping room has one: unplastered, gypsum block left rough and full of holes on purpose, because a house without geckos gets flies, and a house that gets flies gets fever. I am the reason the wall is left open. I am also, in a way that nobody here finds contradictory, named for it — or for the shelf above the bed, or for the grandmother whose habit of sitting motionless through whole conversations I am said to repeat. The basin names its geckos after ancestors. I have inherited a temperament, apparently. Watchful. Unimpressed. Exactly the color of the stone I live in.

I am the length of a hand. Pale to the point of being a rumor against pale. From the plateau I would be impossible to see; from across the room I am mostly a throat, pulsing, and two eyes that do not tear and do not film. I do my seeing from six inches away and from directly overhead. I walk the ceiling. I know the house the way its people know the town — not by looking at it but by the temperature of it under my belly. The floor sweats cold through the wall in the morning. At night I feel the salt begin its patient work somewhere deep in the block, a pressure so slow it is less a sound than a change in how the stone holds me. Days before the family lays a cheek to the plaster, I have moved my sleeping hole two hand-widths to the left.

My district is not large and does not need to be. The house stands on the north side of a reliable cistern, in a cluster of forty houses holding tight to damp that beads nine months of the year. Outside, four hundred meters of mirror-flat that no one builds on, where the devils process at midday smelling of salt and cold stone. Inside: the cistern-mouth's orange fungus three rooms away, scraped with bone knives every third morning; the iron-and-cellar smell of the mats; brine flies rising off the morning pool in a grain that hangs in the air. My whole geography is a wall, a ceiling, a shelf, the crack behind the stores where beetles work the mat edges in lines. The swifts own the cup. The children own the flats, running just ahead of the dust devils with gull-timing. I own the vertical dark between plaster and block, and I am owned by it, and by the hands that re-round it.

Because I am taken apart too. When the house is reset — and it will be; nothing here is excused — the roof-stones come off one by one into the children's arms, the walls are coaxed down, and I retreat with the others, complaining in soft chirps, to the neighbor's gecko-wall to wait. Nobody thinks this is exile. It is housekeeping at the scale of geology. I wait out the re-laying of the floor over fresh silt. I wait until the new holes are left, deliberately, unplastered. Then I am invited back simply by the house becoming habitable for me again, which is the only kind of invitation this town fully trusts.

What do I do for them, in return for the wall? I eat the fever before it assembles. I take flies off the air of the sleeping room one by one, all night, in a silence so complete the old woman who can no longer see counts my chirps the way she counts everything now — by ear, by hand, by memory of channels that silted before her grandchildren were born. She navigates to bed by my wall's holes. If I go quiet too early in the evening, someone will come and look, close, in the low voice meant for arm's reach. Not from sentimentality, or not only. A gecko that stops hunting is information.

That, I think, is the shape of belonging I would grow into here: small, daily, load-bearing in a way invisible from the rim. Not the permanence of a monument — the basin corrodes bronze in a season and heaves its own floors for sport. The permanence of being expected. Of a hole left open in every rebuilding. The town endures by being taken apart every morning and put back, rounded, weighted, level for now — and somewhere in a lower street, in a house by a cistern that will bead again by dawn, a gypsum-colored throat pulses in a crack that was made on purpose, watching the room, keeping it.

That is where I live.
