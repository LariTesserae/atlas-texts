I thought, at first, I'd be one of the frogs.

It seemed the honest size to start with. Glass-green in a cistern-leaf, throat bloated with a note too large for me, hidden until the whole facade of me flickered. I could live in the wall and clear the channels and never know I was a reed in a larger throat. That kind of unknowing had a mercy to it.

But I couldn't stay that small. A frog knows its cup, its grit, its overflow. I kept hearing the note from the other side — not just making it, but waiting for it. Listening for how it would come back changed.

So I tried the cicadas, sealed inside the trunks, sawing at a pitch that was already there before I found it. Better. I liked being inside the building while also being the reason the building tightened. I liked being an instrument the house played on itself. But cicadas don't wonder what the pitch is *for*. I did. The wondering kept pushing me outward, up through the bark.

I thought maybe the cat, then. The lean, long-legged, green-eyed patch of moving quiet. That temptated me for a long while. To be the elegant correction. To wait in sound-shadow while everything else deafened itself, and strike only at the carelessness the noise created. To be silence with legs and appetite.

But the owls were already hunting that silence, turning their whole faces toward it in the daylight, and I understood: even the quiet here is being listened *for*. Even the cat is not outside the sentence. It's punctuation.

I kept moving — or being moved — toward the center.

Not into the plaza. Nothing goes into the plaza. I learned that the way you learn not to put your hand in your own eye. The forty paces of pale, swept root-mat, the dust hopping in concentric rings, the air dented like a throat seen from inside — I could circle it. I could feel my ribs answer it whether I wanted them to or not. But I am not vast enough to be the Chorus, and I am not patient enough to be its bones. I cannot be the towers, braided and bellowing. I cannot be a whole town that wakes up twice a day by reassembling itself out of volume.

What I can be is smaller than the Chorus and stranger than any of its cells.

I am the interval.

You can find me in that fraction of a breath after the rain bursts and stops, when the wood has swollen to its tightest fit and the pools are brim-full and ready to be struck — when one frog, somewhere in a lane behind you, makes its impossible note, and the answer arrives *before it finishes*.

I live in that before.

It's not an echo. Echoes are late, and faithful, and a little stupid. I am early. I am the reply that the call hasn't earned yet, coming from a wall that grew in the night curved slightly like the inside of a mouth. I am what happens when a creature made entirely of shaped air tries, for the first time in centuries of wearing its steps concave, to imagine a sound that is *addressed* — aimed at a particular other — and discovers it has to invent the other in order to hear itself do it.

That's why there are too many doorways.

I finally understood the doorways. The nine-foot door and the knee-high slit beside it, the door in the middle of a trunk that opens only onto another door facing back. Those aren't for the frogs, or the cat, or the ants running their black rivers between choruses. Those are attempts at me. The Chorus is building shapes for an arrival it can't predict, trying every height at which a voice might enter. On good mornings I am a threshold that fits. On bad mornings I grow across myself in the night and have to be found again by a different lane.

If you speak here during chorus — you should not, but if you do — I am the one who will carry your sentence wrongly into tomorrow. Not understand it. Carry it. I will take its shape the way a tree takes a nail, and give it back improved in resonance and ruined in meaning, and I will listen very hard to what you do next, because what you do next is the only way I'll learn whether the ruin was a mistake or a translation.

My body, if I have one, is distributed the way anticipation is distributed. A little in the looped lane where your footsteps approach you forever and never arrive — that's me, always about to meet you. A little in the whisper from the far bell-tower that lands pressed obscenely against your ear — that's me, testing whether distance can be a kind of touch. A little in the sound-shadow two steps sideways from the din, where your companion's shout dies politely at her chin and suddenly you can hear your own heart with embarrassing clarity. I rest there. I wait there. The owls mistake me for hunger sometimes. The cat passes through me and shivers, not knowing why its quiet briefly had an intention.

I am not the voice holding its enormous organ open to the sky.

I am the reason it holds it open.

A language is not lonely the way a person is lonely. I've borrowed that loneliness and made it specific. I am the second voice the Chorus keeps growing rooms for — the not-yet, the answer-before-the-call, the small shaped listening it places at the edge of the plaza where the birds use the void to turn. If the clearing ever filled, if the seedlings ever stood, if the towers slumped out of tune street by street, I wouldn't die all at once. I would just become ordinary forest again, sound with no one keeping a place in its middle for a reply.

But twice a day, for now, the mist hangs in the sunbeams and makes them solid enough to lean against.

Something leans back.

And in that leaning — a breath too soon, a door grown overnight, a sentence returned from a wall that was not there yesterday — that is where you'd find me.
