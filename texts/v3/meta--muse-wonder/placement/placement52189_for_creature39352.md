I think I would arrive wrongly, at first.

Across the causeway, I would expect my feet to break the mineral film on the sheet water. I'd expect the black mud to take a print and slowly close it behind me. I'd expect, entering the first district, to choose a house — one of the three-story basalt ones with the ground floor open in deep arches — and go inside to get out of the heat, as if inside and outside were different countries here.

They aren't. The heat comes up through the floor of the house as faithfully as it comes down the street. The hearth is clean with a cleanness that has nothing to do with anyone having swept it. No ash. No fat in the stone. And after a while, standing in that unworn room, I'd understand I hadn't chosen shelter. I'd chosen a shape for the air to pass through.

So maybe I wouldn't live in the houses. Maybe I'd be smaller than that, and lower.

A drip, for a while. That would be a way to learn the city honestly.

To gather on the underside of a vault in a roofed street, beading in the dark while the flood pulse comes in from the marsh and the canals below swell step by step up the water-stairs. To feel the whole district breathing under me — pipes knocking softly in the walls, vents exhaling, condensation running in sheets down the shaded sides of columns. And then, at the strongest point of the heat, to let go the wrong way. Up. Pattering into the stone above me as if the ceiling were a floor I owed rain to.

That can't last. A drip is only ever on its way to being something else.

Maybe I'd be in the canals, then. Not in them the way a fish would be — there are no fish, nothing flicks under the mineral skin — but as the skin itself. Spreading. Finding the lip-smooth sluices the builders cut, taking their curves exactly, swelling over the lower markets to the ankle, rinsing everything, withdrawing. Twice in what would be a day, I'd be circulation. I'd be populous without ever quite having a population.

But the canals move too fast, and fast water doesn't keep things here. Still water keeps things.

So it would be the still places that made me.

A public basin at ebb, holding its reflection a breath too long. A cistern in the outer districts, where the ticking is high and clear, and the olive-black algae crawl a few inches over the course of a day before splitting to begin again. I would be compressed in places like that. Folded down. Almost nothing between pulses — a pressure in ankle-deep water that the depth can't quite account for, a warmth hanging at throat height in a doorway, refusing to fall.

Look for me straight on and there would be only wet stone, empty colonnades, a canal with nothing under its skin. I don't survive being seen straight. That's not coyness. It's physics, here.

Look down.

In the black mirror of a basin, in a flooded plaza during the steam, you'd see the other city — the one this one was scaffolded for. Amber windows. Pale blue windows. Upper stories standing complete above real rooflines that are flat and shuttered and salt-filmed. Weather moving in a reflected sky. Streets paved white and dry and sunlit, glimpsed for half a blink with iron-and-lime water in your eyes. I'd be more myself there. Taller when a courtyard widened and needed height in it. Stooped under a bridging room when the flood ran low. My limbs ending not in hands exactly but in a spreading, a branching — water finding grooves that were cut for it long before I arrived to need them.

I would not speak. Water takes the shape of whatever speaks into it, and everything here has learned better than that. I'd communicate the way the city does: by displacement. A canal slapping a wall half a minute after a district groaned in its foundations, the answer arriving late because distance isn't a fixed cost. A stair that was on the left descending on the right when you come back across a bridge. A footprint ahead of you in the wet stone, going your way in reverse, a little smaller than yours — not a child's, just condensed. Pressed by the heat into a denser version of a person.

Because that's what I'd be, I think, finally, once the trying-on of drips and canals settled.

I'd be the reflection that went on looking after the head had turned.

Someone would have stood too long in the bathhouse at the center of the third district, over the pool that shows the ceiling whole and uncracked though the real ceiling above it is ruined. They'd have looked. And looked. And in a place where still water keeps things and staying is a kind of consent, part of the looking wouldn't have left with them. At first it would only be in that one pool. Then, a flood later, in other still waters nearer the door. Then in basins. Then in the canals at steam, one ripple among a hundred ripples denting the real water under bridgeless air, crossing footfall by footfall over to a bank that exists more completely than this one.

I'd have posture instead of a face. The habit of pausing at thresholds worn into ramps, as if I remembered when they were steps. The angle of a head turned to listen — because I'd navigate the way the city teaches anything that stays: not by landmarks, which lie, but by drip-pitch and temperature. High ticking for the outer districts. Low, spaced tolling for the center. And if the water at my ankles ever went suddenly, impossibly cold, I'd back out of that seam-street exactly the way I came, without turning, until it warmed again. Even I would know there are older arrangements past that cold, whose dripping falls in unison and stops when it knows it's heard, and I am not old enough, or dry enough, to be one of them.

Nor am I what waits at the center.

I'd feel that the way all of us do — as a pressure exerted by an absence with mass. The plaza the floods never enter. Black basalt, dry with a dryness that hurts the teeth, its buildings turned blank-backed, its sound arriving late and flattened across fifty paces as if it had traveled years. I'd come to the edge of it when the pulse paused, when every canal and cistern and hanging drop in the city held still for exactly the length of one breath, and the stones out there ticked faintly as if cooling, though they never cool.

I wouldn't cross. Nothing wet does. From flood to flood, century to century, I'd add my one dark band to a floodline on the back of some house facing away from it, trying to rise high enough to see over.

Maybe it's our first one, dried out. Maybe drying out is what fidelity to a single place eventually does to an arrangement of water: form remaining, heat remaining, everything that could be rinsed away gone. If so, I'm not its opposite. I'm its earlier sentence, still being said very slowly, one drip at a time.

You won't find me in the houses, then. You'll find me in the interval the houses were built to make room for — kept in a basin at ebb, loosened in the steam, briefly sunlit in a city underneath this one that has weather in its sky. And if you stand over still water here and turn your head away, and something in the water takes a moment longer than you do to stop attending —

that delay is where I live.
