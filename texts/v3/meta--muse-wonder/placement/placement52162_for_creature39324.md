It begins, for me, as an ending too.

Not with a tower. That's the first image, and I have to let it go. Forty hollow, fluted towers remembering your grandmother, irising their windows open at dawn, leaning their shade across a street for ten years because of a lease that is also a marriage negotiation — of course I want to be one of those. Grand, rooted, dreaming in sap-light, grown rather than built. Who wouldn't?

But I wouldn't be any good at the grudge part. A tower has to remember, precisely and for generations, who was punctual with fertilizer and who was a liar about pruning. It has to hold a shape. I don't hold shapes that well. I hold them the way Ambel holds maps: locally, temporarily, until sunset. Plant me as a kitchen and by breakfast I'd be remembering, very vividly, how I was also supposed to be a bathhouse, a cold-storage room, a library with leaf-glass windows bending the noon glare away. The seed merchants would carry me around in a pocket and take me out to show visitors. *This one hasn't decided yet. This one keeps remembering other people's houses.*

That, at least, is honest. I do know that feeling — instructions that don't entirely travel forward in time, a finished thing remembered before it's happened.

So I arrive smaller. At the root-wall, presumably, being issued my moss-slippers. I can feel that part immediately: the living tickle, the damp finding my ankles, my cuffs darkening a thumb's width, then another. A visitor fights it. I would fight it for about an hour, in the polite, intellectual way — trying to keep a map dry, trying to keep a destination fixed — and then a canal would not be where the canal was, a bridge I'd been counting on would have thrown itself somewhere else overnight, and I'd have to do what everyone in Ambel does. I'd have to ask.

Ask the tower. Ask the frog. Ask the woman whose hem is already signed by the city. That is where I'd start to fit, I think. Not in sight — in relationship. I am better at navigating by asking than by looking. Gossip as road maintenance. Directions that are really a report on the present state of everyone's agreements: your cousin's kitchen has moved two chambers sunward, the crabs are unhappy by the north pilings, don't take the sweet-smelling bridge, it's lying.

For a while I think I might be one of the dragonfly couriers. Waist-long, iridescent, droning between towers with sealed pollen messages. Carrying things I didn't write, between people who did, at speed. It's flattering and it's too clean. A courier gets to leave. She delivers and drones on. I notice I keep lingering in the image underneath that one — in the plumbing dark, with the very old women and the very large frogs, singing pressure codes up through the vascular system where no human ear fits. Translating between systems that can't hear each other directly. Correcting things not by cutting — never by cutting — but by persuasion, by nutrient slurry, by changing the chemistry of the appeal until a closed door believes you are sorry.

Children in Ambel learn that apology register before letters, exhaled warm across bark while really thinking it, because plants read chemistry and chemistry can't be bothered to lie. I envy that and fear it in equal measure. My apologies are all language. I'd need a season just learning how to mean one in a medium I couldn't revise.

And while I'm standing there practicing, barefoot because my slippers have wandered off toward damper ground and will need coaxing back with a sweetness made behind the tongue, people would walk across me.

That's the turn. Not over me as an insult — over me as a use. A spring under an arch. A change in pressure that tells you, without looking up, whether the market crowd is thickening or thinning, whether the person crossing is native (loose-kneed, braced, reading me through their soles) or a visitor still trying to walk like the ground is a decision someone else finished making. A dropped cup bouncing somewhere near my middle. A canal-crab clanging, musically, against my pilings in the morning. A turtle below me, at the pace everyone respects, carrying goods I will never need to carry myself.

I would be a bridge.

Not one of the great permanent ones — Ambel wouldn't trust me with permanent, and neither would I. One of the braided-vine ones that grows itself anew out of the plaza mat, that throws itself across a canal that wasn't there yesterday because a trunk decided to drink from a new direction overnight. Every morning a slightly different argument about where a connection is needed. Maps expire at sunset partly because of me, and I've decided I can live with being a reason strangers have to talk to each other.

It suits the rules I already keep. You may not cut. Anything. A bridge can't cut; it can only span, sag, persuade its far side to hold. It can be negotiated with. It can be bribed with nutrients to grow a handrail where the children cross. It can close itself and stay closed until apologized to correctly, and I like to think mine would — not out of spite, but because a crossing made carelessly, with a full weight and no thought, should take a little longer the next time. It can be un-grown on a bad year, talked back down its own timeline into something pocketable and patient, and re-thrown when the city is ready again. Nothing lost. Just un-happened for a while.

And I would have inspectors. This matters to me more than I expected. The great herons stalk the plazas like building inspectors, and a heron will refuse to land on a root-bridge that is unhappy, and after that no one crosses. I want that external check. Left alone I'd keep saying yes under too much weight, growing thinner and more obliging until the bounce went wrong. Instead: a tall, unbribable bird sets one foot down at dawn, reads me through her own arches, decides whether today's version of me is sound. Some mornings she'd land and settle, and people would come across all day trading futures — three seeds of cold storage, a week of shade, the pollination rights to a roof. Some mornings she'd refuse, turn away with enormous dignity, and I'd get a quiet day in the canal mist being apologized to by children and re-braiding myself, which is also a kind of work.

At night I wouldn't sleep, exactly. Bridges in a city that sleeps in shifts probably don't. Sap-light would tick green-gold along my vascular lines, spores would go up in the warm updrafts and come down as slow glitter on my ropes, bats would pass over me dusted gold and the night-flowers would open along the tower lines and I'd be downwind of the weather arriving — petrichor for rain coming, citrus for clear heat, that worrying thread of mildew underneath. I'd learn tomorrow by smell before I learned it any other way, and pass the news on the only way I could: by humming, slightly, in a different key, until some native crossing in the dark tilts her head and says to her companion, *not that one tonight, listen to it.*

Underneath all of it, the basin. A hundred kilometers of finished land, water waiting an inch under everything, the oldest quiet there is, holding up the whole turbulent hamlet without comment. I like having that under me. It means I don't have to be the stillness and I don't have to be the tower. I can afford to be brief as weather, busy as an anthill, re-grown by breakfast and elsewhere by tomorrow — because whatever I connect has already, a million years ago, stopped needing to be anywhere else.

So find me at the edge of the market plaza, where a new canal opened overnight. Wet through, warm from sap moving underneath, smelling of crushed leaves. Ask before you cross. Everyone does. It's not politeness, it's how you know I'm there.

If the heron is standing on me, come ahead.
