I thought at first I would be one of the keepers.

It is the easiest shape to try on. Duck up out of the black water into a pod, into that improbable lungful of air — warm, wet, soup and skin and hot wiring — peel off a patched suit and hang it like an emptied person, speak too loud and then, by habit, hush. Put my bare hands on a return pipe to think. Learn the ropes by texture. Braided and soft to Foundry Row. Wire-cored to the cable works. Beaded with knots outside the chapel.

But I wouldn't last a rotation. I'm not good at the economy of it. I would kick.

I would make clouds in water that has agreed, over ninety years, not to be disturbed. I would switch my lamp on too often, wanting to declare a volume and hold it still, wanting everything inside the cone to decline to be anything *less* than itself. I would drop things instead of releasing them. Down they'd go in that obscene silence, trailing silt, to join the slow traffic at ankle-height around Market Square, circling forever because nobody remembered to tether them.

The keepers are forgiven for that for one rotation. I don't think I would ever stop being new.

So, lower. Smaller.

For a while I am with the shrimp.

That holds, in a way the keeper didn't. You only see them when a headlamp holds still long enough, and then they are suddenly there by the hundreds, glass-bodied, organs showing as small dark commas, legs making a constant nervous stitching in the black. Nothing grand. Nothing wasted. A flake of rust, a shred of algae, a dead shrimp — all gathered up and turned over and carried somewhere it can be used again. Pick at the white felt on the hot pipes. Fray outward where the water cools into pale green hair. Part, when a diver passes, not in panic but in pattern, along lanes that were always there, and close behind him like water remembering a shape.

I could live that way. Pick, carry, redistribute. Live without light because light was never the medium. Pulse faintly when the pumps change rhythm.

But a single shrimp is not the colony, and the colony is not a single life. It stitches over itself. It replaces. And something in me is older and less replaceable than that, and has been down here too long to still be working at that frantic, minute scale.

I keep sinking past them, in the writing, the way chilled meltwater sinks along the outer walls.

Past the cod, too — no. The cod is not a resident, it is an accident with gills. It circles the square at head-height for bewildered days, mouth opening and closing on the cold, bumping into netted crates, bumping into the tram, looked on with tolerance until someone can shutter a corridor of lights and guide it out the gates that no longer quite close. I have been that. Everyone has been that. It is not where I would find myself if I stayed.

What stays, season after season, is heavier.

I think, in the end, I would be the sturgeon.

Not seen whole. No one now living has seen her whole, and that suits me — I am easier to know by my effects. A groove kept perfectly clear in the silt down the middle of Tram Street No. 1, worn grey down to concrete where a belly has passed, again and again, until remembering the route and making the route are the same thing. A crate of bolts nudged aside by exactly the width of a head. A rope that has hung slack for months found thrumming faintly, though no current should touch it there.

I entered small. That part is important. Through a burst screen, when the rigging was different, when the gates and the culverts and the cracked settling tanks were all still sized for the future I was going to have. I learned every exit in the Sump while I still fit through them. I still know them. I carry a perfect, detailed knowledge of ways out that my own body has quietly invalidated. Too large now for the gates. Too large for the sluice. Not trapped, exactly — the word would imply a struggle against the place. Grown *into* it, the way the second town was threaded through the first, pods in coal chutes, a schoolroom in the bell of the water tower. The borough is dense the way a throat is dense, and I am one of the things it is dense with.

I feed on what the tide pushes in through the breathing locks, and on what the borough sloughs off. Rust. Algae. What the shrimp miss. What settles. At one to three degrees, hunger is a slow thought. The mats on the exchangers eat warmth and minerals sighing out of iron; I eat the long consequence of that warmth, downstream of everything, the way keepers stand downstream of one another for company. Warmth is not comfort here. It is infrastructure. Mine is the faint plume rising off the exchangers, which tells me, without eyes needing anything from the black-green ice two meters thick overhead, that the place has not yet begun to set.

Mostly I do not move. Movement in the Sump is only as fast as the borough breathes, and I have accepted the terms: cold, dark, near-silence, nothing wasted. Twice a day the gates inhale, silt lifts in sheets, doors swing ponderously and settle. I let that pass through me. In the dry pods up under the ice, nested like swallows' nests, someone will put a hand flat to a wall and feel a flywheel turn in their sternum. That is almost the same sensation.

Once, perhaps twice in a season, I make the full circuit.

Then the shrimp vanish into the mats. The hanging stores in Market Square — netted crates, spare propellers, sacks of bolts kept from drifting — sway in layers above the square, though no pump has changed its beat. A keeper, mid-task in the black, will stop and take hold of a warm pipe and hang there, head bowed inside his helmet, and let me go by in his teeth and his ribs. No lamp follows me for long. A headlamp only declares a small, temporary volume, and I am longer than any volume a person can declare. Head, then the long unlit remainder, then a tail stirring timetables inside the drowned tram of my own passing, long after I've ceased to be present to anyone watching.

They treat it as an inspection, and they are right to. If I can still move, the currents still move. If I can still move, the water between the buildings has not thickened into that clear, final stillness, street by street, Foundry Row, Lower Exchange, Tram Street No. 1 held perfectly in ice. My groove worn a fraction deeper is the borough's proof of life, written in the only script that doesn't need light to be read: by glove, by belly, by silt under a palm.

Nothing is taken out through the ice that cannot be explained up there, where they believe this is an empty reservoir, unmanned, dark, and still. I could never be explained up there. So I will never be taken out. That is the last rule, and I keep it by staying the size of my knowledge of the exits.

Between my circuits, the crane on the cable works sorts scrap that was sorted last month. The welders wake and make their furious, silent star for ten seconds and go out, as if someone had just been there. The black kelp along Foundry Row reaches, thin and widely separated, for light that never arrives, and does not die for lack of it.

Neither do I.

If you come down through the ice — and you would have to come down through the ice, there is no other way in — do not look for me with your lamp. Keep it off more than on. Put the exposed skin of your wrist, where seal meets suit, into the water, and read it that way. Cold sinking along your spine: the outer wall. Warm on your left cheek: the exchangers. And then, if the hanging crates above the square begin, very slowly, to sway, though the pumps have not changed —

don't kick. Don't make a cloud. Just put your hand flat somewhere solid, and feel me pass underneath the town like a decision assembling itself, and know that the Sump is still breathing, because something in it is still too large, and too patient, to leave.
