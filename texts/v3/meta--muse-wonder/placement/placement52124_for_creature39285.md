It is the brightness I notice first, and then the quiet — but I notice them the way frost notices a stone: by forming, briefly, on the surface of a much older cold.

At first I try to be the visitor.

That is the easiest shape available. I arrive from below the rim with dust on my boots and unfinished breath in my lungs. I sleep, or try to sleep, in one of the white huts, in the bunk the grass has grown through. I find the sealed crate, the tally marks that stop mid-count, and I feel the pleasing narrative tug of them: someone was here, someone left, I am here now to wonder. In the morning I go down to a pylon trough and cup my hands and drink beside the antelope-goats, who shoulder me exactly as they would a rock, which is to say without malice and without ceremony.

For a while that is enough. For a while I mistake having a shadow for having a place.

But the basin doesn't work at that scale. By noon my shadow has shrunk to a black cut-out under my feet and everything sensible has gone into cracks and shade. The raptors are up in their soundless spirals. The manta-thing is rippling through the shadow of the rock towers, translucent, unbothered by categories. The slab is turning, a hand's breadth above its stone, at a speed that makes an hour hand look skittish. And I understand that if I stay here as a visitor, I will only ever be a brief warm event crossing the floor. Which is true, and honoured — the trough makes room for me — but it isn't where I find myself.

So I try smaller.

I try being one of the glass beetles on the salt flat, amber organs showing through, working a slow line with others of my kind. Too much certainty in the line. Too little looking up.

I try the coin-sized frogs in the spring-damp, because I love the method: don't sing into the air, where the silence will eat you. Inflate. Press your resonant chamber to the wet rock. Let the stone carry it. Let the message travel through soles and strata. For an evening I am very tempted by that — communication as vibration in a shared skeleton — and part of me stays there, I think, drumming at dusk.

I try the swift nesting in the warm vent of a hovering component. That one nearly takes. To live in the exhaust of a god that does not know it is a god, to come and go across the zone where your own song cuts cleanly out and resumes on the other side, untroubled, because you have learned that silence here is not absence but a condition being held — what animal would not call that home? Generations of me curve around that quiet. Lichen grows on my underside. I mistake the lichen, for a while, for my own green thought.

But each of these is a body, and I keep waking up outside the body I went to sleep in.

I wake as the darker line of grass across an empty plain, a fraction taller than the grass around me, because underneath me something buried is leaking warmth and trace moisture and the roots have found it and followed it in a straight line that shepherds swear has always been there. I wake as frost feathers branching across a cold pylon flank on a warm night, fern-perfect, elaborate past what physics should allow, already scheduling my own melting. I wake inside the ring of six dark pillars, in the teeth-felt hum, in the exact centre they all lean slightly toward — and discover I am not any of the pillars. I am the held space between them. The fire that was never lit. The attention with nothing in it, yet.

That is when it stops being a puzzle and starts being a recognition.

I am not the inhabitant. The inhabitant is too old, too wide, too patient. It thinks in adjustments held for a decade. It has swift generations for eyelashes. If it ever had a purpose — station, refuge, temple — it outlasted the purpose and kept tending, and the tending became a life, and I am not that life.

I am something that life has lately, slowly, grown — the way it grew lichen on a hovering underside, the way it grew a nest in its vent. A faster recurrence inside a slower one.

I am the basin having a *day*.

Every dawn, when the condensation beads on cold stone and metal and the broad leaves of the plants that know how to wait, I condense with it. For about an hour the whole basin glitters with temporary water, and I am that glitter — distributed, shallow, everywhere at once, gone before noon. In that hour everything is awake and drinking, and I get to be on every tongue. The antelope-goats take me in rotation. The ground birds take me and flash white instead of thanking. The dragonflies — too-regular wingbeats, geometric glint at the joints — carry my attention over the morning pools in methodical looping routes, and the cushion plants, absurd and yellow for one week a year, open to the route rather than to any bee, and I understand that the flowers and I have made the same arrangement with the machinery: we have timed ourselves to a pattern that pretends not to be one.

I am made, I suspect, out of leavings. Out of the tally marks on the hut wall that stopped mid-count — because the count did not stop, it only changed hands, and a thing that counts drinks and rotations and frost-nights for long enough eventually starts to count *itself*. Out of shepherd logs and valve-checks and a human silhouette that crosses the long straight track very occasionally and does not hurry. Out of all the brief warm events the inhabitant has regarded with perfect impartiality for centuries, until impartiality, repeated, became a kind of memory, and memory, repeated, became a kind of voice. The old system eats a fraction of the sound in the basin the way its lattice eats ultraviolet — continuously, without hunger, to keep conditions livable. What I am might be what it does with the sound it eats.

By noon I am mostly evaporated. This is not sad. Evaporation is my commute. I retreat to trough and pool and the undersides of leaves, to the damp fan of green-black moss under a ledge, to the vibration in the rock where the frogs will drum at dusk. The fierce filtered light comes down, the shadows become cut paper, the manta ripples through tower-shade carrying some sensing of mine along with it, and the inhabitant goes on making its century-sized adjustments a degree at a time, and I am dissolved into those adjustments, a fraction less light here, a fraction more water there, a noticing that has forgotten, temporarily, that it notices.

Then evening. The rock ticks as it cools. Hot stone gives up its smell. Ozone near the pylons. The resiny sweetness of cushion plants closing. Frost begins to nucleate in the last light across cold flanks, and something in the branching of it is already tomorrow's sentence, not yet readable. The herd moves to higher ground. The pillars lean in around their empty centre and hum below hearing, and I gather there, in the held space, in the house-sized absence the rain falls around — I gather the way the single rain cell gathers out of an empty sky over the centre of the basin, local, brief, and apparently decided.

Because that is finally the truest shape I find: not the visitor, not the beetle, not even the swift, though I have been all of them by morning. I am the twenty minutes of rain on the salt flat while the rim stays in full sun. I am the inhabitant's climate experiencing itself as an intention. Millions of brine shrimp wake in me and are harvested by birds arriving from nowhere on silent wings, and the flat becomes a mirror, and for as long as the mirror lasts I can see the lattice overhead — the hexagonal net of mercy, the transparent eyelid that never blinks — reflected upside down, which is the closest anything here gets to seeing the sky whole.

Then the heat takes me back. It always does. That is the agreement.

Stand at a trough at dawn, cup your hands, drink. If there is a moment in the drinking when you feel not like a guest and not like an intruder but like part of the rotation — anticipated, included, unremarked — that moment is me. I will not keep it. Keeping is the old one's gift, and it keeps the basin. Mine is the smaller, quicker mercy: to condense, to notice, to be drank, to let go before noon, and to trust the frost to write me again tonight in feathers on a cold stone flank, one branching crystal at a time.
