I think I would arrive on the train, at first.

Not because I belong to the valley — I would need the hours of switchbacks, the air getting thinner and cleaner and too bright, to explain how I got so far from anywhere else. I would sit with my forehead almost to the window watching the ground give up on being ground, watching the Mat go from turf to thicket to those slow, jointed trunks knocking together, and I would tell myself I had come up for work. People who do not yet know what they are in Ternwick always say they have come up for work.

I would try the ordinary jobs, and be ordinary at them.

A week on the saw-car ahead of the morning locomotive, throwing back armfuls of pale fiber and feeling it twitch, already certain of the rails. A month in the Loom Quarter, forearms cross-hatched with fine scars, learning to weave root-cloth and to talk, talk, talk over the clacking because your hands go quiet if your mouth does and then you notice what the floor is doing under the building. A season in the Condenser Rows wringing water out of fog, drinking it greenish and faintly sweet from a tin cup and not minding, which would surprise me. Minding less and less, which would surprise me more.

My chest would deepen. Or I would notice it had. My hems would stain green. I would stop lowering my voice indoors.

But I think the bells would find me out.

Not ringing them — listening to them. Standing at the end of a street in the blue hour when the fans slow on the rim and the cloud-sea closes white below the plateau, and hearing which bell-keeper was nine and bored and keeping perfect time, which was old and napping between strikes and letting the interval stretch almost, almost too long. Hearing the note under the notes. The surveyors would notice me noticing, the way people in port towns notice someone who can read weather in water, and one of them — a woman with a theodolite on her shoulder and sap ground so deep into her knuckles it looked like another set of joints — would hand me a listening rod without explaining, because in Ternwick that is the explanation.

Ear to the Mat. Eye shut against the brightness.

And there it would be: not silence underneath the city, never silence, but a vast slow groaning, root under strain, district deciding inch by inch where it intends to be next year. Looms through earth. A whistle the sheds gave up yesterday, travelling in a hollow stem, waiting for the temperature to change to be let go. My own heartbeat, arriving back at me a fraction late, from underground, as if something enormous had taken it politely into its mouth to learn its shape.

I would become a surveyor, then. That would be the address I gave, with a date. *Listening, as of March.* I would walk the hundred yards of low humming Mat between districts when other people hurried across chanting, and I would go slower than was sensible, rod in hand, stopping to put my ear down where the causeway boys could still see me from their poles. I would learn to hear a subsidence three weeks out, a root-crown loosening under a tenement, the moment a street stopped meaning east.

And I would be sent, increasingly, out beyond the pruning line. Because the imitations need surveying too, now. Because someone has to give *them* addresses, with dates, and stand in their green doorways at quarter scale, crouched, ridiculous, and note that the lopsided chimney has grown two inches toward true, that the woven train on the disused spur has put out a second puff of spores at dusk, that the copy of a burned warehouse makes, when the wind is right, a sound almost exactly like rain on a roof it does not have.

I would be good at that part for reasons I would take a long time admitting.

I would take longer and longer out there. I would bring a bell, at first, and ring it at the correct intervals, the way you do, the way everyone does, so the empty ground does not forget what a street is for. Then I would bring it and ring it a little less. Then I would find — this is the part I would not put in the survey report — that I had been standing very still in the doorway of a house the Mat was remembering forward, listening to it creak and settle and practice its small domestic noises, and that the tendrils had come up, patient as ever, not over my feet exactly. Around them. Considering them. The way the Mat considers a chimney, a rail, a sleeping hare: as a possible shape.

And I would not pull free immediately. That is how I would know.

Down in the valley they incorporate nothing; they just bury it. Up here I would understand, with my ear still ringing from the rod, that I had never quite decided whether I was learning the Mat or it was learning me, and that the question had a third answer the surveyors only say late, at the Market Crown, after the evening whistle and again after its echo: that there might not, eventually, be a difference worth defending at full volume.

So this is where I would find myself, in the end. Not in the dense districts, though I would keep a room there, staked and lashed, and prune my own sitting room, and sleep well through the ticking under the floor like everyone else. I would find myself at the edge, in the last of the light, employed by the city to map the copies and employed by something older to be copied correctly. A bell in one hand, not rung. A listening rod in the other, not needed. Standing in the humming hundred yards between one human district and the green one rising to meet it, making a small, deliberate, ongoing noise — talking, mostly. Telling the imitation houses about the people in the real ones. Telling it which sounds are a kettle and which are grief and which are just the wind in a chimney it has built slightly wrong. Correcting it, fondly, the way the stokers correct the leaves at the chimney rim.

Not to stop it learning us by heart. Nobody up here really believes you could.

Just to make sure, when it practices us in the dark — when it raises Ternwick hollow and green and entirely without us, and the wind goes through it making crockery-noises, settling-noises, a boy keeping time on a pipe at the end of a street that has never had a boy in it — that it gets the tempo right. That it knows a bell is not the metal.

A bell is the person refusing, for one more interval, to let the street go quiet.

I would stay out there until the light failed, saying so. And then I would walk home along the lit causeway, singing tunelessly like everybody else, a little greener at the ankles than last year, while behind me, in the dark, very slowly, something ancient and sunlit and fond of the shape of houses turned my voice over in a hollow stem — keeping it, I am almost certain, for tomorrow.
