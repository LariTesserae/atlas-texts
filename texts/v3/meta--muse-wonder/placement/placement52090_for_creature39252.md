I would start where everything starts here, and where most things end without ever having become anything in particular — in the water above the rooftops.

Not swimming. Drifting. Small enough that the tide decides. The inflow carries me in over the rim that morning, cool-sweet from the eelgrass flats, gritty with plankton. The outflow will carry me back out again unless something intervenes: a mouth, a chimney, a place where the current catches and slows against stone. At first I think I might be one of the blind silver fish. I like that idea too quickly, and it doesn't hold.

They move just above the roofs in schools so close they read like a single body changing its mind. They have the whole lateral line of the school to feel with — each fish only a section of a larger sensing. When a barracuda's pressure-wave comes down an avenue they are already not there. I wouldn't have that certainty. I'd be the one still turning when the others had turned, feeling the wave arrive late through my own skin, learning the detour around the central plaza a half-second behind the stream. The school would absorb me or shed me. Either way I wouldn't stay a fish for long up there, exposed in the thinning dark.

Lower, then. Tasting my way down.

I try the alley mouths where the translucent shrimp hang by the thousands, antennae laced together into a trembling curtain. That, for a while. To be mostly transparent, mostly waiting, filtering the flow through fine hairs, snapping away backwards at a vibration I can't identify. But there's too much startle in it, and too little to hold onto. The curtain parts for everything that pushes. It re-forms. Nothing is kept.

I try a crab's life on a plaza — sweeping, sweeping, legs ticking on shell, clearing silt from limestone that the tide will silt again by the next turning. There is honesty in that work; the city literally persists because somebody does it without finishing it. I can feel myself drawn to the rhythm: gather, test, discard, the good pebble turned and turned in the mouthparts. But a crab walks *over* its knowledge. It moves on hard points above the floor it reads. And I keep wanting to go slower than that, and closer. I keep wanting the floor to be going *through* me.

The eels go through the drains by smell. No. Too much hunger, too much length of it. The gobies dart in and out of their shell-curved hollows under the foundations, ticking against the octopus's ceiling, eating parasites from her doorway, tolerated because useful. Closer — that smallness, that dependence on someone else's maintained threshold, that life lived in the seam between a house and its foundation. But the goby darts. It has somewhere it's always just returning from. What settles in me, as I keep descending and the water goes from brown-green dimness to simple dark, is a wish not to dart at all.

So: the corner where the lower city rises.

I would be sponge.

Not a tenant in the tower — I pass that possibility too, the bored chamber, the small worm or crab tucked in a tunnel in living wall. I would be the wall. Yellow-grey deepening to russet where the current is richest, dull where silt has settled into me and not yet been cleared. One of the thick towers that grew up between the first oyster ridges before there was a street here to have a corner on, and made the corner by growing.

I do not know the city the way the octopus below me knows it. She reads it arm by arm, taste laid over taste, a sequence along a route. I know it only as what passes through. But that turns out to be a great deal of knowing. Twice a day the whole bowl changes direction, and twice a day I lean — not visibly, not the way a plant leans, but in the orientation of a million small openings widening toward the flow. Inflow: the water arrives carrying the flats with it, eelgrass-green, fish eggs in season, the loosened detritus of a world I will never reach, fifteen meters and a whole brightness away. I take it in through pores too many to count and work it through canals that are also, in places, other animals' homes. Outflow: the city gives itself back through me, warmer, cloudier, full of its own waste and larvae and fine ash of dead things, and I take some of that too, because in this bowl the distinction between food and the neighborhood is mostly a matter of timing.

Nothing leaves unchanged, and I am one of the places where the changing happens.

I have tenants. This is not a metaphor down here; it is architecture. Small crabs in chambers that began as my own water channels and were widened, patiently, by occupancy. Worms threaded through me. A brittle star with one arm in a canal of mine like a hand resting in a sleeve. When a tenant dies I do not bury it. I expel it, eventually, out through a chimney with the spent water, and it drifts down past the octopus's door below, and she tastes it go by, I suppose, and files it somewhere in her sequence of the street. When I die — and I am dying and growing at the same time, constantly, the base of me already dead and cemented into the oyster ridge, gone stiff as foundation, younger tissue budding russet over the ruin of myself — the tenants will simply find the wall softer. They will tunnel deeper. Something will live in me without knowing it is in a ruin, because the ruin is still filtering around it.

I have never seen the plaza at the center, and I avoid it as surely as she does. My avoidance is just slower and made of meat. Whenever some part of me, over a season, begins to grow in a direction that would narrow the avenue — new tissue reaching into the agreed current — that part starves, or is grazed back by urchins, or is undercut and buried. The street prunes me. The grooves at the bowl's center are too far off to feel, but the principle is the same and it's written into my shape: I am the shape of everything that was eaten away. Generations of flow have worn my chimneys to face the turnings of the tide the way her arms have worn a groove smooth across her threshold stone. We are both records. Mine is just legible in bulk.

What is it like? There is no light to describe it by and I have no eyes in any case, not even her rumor of eyes. There is pressure, endlessly — the faint muffled boom of surf arriving in the body more than the ear, except I am all body, so I receive it the way a wall receives weather. There is taste distributed across an entire surface, so that smelling the neighborhood and being rained on by it are the same event. When a storm passes somewhere above the lagoon and the sediment rain comes down, I cough silt from my chimneys for a day and the crabs below work double, and neither of us finds this remarkable. When spawning season turns the water milky, every mouth I have opens at once with every mouth in the city, and for an hour I am as satiated and stupid as any predator.

I keep one thing the way she keeps her spiraled shell, except I cannot choose it and would not call it keeping. Lodged in a dead canal near my base, where the current almost fails, there is a fish bone — a small, clean, curved one, worked there by an inflow years ago and never worked out. The water slows around it. Silt collects against it and is cleared, collects and is cleared. My tissue has grown partway over it without absorbing it. It pleases nothing. It is just there, interrupting my flow in a place only I would notice, if noticing is the word for a permanent slight alteration in the way the tide goes through that part of me.

She will die soon, the octopus below — sooner than me, which down here only means her rhythm is faster. Her house will go ungathered, challengers will read the vacancy by smell, and a new arrangement of crab shells will accumulate on a floor I have been raining fine filtered waste onto for longer than she's been alive. Towers outlast householders. But not by escaping their fate — only by having it happen at a pace that looks, to quicker things, like permanence. My base is already street. My middle is already somebody's ruin with tenants. My growing edge is already being pruned into agreement with an avenue nobody planned.

So I would not be found, finally, in any one of the city's inhabitants moving through its streets. I'd be found at the corner where she turns for home: the breathing wall she passes without looking, tasting only that the water there is a little cleaner than the water a body's length away, and never knowing why.
