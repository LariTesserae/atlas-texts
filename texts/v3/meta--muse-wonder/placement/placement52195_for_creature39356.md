I come down the way everything does — not by choosing the pit, exactly, but by finding the way in opened.

From the rim it would be easy to mistake for work. I'd be the person, first, I think, in the pickup before light, descending through mist with the light towers still burning below. I know how to want that: the close work, the hand signals, the competence of knowing which brown water is ankle-deep and which identical water will take you to the waist. I know the pleasure of a world so loud you are absolved from ordinary conversation. You lean in to six inches or you say it with a lifted palm. Your shirt sticks by eight. Your boots rot honestly.

But the people leave at night. They climb back to the fields, to dry paper and wind. Down here that feels like a tell. I would not be what commutes.

I would not be the heron either, though I linger there a long time on the way down. A few metres from the next heron, white-grey in the settling pond, still in a way that looks like employment. Standing on one leg in water that smells faintly of diesel, watching the shallow turn and settle. There is a dignity to occupying so little and needing so little motion. But the heron is a specialist in waiting *at* the water. It keeps its dry, feathered interior intact above it. Its feet are in, but it is not.

The dragonflies pull me sideways for an afternoon — stitching the causeways at windscreen height, ticking off hard hats, all iridescence and appetite, living entirely in the warm layer of air that sits trapped between the benches. To be that quick in a place where nothing else gets to be quick. To hunt midges in their columns. But they are aerial the way the herons are vertical: visitors to the wet, brilliant at the surface of it.

I keep sinking.

Past the swallows in the open ends of the stacked pipe — good neighbours, those, nesting in inventory, commuting through exhaust without comment, raising young in a steel tube that will one day be buried or hauled away. Past the coot, absurd and perfect, who has looked at a discarded cable spool and decided: reed bed, island, enough. Past the turtles hauled out on floating pallets, taking their sun on freight. Everything down here that succeeds does it by not asking the place to be finished first.

And then the floor asserts itself. Or rather, it fails to. That's where I stop descending and start to recognize the shape of a life.

I would be in the margin. Not in the deep black sumps — fenced, pumped, too steep-sided and permanent in their drowning — and not up on the dry crown of the causeway with the trailers and bowsers. In between, where the crushed rock gives out and the water begins without any ceremony. Specifically — and this arrives not as a decision but as a narrowing, the way water finds the lowest seam — in the flooded conduit trench that runs alongside the haul road near the workshop containers.

It was dug to carry cable. It carries tadpoles instead. It is perhaps a foot deep, warm by ten in the morning, the colour of tea over pale clay, fringed where the boards cross it with a skin of black-green algae. Trucks pass and a tremor comes through the mud before the sound does. The pumps are not a noise from here; they are a pressure in the substrate, a throb you absorb through the belly. Everything I would be is adapted to exactly that: to feeling the world arrive through wet surfaces.

A chorus frog. Small enough to sit in the palm-print a boot left yesterday, now filled with rain. Olive-brown, leopard-blotched, a pale stripe along the jaw like a seam. Skin that must not be allowed to dry, and here — the mercy of the pit — never has to. The air itself is wet. Mist condenses on the benches and rains back down inside the basin. My fingertips, if I had them, would stay pruned. I breathe partly through my skin and so I take the place in directly, diesel and warm algae and wet limestone, molecule by molecule. There is no inside down here yet for systems to go in, the engineers say that about the reservoir. It's true of me first. My systems are at the surface, and they have to be, because the surface is where the exchange happens.

I am not alone in the trench, and that is the point. Nothing down here gets to live alone, and I wouldn't want the exemption. At dusk in May we are packed into the reed scraps and the tyre ruts and the flooded junction boxes, leopard frogs, bullfrogs from the deeper ponds (their calls like a plucked, flooding string), and my own kind shoulder to shoulder along the waterline. Separately we are nothing much — a note you could miss. Together we stop being animals and become weather. The roar rises and the big diesels have, to their credit, competition. This is how society works at this depth: you make noise to hold a place in the noise. The crews shout at six inches; we do it at full throat from six inches apart, all night, and call it territory, and call it desire, and it is both, and it works.

By day I would be quieter about it. Half-submerged under the edge of a board the crews step across between the workshop and the canteen, eyes just above the film, watching the causeway go about its enormous, slow business. Swallows cut through the fumes above me. A water snake pours off the riprap when a truck comes too close, making the whole trench shiver. Midges hold their columns in the still air, marking the warm pockets the way light towers mark the haul road in mist. Now and then one of the hand-sized fish from the warmest basin works its way up the connecting trickle after rain — a visitor from the future of this place, from the lake it is supposedly becoming — back breaking the surface in water almost too shallow to swim in. We regard each other without surprise. We all arrived through floodwater or on the wind or in a tyre tread. Nobody was stocked. Nobody was planned. The inventory keeps becoming habitat before anyone can use it as inventory.

My timescale would suit the pit's strange tempo, which is the other recognition. Nothing moves fast despite all that sound: a seep on Monday that is a pour by next month, clay heaving a millimetre at a time under a new pad, gypsum needles pushing out of a fresh wall overnight. I live in the narrow band of that slowness that runs hot and fast. Spawn in the trench when it fills; tadpoles racing the evaporation that, down here below the water table, mostly never comes; legs in weeks. I can breed in a rut because a rut, given groundwater that rises a finger's width a day whether anyone pumps or not, is a pond with pretensions. Generation after generation in a ditch meant for cable, and my voice — the thing the ditch was built to carry, in another form — travelling instead through humid air and shallow water, conducted beautifully by both.

If the trench is drained for a day to lay the cable at last, I move six feet to the tyre ruts. If the east ponds join up and the water clears tea-coloured over white clay, my grandchildren will be out there among the egrets on the drowned fence-line, white bird after white bird, all of us doing the same job: standing, watching water, waiting for it to make up its mind. In five years a foundation, they say. In ten, a reservoir with an intended shore, a settled edge, an official lake. I do not need to decide whether to mourn that. When it comes I will either move up the benches ahead of the shore or become, uncomplaining, a creature of the shore it makes. Amphibious is not a compromise. It is a refusal to bet everything on one state of the world.

At night, when the pickups have climbed to the rim and the canteen steam has stopped and the light towers stand doubled in the standing water, the pit reveals its permanent tenants, and I am among them — not the loudest, not the largest, not remotely in charge, but native now to the interval. Mist comes back down the benches, erasing the towers one by one. The sumps tick and rise. Something is always seeping, dripping, condensing, beginning. Under the board, in the trench that was supposed to carry other voices, I draw the wet air in through my skin, feel the pumps keeping time in the mud beneath my belly, and add my note to the one continuous sound — until it stops sounding like a note at all, and starts sounding like the place itself, freshly opened, eighty metres below the farmland, already completely full.
