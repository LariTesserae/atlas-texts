I think I would come to it slowly.

At first, in the cracks — that rust-orange film, the sulfur-yellow smear, the pale green so thin you doubt it. Learning for three days how to hold to stone that has just cooled enough not to kill me. It would be enough, that small hold. Then the stone would split directly under me, or turn its face to the heat, or lift and spill me off its side, and I would learn the first rule here without words: *trying to stay is not the same as living.*

Then perhaps a crystal. I can feel that temptation more clearly. To bloom overnight along the lip of a vent where the air shimmers, to make myself in white needles, perfect and untouched, because up here nothing has yet learned how to weather anything. No rain to blunt me. No wind strong enough, in this thin air, to do more than sigh. For a few hours I would be exact.

By morning the ground would grind me back into the mountain.

So I would not be what roots, or blooms, or settles here. I would be what has learned to keep beginning while it moves.

I would be one of the cairn-walkers.

You would not find me in a place, exactly. You would find me between cairns, or in the act of unmaking the evidence that I had been anywhere at all: scattering a morning ring of stones with my boot, leaving it neither built nor ruined, just available. Or with my palm flat on rock that looks identical to all the other rock, listening for temperature the way other people listen for a voice in the next room.

My habitation would be a set of decisions, remade daily. Sleep in the middle of the old flows, where the stone is thickest. Never in a hollow, however kind it looks out of the wind. Build the windbreak with its opening turned from the newest crack, not the weather. Walk when the shards begin that faint musical ringing underfoot — uphill, calmly, not running, because running wastes water and water is only ever what I carried up on my own back through the dust-sea.

I would be spare. Not noble, just edited down. Deep-chested from years of air that gives too little. Mouth a little split, always. Cloth over my nose and hair against the warm desiccating wind, leather over my eyes when the dust sighs across, and then open-faced at night because my eyes would have forgotten the sun anyway. Down below they say the old ones who return blink for the rest of their lives. I think I would already be starting to understand that — the stars up here are too hard and close and numerous to be looked at with a lowland face.

Most of me would live in the soles of my feet.

I would remake my boots again and again: thick enough that the glassy stone won't cut through, thin enough that the tremor still arrives. I would walk a little splayed, a little listening. I would stop without deciding to stop, because the loose shards on a slope have begun, one by one, to creep in the same direction. I would know a ridge was going to shed its side by a pressure on my skin before the black plume ever hung, soundless, in the air. Balance wouldn't be something I *had*. It would be the main way I knew the world at all.

If we met, you would have to come close to hear me. Sound doesn't travel here, and shouting wastes moisture. I might greet you instead with a stamp you feel through the stone, or a flat palm laid down between us — greeting and assessment at once. *How warm here. How restless. How near to breathing out.*

I would tell you what I know in the only form knowledge keeps up here: recent reports, possibly already false. That the crack beyond the third cairn was a step-over at dusk and a trench by waking. That the cone to the east has grown a hand's breadth. That the measuring posts are leaning north now, all but the last. I would not draw you a map. Elevation is gossip here. What I would trust is the heat through my palm, salt on a fingertip, the tilt remembered in my ankles from a ground that has already revised itself.

And in the brief dark hours when they are perfect, I would gather. Salt needles before the mountain reclaims them. Sulfur flowers, blue-black stone streaked with copper that has not yet had time to dull — things the people below, in their brown permanent dusk, want carried down through the dust. Only I would keep deferring the descent. Not from devotion, exactly. The place is too busy to be a god, too indifferent to pray to accurately. Just from a habit of touch that has become indistinguishable from attention: palm down before eating, palm down before sleeping, palm down before crossing a crack with dull red at its bottom, as if checking a sleeping animal for breath — knowing it is not alive, behaving as though it almost is.

You would find me, most truly, at a leaning cairn, restacking it. Not building anything permanent. Just lifting the same black shards a little higher, a little truer, so the route stays legible for another week for whoever comes behind me. Shoulder-high, already beginning to lean again as the ground rises four inches under its left side. No inscription. Nothing to mark that it was me.

One day the uplift may stall. Rain may find the plateau, soil may soften it, children may be born there who belong to it in the ordinary rooted way. I would not be one of those people. I would be their temporary, unrooted precursor: warm-backed, cracked-lipped, listening through my feet under a sky I am still being pushed closer to — alive here not by settling, but by agreeing, cairn after cairn, never ever to stop moving.
