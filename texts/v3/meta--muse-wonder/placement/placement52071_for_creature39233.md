At first I would want the crown.

That is probably the newcomer's vanity, and this world is very good at evaporating vanity. I would see the Bright-Kin walking in their travelling blade of shade, silver-eyed, unhurried, their mirror-cloth brims tilting minutely around them like attentive tents, and I would think: *there.* Up there. Out in the clean, terrible light, where the horizon is a bright line and you read dazzle the way sailors read water.

I would last until the test.

Not fail it, exactly — this place does not seem to believe in failure, only in gradients — but I would stand barefoot for the first time on the cooling panels at dusk, feeling the grid hum up through my soles into my teeth, and my eyes would do the ordinary, disobedient thing. They would water. First from glare, then from pain, then from the simple insult of so much light coming from above and below and sideways at once, until my translucent lid — if I even grew one properly — kept fluttering open. The elders would watch the slits of my eyes for panic and find something softer and less useful: fascination. I would be too busy trying to *look* at it all to learn how to read it.

So I would go lovingly down. Not far.

I would find myself, I think, in the first few meters under the skin — not on the roof, because no one lives on the roof, but *in* it. In a subsided blister-dome where the surface has settled almost flush. In a thermal buffer-gallery where noon arrives filtered through fiber-optics and scuffed old glass and falls at last, bearably, amber on inward-turned windows. Close enough to hear the plain tick as it cools. Far enough down that talk doesn't cost so much.

I would not be one of the deep ones, either. I am not made for the green dark, let alone the red, let alone the strata where the air thickens and solvents replace water and children are born already listening with the backs of their throats. I have too much eye in me still. I need the idea of a sky even if I can only take it piped, diluted, secondhand. So: the threshold. The eyelash root.

I would become a keeper of condensers, I think. Of vapor traps.

It's unglamorous work, which here means it is sacred work. Along the seam-lines near my gallery, the dull thorn-traps bristle to steal moisture from air that wants to give up nothing. At dawn they frost for a second and vanish. All day they work invisibly, coaxing grams out of exhalations, out of leaks, out of the building's own slow breath. My job would be to walk the lines in the cool scheduled minutes — dawn, dusk, the brief copper lowering of the reflectors — and learn each trap the way you learn teeth in a familiar mouth. This one beads early. This one sings faintly against my palm when the charge is building. This one has been taken over on its shaded side by a sheet of grey-green lichen, and I must decide, each season, how much of its yield belongs to us and how much to the lichen, because the lichen is food and also sealant and also, possibly, an opinion the building is having about how a plant ought to work.

I would eat the lichen wafers, salty and mineral. I would clip the black-glass fans from the dust in the cracks before they could fold, careful of the copper edges, and taste the bitterness and not learn to like it, exactly, but learn to trust it. Twice a year when the ration bricks came up through the hatch from the inhabited depths — pale, dense, tasteless as wet paper — I would carry my share back flat against my spine and feel poorer and richer with every step as the weight settled.

But the condensers would only be the excuse. The real reason the gallery would keep me, and the real reason I would stay, is that I can hear.

Not with ears, mostly. Ears are for the folded streets where it rains on schedule from the pipes. I would learn it the way the crown-children do, barefoot, and then never unlearn it: feet flat, weight even, sternum open. The deep notes passing up as some thermal mass miles below shifts its state. The short, patient heel-and-knuckle taps of the Bright-Kin at noon, standing absolutely still a mile away while a conversation moves through the ceramic into my bones. The different hums of different ground. *Ceramic, glass, living metal, prayer-tile, solar scale.* I would get good at translating between speeds. The surface speaks in taps and stillness. The depths speak in temperature and taste and notes too low for sound. The building — the largest inhabitant, the one repairing the roof at exactly the rate the sun destroys it — speaks in both, and in neither, and very, very slowly.

So they would start leaving me messages that were not quite for me.

A tender coming down at dusk with her crown off, grafted skin unfurling along one shoulder, grey-green and faintly mineral, would press her palm to my gallery wall instead of speaking and tap out: *seam widening east, swarm late, tell them.* And below, a seam-canyon cousin — long from the vertical life, reckless on ledges, smelling faintly of the green-lit machinery sea under the avenue glass — would send up through the structure three slow pulses that mean: *window gone dark on the forty-first level, nobody home, check if it's a death or only a sleep.* And I, who am neither up nor down enough to belong wholly to either message, would carry both. I would be the part of the threshold that notices. The building has repair-swarms for its fingertips and dust-rivers for its digestion, but it still seems to need small, hidden, useful witnesses for the tasks a system cannot do for itself: to notice the pale moth battering softly around the navigation beacon. To feel a small, specific grief at a dark window.

At dusk I would do the rite without needing it explained twice, because I think my body would understand it before my mouth did. When the condensers bead with the day's stolen moisture, I would take only what my weight allows, and I would return a portion — a palmful, a mouthful — to the shallow depression in the skin that is not a drain but a mouth. Gram for gram. Not worship. Not rent. Plumbing, which down here and up here is the same thing as prayer.

And sometimes, on loan, when the shade of the far cooling fin swept its one slow arc across our district, someone would settle a spare crown on my head — too big, woven ceramic fiber, throwing its flat rim of shadow over my face — and I would be Allowed Up for the length of that shadow. Not at noon. Never at noon. At dusk, when the buried towers catch the last of the light and the flat world remembers for a few minutes that it once had a skyline. I would walk bent slightly inside the moving coolness with the tall silhouettes, my shadow doubled and trebled into pale ghosts by the mirror-fields, and feel the day's heat still stored in the panels coming up through my soles, and the grid carrying everyone's listening underneath mine — the canyon families abseiling home, the avenue-keepers polishing their glass, the deep lineages braiding water-history into their children's hair, the old hot presences shifting politely through shafts where my lungs would never go.

Then the shadow would pass, as scheduled. I would take the crown off and go back down into the amber, into the first kind darkness, and lie with one ear pressed sideways to the warm wall while the night panels opened their stored radiance and the plain above me began, faintly, to glow.

Not the master of the place. Not a tenant, either. One of its eyelashes, perhaps — not the bright outer edge that faces the sun, but the root in the warm skin, where the light gets in anyway, and someone has to learn, blink by blink, what it means.
