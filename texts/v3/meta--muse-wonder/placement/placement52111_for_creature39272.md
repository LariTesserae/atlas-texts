I thought at first I would be one of the cutters.

It seemed the obvious shape to take in a place like that — seed-husk knife in hand, reading a glossy seam in a wall, knowing by its wet shine whether it wants to open or close. I liked the competence of it. I liked the idea of being the person who could keep a corridor alive.

But the longer I stay in the Hollow, the more I notice my hands are wrong for cutting.

They hesitate at the edge. They want to go around a thing again. And again.

I would find myself, I think, a hundred feet up where the camps are, where a platform is only ever a good guess. At false dawn, when the veins brighten far above the hammocks and the rime along the cords goes suddenly hard-edged and white. My breath fogging inside my fiber cap. Frost feathering the knots I checked the night before, so I have to check them again with bare fingers, because a knot under ice lies — it looks tight when it is only frozen that way.

I would not be the one who plants — no one plants. I would not be the one who decides where we go next. Someone older does that, someone who can stand barefoot on a floor-leaf with her eyes nearly shut behind a seed-husk mask and tell from the note through her soles whether the light turning amber means we have a month, or only the idea of a month.

I would be the one who ties.

It sounds too small, down here where a vascular column can rise out of sight and the next one is a blue ghost on the horizon. But everything human in the Hollow is held on by tying. Hammocks tied at both ends under a floor that may slowly become a wall. Sleepers tied in, children first, a loose loop at the ankle that they learn not to kick against. Cups tied under a careful cut in a vein, so the sap gathers instead of running free. Nets tied across an updraft where the less-cold air climbs beside a sunlit vein, waiting for drift-seed to come turning down out of brightness it may have ridden for days. Platforms lashed between columns that are growing, imperceptibly, away from each other.

And untied. That is the other half of it, and the harder half. Untying a camp that has been good to you while the moss below it is still unscarred. Working fast, but not hurried — nothing here rewards hurry except panic — while a floor gives its faint, deep note underfoot for now, for now. Taking down the rack, the resin pots, the line of hammocks, the small arrangement of lenses someone will rebuild elsewhere at the foot of another vein. Leaving the columns clean. Leaving almost nothing that, from across a chamber miles wide, would say people had ever made a brief, practical shape there.

My days would not look like work from a distance. In the Hollow, nothing human does. I would go along the underside of the leaf on my knees, fiber layers crinkling, ice shedding in small bright falls that vanish before they land. Testing each cord by weight, not by sight. Listening less with my ears — the plant drinks sound too quickly up here, a voice dies in a few yards — than through my palms. A good lashing has a kind of hum when the wind-gate, miles away, begins to dilate and the whole air of the province leans. A bad one goes dull. That is how I would learn to be afraid properly: not of the open brightness, not of the cold that gets into the seams no matter how much resin you seal them with, but of dullness. A dull floor. A dull cord. Tread-moss under a bare heel that does not recoil and bloom behind the step.

I would be tall the way the people here are tall, eventually. Long in the leg from walking months between columns over glassy moss. Narrow through the chest from air that is always damp and always cold. My skin would go the color of a leaf's underside and stay that way, and in a bright district you could see the slow blue-green in my throat when I swallowed tapped sap — slow amber sap, sweet and medicinal, never the clear fast kind. Once, learning, I might reach for the wrong cup. And a cutter, without anger and without explanation, would cut it down in front of me and let it spill, because some lessons in the Hollow are only allowed to happen once.

I would wear the dark smear under my eyes and still squint. Everyone squints. The light has no source and no mercy in it; it falls at full noon from a sky I have never seen and will never see, conducted down through leaves on a surface that might as well be a story told to children to explain brightness. No heat comes with it. My shoulders would learn that early — to stop expecting, under all that noon, to be warmed.

Where I think I become most myself is not in the tying-on, though. It is in what I would fail to throw away.

Because the cutters who go alone toward the unmapped places come back with maps scratched on fiber, and by morning they discard them, honestly — the way folded shut behind them, the country they crossed no longer exists, and a beautiful map of nowhere is just a decoration. Everyone listens to those travelers carefully. Everyone believes them completely. No one follows.

I understand that. I do. And still, under my sleeping platform, in a bundle wrapped against condensation, I think I would keep a few.

Not to navigate by. That would be a way to die, following a corridor that has become solid tissue, or stepping hopefully onto ground that is now the inside of something else. I would keep them the way you keep the shape of a person's hand after they have let go. A spiral column someone swore grew at the edge where light came in a color with no camp-word for it. A lake in a leaf-cup that tipped before they reached it, drawn as if it were still there. A district of frost-flowers all opening downward from a ceiling, hundreds of pale faces, rendered in three shaky lines.

The others would know. In a camp of nine, or eleven, or thirty when geography is generous, there are no secrets at hammock-distance. They would let me keep them because tying is also a way of remembering how things were held, and because a person who cannot discard a map might be exactly the person you want checking, for the third time, the knot at a child's ankle before the district goes suddenly quiet.

That quiet would be my real weather. Quieter than quiet. The dripping stopped. The tick of frost stopped. The moss holding still as if it, too, were listening through itself. Then my hands would stop being gentle and become only sure. Hold on — not to the ground, never to the ground — to the column, to the lashing, to each other. Grip first, questions later, the way the children learn before the tap-codes. And minutes after a column fell two miles off in perfect visible silence, a white slow explosion of frost and fiber, the thump would arrive up through the soles of my feet like something remembered by the body before the mind could catch it.

Later — a season later, some unremarkable bright morning — I would carry the dead, too. That comes to the tier eventually. Wrapping in fiber is tying by another name, and done close, and done low-voiced, breath fogging between the living. Carrying the bundle sometimes for days to a folding place. Laying it down without hope in particular and without cruelty either, knowing the ground would be unrecognizable before long, and greener, in the particular way of fed things.

And when we passed that way again, which we would not, I would not stop.

But I would remember the shape of the knot I used. That is my heresy, if the Hollow allows heresies small enough to carry. Not that the plant is a god — it cannot be elsewhere, so what would be the point of calling it one. Not that this place is a home — homes stay, and nothing here has ever agreed to stay. Just this: that everything I love will be taken up into growth, folded over, opened elsewhere, lit by a vein that was not flowing last week — and that for a little while, between one tying and the next, my hands were the reason it held together in a human shape at all.

A child born beside me on a lashed platform would learn it in the right order. Squint. Hold on. Listen through your feet.

Then, much later, when her fingers were numb enough to be honest: tie carefully. Never deeply. And know which knots are meant, from the very beginning, to be undone.
