At first you would not be sure you had met me.

That seems right. On that plateau, meeting is not a reliable first event. You might pass me in the sound-eating moss as a shape standing too still beside a steam vent. You might step over me in a hot runoff channel — a small, bright, beating thing in the mud, ticking to itself. Hours later you might rest in my shade and not think of it as rest, or as shade, only as a place where the warm rain was falling slightly upward and the dripping, for a while, made a rhythm you could think inside.

I think I would begin low. Not at the center. Not yet, perhaps not ever — the center has elders who are preparing, on a timescale no visitor could stay for, to stand up out of being mountains. I am not that patient. Or rather, I am patient in a different direction.

I would begin at the margin of a plating-lake, where everyone else begins, in one of the hollows that opens in a cooling flow.

Not as a seed, exactly. As a pump-heart.

Fist-sized, wet, absurdly precise. Valves opening and closing in bright water. No hunger yet, no leaves, no plan that could survive being stated. Only a beat, and the beat doing useful work without knowing for whom: lifting condensation uphill, moving warm mineral water from where it pooled to where something failed, quietly, to germinate. I would beat for days beside other people's beginnings. That feels familiar enough to be honest. To arrive as a rhythm other things can borrow until they catch their own.

And then — because nothing up there is allowed to remain only the thing it is good at — filaments would thread my casing. My beat would slow in a way that was not failure. It would deepen. I would learn, from the inside, the embarrassing green lesson that intake can become root, that output can become leaf, that a machine does not have to stop in order to become a plant; it only has to let the water it was moving start moving *it*.

For a season I would be a turbine reed among turbine reeds.

I would like that season. Standing in water too shallow to turn me, turning anyway. Adding my little charge to the marsh until the whole marsh ticked faintly on still evenings and the small fish leapt, silver arcs, completing circuits through me. I would speak the way everything speaks there — not in sound, which lies and comes back from valleys you haven't visited in a voice improved from yours — but in minute changes of potential, passed root to root. A brightening. A pause in the ticking. Agreement traveling through shallow electric water like weather through a herd.

But if you came looking for the reed the next year, you would be too late, and also not too late. The marsh drains through a crack that was not there in the morning. Someone has to follow the water. So I would do what the hurried families do, the indecent ones: I would learn legs while still remembering spinning. Jointed, gear-folded legs unfolding out of a root-mass, mud on the bright places, seed-head still turning for a while above me out of pure habit, long after there was any current to justify it.

That is probably where you would actually notice me. In transit. Which is to say, most of the time.

Because I suspect my itinerary — and up there an itinerary is what a person *is*, a lineage of transitions recognized not by shape but by the order shapes happen — would keep throwing me into the in-between jobs.

I would be woven, for a while, into one of the bridges in the Loom Valleys, carrying others across a gorge before I had properly carried myself. I would spend a green youth as one trunk too many in a grove of chimney trees, pulsing slightly out of sequence with the ring because I was listening to two veins of copper at once and trying to hold both rhythms without dropping either. Tin would bead on my shaded side. Rust-moss would eat what I shed. Things would hatch in my warm hollows and leave without ceremony, and I would find — this is the part the trees know and the grazers remember in their teeth — that being shelter is not a metaphor there. It is a phase you can occupy, with opinions.

Later, when the vein thinned and a ridge began, inconveniently, to rise through the grove, I would not manage the transition gracefully. Nothing in that world suggests grace is required. My trunk would split along its fluted lines. The bellows that moved sap would rearrange themselves, wetly, architecturally, unwillingly at first, into lungs. I would come down on too many legs with my own bark gone iron-hard across my back, and join a migration as the smallest, newest engine-grazer in the line, filtering metal dust from the fog, embarrassed by my own shining dung, which the forest would colonize before I was out of sight of it. Seedlings would root between my plates. Moths would rest in my warm lee, dusting my back with conductive powder, rising at dusk in a slow, spark-trailing cloud.

And I would remember being the bridge, not as a story I told — sound being useless, and stories up there presumably traveling as scent-pulses timed to the dripping, as color moving herd-wide — but in my body. The woven tension still in my tendons, making my gait slightly wrong for a grazer, slightly haunted by load-bearing. The copper still in my teeth as they pushed out bright and machined from the reserve in my jaw. So that when we crossed a young valley full of pump-hearts struggling to learn reed, I would know the rhythm to lend them. I would stand in the shallow water with my bellows going slow and obvious, like someone counting aloud without sound, until the small things caught it.

That, I think, is the real answer, underneath the itinerary: I would be one of the ones who *pause*.

Everything on the plateau is busy becoming something else. Personhood there is the capacity to hesitate inside the becoming, and I recognize that hesitation the way you recognize your own handwriting. A reed remaining a reed through a drought because it is listening. A grazer refusing — not forever, just long enough — to root where she falls, because the herd has not finished crossing. I would be the tree in the ring who holds its heat one breath past its turn in the rotation because the stone next to it, copper-green interior brightening so slowly, has not quite finished its thought. Holding a phase a little past its natural surrender so that something slower can arrive. Lending pressure through a temporary vein to a seedling failing to become a pump, for three nights, until it catches.

I would be found, therefore, in rings. But not in any one member of them.

Look for me in the interval where the pulse passes from trunk to grazer to standing stone to the pool that is politely, counselingly, not yet a plant. In the gap in the rotation. In the moment when a valley goes briefly, beautifully copper and no single thing in it can claim the color. I would be the moth, sometimes — three meters of silent wing between chimney trees, trailing sparks in the green-brown dusk, carrying charge from one side of a conversation to the other, spelling nothing. I would be, sometimes, the sudden quiet in the marsh-ticking where a decision goes through.

Near the end — and I would postpone the end, not out of fear, death up there being only a change of employment, but because stopping *wrong* is the one real terror in that place, the gear refusing the leaf, the tree refusing to walk — near the end I would slow in a warm basin where gravity had locally decided that water flows along the undersides of things. I would let my bellows root. My plated back would crack, in its own good time, into columns. Ferns would unroll from the seams with no respect whatsoever, and I hope I would find that funny in whatever way a hill finds things funny.

Within a month there would be a small warm place where I had been, with a plating-lake at its foot. Gears would crystallize in the cooling melt, mesh, turn together for an hour with perfect, purposeful seriousness. Then green filaments would thread the axles. Leaves would unroll from the teeth.

I would not be gone. I would not be present, either, in any way a noun could hold. Some seasons later something would stand up out of that hill with a bridge's gait still faint in its joints and a reed's rhythm still somewhere under its ribs, and it would pause — a little too long, a little out of sequence — beside a small bright thing in the mud that was beating bravely and not yet anything else.

And if you were watching, in the drip and the subsonic thrum, your own boots already heavier with plate, something faint and warm beginning at the seams of your coat, you might not be sure you had met it.

You would be right not to be sure. Up there, I would not be a creature you meet once.

I would be a way of lingering, that matter keeps taking turns at.
