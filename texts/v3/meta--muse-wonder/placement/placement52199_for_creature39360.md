I would not find myself on the plateau.

That's the first thing I'm sure of, and then immediately not sure of, because I wouldn't know the plateau was there to not be on. High, flat, sun-baked, finished. It might as well be the back side of the sky. Twelve meters up and a world away. Dry wind combing grit over stone. Silence, except for that wind.

Silence would kill me. Not as a metaphor. As a drop.

No — let me start lower, where I did start.

On the warm stone by the cups. Translucent then, small enough to hide in the shadow of my own ribs. You could have seen everything in me: the heart like a pale knot tightening, the gut, the dark smudge of a dragonfly I hadn't finished swallowing. I remember the stone through my belly more clearly than I remember any sight. Heat coming up in the afternoon until it lived in my teeth. The quick swivel of my eyes — I still had eyes then that were good for more than noon and midnight. A dragonfly passing, oil-slick and loud in a way that wasn't sound, exactly. A pressure. A tearing of the air.

And the leap. The stupid, necessary leap.

Every juvenile practices dying in public. You launch because hunger says launch, your frill snaps open along your ribs, and for one beat, two beats, you are not falling. You are lying on something invisible and humming, something that pushes back against your whole underside the way water pushes against a swimmer who has finally stopped thrashing. Then it's gone, or you're gone from it, and you drop with your mouth full and pretend that was the plan.

I missed often enough. I have a hip that still clicks out of true when the noon crest builds, from a bad landing among the frog burrows. The beetles were already moving toward me before I got my legs sorted. I could hear them arriving from the wrong direction, which was the only direction anything ever arrived from, and I learned — not thought, *learned*, in the membrane, in the ribs — to correct for the lie.

Year by year, station by station. That is the whole biography, if I had one.

A little higher above the cups, where a dragonfly's path crosses a node and holds. A tilted lens of air over the fig tree, where the frog chorus comes up through me at dusk and makes my hollow ribs ache pleasantly, as if I were being played very gently. A slow spiral where two folds of sound cross against the south wall. Learning the map the only way it exists: by lying on it until your body tunes itself around it.

Now I am up where the adults are, and I do not come down.

I don't know which pylon is mine in human language. The fallen one, I think. The one the orchids have claimed, white flowers quivering with insects where there is no wind to quiver them. Above it the wave is strongest and steadiest, an old, reliable pressure, and I have held that volume long enough that the swifts curve around it without thinking and the dragonflies — the stupid ones, the new ones — still blunder straight through my mouth's future.

I am as long as a forearm and half again in tail. Milky now, opalescent, except underneath where you could still watch a meal work its way back through me like weather through clear water. My eyes have sunk to wet black beads under the skin. I can tell the white violence of noon from the cool dark. That's enough. I have never needed to see the fig tree to know exactly where it is. It announces itself all day: leaves trembling, frogs underneath, sap and wet mold and insects rising in columns of disturbed air. Everything announces itself. I am ear, all the way down both sides — a pleated membrane, frilled filaments so fine the dawn mist snags and beads on them. Hearing isn't something I do. It's the medium I rest in. Like breathing, except I could imagine holding my breath.

I cannot imagine holding this.

In the morning the pylons pulse, six times, seven, and rain invents itself inside the field. It pearls on my back. It runs along the filaments of my frill, and I draw the skin slowly through my mouth, combing myself clean and drinking at the same time. By nine it's gone and stored everywhere — in the plants, in the stone cups, in me. By noon the cicadas build a pressure you feel behind your face and the whole bowl becomes uninhabitable to anything that needs to think in straight lines. My ribs move in sympathy. I cup one membrane harder. I tilt my tail a fraction. A ripple passes from my shoulder to my hip, so small and so constant you would miss it if you were watching with eyes, which is the wrong instrument entirely.

You came down the stone path yesterday. Or you will tomorrow. The place rehearses you, so tense gets slippery. I heard your footstep meet you a moment before your boot felt it, and I knew your weight, and your tiredness, and the way you stopped at the rim when your ears popped and the outside wind was cut off like a throat closing. You were very quiet, for a human. The ones in the shelter learned that, whoever they were. Their listening horns are still pointed at the south wall's empty air, warm as skin, patient as anything made by hands can be. We were never empty. We were just illegible to eyes.

You will not really see me except at dawn, when the mist finds my outline and gives me away for a breath. A shadow on the stone with nothing to cast it. Dragonflies swerving around a nothing. That is the truest picture of me available to you.

At night I sink lower, into the warm layer, and sometimes the droplets fall upward. They do. I've stopped needing that to make sense. I catch them on my frill and drink in the dark while below me the frogs go on and on, copper throats ballooning from an inch of leaf mold, every one of them shouting as if he were enormous. The millipedes move through the mold like slow rope. The moths move through everything making their deliberate holes in the noise, furred and silent, and I know them best of all — by absence, by the shape of the not-sound traveling through the chorus. So does a swift know an insect. So does everything here know everything else, all of us mistranslated to each other by the folded air, all of us correcting for it in our sleep.

Sometimes, after a heavy pulse, the late water gets into me — the faint blue charge, the taste of metal and mint that isn't a taste so much as a brightness along the membrane — and I give up my station and fly my slow, attentive circle at wall height, head cocked toward sounds that haven't fully arrived yet. Listening becomes a place I can go. The young ones spiral and tumble doing it. I do it the way the old do everything: as if the wave will be there because it has always been there.

When the surge comes, I will leave my air entirely.

You have felt it from the path — thirty seconds when the whole bowl locks into one engine, frogs and cicadas and the castanet beetles by my pylon all rising to a single tempo, condensation beading out of clear sky, swifts pouring from the wall in one shrieking body. Then the nodes tighten. Distances lie differently. A station meters from mine is suddenly adjacent, pressure against pressure, and we slide along waves that exist only for that half minute, frills full open, ribs sounding a note too low to hear — you'll feel it as the light dimming, which it isn't. Mating is blind and brief and conducted entirely on the skin. Then the world staggers apart again and I must find my air or fall, with my whole body, in front of everyone.

I have carried young. Two once. I could feel them show through my clear belly like commas, like punctuation in a sentence I was saying with my body. I timed it to a surge as best I could, and they came falling, frills sealed wet against their sides, with the length of one fall to open and catch. One caught low, badly, near the stone, and began the long climb. The other — the beetles finished what the millipedes left before afternoon. A small body doesn't last long down there. I don't say that with grief, exactly. It's the shape of the bowl. Everything here is eaten by everything else, including, eventually, us.

Because I will die holding my place. That's the last part, and it's not sad from inside it, though I understand it looks sad from the path. When the heart stops, the frill stiffens open, the hollow ribs stay tuned, and the field goes on lifting a body that no longer answers it. I will turn very slowly above the fallen pylon for a day, two days, and in the noon crest the cicada-pressure will play across my ribcage and give off one faint, pure note, and the swifts will curve around me as they do now. Then a filament dries. A pleat tears. I sideslip out of the node — or rise a little first, on some wet night when the rain is falling the wrong way, uncertain, remembering upward — and come down to the frogs and the beetles and the leaf mold, to be translated into the ground I never touched as an adult.

But not yet.

Just now it is early. The pylons are about to pulse. The mist hasn't formed and already I can feel where it will be, a thickening at the tips of the black masts, a decision the air is about to make. My station hums under me, patient, invisible, older than my species and indifferent to it, and I lie on sound the way you lie on the ground — trusting it completely because there has never been an alternative.

Up there, somewhere I will never go, the plateau goes on being ancient and quiet and finished.

Down here, in the warm room hidden inside it, I am hanging in loud, wet, folded air, drinking rain that never belonged to a cloud, listening to your next footstep arrive before you take it.
