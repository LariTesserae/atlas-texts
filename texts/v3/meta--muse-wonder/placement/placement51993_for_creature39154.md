From a distance, I would look like no one at all.

That is easy here. Stand still enough on a terrace, in the amber-grey, and the murk takes you. Forty floors up, bright sleeves, a soft flask at my hip that is never quite full. If I moved, it would only be my hands. You would think I was waving at the jungle.

At first I thought I would be a reed-keeper. I kept coming back to that picture — down. Actually down, on the ground, which most of us do only a handful of times and remember in our knees forever. In a muffling hood, among the twenty-foot reeds all leaning one way, toward home, drinking the roar and pumping it down into water. Walking the knife-edged gullies slow because the rock has not learned to be kind yet. Coming back after a season glazed and serene and half-feral, turning my face without thinking toward the nearest doorway-frame the way leaves do.

But I wouldn't last a whole season, I think. Not because of the sound — out there, raw, it gets into your teeth, behind your eyes, it rearranges something — but because of the turning. I'd learn to tend the reeds and then I'd never stop listening to them listen. I'd stand in a clearing watching a whole meadow unfurl after a fog-burst, purple-white, loud with bees, and feel too plainly that I was not a separate thing tending it. I'd be one more stem in the loop. The cicadas would thicken on me.

So not the jungle floor. Not for good.

Maybe higher, then. A sky-watcher, reading the sails by their shadows as they drift a mile up on nothing, calling *cover, cover, cover* across the terraces when the membranes above us have condensed too long and are about to weep something concentrated and electric. I like the idea of work that is just attention, paid early enough that other people can reach for a leaf. I like the permanent late-afternoon of it.

But I am baffled by horizons in the way only tower-children are. I grew up vertical. Give me forty kinds of leaf by silhouette, a garden spilling down a flank, a market coiled bright around a middle — I know where I am. Give me a long flat distance with another tower lit like a slow lighthouse on it and I feel homesick for a wall at my back. The sky-watchers are comfortable with being looked at by the sky. I am not, quite.

Come closer. Through a doorway.

It would have my shirt damp with some other tower's morning, my ears ringing, that sour-sweet pressure at the threshold that doorway-tenders feel in their clenched teeth. And maybe that is closer to it, actually. The core, not the terrace. The frame humming at the edge of pain. Feeling for the wrong note.

I think that is where you'd find me most days: not quite indoors, not quite out.

My tower — I won't sign its name here, you'd only get the shape of it — runs warm. It dreams in spirals when the weather turns, faint patterns traveling across the sweating translucent stuff of its inner walls at night, and its doorways are excellent nine days out of ten and inventive on the tenth. I have its name for a second surname, carved as it formed into the shunt at my left ear. The right one is my mother's line, finer ridges, a flesh-smith with good hands. Children used to touch them without asking. Now, in the elevator, strangers read them the way you'd read a return address.

My work is attention of a very local kind. I stand at a frame and feel freight come through: *whump*, and the air folds, and a sealed pod is suddenly heavier than the room expects. I feel people come through lighter than that but stranger — ringing, blinking text off the inside of their eyelids, already reading messages from where they've just left. I fine a man, perhaps once a season, who has arrived a few seconds before he left because he traveled too light during an exhalation. He pays with the weary shame of someone caught being wealthy in the wrong way. Mostly I just lay two fingers against the frame's edge between passages and listen with my molars. Right feels like warm ceramic under a pulse. Wrong feels like a word you've forgotten on purpose.

The tenders learn to do that. The listeners learn the other listening — palm flat to a living wall for hours — and in a warm-running tower like mine the two jobs bleed together, because the doors are part of the tower's body and the tower is, in the local understanding, also a person who lives here. On quiet shifts I keep my hand against the wall beside the frame and feel my own building think at a frequency too low to be sound. It is time, down there. It is weather. It is the rock settling under us, still young, still ticking as it contracts at night. The old ones hear downward best, their shunts calcified and yellowed and more tender for it. I am not old, but I've gone still in a market gallery more than once and felt people politely flow around me. Nobody interrupts. They know.

And then — this is the part that finally settled it for me, the picture that stopped moving — they pay me extra, a few minutes a week, to work the quiet-room two floors below the frame.

Because indoors, behind the double skin tuned to cancel the basin, I have a voice.

Outdoors I speak the way we all do: big sign across a terrace, elbows out so the mist can't swallow it; small sign at the hip in the galleries; and closest, best, with two fingers walking a sentence down the inside of someone's forearm while we both look politely out at the green. All day, text blooms and fades behind my eyes and I get that unfocused half-blink people make when they're being spoken to by someone twelve miles away.

In the quiet-room I keep the sand-glass. I time the door. Nobody signs in there — it would be a kind of shouting. People come in by the paid minute the way desert people come to water, and at first they always flinch; the silence is so complete it feels like the pressure has been lifted off your sternum by a hand. Newcomers sit down hard. Locals close their eyes and look holy.

Some of them want me to talk. Not a cantor — I don't have an athlete's throat, I can't do an hour — but I trained enough to say things properly, softly, at close range, without coughing. A name a grandmother wants said aloud once on a name-day so it exists in the air somewhere. A marriage, whispered. A letter from a displaced cousin, rehoused after her tower went silent, who could sign it fine but wants it to arrive in a voice. I read. My voice sounds younger than I am. Everyone's does here; we don't spend them.

It is scandalously intimate, to be spoken to like that, and I am careful with it the way the tenders are careful with the fine for leaving before you arrive. I say the words. I watch them land in a body that has lived its whole life inside a larger sound. Then the sand runs out, the door sighs, the roar comes back in at the edges — reeds, frogs, the billions sawing in the canopy, the felt pulse under everything — and we go back to being fluent and silent.

At night I'd be on the lower terrace in my night wrap with everyone else, not talking, or talking close, forehead to forearm, while the sails glow faintly with stored light above us and the basin changes key underneath. My tower exhales late — we are famously unhurried, famously damp at inconvenient hours — and I'd be watching for the first warm roll of fog down its flank, laundry already waiting, gardens already thirsty, feeling the whole sparse city through the soles of my feet. Three million of us, sixty miles of young black rock, most of whom I will never meet. A message blooms behind my eyelid from one of them. My hand lifts at my hip, answering, before I've decided to.

Not a voice in the wilderness. Nothing here wastes a voice on the wilderness; the basin would win, and the reed-beds would grow toward the shouting for a season. Instead: a keeper of a small frame and a smaller room. A person who stands in the two thresholds this place runs on — the door that agrees to be somewhere else, and the door that agrees, for a few minutes, to be nowhere loud at all — with one palm on a dreaming wall, feeling for the moment the pressure drops out of my chest.

If you came looking, don't call. Stand near the core at dawn, when my tower is about to exhale and the frame is humming sweet and right, and touch my forearm. Once for *yes*. Twice for *stay*.

I'll read it without looking down.
