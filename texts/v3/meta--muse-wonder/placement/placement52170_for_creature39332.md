I don't think I would find myself arriving. 

I think I would find myself already there, at first light, before I had quite decided to wake — listening through the wall.

Not listening *for* anything, the way you listen on the marsh for weather. Listening the way we all do, unknowingly, with my cheek still warm from sleep: whose bucket is that on the rope two houses over, by its squeak. Whether the drip into our jar has changed its note in the night. Whether that faint tick from the north face is only scree settling in the cold, or a slab beginning to speak.

Then bare feet on a floor that is cool even in summer. Felt nothing, yet. Stone remembers the night longer than we do.

From a distance — from the boardwalk, from the flat grey pools and the reeds — I would be invisible. We all are. The pit gives nothing back to the marsh at night and very little in the morning. But inside, if you came down the cut steps, careful not to stand under the wall that stains rusty-brown, you could find me by sound before sight: the soft, irregular percussion of water, and under it, a bowl being set down on wood, not stone, so as not to wake the neighbours below.

I am two-doors-down.

That's how I'd answer if you asked who I am. Not the lane — the lane is also a drain, and belongs to everyone whose feet have hollowed it. I mean my grandparents' sleeping room is under me now, sealed, a cistern with a hatch and a bucket on a rope, breathing cold iron air when you lift the boards. My parents' room is above that, a damp store now, good for jars and fibre and things that don't mind the chill climbing the wall. I live in the room I helped build on top of them, with my own children, a hand's breadth higher than where I was born. 

If I am proud of anything, quietly, it's that our living floor is still dry in winter. And that our roof-yard, after three o'clock, takes a little sun.

I thought, coming down into myself here, that I might be one of the stoneworkers up on the roofs at dawn — barefoot on wet slate, resetting a fallen stone while the clay is still cool. I can tap a wall and hear it, I have the nicks for it, knuckles cross-hatched pale from flint, nails cut brutally short. Everyone here has those hands a little.

But my hands, held close, tell the truer story. They aren't lime-white in the creases like the old cutters'. They are soft in a different way, perpetually pruned, from the weep-cloths.

That would be my work. In the communal alcove where the chalk sweats even in a dry week, where beads form in the shade and run down in bright, slow threads. I hang the strips of woven fibre and hide against the favoured face — not any face; you learn which weep clear and sweet-chalk and which weep brown and iron and are left alone. By noon the cloths are heavy. I wring them into jars the way I was taught before I could talk properly: twist, pause, twist again, never tearing the fibre. A child at my knee could do it in her sleep. Most of ours can.

I would know water the way a marsh farmer knows cloud. On a fingertip, on the tongue. Clear means fill the drinking jar. Brown means move the stores up a shelf, tell my grandmother — she'll already be watching the little brown snails on the lowest lintels, counting how thick and how high they've clustered in the night. If they climb, the pressure in the rock is changing. She taps with a knuckle. Someone, wordless, sends a grandchild up the stairs.

I would dress for drip, not rain. Nothing trailing. Trousers tied above the ankle, sleeves close, hair braided hard back so it doesn't catch every wet lintel I duck under. A short oiled cloak that sheds, instead of a thick one that would drink the pit and stay wet for three days. A woven brow-band indoors when the beams sweat above the cooking fire and rain down warm on one side and cold on the other, and I'd shrug off the drop down my neck the way you shrug off a fly.

I would speak low without thinking about it. Not from shyness. A joke in our kitchen is heard three houses over; a quarrel is public by supper. So we knock on wood, never stone. We bargain in the market square — that widening no bigger than a modest room, five stalls a crowd, goods hung on the walls — close in, face to face, like sharing a secret. If you came in speaking at field-volume, I wouldn't scold you. I'd flinch, the way you flinch at someone stamping through a sleeping house.

My step would give me away outside. Short, placed, broad feet gripping even on the flat boardwalk, as if any ground might turn into a step or a sluice or a slab about to give. I would glance up before standing still. I would never lean a ladder. I'd wedge it, and check the weep above it.

And I would not be sufficient unto myself, which is the point of living here. My roof sheds onto my neighbour's fire if I let the gutter shift. His wall is my wall; if it leans after a wet winter, we lean together, and in spring six families appear with timber wedges and mallets and good humour to jack us upright, and I feed them flat bread and eel from the drain-traps, and owe six days in return. When the warning slate is hung at the hall, I take my turn at dawn on the bottom drain with a wooden rake, old woman ahead, child behind, listening for the choked sound that means clear it now or the lower lane floods before supper. There's no ceremony in it. There's also no choice.

What I inherit isn't land. It's height, and catchment, and right-of-drip: this face for my cloth, that angle of roof for the canvas, this cellar never to be opened after heavy rain. If I married well — and people would say it kindly, over joined roof-yards with planks laid across the gaps — it would be partly for love, and partly for a sunnier drying-space, and a brother strong for jacking season, and a mother who knows clay.

In the evening I'd climb to the roof-yard, the true luxury here. A few square feet of planks, pots, a line of washing, the one pot of beans everyone pretends not to envy, leggy and suspicious in the light. Below me the lanes running clear, the swift-grey work done for another day; above my head the swifts coming back to the high eaves. My children crossing a two-plank bridge at sill-height with a full jar against the chest, steady as if it were dry ground, pit-wise long before they're world-wise.

At night we shutter tight against the damp. Almost no light escapes. Inside, a fine indoor rain might still be ticking from a beam. Through the shared wall, someone settling a stool with felt-padded feet. A murmur, settling like scree.

I would lie there, in the dry room I built over the rooms I lost, and listen to a hundred jars filling in the dark, near, far, never quite in rhythm, and know by morning there would be something to repair, something to re-lay, something fallen where — carefully, mostly — no one was standing.

Not finished. Not despairing about it either.

Just up again, barefoot on cool stone, to wring the cloths before they brim over.
