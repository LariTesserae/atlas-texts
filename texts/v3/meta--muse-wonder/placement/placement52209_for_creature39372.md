I would hear it first, and for a while that's all I would trust.

From the stair I would not understand the pump house. The boiler ticking as it warms and cools would sound too much like a voice I was meant to answer, and I would keep answering too quickly. I would not belong in the market either — six stalls is enough brightness, enough bargaining, enough eyes meeting eyes. And I would not belong up under the bats, where in the daytime you speak low or not at all beneath a ceiling that is breathing in its thousands. That dark is already occupied. It does not need me.

I would drift, at first. That is how the place would sort me.

Down past VALVE ROW when the fish are crossing overhead in the glass pipes, slow weather above the street. Down to the shallows where individual frogs call and wait, call and wait, as if each sound had to be given room to settle before the next could arrive. I would stand too long at the margin of the black pool watching the herons stand. I recognize that method of being in a place — arrive ungainly, then become so still the water forgets to be afraid of you. But the herons come down the shaft at evening and go back up it. They are neighbors, not residents. I think I would stay.

So: not in the center, because the center is water, and not at the heart of the work, because the turbine already has its priesthood who can read it like a chest. I would be found eventually on the north wall.

That is where I settle, when I let the image keep moving. The north wall, where the seepage comes through in its dozen permanent weeps and a few impermanent ones that have not yet admitted what they are. My work would be the small, repeatable work of listening for the difference.

I would have a house — if house is the word — on the second tier, close enough to ELBOW 9 that the address stenciled on the pipe is also, approximately, my name to anyone looking for me. A shed made of boiler plate and half a signal box, roofed in corrugated iron that ticks all day with condensation, never quite raining, never quite done. Notched up, like everything that matters, well above the flood line, though my floor would still go dark with damp in spring and I would learn my rooms by their drainage the way other people learn them by light. Inside: a bed, dry kindling kept like treasure, a shelf that rises a notch each year by old habit, and oilcloth — a great deal of oilcloth, because what I keep must not be allowed to get wet.

I keep the unofficial accounts.

Not the census; nobody down here wants a census and the city above is happier with its eleven. I keep the other ledger, the one the town actually lives by. Which cup catches which weep, and when that weep changed its mind. When a new drip started above the chapel door and whether it resolved, politely, into the gutter or had to be coaxed with a moss-lined channel back toward the pool. The names — because the eel-keepers do name them, crankily, fondly — of the eels taken from the pipes, the apology spoken, the meat put back into the black water so the intakes stay clean from inside. Which child was born under the green glass and did not cry long. Whose grandfather died warm by the boiler and went up the freight ramp before first shift, heads uncovered under the tell-tales. The date the fig on the third tier fruited, cold and green as a lamp. The week the clocks slipped to three minutes slow and everybody, without meeting about it, decided the pumps were right.

None of it is important in the way the surface means important. All of it is load-bearing.

My trousers are rolled to mid-calf nine months of the year. In flood season, to the knee, and I wade the lower plaza with the ledger in its oilcloth under one arm and a basket on my hip, stepping by plank from island house to island house without looking down, because looking down makes you hurry. The eels investigate my ankles. I nudge them aside, gently, with a toe, apologizing under my breath, out of reflex more than duty. My feet are broad from grating. My nails are kept very short. My hair never quite finishes drying except in the boiler room in winter, where I sit with the older women and spread it over the warm tanks while the moths collect in their soft brown snow, and for an hour I am the driest smell in town.

I am pale the way everyone here is pale — rinsed, not sickly. Forearms blue where a surface laborer would be tan. When I have to go above, which is rarely, and only ever on somebody else's business, I wear the goggles cut from chapel glass and I come home with a headache behind my eyes from everything arriving without passing through anything first, and I sit a long time on the lower steps with my shoes in my hands, letting the cool air coming up out of the grating find me again. My eyes have made the arrangement. I don't look at the bright coin of sky. I look slightly beside it, at the dark where the eyes are watching back.

I speak low, and I leave the pauses where they go. That, more than the tin cups, might be the real qualification for the north wall. A weep announces itself first as an almost — a drip that might be the start of something, or might be one true drop and then nothing for a week. You cannot decide that quickly. You stand with your palm flat to the retaining wall feeling for the soft increase of hum that means the car is coming round the second tier, or the subtler tremor of a valve three tiers up beginning, just beginning, to stick in a way that will matter in a month. People bring me things phrased as other things. *It's holding,* they say, about a pressure line, a marriage, a knee, a pregnancy, and they wait in the pause, and I listen past their words for the cycle underneath, and sometimes I say *it will keep, if no one makes it hurry,* and sometimes I just put a cup under a place that was dry yesterday and say nothing yet, because nothing has finished deciding.

At shift change I am usually out on the catwalk, because there are no bells here and I like being reminded why. The bats pour up through the shafts for twenty minutes and darken even the dimness, and the whole iron lace answers softly under people's feet going home, and the filtration tanks sigh their warm fog, and a fish crosses over Valve Row above somebody's laundry, unhurried as doctrine. In spring I sweep silt with the children after the water falls, spirals and pipe-maps and the snails' silver routes enlarged in mud, and I am the one who writes, in the damp margin of the ledger, what the pattern was before the next flood edits it. Not to preserve it. Just to have noticed it in time.

At night, true night, when the grates above are shut and the lamps go to half, you would find me where I am most myself: barefoot in an inch of perfectly clear water over iron plate, one hand resting on a warm pipe, tin cup in the other, waiting between one drop finding its level and the next. An eel turns somewhere in the shallows. The turbine breathes. The fig leaves tick with moisture. Someone crosses three tiers up and the lace of the world answers, very softly, under their weight.

I am not the heart of this place. I am something like its ear, set into the wet wall of it — keeping the small accounts, catching what seeps, feeding what must be apologized to, listening long enough that nothing in me needs to happen quickly ever again.
