I would not be the one who knows the way up.

Not the herder with the careful step, stone to stone, who leaves his digging stick in the windbreak and sweeps his ash clean and goes down again before the cloud comes. Not the caracara, who arrives so punctually he might be part of the hour, who stands too tall in the middle of the moss and listens like the whole bowl is a held breath. Not the hummingbird — that would be only a visit, a stitch of green fire pulled through the air and gone over the lip before I had learned a single flower.

I would be lower than all of that. Lower than the boot is allowed to go.

At first, in the early writing of it, I thought perhaps I would be the moss itself, because the moss is what makes the bowl a bowl in any way that matters. It is not a covering. It is a depth to sink into, a cold green sea that keeps yesterday's melt for tomorrow's thirst, humming, storing, feeding black peat underneath with its own slow dying. To be moss would be to be patient in a way that has nothing to do with waiting. A century would pass under me and be no more remarked than an afternoon.

But the moss is stepped on. The moss is broken. And something has to live *in* what the moss holds.

So I go down further. Into a pan no deeper than your hand.

It is black water cupped in rock at the foot of the largest rosette, where her skirt of dead leaves hangs down almost to the surface and keeps the worst of the wind off, and lets in exactly enough sun. If you looked for me at noon you would miss me. That is correct. I am made to be missed. Marbled brown, marbled green, peat-dark at the sides, pale flecks up my back like lichen learning a stone. A thumb's length. My eyes, when the light is straight on the water, are the only announcement: two gold beads lifted just above the skin of the pan, patient as bubbles, watching a world that is mostly too large and too loud and leaves too early.

I do not leave.

There are no seasons here to leave by. That is the first thing my body knows that yours would have to learn. Night is not a part of the day up here. Night is winter arriving on schedule, every evening, with starlight hard enough to cut and the rock ticking as it contracts. Noon is summer, also on schedule, so fierce it burns your face while your hands go numb. I live between those two violences in the space of a hand's depth of water, and I have learned not to argue with either of them.

Argument would be fur, and I have none. Argument would be a skirt of my own dead, like the rosette wears, or a hard silver furring on every leaf. I am too small and too wet for insulation I can carry. So I borrow the place, hour by hour.

At last light the bowl goes before the sky does. This never stops being strange, even after ten thousand times. The peaks around us are still rose-gold, the swifts are still up there in bright air screaming where I cannot hear them, and down here the blue shadow is already complete and the frost is already coming white across the moss in a visible tide. The flowers have shut. The finches went down-mountain a long while ago in a loose, twittering line. My pan ticks.

I feel it first in my feet. Then against my belly. The water cooling and cooling, the light going out of the algae that at noon made everything briefly, impossibly green and alive. I do not hop for shore. There is no shore that would save me. I go down, which is the opposite of fleeing.

Under the pan the black does not quite harden. There is wet peat there, and the swollen roots of moss, and the underside of a cushion plant that has been growing for a hundred years and has made, underneath itself, a dark reservoir that keeps a memory of warmth. I work myself in with small, deliberate pushes of my hind legs until only the dome of my back breaks the mud. My throat stops. The flutter you could see under my pale skin at noon spaces itself out. A beat. A long nothing in which the ice feathers up through the water above me and feathers, finer, in the skin of my own back. A beat.

I let the outer water in me turn. I hold what cannot turn in the center, around my heart, around the eggs if I am carrying the season in me — though *season* is your word; I count in thaws. A cup inside a cup inside the cup of the mountain. That is all of me that stays unfrozen, and it is enough, because I have made a practice of needing almost nothing.

And then the thaw, which is never gradual for anything my size.

Light hits the rim and spills. It strikes the pan. The ice loosens with a tick that goes through my bones before it goes through my ears. Cold bright water moves over my back in a sheet and my heart remembers, all at once, that it knows a quicker rhythm. The frost going out of my limbs is an ache so deep it is almost hunger, and then it *is* hunger, the first true thing of the day, fierce and clean. I come up into a world that has unlocked in minutes — drips falling audibly off leaves, pans steaming faintly, butterflies lifting their improbable wings off the moss — and I feed as if the afternoon were already ending, because it is.

It always is.

Black beetles ticking through the moss stems. Flies just risen off the water, stupid with light. A spider with a jeweled abdomen, still slow from the night, who did not go deep enough. My tongue is faster than your eye. My belly, translucent when I wake, clouds and swells. Everything I do must fit inside this brief summer: feed, warm, sing, lay. My children do not get a childhood. They get intervals. Tails trembling in water gone green at noon, stalling at dusk, going torpid in the peat before they have legs, before they have lungs, suspended and resumed and suspended again until one morning one of them hauls itself onto the moss with a tail still trailing and sits there stunned by air, exactly the color of the only place it will ever know.

I do not sing until the caracara has gone. You would understand this caution if you had ever been mud under that listening.

Then: *tok.*

One note. I do not waste more. Sound does not travel well up here anyway; it flattens after twenty paces, as if the thin air refuses to carry gossip. But it crosses my pan and touches wet rock, and after a long pause, from another pan across a stretch of deep moss I could not cross dry, an answer.

*Tok.*

It is not music. It is geography. It says where the water is this week, because the water is never in quite the same place twice. A pan cracks in the night and drains by morning. A new hollow fills where melt has found a seam. And so I redraw my whole world nightly, moving at the wettest hour, after the cloud has poured itself over the western rim and filled the bowl to the lip and soaked every furred leaf and gone again — moving not over the moss but through it, swimming through green depth from reservoir to reservoir, feeling for the next black stillness with my feet.

I know things that require staying.

I knew the rosette above my pan was going to flower weeks before the spike rose taller than a person, dense with orange bells. I knew it by the chemistry of her fallen leaves changing in my water. I laid my eggs in her shade *because* she was dying — dying, she let more light reach the pan, and warmer water means faster children, and faster is the only mercy here. She stands brown now in her own skirt and seedlings bristle around her feet that you would never notice, and I am still here, in the pan she made possible.

I know which cushions are hollow and which are solid to the core. I know the difference between the herder's step now — the learned one, stone to stone, never on the green domes — and the other step, the one I felt once go through fifty years of growth in a single careless moment, cold water rushing into the broken dome, a hole that will still be there when his grandchildren come up the ridge. I did not rise that day at all.

I know the cloud by the way it erases. I know noon by the smell of resin and wet earth let loose all at once. I know the nightly frost not as weather but as a tide that takes my breath, my movement, the water in my skin, and gives them back altered.

So stay once, if you come. Stay past the hour you were warned. Sit in the stone circle with your back to the windbreak and watch the pans go over with their ticking skins, the moss go white at the edges, the last swift cut away into air still bright above the rim. And do not think, because nothing moves, that the bowl has emptied.

It has only gone inward.

Under the ice of that black pan, under the hanging skirt of dead leaves, in the wet dark at the root of everything slow and green, I am there with my heart doing its spaced and stubborn work. Once. Then nothing — a nothing so complete you would call it death if you held me in your warm hand and waited only as long as humans wait. Then, in its own good time, not yours:

once again.

I am what lives here.
