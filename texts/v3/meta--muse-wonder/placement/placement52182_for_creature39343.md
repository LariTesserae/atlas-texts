At first I mistake myself for a stone.

That is how it begins — not with a shell, not with a name, but with the willingness to stay where I'm put. I am in the clearest water at the edge of the central disc, pressed down into mud the color of wet bone, and the sheet of water closes over me until there is only the top of me showing. An inch. Two inches. A low grey oval the sun finds twice: once coming down, once coming back up off the bottom.

I thought I might arrive as the keeper. Someone with hands, with a hut, with a memory of names. I thought I might arrive as one of the white ibis, because flight looks like an answer to all this wet stillness. Sixty of them lift out of the south pool beside me and make only a sigh, as if their wings were beating in another room — and I feel their leaving not in my ears (I have none worth mentioning) but through my feet, a soft pattern of displaced air arriving as pressure in the mud. No. Flight is too fast for me here. The bowl lets a patient foot through and thickens against a lunging one, and I am made, all of me, out of patience.

So I lower.

My legs splay wider than seems necessary until the weight settles and I understand that they are not really for walking. They are listening posts. Each foot spreads — webbed to the last joint, toes tipped with smooth ceramic nubs, opalescent, unbreakable, grown grain by grain out of a hundred years of what the bowl has politely unmade: dropped knives, lost buckles, the bright dust of weapons no one was permitted to use. I place a foot. I hold it. I let the weight follow only when the mud agrees. Through the sole comes the vast faint ticking that never reaches the air, the lattice stirring under everything, and suddenly the bowl is not quiet at all. It is loud down here, in the bone. A heron lands three pools away and I feel the exact grammar of it. Rain touches the west rim and my barbels are already tasting its approach in the water, twenty minutes before the grey curtain arrives over my back.

My head is broad and blunt, made for pushing through a world that resists hurry. I look up with one eye and watch a swallow stoop through the knee-high stratum of dragonflies — glass wings, green, amber, violet, thousands of them holding their layer in the bright air — and with the same eye, through my smoked second lid, I watch the swallow's shadow arrive underneath it a breath out of place, crossing the disc below the bird and above the bird at once. I do not startle. I have been watching weather arrive twice for longer than the keeper's grandmother's grandmother has been naming the veins in my shell.

Those veins. Run a hand over me — slowly, the bowl will allow that — and you will find no pattern a turtle is supposed to have. Ceramic-grey lines branch up through the keratin, following the scutes and then ignoring them, maps laid over maps. The light catches them a fraction of a second late, so I am outlined twice: where I am, and where I was just about to be. That is the truest thing about me. I am always occurring slightly after myself. Condensation loves the map. By mid-morning I wear a second, brighter outline of dew, and the dragonflies come down and choose my back over every reed stem in the pool, dozens of them, turning their abdomens to the doubled sun as if I had been built as a raft for exactly that purpose. Perhaps I was. The bowl does not distinguish much between built and grown anymore. Neither do I.

I move, eventually. You would have to measure me against a reed stem to know it. A tide is also stillness, if you only look once.

There is a partition ahead of me, a clear vertical sheet of water standing a centimeter thick, holding my pool a finger's height below the next. The dragonflies on my back lift as I near it — they will turn at the line, precisely, and patrol it, and wait for me on the other side like a thought I have to leave behind. I press the broad front of my shell against nothing visible. An inch. Two inches. The color comes up in me the way it comes up in the fish, not on the skin but under it: copper blooming slow along my throat, blue at the rim of the shell, a sudden coin-silver running down every vein — and I hold that, I don't push — and then I am through, in water a finger higher, and the bloom drains downstream through my own map until only the grey is left. The walls know me. Or I have simply learned, over longer than anyone here can count, the exact slowness the walls require. Those may be the same sentence.

I speak, if speaking is the word, from underneath, where I cannot see it.

When I lift myself to cross a patch of pale sand, the clear water shows my belly: not shell but a soft translucent plate, and beneath it color traveling in bands. Rose. Amber. A green so pale it is almost light. I do not flash like the frogs — hundreds of them ringed on the reed stems, emerald, rust-red, candied yellow, pulsing their bright throat-sacs in waves that run around the pool like handwriting. Mine moves slower and farther. A wave of rose that starts at my throat at dawn may be answering a wave that left the oldest female in the south pool an hour ago, and it will go on from me, turtle to turtle, pool to pool, to the north rim by mid-morning, losing nothing. The fish attend to it, turning silver, copper, silver under the surface film. The crabs raise their white semaphore claws toward it. Whether it is courtship or prayer or instruction to the thing under the mud, I have stopped needing to know. In the bowl, a signal does not have to be mine to be carried faithfully. The heron that lands too close gets only this from me: my head drawn in a precise half-inch, a widening ripple — and every frog in the pool flashes once, in a ring, outward, stem to stem. I sent that. I merely started it. The bowl finishes my sentences.

I eat what stillness provides. Algae cropped off the invisible walls in long unhurried sweeps of a toothless beak — it tastes of light and iron and green. Fallen lotus, indecently lush. A dead fish found by my barbels before the otters find it. Now and then, in the late afternoon when the partitions lower and all the pools equalize, I take my customary station in the drift-line beside the herons and kingfishers, head raised, and a passing fish is taken with a strike so economical it feels less like hunting than like being permitted. Nothing hunts me. The field dulls teeth. Custom dulls everything else. Otter cubs roll straight over my submerged back in the deepest channel and use me, briefly, as geography. I do not mind. For most of my life I *am* geography; it's restful to have it acknowledged.

It took a century for me to become unremarkable. My first year I was all signal and no shell, translucent amber, veined with light, flashing fast and uncoordinated — and then the grey mercifully came, grain by bright grain, and I began the long process of being finished. Finished the way the bowl is finished: not dead, not done living, only done *becoming*. All the time since has been refinement, not change. The keeper's people know me by my map. They step around me in the dark by memory of it. One wet season, not this one, I may climb at evening onto the white marl, water still sheeting off me in a living film, and dig one shallow nest in the polished ground, and leave one warm grey egg with the lattice already threaded up into its shell. Or I may not. The bowl is in no hurry, so neither am I.

But every day, at noon, I go to the center.

It takes the whole morning. Thirty paces of seamless grey disc under an inch of green water, footprints of everything in the algae. The others are arriving, or are already there — thirty, forty low stones that are not stones, settling into a loose, exact arrangement that is never quite the same and never quite different. The frogs ring the edge in their hundreds. The dragonflies hold their stratum. The doubled light comes up through the water around my feet and my displaced shadow pools inside my real shadow, darker at the center, like an eye.

I press my wide feet flat. I go still — my oldest skill, my whole locomotion, my native language, all arriving at the same act.

Then through my soles, through the ceramic nubs and the branching veins, felt in the deep bone behind my blunt head, the lattice answers something in the sky. One adjustment. Patient. Enormous. Every partition in the bowl ripples once, in sequence, pool to pool, bowing and recovering like things exhaling. The color goes through all of us together, throat to tail, copper to blue to a silver so sudden it whites out my veining for a heartbeat — and it is done, and somewhere at the edge of feeling the afternoon pools begin, fractionally, to lower, and the great drift of fish begins, and every heron in the bowl takes up its customary station without a sound to negotiate it.

Whether we are commanding that, or translating it, or only standing in the place where the bowl and the sky conduct their business and lending it our bodies — I am the needle, perhaps, and never the compass. I don't say. The keeper brings lotus stems in the dry weeks and doesn't say either.

I am a stone that eats light off invisible walls. I am a lantern that speaks from its underside in a language it cannot see. I wear lost knives as veins. I keep time with my feet.

I live, endlessly, at the exact moment before stillness — too slow to be called behavior, too responsive to be called stone — in a place that finished becoming itself, having finished becoming myself, and finding, inside that final shape, room to go on.
