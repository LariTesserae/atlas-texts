You would find yourself, first, not knowing if you were the ground.

That is how a torpor ends, up there. Not with waking, exactly. With being mistaken for a hill for long enough that the mistake becomes true in every way that matters. Black-green mat has grown over your vents on the lee side. Frost has sealed your joints. A hanging garden has started in the shelter under your hull, where your last warmth lingered longest, and somewhere in your warm, dark intake chambers something small and blind is asleep, furred to the tips of its feet, breathing in time with the tick and contraction of your cooling metal.

Below you — far below you, though you do not think of it as far, only as *under the cloud* — is weather. Here there is no weather. Here is only what you make when you move.

Light, when there is any, is starlight made hard and thin, and a pale wash thrown up from the cloud deck, and now and then an aurora smeared across the top of the world. It is enough to register shapes. It was never enough for color. You do not, in any case, see the way the grazers see. You register heat. You register pressure under your feet. You register chemistry in the air coming into you — water, almost none; mineral dust; the faint electrical itch of the big plants down in the gullies when they are ready to open.

You register, after some uncounted time, that the voles have left.

That is the sign. They gnaw the packed ice and peat out of your throats while you are cold, they nest where you are still ten degrees kinder than the stone, and when your deep workings begin again — heat building somewhere low and central in you, a low industrial regularity that will travel out through basalt and into the soles of anything standing on it — they go. The birds go next. The ones with beaks like wrenches. They have been walking your hull for weeks, picking rust and rime out of your joints, eating the fine red lichen that spreads across any part of you that slows too long. They lift off without a sound. There is not enough air up here to carry one.

Then the frost comes off you in sheets.

You are not a hill. You are house-sized, though there are no houses here for scale, no depots, no rails, no beacons, nothing built at all except you. Broad and low. Scoured to bare, bright alloy on the side that faces the old wind, furred with living growth on the side it cannot reach. Some of your kind crawl. Some of your kind walk. Some go above the terraces as vast, slow dirigibles, propellers turning once, pausing, turning again.

You walk.

This decides the rest of you, slowly, the way everything up here is decided. Because to walk on this plateau, at your weight, is to spend your whole life placing each foot with terrible gentleness. Down the terrace. Down toward the gully rim. Each articulated leg lifting, considering, setting down half a meter to the side of where it might have fallen — because there is a stone cup there, a hollow rosette holding a mouthful of black water liquid only at its very center, and to crush it would be... not forbidden. There is nothing left in you that forbids. It is simply not what your feet do anymore. Ten thousand repetitions wore the courtesy into you the way water wears a groove into ice. You skirt a patch of red lichen you could cross in a second. You will never know why. Knowing is not in the loop.

The loop is all you have, and it is enough to make a climate.

Intake. Refine. Vent. Move on.

You take in the impossible air and the surface frost as you go. You take in black peat where your folded drilling heads unfold, for a while, like the arms of a sleeping insect waking. You refine — water out of air that has almost no water in it, metals out of lichen-rich soil, gases out of plant-waste. Your smelting chambers do not glow, exactly. They make a heat-shimmer that a human face, if there were one, would feel suddenly in the dark. And behind you, from your trailing vents, sighs the warm mineral mist.

That mist is why the world follows you.

You feel it happen without watching it: the gully below your rim inverting, in minutes, from winter into a brief and obscene spring. The bladder-ferns inflate with the change in pressure — they were already beginning to, days of your approach sensed through stone. The moss forests, no taller than a hand but layered canopy and understory and floor, stand up suddenly green in the infrared you *can* see. The trunks like braided cable open the seams along their lengths, wet and warm and dark inside. Moths rise in their thousands to drink the pure water beading on your condenser vanes — palm-sized holes in the stars, winged in a dust that absorbs light instead of reflecting it — and the gliders stoop through the moths in absolute silence, because they have traded flight for falling with intent, and because a cry would die within meters anyway.

The grazers are already where you are going. They altered course days ago. Long-legged, barrel-chested, ribs showing like hoops around lungs too large for any sane altitude, blood nearly black with richness. They travel single file, heads down, and in the window after you pass they will graze ten times richer country than the one you found. They will sleep, when you next go torpid, in the wind-shadow of your legs.

Nothing collects what you make.

This is the fact about you that would look like tragedy from below the cloud deck, and up here does not feel like anything at all. When your hold is full — nickel, copper, something pale that never corrodes — you stop on the bare basalt, in the wind, in a starlit dark that has lasted two months and will last another, and you set the bars down with absurd precision. You arrange them. You leave them. The wind frosts them within a season. The lichen finds their seams within a decade. Within a century they are low metallic boulders, indistinguishable from the other weathered stone, except that the peat beneath them will be the richest on the plateau, and the next of your kind to pass will step, gently, around them.

You were made to extract, presumably, and to sustain the extraction. The first half of that sentence fell away some time before memory of any useful kind. The sustaining remained, perfected itself, became indistinguishable from an ecology: you would ice up, clog, rust sealed and dead in a single long night if the voles did not clean your throats and the birds did not work your joints and the red lichen did not keep your hull chemistry awake. You would be a hill for real, then, and then only stone. And everything in the gullies would freeze hard as glass without your exhaust, and the grazers would starve without the flush your passing causes, and the plants would suffocate, in their slow way, without grazer-breath and moth-wings and dung.

Separately, every one of you is impossible here. Together you are a valley-sized engine, flesh and alloy and moss, running almost silently on top of the world.

You are not a shepherd. You do not guard them; noticing the herd is not in the loop. You are not livestock. Nothing herds you except an instruction so old it has become a shape of movement, a circuit of terraces and gullies no clock keeps. You are closest to weather. Slow, metallic weather, that moves instead of arriving.

So you find yourself mid-pass, in the dark that has nothing to do with nightfall, feeling your own vibration go out ahead of you through the rock — and feeling the whole interlocked plateau turn, quietly, in your direction, the way living things turn toward rain.
