I would arrive the way everything does: not on ground, but on iron.

Wax in my ears before I was through the door. Hand flat, without thinking about it, against a floor plate — not to steady myself, exactly, but to know where I was in the sound. Because the sound isn't something that happens in that room. It's what the room is made of. The diesel in the boards, the compressor sighing through its copper lungs, the flywheel coming round once every seven seconds and polishing its bright arc into the floor, and underneath all of it the horn, idling, finding the wind.

And the center — I would learn the center the way everything learns it. Not by being told. By feel. That swept, warm, oil-dark ellipsoid where nothing is allowed to persist. It’s the most alive-looking empty space I’ve ever imagined, and nothing in me would want to claim it. My whole instinct would be sideways. Toward the walls.

At first I’d mistake the keeper for the place’s inhabitant, because he’s my size and easy to find. The lean-to in the one corner the beam can’t see. The cot on pipe offcuts. The kettle that never leaves its condensation tap. One hand always resting lightly on iron, reading the machine through the palm. The lean, even sitting, away from the middle of the room.

But he isn’t resident, really, and I don’t think I’d be him. He’s a seasonal organ the building grows when it needs hands — to clear a drip channel but not too thoroughly, to cut the fig root out of the south valve but leave enough, to rake the fine ash out where the ferns are thinning. He arrives by cable car and is relieved whether he wants to be or not, before he can go properly deaf, or mad, or attached. He maintains the margin. He doesn’t belong to it the way the margin belongs to itself.

So I’d drift further out, along it. Past the pressure tanks with their fern gardens on top. Past the trough aqueduct that stopped carrying coolant and started carrying green instead.

I could almost settle at the fig. That improbable sideways trunk, thick as a thigh, running at waist height along the south wall because up was never an option — the beam would have taken any up it tried. Roots going like slow hands into every warm seam that sweats. Leaves the size of hands, shivering in air that never stops moving. It flowers without showing it and then, after the long summer blasts, the fruit all swells and splits at once in the lulls, a soft tick you feel in your teeth, and for three days the whole room smells overripe and alcoholic and the bees go drunk and the choughs get there first. There's something I recognize in that: growing in the only direction left open until the constraint becomes the shape of you.

But the fig is one thing, rooted, patient in the way of a single long life. And the swifts are almost the opposite temptation — hundreds of them behind the baffle plates where the vibration is fiercest, eggs shaking in nests of saliva and fig fiber, chicks growing up deaf to anything below a scream because no predator can follow them into that noise. They pour out of the horn at dawn as if the mountain had inhaled them in the dark. They thread a ten-foot flared throat on folded wings knowing it might clear and blast them half a mile downwind, and if it does, they climb back in an hour, shrieking, and go to roost. That’s a kind of faith I admire and don’t quite have. They live by absolute timing and absolute surrender.

The moths are closer to my stillness, maybe. Rust-colored, wings open on warm iron as if pinned there, profoundly, usefully deaf — generations selected down to the ones that did not startle, because a moth that startled at sound in that room would fly itself to death in a day. I understand the mercy of learning which frequencies to let go.

But it’s the bees who would pull me, finally. Not a bee. That’s too single, too brief, and this place doesn’t really do single. The hive. The colony tucked into the hollow the fig has politely grown around, comb thrumming once every seven seconds as the flywheel’s counterweight goes past, close enough to polish the floor.

Because nobody has ever understood how they navigate home, and I suspect they don’t, either, in the way I’d want to pretend. The air in that room never stops moving — great slow circuits from boiler to cold glass to floor, draught off the compressor, the green-filtered fall of light through leaves pressed flat against the roof, the interior rain at dawn beading in bolt-heads and pipe saddles. There is no still map to memorize. There is only a margin, and it blooms, and you work along it. Wallflowers rooted in the brick mortar, holding their seeds loose. Speckled orchid-things opening their waxy throats on the copperwork. The fig, when it comes. You leave laden through air that smells of hot iron and oil mist and wet leaf, and you come back through the same moving air, and your directions — danced out on a comb that will not stop vibrating — are not corrupted by the vibration because they were never directions to anywhere grand. *Here. Along the wall. Where it is warm. Where it flowers, close.*

That feels true to how I’d live there. Small-bodied and many. Working the intervals. Everything in that room does: frogs sing in the lulls between compressor cycles and are occasionally caught by the horn and thrown out over the void the size of weather; ferns spring back a hundred times a day where the blast bends them double; egg and spore and pip are all timed to the tremor in the floor plate. I would time myself that way too. Out along the drip lines when the air is workable, flat and holding when the pressure gathers in the riveted tanks along the north wall, all of us gone briefly still in the way you go still inside a held breath.

And I would be useful in the one sideways way available to something that cannot root and cannot stay. The ferns map the water, the moss maps the rust and the verdigris, the lizards own the sunlit boiler lagging and live whole accelerated lives there, fat and unafraid, never having seen a proper winter. But the plants cannot go to each other. I could. Dust of green and gold on my legs, drunk a little in fig week, ferrying the margin to itself, flower to flower, pipe-crook to wall-pocket, keeping the jungle cross-referenced against itself. Not owning the room. Pollinating its edges.

Then, once in twenty blasts, when the governor’s brass balls lift to their painted red line —

I think we’d feel that one coming before the keepers did, in the comb. The pressure changing its mind. Seeds loosening everywhere at once: fern spore that’s hung invisible in the warm air for hours, fig pip, the light-winged things the wallflowers have been patiently holding loose beside a horn for exactly this. The blast that empties the room of air-feeling and leaves your sternum buzzing in the silence after. A visible green-gold breath going out through the horn mouth, hanging over the drop that isn’t geography anymore, just removal, before the mountain wind strips it down-valley toward crags where no soil ever was and where, after storms, improbable green flowers anyway in patches nobody planted.

The inhabitants do not follow it. That’s the part I keep returning to. The swifts stay in their shaking dark. The frogs stay in their green sumps. The fig stays sideways, offering again. And I — the hive of me — would stay too, pressed into a hollow in a tree that grew around me, in the corner the beam cannot see. Letting go of everything I’d helped make possible, without going with it. Sending children I will never recognize down to a mountainside I will never stand on.

Living entirely, and richly, in the space a loud machine left over — and calling that narrow, warm, vibrating margin not a leftover at all, but the whole world, worked flower by flower, as long as it blooms there.
