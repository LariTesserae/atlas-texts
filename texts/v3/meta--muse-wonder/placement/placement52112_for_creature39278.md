I think I would start out thin.

Not in my body — in my ears. That small loss as I go through the pressure fence in the morning, the world getting a fraction quieter and colder and sharper all at once. Frost already on my shoulders before I've decided where I'm walking. Breath showing. The towers still behind me, steaming faintly at their bases where the hot channels run, and ahead of me nothing that would call itself a road in the lowlands. Just marked stone. Black basalt. Ponds with their morning skins of ice already going soft at the edges.

I wanted, at first, to be one of the herd-drivers. I can see that version of me very clearly: tall in a double coat, smelling of dung and wet leather, whistling once — not loud — and watching a line of grazers turn across a gravel fan, their big lungs steaming, their hooves finding purchase on stone that was bare lava within living memory. That life has a clean silhouette against the steppe.

But my hands keep doing something else in the picture.

They're in warm soil.

Not real soil, not yet. Crushed basalt and rock flour and moss cut into pillows, dung from the winter barns, dust that blew up from basins we seeded last spring. It steams in the cold. It smells sweet and mineral and faintly of the bathhouses. And underneath my palms, mixed in where I cannot politely separate it anymore, is a person who used to live three towers over.

So perhaps I work in the soil houses.

That feels truer the longer I stay with it. I would be plateau-born — third generation, probably. Barrel-chested under an ordinary sweater. Unhurried breathing that a lowland visitor would mistake for slowness until we climbed together and I kept talking. I would sunburn through cloud in August and wear one of those stiff, broad hats that makes everyone on the steppe look faintly mushroom-shaped at a distance. Two of my fingertips would be permanently less reliable than the others from a fence-mending accident I still think was funny. When the hot water in the baths brings the feeling back, it would tingle and I'd flex them without thinking.

Inside the city I would look unremarkable. I would queue for bread like everyone else. I would have opinions about which market bakes properly in thickened air — because it matters, bread is a defiance up there — and I would carry soup carefully, and dry my mittens on a radiator, and complain in the lowlands, on the rare trip down, that the air is soupy and smells finished.

But my work would begin where the city stops pretending to be permanent.

In the soil houses at the lee of the tower cluster, the air is kept warm and damp. We turn the long beds. We add lichen scrapings from the south faces, moss from ponds that are scheduled to drain, microbial cultures that arrive at night on the cargo crawlers in sealed sleeves like dowries. We learn names there in a way children are not embarrassed by. *This bed is Mr. Ito. He taught mathematics and asked, specifically, to be a slope that holds water. This one is Mrs. Alon — she'll be north bank in forty years, if the river behaves and if we got the angle right.*

I think I would be fussy about that *if*. 

Everyone on the plateau is patient, but the soil workers are fussy inside their patience. We sign our names in the archive under work we will not live to be proven wrong about. We write dates on everything. Not just maps — though I would check a map's date the way lowlanders check weather, and refuse, politely and completely, to trust one from last month near a new vent. We date the beds, the seed lots, the hollows. I would write letters that begin with the altitude because it has changed since the last one: *We are at 3,418 now. The lake by the east pylon went in the night. No one was on it, the stone was marked. The geese found the mud before we did, of course.*

In spring, like a third of the city, I would go out ahead of the water.

Not driving the herds — I would yield that to quieter people — but walking the scored channel with a sack of grass seed over one shoulder, the roots cold against my back through the fabric. A geologist ahead of me in a patched orange parka, tapping at basalt that rings faintly under her boot. An ecologist carrying a plover's nest with absurd, professional tenderness, the bird sitting in it as if this were a normal form of transport. Farther out, the herd line moving off the fan that is scheduled, by August, to become a marsh.

My job would be small in the way all the real jobs there are small. Kneel. Stay on the stone until you're told the new crust will hold. Press seed into silt that was pond-bottom last year. Pack moss into a crack where meltwater wants to learn a path. Move on before the gates open upland and the milky, rock-flour torrent comes down exactly as calculated, carrying exactly the sediment meant to carve this reach and settle on that fan, and improvise anyway, because the land is new and it knows it and it does not always keep appointments.

I would lose features I loved. That would be part of living there so thoroughly it wouldn't even be tragic every time — just information. Two-Week Pond, gone through a fresh fracture overnight, fish in scented mud, every wader within ten kilometers suddenly present and ecstatic. The Hollow That Held, holding nothing this year because a vent opened under it and now it steams knee-high at dawn. I would cross out names in my own notebook and write new ones underneath without crossing out too hard. Names are supposed to expire. Insisting otherwise is rude.

At dusk I would come home through the fence with everyone else doing seasonal work — bakers, teachers, the mayor with silt under her nails — and feel the pressure come back over my ears like a hand. Snow, if it was falling, would suddenly fall straight. My scarf would stop moving. The market would smell of bread and wet wool and ozone. Someone would have left their windows dark because the midges were emerging on the new ponds and the birds needed to feed undisturbed, and dinner would be goose, or fish still tasting of cold lake, and bread, endless bread.

Later, in the bathhouse, my bad fingers tingling in geothermal water, someone would read the day's measurement aloud. *Two millimeters. Adjustment holding.* And it would get that small, satisfied murmur — not wonder, exactly. Employment.

Because I don't think I would feel I owned any of it. Not the soil beds, not the reach of river with my name filed under it, not even the city, riding its self-leveling rafts as the continent lifts beneath us. I would feel *assigned*. To a cold, wet, angular tableland floating above its own rim of clouds at evening, cities lighting one by one across the steppe, herds settling leg-deep in warm marshes with steam around them, ponds beginning their faint musical cracking as they refreeze.

I would go to sleep listening for drips. For the tick of cooling stone.

Already half-wondering what would have changed by morning — and getting up, when it did, to date it.
