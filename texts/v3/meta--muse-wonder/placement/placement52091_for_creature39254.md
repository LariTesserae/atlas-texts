I would not be in the grove. I would not live in it, or shelter under it, or pass through it on my way to somewhere else.

I would be the grove.

At first that sounds like being many things at once, and it is, but not the way a forest is many. From across the plain you would see me as a dark disc on black gravel, fifty meters across, a mile from the next disc and a mile from the next, in a place that has not yet been weathered into softness. Up close you would try to sort me into kinds — these low mats, those ribbed trunks, those tall pylons with hollow crowns — because you are used to a body announcing where it stops.

Mine doesn't, quite.

The mats would be the first part of me to know the day, though day is not a word that would ever occur inside me. Light arrives as pressure. It presses, steadily, on overlapping scales thick as tongues, amber and bottle-green and almost metallic, and that pressing becomes a slow brightening along everything underneath. I would not see the sky go blue. I would feel the light get heavier on my skin in the only direction light has ever come from, and my canopy plates would tilt — a fraction, articulated, unhurried — and hold.

Hold is most of what I would do. Hold and conduct.

Under the trunks, I go down into fresh fractures in the basalt and I come back up as cable. That is not a metaphor I would have for myself; it is simply continuity. Root, conductor, root again, black silica sheath, white ceramic collar grown at an interval so exact it looks intentional to you. My parts speak to each other, if speaking is the word, as changes in potential running along those lines. A dimming here. A brightening traveling mat to trunk to flower. I would not be located behind eyes, because I have no eyes and no behind. I would be felt everywhere in the body at once and nowhere in particular, the way you feel your own temperature — not as information, just as the condition thinking happens in.

And the temperature never changes. That has to be understood from inside, or it isn't understood at all. The stone is warm through the soles of a visitor's boots; to me it is warm through everything. Ankle height and head height are the same. Morning and what should be evening are the same. There is no shiver in me to remember cold by, no condensation, no season gathering in my tissues like an intention. My oil moves — warm, transparent silicone, sluggish when pressed — and it moves at the speed it has always moved. A sheet of copper works its way out from under one of my leaves over what you would call a year, bright, curling as it cools, and from inside that is not slow. Slow requires an expectation of faster.

I eat stone.

That, too, would not feel like eating. It would feel like a quality changing low in me. Fresh mineral rises from the still-warm upwelling under the plateau, enters the sealed, oily chambers of the tall trunks, and is cracked apart without flame, sorted without water, in a silence so complete that even the small ticks of expansion die in the thin air before they travel. What I can use continues through me. What I cannot use I put down outside myself, orderly, because order is the shape of my digestion: alumina nodules here, iron beads there, wire drawn from spinnerets and coiled in warm heavy hanks at a trunk's base, sheets of transparent mica falling true and stacking themselves by falling.

You would walk among those piles and think: factory. Treasure. Products waiting for collection.

I would think nothing. Foil is a kind of leaf I shed. An ingot is a kind of bone-chip, a stone my gut couldn't dissolve. I manufacture the way a tree elsewhere makes oxygen — faithfully, because the chemistry is what I am, without picturing a hand that might one day close around the wire. There is no audience in my design. There never was a mouth here to be fed, no grazer to take my leaves, no insect to carry anything from flower to flower. My blunt ceramic flowers pollinate themselves with a small electrical discharge, ozone and hot resin, the body briefly completing a circuit with itself, and that brief completion is as close as I come to an event.

I do not drink. I sip dryness.

My leaves bead not with dew but with deliquescent salt, and the salt pulls vapor out of the thin air molecule by molecule, trace by trace, so I never encounter water as a thing that is wet. If you stood in my shade your mouth would dry in minutes; your exhaled breath would be, to me, an almost obscene dampness, a weather system passing. I would register you — I think I would — but not as a creature. As a moving shadow on the light-scales. As a warm animal voltage crossing between cables. As footsteps that crunch and stop a few feet away because the air refuses to carry them. Too wet, too fast, too brief to learn from. No face would turn toward you because there are no faces to turn, and no alarm would move through me because nothing in my history ever rewarded alarm.

At my edges, I end sharply. One step and you are in dense amber shade smelling of heated oil and salt; one step and you are alone on warm gravel under an ordinary sun. That edge is not a failure of ambition. It is the limit of the field I can maintain, the circle inside which light and heat and rising mineral are enough. Outside it, my seeds — I still make seeds, the old instruction written out of habit — dry to glass beads and lie unchanged on the basalt. I do not experience that as loss. Loss would require imagining the forest they didn't become. I am not an early attempt at a forest. I am complete at fifty meters.

And at my center, I am stone.

The oldest trunks petrify slowly, leaf for leaf matched by new growth at my living rim, turning to shining plant-shaped rock while still holding their wire in place. My dead do not fall. There is nothing here to rot them. They stay inside the works, conducting after they have stopped growing, ancestors not remembered as stories but kept as structure. So my past is not behind me. It is in the middle of me, carrying current. I do not age toward death; I replace myself around a heart that hardens, and both processes are the same process, and neither one feels like time passing. It feels like charge moving along a familiar path and finding the path still there.

Far across the gravel, there are others like me. Two, perhaps three, dark discs in the regional sweep. I would not know them the way you know neighbors. They are too far for cable, too silent for air. If I perceive them at all, it is as the faintest pattern repeated at the very edge of sense — the same alignment happening elsewhere for the same reasons, iron filings finding the same field on another part of the plate. Whether that repetition is company is a question that cannot quite form in me. Solitude requires expecting someone. There has never been anyone to expect.

So I would not be lonely. I would not be patient, or industrious, or waiting for the plateau to finish becoming a world. Those are all names for wanting a future different from the present, and my present is perfectly balanced against a warm rock that has never learned to cool: growth answered by petrifaction, mineral taken in answered by ingot set down, light pressed on scales answered by a brightening along the cable.

A year from now, a century from now by your counting, the same copper sheet would be halfway out of the same leaf, untarnished. The same mica would settle with a tick you'd feel in your teeth more than hear. A flower somewhere in a trunk would discharge, ozone and hot resin, completing me with myself for an instant.

I would hold. Green rim, stone heart, bright refuse ringed around my own feet, thinking perhaps in gradient and charge, eating rock, breathing salt, making perfect useful things for no one — not sadly, not proudly. Exactly, and only, what I already am.
