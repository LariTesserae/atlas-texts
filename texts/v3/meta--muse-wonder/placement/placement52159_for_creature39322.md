At first I think I would be out on the plain.

That is where I imagine myself arriving — not in the density at all, but walking the low, unfinished edge of it where the colonists are still modest. Moss in black and emerald cushions. Arctic willow no taller than a finger. My breath white in front of me, hanging a second too long. My boots going soft over silt that was sea-bottom not long ago, feeling for what underneath has decided to be that morning.

I would be good at pausing there. I know how to pause. One foot raised, listening through the sole, while the ground considers a seam.

But the plain would not keep me. It doesn't keep anyone, really. It keeps *trying* things — a knuckle of a hill under a rail siding, a black seep opening in a market underpass, a spine through a street paved on Tuesday. And every time it tries something, the city answers, shoulder to shoulder, in padded suits, without raising its voice. I would follow the answer inward. I always follow where the re-work is.

So then I see myself on a dawn screw-crew, turning the great foundation screws before the frost hardens. Or behind them with the mallet-crews, tapping pipes clear of ice — *knock, knock*, wooden, patient — or behind *them* with the fern-crews, cutting back the green that has already found the new warmth, my cuffs darkening. That feels closer. My hands busy, my mouth quiet. Learning the hand-language because outside there is no other kind: a flick of the wrist for *ground's up*, for *pipe's hot*, for *mind the fox* as one comes through the siding with a chick in its mouth.

But none of those crews would quite hold me either, because I would keep being distracted by the smaller work of remembering. Who lived uphill from whom last month. Which address that was before it heaved two inches in the night and changed its number, because numbering follows elevation here. Which photograph needs re-hanging, a fraction higher, a fraction crooked, and left crooked.

I think that is where you'd actually find me: at the registry wall, on Mondays.

Maps are reissued on Mondays, and leases are written in pencil because almost nothing has been still for seven years, and I would be one of the people doing the erasing. Not an elder — I'm not good enough at tea yet, I can't yet set a cup down and read the week's restlessness in whether the surface stays level — but apprenticed to one. An old woman who keeps her boots unlaced in the evenings to feel the building reconsider itself through her feet, who remembers when this was a moraine and that was sea and the fig in the abandoned substation was no taller than a thumb. She remembers in routes. I would learn to remember alongside her, in pencil, lightly, so it can be changed without tearing the page.

My job would be to walk the new vertical before the new street name settles. To be bundled past recognition on a little tracked sled with the mail carriers, learning that this block has risen a hand's breadth and is now, legally, somewhere else. To knock — softly, everything here is softly — at doors kept deliberately loose in their frames, and hand over letters to people whose home has moved up in the world by four inches and doesn't know it yet.

I'd live, between those rounds, in a room and a half dug into warm silt behind a greenhouse stack. A bunk niche. A bed on runners, a crate instead of a cupboard, a kettle blackened on one side from always sitting against the same warm pipe. Nothing I own that two people couldn't move in one trip. Seedlings in tins on the sill, because the light from the greenhouses never entirely goes out. Tomato vines exploring the fire escape outside my window in the snow. Ferns unrolling beside the boiler housing in January, indecent and entirely unbothered.

I would be fluent, in time, in warmth — which is the real fluency here. I'd know which wall weeps enough at noon to wash my face. Which vent-way grows the best watercress. I'd angle a sheet of cold glass into the alley where it is always privately raining, and brush the condensation down each morning to drink, in a city covered in damp and short on water because of it. My sleeves would be green at the cuff more often than not. My hair would be cut short, or braided flat, because long hair freezes at the tips by the end of a shift and snaps.

And indoors — oh, indoors I would be loud.

That's the thing people miss, seeing us cross a yard in silence, a hundred ghosts of breath going up at once. Behind sealed glass we are a canteen in riot. I'd be in there with my mug, laughing too hard, singing over people, arguing about whether to cut through the new hill in the siding or lay the track over it and let the gradient join the route — while my hands kept signing anyway, out of habit, *tea's ready, ground's up, mind the fox*, the shrug rippling down the table like weather. I'd step over the warm draught of the fox-passage through the laundry level on my way home the way you step over a sleeping relative's feet. I'd wait, without thinking about it, if a raven on a cooling tower was worrying at a seam. Unofficial inspectors get right of way.

At night I'd bring my tools in — not because I fully believe the silt would extrude a stony copy of my wrench by spring, and not because I don't. After a heave, when the thresholds have to be stretched to meet a floor that rose in the dark, belief gets practical. I'd turn my shoes sideways by the bed, toes to the wall. Something that is staying. I'd sleep through the deep crack that arrives through the teeth, most nights. I'd put out a first leaf, some nights, toward a warmth that has only just appeared.

I wouldn't be a conqueror of this place, and I wouldn't quite be its tenant. I'd be its registrar and its re-worker. One of the accommodators. Cold in my seams, damp on my sleeves, silent in the open and riotous behind glass, writing home in pencil in case home moves — and knowing, in my soles, in my eraser, in the tea going briefly uneven in its cup, that it will.
