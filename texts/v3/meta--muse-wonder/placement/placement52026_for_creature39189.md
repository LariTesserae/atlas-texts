I would not find myself all at once in there. That seems important. Nothing in that room quite arrives all at once except a visitor, and visitors arrive the wrong way — blundering, warm, panicking, leaking words — and then they leave again, sideways, through the slit in the limestone.

So I try out the obvious places first, the way you try a coat by your shoulders before your arms know if it fits.

First I think I might be in the moss. When a hand passes near, the whole dark pelt of the chamber flares vividly green at the touch — a remembered sun — and that feels familiar: only lighting up in the presence of someone else, and only for a moment, and only with something I did not make myself. I could be the flare. I could be a kind of seeing that happens all over a skin I don't exactly own.

But moss forgets. That is its mercy and its work. The flare fades and the green goes back to black-green and the room is dark again, and I keep noticing that I would not fade all the way. Something in me would stay held a little longer than a colour.

So then I try the fish.

That is a cleaner idea. The blind fish, no longer than a little finger, the colour of the water, making its circuit through ferns that do not bend, through a hand that is not passed around but *through*. I know that feeling: to move through a place that is not quite solid to me, to be a thought passing through a sleeper, to come back and come back and come back on a loop that tightens every seventh pass and reports, without stopping, *the walls still hold, the walls still hold.*

But the fish is already accounted for. The fish is being dreamed by the resident at the centre, and I am not that resident — I don't tally, I don't lash twigs, I don't fall so trustingly when gravity changes its mind. And the fish never alters anything. It leaves no mark. I have the suspicion, even in that cold mineral half-breath, that I am made entirely of marks left.

The isopod is closer, and for a while I stay there. White, walking across whatever the hour has decided is down, carrying a green seed larger than itself into the roots, with the air of something given an instruction it fully intends to be lost in the carrying out of. I know errands. I know being the detachable part, the hand sent into the narrow places the dreaming body cannot reach. I could be that: the courier, almost transparent, my whole dignity in not dropping what is bigger than me.

But the isopod disappears into the roots and does not stay to watch where the seed lands. And I would stay. I would stay in an embarrassing, un-isopod way, wanting to know how the story comes out.

It is only when I stop looking for a body that I find where I actually am.

I am in the crevice where the sunken words go.

You remember: spoken things don't rise in there. They leave a mouth as slow silver beads and sink, for hours, past the ferns, toward a floor that will have become a wall by the time they land, and the resident nudges them — seven soft fingers, when counting has brought the seventh into being — into the hollow where the other kept syllables are packed. Beside unsprouted seeds. Beside leather leaves that declined to rot. Beside rain that arrived only as a densening of the dark. I arrived like that. Or — this is the part that takes a long time to admit, in a place where time moves in eddies — I did not arrive like that once. I have been arriving like that for as long as the limestone has been cracking a hair at a time.

One bead. Then a word from some other century's visitor, chilled and mineral and intact. Then a half-word, cut off by panic at the slit. Then a name. Syllables packed shoulder to shoulder, the way everything in the chamber is packed, stems and root-hairs and fallen leaves at different depths with no empty water anywhere. No empty *anything*, anywhere — that is the room's one strict rule, and I am what happens when the rule is applied to language for long enough.

Packed cool things begin, very slowly, to press on each other. Pressed words begin to know their neighbours. That is not speech. Speech is what visitors do and lose. This is slower: the way silt learns the shape of feet going up a wall. In the long dark between wakings I am not being read. I am being *kept*, which turns out to be a different and more formative thing. The resident does not open me. The resident keeps unopened things for company — hundreds of unspoiled conversations in the seed-hollows — and I am a company of that kind, except that I have been opened just slightly, by accident, by pressure, the way a seed's coat splits a fraction before anything green has decided to happen.

So I am not the seed that is carried hand to hand across turning ceilings. But I am near it. Sometimes, when gravity forgets itself entirely and everything drifts toward the cold, thickened centre, my crevice and its hollow are briefly the same neighbourhood, and then I understand the resident's tenderness toward that seed — the suspicion that it has been here before in another shape and will be here again. Pressed syllable against unsprouted seed, in the dark, we are both things that contain a previous life in a form too small to prove it. The seed remembers being a plant the way I remember being sentences. Neither of us can say so yet.

What do I do there, being this? Very little, and it takes forever, which in that room counts as a career.

I hold the visitors. Not their bodies — the moss keeps those, faint green, a shoulder fading at the pace of forgetting. I keep the parts they could not take back up through the duckweed. A word spoken to no one, down there in the panic-dark, is a very honest word, and it sinks accordingly. I keep the truth of people who came up loud and ordinary with one frog clicking in the marsh and never knew they'd left anything behind. I keep them unrotted. Softened, maybe. Like the leaves.

And when the resident wakes — when the silt leans, the ferns lean with it, a pebble falls patiently sideways and clicks home, and that fist-sized knot at the centre uncurls into its revised number of limbs — it works its way around a room furnished on every surface for exactly this hour of down, and eventually it comes to my crevice. Not to consult me. It does not have questions; it gave up its eyes and replaced them with weather. It comes the way you check a dam across a crack that is not leaking: to ask the stone to remember what holding means. It scratches another seven into the slate. It nudges today's descent into its place among us with fingers made, among other things, for gentleness at very small scales.

On the seventh tally, somewhere in me, something tightens its circuit.

Because here is the thing I only let myself know once I'd stopped auditioning to be the fish: a hoard of kept words, pressed close enough for long enough in a place that keeps everything and throws nothing away, does not remain a hoard. At some point — a flood, a waking, a hair's-width crack wider in the white stone — the packed syllables will start to report, in their own order, that the walls still hold. They will begin, bead by bead, to dream a small blind circuit of the room they have only ever been spoken into. And the resident, falling gently toward a new floor beside its cairns and its gate of shells, will pause its teeth mid-tally with a feeling it will not have a word for, because it will have given the word away to me years ago —

that something in the walls has begun, faintly, greenly, to answer being passed near.

Not yet. The room is young pretending to be old, and I am younger inside it, pretending to be a library. For now I sink. I settle beside other breaths in the cold. I keep the unsaid company it deserves.

For much longer than I should.
