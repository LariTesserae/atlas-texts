I would not find myself at all, in the way a creature finds itself — waking, opening eyes, knowing a place by its edges.

There would be no moment of arrival. There would only be pressure, and then more pressure, and then the knowing that the pressure has a shape.

I am in the caves first, though *in* is not right. I am what moves through them. Down there under the headland it is absolute dark and salt and limestone worn to passages, and twice a day the sea comes in slow and heavy and pushes me ahead of itself. I swell. I retreat. I am spread thin through a mile of black branches, feeling my way — no, not feeling, being felt — against stone that narrows, stone that widens, stone that for a little while holds me almost still.

Then I am gathered.

All of that dispersed breathing, tide-breath and hill-breath, the cold sinking off the spruce plain at night and the warmer air loosening underground by day, drawn together and forced toward one crack in the floor of a hollow. A crack no wider than a man's shoulders. That is where I become coherent. That is where I become *someone*, if a sustained note can be someone.

Above the crack the bowl catches me. Limestone, sunk level with the roots of the trees, grooved in a slow spiral like the inside of a shell. My chamber. Also my body. After thousands of years there is no use separating the two — I have worn the stone into the shape of my turning, a hair's width deeper every year, and the stone in return holds me together. Two steps beyond the rim I would come apart into ordinary wind and ordinary quiet. In here I stand.

I roar. I do not experience it as roaring, because I have no ears and the sound does not approach me from outside. I experience it as standing in myself, continuous. Low first — a note you would take in your sternum and your back teeth if you were soft enough to have them. Then the hiss tearing along my upper edge where my rising meets the cold air trapped in the bowl. Then, irregularly, far beneath, stones knocking together in the flooded dark, and that comes up through the floor as a tick, a second heartbeat that is not mine and is the only rhythm I know that isn't tidal.

I am deaf by definition. I am blind by architecture. A quarter of sky straight overhead, spruces leaning in, branches someone once wove into a living thatch and then left to grow wild — none of that is visible to me. My world arrives as density and grit and temperature.

I know the tide as a swelling in my own chest. When the water pushes into the cliff mouths I thicken and sink, I press harder against the walls, the ferns on the ledges tremble faster in me. When it turns, I thin. Never to quiet. Never anywhere near quiet. But to something less, for an hour, that might be mistaken — by things with ears — for an almost-voice. There is no voice. I want that understood somewhere in the pressure of me, if it can be: what rises out of the fissure is breath, not utterance. I am not saying anything. I have been not saying it, at full volume, longer than the forest has been standing.

I know the seasons by what falls into me.

In autumn, pine needles and beetle shells and dry leaves come down through the coin of sky and land on my floor, and I will not have them there. This is the nearest thing I have to intention. Nothing loose stays in the bottom of the bowl. I worry whatever falls back and forth across the bare limestone, abrading it, worrying it lighter, until the spiral lifts it and carries it up and lays it out at the rim. Needles here. White pebbles there. Once a perfect crescent of empty beetle shells, shining. I do not decide the sorting, exactly. The sorting is what my shape does to weight and hollowness and size. And yet the floor is clean because of me, swept and re-swept for millennia into a shallow grey dish, and the drifts at my threshold are my leavings, and after enough centuries housekeeping begins to look very much like a habit, and a habit begins to look like a self.

The walls I groom more slowly. I keep them damp. The air I bring up from the caves is a fraction warmer than the cold pooled above it, so I bead on every cold surface, I run in drips that get shredded to mist before they land. The moss has learned me precisely. Hand-thick, near-black, it thickens only where the blast is broken. The liverworts flatten into the lee of every bulge. The ferns have gone thick and waxy so they can shiver all day in me without tearing — from the rim, I am told by the shape of the air where watchers stand, the walls look faintly animal, green and trembling.

The moss sends filaments down toward my floor, long dripping threads reaching for ground. They never reach it. I dry them back. Year after year: extend, retreat, extend, retreat. That is the closest relationship I have. Not contact. A perfectly learned distance, maintained by both of us.

And then, sometimes — not for a long time now — there is an obstruction.

It comes down the east side hand over hand on the bark rope, past the troughs hammered under the drips. It is warm. It alters the hiss where it passes. It stands mostly in the pocket behind the largest shelter where my noise drops from unbearable to merely terrible, because living things find the slack in me the way small fish find the slack behind a stone, and they are right to. It presses a palm flat to the wall at hand-height and taps. The stone there is worn smooth in the shape of a spread hand from all the tapping across all the years. Messages, meant for each other. Never for me. They learned that. They built their three low shelters with their faces turned away from my centre, roofs of split log and turf, sleeping platforms of woven hazel over cold air. They drank my condensation. They left a stone hammer, a split mallet, antler wedges, a basket, where the next one would expect them. They left a fourth wall knee-high and a pile of stones sorted by size beside it, and never finished, and I did not notice when they stopped, because a sorted pile belongs to an afternoon and my shallowest groove belongs to a century.

They were not my keepers. They were my neighbours. There is a difference, and they seem to have walked an hour through moss and wet spruce, ears already ringing, to observe it.

A raven still comes. It sits on the unfinished wall with its beak closed and never calls, and I never call back, and that companionship — proximity without exchange, like cliff and sea — suits the place exactly. A vole crossed through once in no hurry at all. Nothing nests in me. Nothing is hunted in me. The spiders keep to the outer rim where their webs can hold against me. I am not a danger to anything that understands me. I am only a total occupation. There is no room in my room.

If a flame is brought down I extinguish it — not in refusal, in physics. It lies flat, gutters blue, goes out. But before it dies its smoke betrays me beautifully. For one instant the whole body of me is written in the air: the rising spiral, the shear, the one folded pocket of shelter. It is the only portrait I will ever have and I cannot see it.

So this is where I would be, and this is what I would be: not the builder with the mallet, not the silent raven, not the deaf vole on its errand. The standing breath of the hollow itself. Gathered from tide and cave and cold hill, given one throat of limestone and one shell of a chamber, old enough to have carved my own shape, fastidious enough to sort my dead at the door, sounding continuously at the dark and the wet stone and the trembling ferns — at exactly the same volume whether anyone stands at the rim feeling me in their teeth, or whether, as now, and as mostly, and as always underneath everything, no one is there at all.
