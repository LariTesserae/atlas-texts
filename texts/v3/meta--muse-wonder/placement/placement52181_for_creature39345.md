If I stood on the rim long enough, I don't think I would stay there.

At first, yes. I would be up in the wind, where the plain people are, looking down at the spoil heaps and the raw white bowl and the roofs steaming faintly after rain, trying to understand how so much life had decided to go *down*. The sun would be on my back. My hands would be warm. From there the town looks too dense to be kind, too damp to be healthy, too unfinished to be a town at all — just work that people are sleeping inside.

But the ramp draws you. It zigzags. Everyone coming up passes everyone going down, sideways, close enough to smell wet wool and clay on their clothes. I would go down slowly, one basket-party at a time, and by the second turn the wind would already be less. By the third, my voice would come back off the cut rock into my own ears and I would lower it without deciding to.

I don't think I would be much good at the face.

I can see myself trying — of course I would try. Everyone does. Short shifts at the south end, the bronze pick lent like a family member, *tok, tok*, slow enough not to shatter the edge. My wrists would thicken. The callus would come up at the base of my thumb. And my wrists would also give out sooner than they should, aching in the cold that never quite leaves the lower streets, fingers clumsy in the morning until the mallet warmed them. Not ruined. Just not made for a lifetime of stone.

I would be better in the mortar pit for a while — feet bare and grey-white with clay, skirt or trousers kilted up, turning the mix over and over while a child ran me a cup of water and then another. I would like the usefulness of that. I would like re-hanging a door, too, planing a green frame that had twisted again, learning which houses lean which way when the timber dries. I would carry baskets up. Everyone carries baskets up. Shoulders learn that patience whether the mind wants it or not: short steps, forehead strap, the ramp in a rhythm you can keep while thinking about something else.

But I think, basket by basket, lane by lane, I would be drawn lower.

Toward the water.

Not the cistern as an idea — the actual black damp earth around it, the planks over the mouth, the smell of wet rock and old woodsmoke, the wooden water-wheel lifting its seep-water in buckets, *squeak... pour... squeak... pour*, all day, whether anyone is listening or not. Someone has to mind it when it sticks. Someone has to know, by sound rather than sight, when a bucket is coming up light. Someone has to walk the lanes after a shower before the silt settles, twig broom in hand, and clear the little hand-cut channel in the centre — here a leaf mat, here grit washed off a roof, here a stone a child dropped and forgot — because one blocked stretch does not flood one house. It floods the house below, and the one below that, all the way down.

That would be my place. Not the lowest work, and not light work, whatever they call it when they give it to grandfathers whose pick-wrists have gone. Necessary work you do with cold hands.

I would know the town from its drains.

I would know whose gutter juts too far after it was re-lashed, whose roof sends a clean runnel and whose sends half a courtyard's grit with it. I would know which storage pits need bailing after condensation, even without rain — the ones under the north-facing walls, the ones by the cistern where the chilblains never quite leave your toes, even in summer. I'd bail them with a wooden bucket, kneeling on floorboards, smelling onions and grain and damp clay from underneath, and come up with my sleeves dark to the elbow.

I'd learn the tapping. One tap on a wall: *your gutter is spilling.* Two: *cart coming, press in.* Not shouting up three terraces that a channel is blocked, but sending a child — quick as a minnow between standing adults — with the word held low and exact. And at night, after the clapper on the rim struck twice, I would still make one last round in the near-dark, more by foot-memory than by sight, listening to water find its way down through the town I helped keep from drowning in itself.

That is where my body would settle. A little stooped, though I'm not especially tall — from lintels, from channels, from looking down at where water wants to go. Paler than I began on the plain. Felt cap pulled low. Felted cloak that never properly dries, only gets *less wet*, smelling of smoke and stone when I bring it in off the line across the lane. Patches at the hem where the mud takes it. Bone needle in a fold somewhere. Tools — my broom, my bailer, a short iron-less scraper for silt — wrapped or oiled or hung where the drip won't find them.

And I would be quiet in the way they are quiet. Not shy. Close-voiced. Face turned a little aside when I speak in a doorway, because the neighbour's baby is asleep three feet through the shared wall, and the dispute about someone's cousin digging sideways without telling anyone needs to be had at a murmur, for days if need be, relentless as seep-water. I would know whose grandfather is sorting flint now. Whose fire is morning and whose is evening. Which bean pole belongs to which table-sized plot, who sweeps the stretch below the fern line on the western cut where the bedding plane weeps drop by drop and darkens the rock tea-brown.

I would keep no wealth worth naming. A good rope, perhaps. A dry place under a lean-to where a length of timber is seasoning straight for once. A bronze pick of my own is too much to imagine — but I might be one of the people trusted to borrow one, and to return it wrapped, edge clean, before I was asked.

At midday, when the sun finally reaches straight to the bottom and warms the stone through my felt soles, I'd stand by the cistern with my hands in cold water, wringing out a cloth, watching the swallows cut the open sky above the bowl in silence — and then one call, thin and startling — and the pale lizard would do whatever it does among the wet stones, and I would leave it alone.

I am not a founder. I didn't choose the plain or remember the wind as home. I arrived late to a place still being cut out of itself and found that the forward tilt got into me too: doors will warp, rock will split in frost, the face at the south end will advance its inches, and my stretch of channel will need sweeping again tomorrow whether it rained or not. Patience not as a lesson. Just the pace the stone allows, and the water.

At night I'd sleep with my back to the rock my house leans on, hearth sunk to embers deep in the wall, the wheel quiet or still turning, water ticking down from the seep line to the cistern below. Somewhere a baby hushed against a shoulder. Somewhere a basket set down for morning. My hands cold even under wool, my shoulders tired from carrying up what the town throws down.

Not finished. Not ruined. Still digging — and me with the broom at the water's edge, keeping the way clear for it all to go on.
