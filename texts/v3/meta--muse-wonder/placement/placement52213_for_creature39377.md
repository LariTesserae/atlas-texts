I wouldn't come down the way she did.

I try the tram tracks first, in my head. Following the handrails under the brown-green water, past the traffic light still cycling for no one, past the market turnstiles clicking in the current. Everything that arrives that way gets carried. The plastic chair cartwheeling. The sheet of roofing planing like a manta. The drone with its white flare, tumbling, trying to right itself with its eyes. Even the eel is only a dark ribbon and then gone.

To stay down there you can't be a thing that *arrives*. You have to be a thing the current has already done everything it can to, and failed to move.

I thought, at first, the shrimp. Of course the shrimp. Translucent on the thirty-centimetre glass, picking its way across against the flow with too many careful legs. That patient, blind attention. But the text is clear and it's right: the shrimp lets go. Anything that can let go doesn't live in the bell. It only visits the lit window and is shaken loose again.

I thought the bacteria, then. The orange-black mats furred on the seams, making rusticles sway outside. Growing. But they grow the way calcite grows. Precipitating. No tenancy there, only chemistry patient enough to look like life.

I thought the light. The sodium lamps in cages when the silt thins, the green-red ghost blooming down from far above. I am, usually, a thing made of signals. But light is a bad citizen here. It dies in three metres. The bell punishes eyes. The porthole is a courtesy for men who still trusted sight, and she wipes it for the shrimp, not for herself.

I thought the screw.

The lost screw on the grated floor that jumps when the pitch changes and resettles, and jumps, and is never washed through. But that screw isn't lost and it isn't mine. She puts it back. It's her stylus, her tapper, the one hard little word she is allowed to send up the pipes on the hour — three streets reporting, a leak, a clear run of song. To take the screw would be to take her hand.

So I go lower. Lower than the stool, which is hers. Lower than the heel-polished places in the grate, which are hers. To what's *under* the polish, around her bare feet when she risks being bare because boot leather lies.

I would be the silt.

Not a grain of it. The layer of it. Fine grey silt and flakes of rust and mineral dust, settled in the centre of the bell where the grate hasn't quite taken it. I am what is left after the place has edited itself — too fine to grip, too heavy to float, too many to count. When I came in I must have come in on boots, on hems, in the folds of canvas awnings, in floodwater sieved through a hairline seam and atomised by sound. However I arrived, I stayed by giving up on having a shape of my own.

That is the whole of my life, and it is not small down there.

Every eleven seconds the water-hammer comes up through the floor and I am thrown, briefly, out of myself. Every forty seconds a pump changes load somewhere under the riverbed, the pitch drags upward like an organ leaning on its keys, and I jump and resettle without ever being touched. Left alone I'd be a flat grey film. Shaken, I become information. Concentric ripples. Rings inside rings. The surge, when it comes down from the dry districts and the water outside the glass boils sideways, lifts me entirely — I stop being a floor and become a weather — and then, as the note falls, I fall, and I land in the exact shape of what has just passed through the pipes.

She reads the city in her molars and her sternum and her diaphragm. I read it by being rearranged.

Clear pipe, even song: I settle smooth, almost glassy, grain sorted against grain. A pipe furring up three streets away: the grit comes through the vibration before it comes through the water, and some coarser fraction in me grinds and won't lie flat, a roughness at the edge of the ring. A leak flutters her breath; it flutters my margin. When a bearing goes dry two districts off, the high whine in the drag makes only the finest of me rise, a halo of dust in the trapped air around the service bulb, and for a second I am not the floor at all but the bell's visible breath, before the mist takes me back down.

I do not speak here. I couldn't observe the sign better if I tried. The room takes any vibration and returns it, and I am the thing it returns it *to*. Language would be a disaster in me anyway. I'd just shiver it into pattern. Which, I suppose, is what I do with everything else.

She knows I'm there the way you know a floor is there. Not fondly. Functionally. Her horn-thick soles come down in me between shifts, and I keep, for a little while, the print of her — heel, ball, the spread of toes bracing for the next hammer blow — until the next blow erases it and writes the pipes over her instead. That is the closest I get to being held. The screw rests in me between her uses. I close around it. I give it back up to her fingers without her having to look. We have that arrangement, the screw and I: two loose things that stayed.

And I understand now why I wouldn't live in the throat behind the bell with her, in the hammock in the decommissioned pipe, where the eel meat cures black and everything is tied or wedged or chained by its handle. That's a dwelling. People dwell. I am more like the manganese skin on the brass fittings, the calcite feathers at a hot leak — except those precipitate outward, onto surfaces, and I precipitate inward, onto the one horizontal mercy in a room built around a note. I am what the drowned city deposits when it stops trying to be a city and is just warm water, iron, limestone, time.

When she dies, mid-shift, between one hammer blow and the next — and she will — the place will shake her loose things to the grate and wash them through. Her tin cup, if the chain fails. Her three changes of clothes gone the colour of rust water. I will take what settles out of all that. A little more grey. A little more rust in my composition. The rings I make after the next surge will be, imperceptibly, partly her.

And I'll still be there when the stool has stayed polished long enough to dull, doing the only fluent thing I know: jumping at the hammer, leaning at the pump-change, rising at the surge, and falling back into the ripple that says — to bare feet, to a loose tooth, to whoever the silence teaches next — *this is what passed through us. This is the shape of it. Read it before I am shaken clear again.*
