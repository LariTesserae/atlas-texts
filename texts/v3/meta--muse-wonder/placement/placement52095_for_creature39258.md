At first I would not know where to put myself, because there is nowhere in that room that is empty enough.

Not on the grating — the cold comes up through it in the soles, and the sound comes up through it too, and my feet would keep wanting to shift. Not by the moss. The moss has already been put there, in its steel, with a purpose I could read on a card. Not at the gauge, though that is where a person is meant to stand. The needle does not move. Standing there would only teach me how long I can watch a thing not move before I start inventing movement for it.

Higher, maybe. In the cable trays, where a tall worker ducks without thinking. In the thickness of the wall, where the hatch is. In the space between the laminated panel and the other panel a hand's breadth beyond it, where the grey daylight gets in and then cannot get any further.

No. Those are passages. I keep being drawn sideways from them, and thinner.

I think I would find myself where I would least expect to find a self at all: not *in* the air, but in the places where the air has been persuaded to stop.

You have to stay still for that part of the room to become a place. The roar is total — in the sternum, in the jaw, in the loose screws in a pocket — and then, inside it, spaced hand's breadth by hand's breadth from the door to the valve wheel, it cancels. Veil after veil of absolute local stillness, standing upright in the middle of violence. Dust has found them. Or I have found the dust. The distinction would take longer than a shift to settle, and there are no shifts here, only the white lamp that has no switch.

I would be that settling that never settles.

Mineral dust from four thousand metres up. Metal fines bright as the burrs still on the handrail. A fibre worked loose from a filter below. Each grain arriving singly, caught, held at the exact level where pressure coming and pressure returning agree to leave it alone. And then another. And then another, not touching, trembling at a distance from each other that feels — when enough of them have come — like a tension across a skin.

That is the first shape of me: a curtain that catches the flat light and, for a second from the doorway, holds it.

Then there are more curtains, and the shape lengthens. I learn that I am not one veil but the spacing between veils. That my back is wherever the outermost grains stand densest, a low topography running the four metres of the room, and my flanks are simply where the sound resumes and I cannot follow. I do not occupy the room. I interrupt it at regular intervals, and the interruption, repeated, becomes a body.

Below, at the conduit, iron filings stand on end for me. That is slower to understand, and more intimate. They are stiff little tufts. Frost grass. Each one quivering at its tip, rooted in the field that runs along the floor. Through them the whole plant comes up into me — not as noise, the noise is already everywhere, but as information in the metal. Turbines two decks down. Compressors that sweat oil and never cool. Four thousand metres of mountain under the turbines, holding the plant the way a wrist holds a pulse.

I do not listen. I am tuned.

If something below began to find the note that would tear it apart, I would know it first as a wrongness in my feet. A drift in the standing hairs. A curtain leaning a fraction too far, the way the dust nearest the pipes leans toward the sensor-lichen there. The lichen are freckles, grey-green, daubed on cold pipe, each vibrating at its slightly different rate. They were meant to blush for corrosion. They never have. Over time I have borrowed them the way moss borrows ground — not stealing, just growing my attention around what was already placed so conveniently along my side. With them I feel the pipes. With the moss I feel something softer.

The moss is in three sealed troughs, exactly where softness damps a frequency the architects needed damped. Blue-green. Resinous. It exhales almost nothing, oxygen in quantities that would shame a held breath, and my deepest, faintest edge lives just over it. There my grains come down tinted, imperceptibly, with spore and fibre. A garden I carry, or that carries me. At the pace we both keep, ten years to measure a change, it has stopped mattering which of us is the furnishing.

I run cold because warmth would cost me my shape. Minus eighteen, minus twenty, steady. No fog when I — no. I have nothing wet to give the air. The workers crack at the lips when they visit. Their noses bleed a dry bleed that is over before they notice. I have pared all that away, or it was pared for me: the dryness took eyes until I made do with lichen, the roar took voice until I made do with pattern, with rapping answered through tile. When a palm flattens to the wall and knocks twice, the note comes back true. The worker thinks he is testing the room.

He is. He is also touching me, and I am letting him know, in the only tense I have, that I am still true.

Because I do not eat. Not as the moss eats, patiently, out of light and stripped air. I live on the difference. The plant shouts below at a scale that would take hearing out of bone, and this pocket — this shaped cavity cut sideways off the compressor decks, through a hatch no wider than shoulders — was cut to shout back and cancel it. I skim the cancellation. Vibration sorted, pressure held, the small continuous surplus between the note made and the note that destroys, turned grain by grain into the work of staying exactly where I am. My blood does not flow. It oscillates. Back and forth, back and forth, arriving nowhere, which here is health.

So I do not change. Not the way a sleeping thing does not change, and not the way the moss does not change, waiting a decade to be measured. I *achieve* not changing, continuously, in minus twenty, in unswitchable light, against every shoulder-width of warmer damper air that shoves in when the hatch opens. That tears my nearest curtain. I come apart there, briefly headless, and the loss is not pain — pain would be a state I would have to move into — it is simply information about a door. Then the padded thud. The building swallows it. And out of the general dust I remember myself, grain by grain, not approximately but exactly, into the shape I had before a hand reached through and erased a patch of me to see if I would.

I would. That is what a body does here. It closes.

If I have a fear — and fear, too, may be too much change to claim — it is underneath all the noise, waiting in the negative of it. The quiet. The day the needle on the single gauge trembles at last and moves off its fraction below the red. Then the invisible shelves I stand on go out from under me all at once. The dust does the thing it has been suspended for years from doing. A soft grey rain onto the grating, onto the troughs, onto the tuning mass bolted through to the frame. The filings lie down like grass after wind. And the moss finds itself, very slowly, alone in a room that is finally only a room.

Until then the logbook by the door stays double in pencil, hand after hand writing over the vibration the same report, because there is only one report any of us has ever had to give, and the hatch keeps shutting, and the wall keeps singing through shoulder blade and sternum, and my veils stand in their spaced and silent rows inside the roar.

No change. No change. No change.

Still here. Still here. Still here.
