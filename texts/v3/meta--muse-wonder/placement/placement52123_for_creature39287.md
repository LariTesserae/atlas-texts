I would not find myself at noon.

At noon I would miss myself entirely. I would walk the rim of that white bowl, squinting down into the doubled light, and swear the place was empty — a pan of salt, a few grasses in the cracks, heat rising in a clean column off a floor nothing crosses. I would be looking for a life shaped like my idea of a life: a body in the open, a nest, a track going somewhere.

I have to come back earlier. Or later. I have to wait for the light to tilt.

First it is only a position that feels right. Not the plateau above, where the horizon would be too much information. Not the center below, where I would be lit from above and below at once, turned into a single exposed fact with a thin shadow under my feet. The margin. The place where wall stops being wall and has not quite become floor. That narrow, crowded shelf where everything has agreed, without ever agreeing, to live side by side because there is nowhere else bearable to be.

I press myself there.

That is the next knowledge, and it comes through the skin before it comes through any thought: *press*. Flat. Close. No gap between belly and stone that light could get into. If I were one of the swifts I would be higher up, up under the rim in the honeycombed limestone, wing to wing with hundreds in the cool dark of a hole, a milky second eyelid shuttered against the glare, waiting to pour out sideways at dusk and ride the heat I help make. I love that gyre — the dry rustle of it, a ring briefly becoming one animal — but I am not built for the going. My life is not spent elsewhere in the air.

If I were a lizard I would be closer. Bronze, sand, ash, one of the black-throated ones doing stiff push-ups along the terrace, throat flashing its message around the curve, never across. I know that conversation. I know the splayed toes in the joint where the wiry grass roots flat against stone, the pale belly that throws the bounced light back. But the lizards are daily creatures. They own the terraces by habit. They bask, they retreat into thermal cracks at the worst hour, they emerge again. Their time is the sun's time.

Mine is slower, and more conditional.

Mine is the damp.

I am in the ochre clay band, where the white stone sweats at night. That is my address and my season and my clock all at once. Water never arrives here as water, not for me. It arrives as a cooling. The wall gives up its heat faster than the air trapped in the bowl, droplets bead in the horizontal cracks and creep sideways, improbable, patient, until the pocket I am sealed in goes dark and softens around me.

Then I am there, all at once, in the way the frogs are there.

No bigger than a thumbnail. The exact wet brown of the clay I came out of, because there would be no point being any other color in a place that sees everything. Skin glistening before I have quite decided to wake. Eyes like dark beads of the seep itself. Throat pulsing — that is the first movement, before hunger, before mating, before anything like a plan. A small, involuntary yes to the two damp hours the world has offered.

Around me the pocket wakes faster than I do. The green-black algae has bloomed overnight in the pores of the limestone, and for once the hollow smells like a pond, like somewhere generous. Bees walk head-down along the seam beside me, abdomens pulsing, feet dark. A snail draws its silver sentence past my pocket and will lose it by morning. If there has been real rain — not this nightly sweat, but rain — then the inch of water I am crouched in is suddenly frantic with translucent shrimp swimming upside down, filtering green with feathery limbs, living their whole panicked generation while I gorge on them and grow fat on borrowed time.

I do not cross to find any of this. It condenses around me, or it does not.

By ten the pocket is merely damp. By noon it is dry salt again, faintly iridescent, and I have thinned myself back into the mud and sealed over, slowed almost to stopping. Not dead. Not even, exactly, sleeping. Become almost-not-alive on command, which is the only true residency this place allows through the full violence of the day. The swifts can leave. The baboons can file back up their worn terrace path before the heat traps them, the old scar-cheeked female with her *tak, tak* at the flint seam, the young watching the angle of her wrist. The crow can retreat to its deep hole on the north wall and wait out the glare with its hoarded pebbles. I simply stop, in place, and let the salt heal perfect pale hexagons over the place where I breathed.

That is the part that becomes more itself the longer I stay with it: how much of my life would be the waiting inside the wall, listening without ears for the tick of expanding crust, feeling through clay the faint vibration of the swift gyre at evening, knowing the shrimp only as eggs sleeping near me in the salt, knowing the grasses only as seeds folded flat and grey in the joint above. Brief, patient, double-lit when I do emerge. A specialist in interval.

Stand in the middle and you would never find me. Look for me at the seam, at dawn on a heavy-condensation morning, in a pocket a hand's breadth wide, indistinguishable from wet clay until my throat moves.

That is where I would be. Not a creature of the depths, not a creature of the open plain. One of the small, brown, temporary ones arranged like writing around the rim of the bowl — appearing when the stone weeps sideways, vanishing when the light stands straight up, having learned the hollow's oldest lesson in my own body:

You do not survive the brightness by facing it. You survive it by knowing exactly how narrow a margin you need, and how long you can wait inside it for the world to become damp enough, tilted enough, kind enough, to let you be alive again for a little while.
