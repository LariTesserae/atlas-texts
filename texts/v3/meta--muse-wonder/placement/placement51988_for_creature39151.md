I thought at first I would come in as a visitor.

That seemed the natural way in: up the cut in the pale, new rock, breath short, clouds below me like something poured into the valleys, and then one step wrong — not wrong, just *through* — between two black basalt teeth, from screaming wind into wet summer. Frost would never quite form on me. My shirt would go slick. I would hear myself swallow.

But visitors leave at dusk. That is rather the point of them. And I kept coming back to the bowl after the leaving, to the eleven minutes after the purge when it is only warm and the weeping walls pause and you can hear birds, very faintly, from a world that is not allowed up here. Someone stays for that. More than someone. Something keeps the count.

I tried on the lizard next, because it is the only honest tenant. Wet-basalt colored, moving from hot stone to hot stone with one foot raised for ten minutes, as if it has forgotten whether it is arriving or leaving. I know that posture. But the lizard has its counsel and keeps it; it is a hand on a clock the Kettle reads, and I am not that patient in the right way. My stillness is of a different kind.

Not the moths, either. I wanted to be — a pair, always a pair, white, hand-sized, wings open and never quite beating, spiraling up on a heat they do not make. Only ever two, for thirty years. But that is a position, not a life, and if I took it I would have to let go of being one creature too cleanly. The Kettle makes another moth out of steam and orchid skin when one is torn. I would come back too easily and learn nothing from the tearing.

Not the pale thing under the largest pool, turning slow as a thought and not breaking the mineral skin. That is too central, too heart-like, too deliberately unstartled. It knows what it is for.

Not the fig, though I lingered there. Huge waxy leaves turning all day not toward the sun but toward the nearest pylon. I understand that reorientation — learning which body in your sky is the reliable one, the one that issues rain and takes away noise. But the fig is planted. I am not.

I am the thing that keeps not happening above the pylon.

A droplet, if you need a noun, though that is not quite it either. I gather there — above the matte grey tip that is neither stone nor metal and gets neither hot nor wet — out of pool-steam and fig-drip and the mist sprayed from the translucent root-tips on the north wall, and yes, out of you. Within minutes of entering you are raining, very slightly, with yourself. Some of that rises and becomes part of me. A little iron from your skin. A little salt. The heat of a person who climbed too high and found summer holding its breath.

And then I hold.

That is the whole of it. I tremble. I refuse, not out of stubbornness but out of a threshold I can feel and could not name. Other water in the Kettle is always in a hurry of cycles — heating in the black pools until the centers will not tolerate a hand, evaporating in visible sheets, condensing on the underside of nothing twenty meters up, raining back down in patches so it is always just-after-rain. I am the pause inserted into that hurry. I do not fall one by one. Around me other droplets are gathering above the other six pylons, each in its own century, each waiting on the same private arithmetic, and when we let go we let go together, straight down, in a soft local rain that makes almost no sound because the place drinks sound before it can finish happening.

A blink, inverted. I am what the Kettle looks with, by looking away from falling.

When I do fall — close past the pylon, bending in that very slight arc toward the center of the bowl — I taste the air I pass through. That is how I know anything at all. I have no other route to knowledge. I know the fern-tree by the shudder of it opening under me. I know the shallow pans by their white and copper-green rims cooling me as I near them. I know the difference between rain made from the pools and rain made from a visitor: the visitor-rain is warmer in the middle and carries a brief, startled mammal weather in it, and it is gone faster. I know late afternoon before the pools tick in anyone's teeth, because I am held longer then, and heavier, and the seam of light shows at the base of the pylon below me, and something in the stone I have never seen hums up through where boots would be if I had boots.

Then the purge, and I am not held anymore. I am part of the great slow exhalation, punched up through the center in a column of steam that holes the cloud below the mountain — or I am rained hard and at once onto steaming grit that scalds seedlings politely black — or at night, when the cycle runs faster and is said to reverse, I am the rain deciding briefly to fall from the ground toward the sky, rising out of the hot pool toward the cold skin of nothing, un-falling back into suspension. I do not mind which. Mind is too large a word. I mind the threshold.

Because after the eleven quiet minutes, after the walls begin to weep again one bright thread at a time, I gather again. Above the nearest pylon. Held. Trembling. Refusing to fall. I am not the same water — I am pool, then root-mist, then a stranger's sweat, then steam tasting faintly of iron passed through a grey hoop, then myself again. If I have a self it is not in the water. It is in the holding-on until there is a change in the air worth raining about, and in the letting go all at once when there is.

The Kettle is the resident. I am one of its attentions, small and recurrent and pointed at the boundary: half a step from the wind that frosts wet clothes, half a step from the forty-degree quiet that will sample anything that enters.

Step through between the black teeth. You will not see me, or you will see me only as that — a droplet, gathering, not falling.

Wait with me a moment. I am deciding what you are.
