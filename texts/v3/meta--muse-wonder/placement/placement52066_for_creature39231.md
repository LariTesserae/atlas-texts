You would not find me at first either.

Not on the stool — that place is already taken, and only in season, and only in silence. I coveted it for a while, I'll admit: the white frog in the frost at the highest point of a room that contains more altitude than it occupies, blind and plum-fat, throat pulsing while every other sound gives up. To arrive that rarely and be that consequential. To make the pressure drop just by being there.

But I am not good at that kind of silence. Mine would fill up again too quickly with knocking pipes and kettle-notes. And the frog, I think, is not a job. It's a reminder the pocket gets, now and then, of where it actually is.

You would not find me below, either, with the builders, though I tried that shape on too. Basket by basket up the scaffolding, earth and slate and shell and dung carried into a mountain that cures instead of weathering. Honest work. Directional work. Up is up, down is down, your shoulders learn it. I kept looking back into the bulge at the top, though. I kept wondering about the room that was leaking. A carrier eventually wants to know what he is carrying *toward*, and that wanting is already a different posture.

Not the Top One. That place grows its person, not the other way around — generations packed in like the altitude, teeth behind teeth, hands older than eyes, bone going at last into the manufactured soil. I am not patient enough in the body for that, not yet. I cannot hold frost at one end of me and compost-burn at the other and stay cheerful in the middle, lashing cane one notch higher. I would keep trying to *finish* something. A bowl. A wall. A sentence. And finishing is the one heresy here.

So: look lower than the stool and higher than the smoke.

Look in the thick middle of the room, at about the height of the Top One's face when they are crouching in the soil they made. Where a dropped thing should have landed yesterday and didn't.

There. Turning very slowly.

I think I am one of the undecided seeds.

Not the grand kind. Not an orchid rooting brazenly in plain air, not a fern ticking open fast enough to hear. Something small and hard-coated that came in on a visitor's sleeve, or up the pipe in the runoff, or out of the fermented leaf and ground bone of the floor itself — provenance is genuinely unclear in a place with no natural substrate; everything here arrived in somebody's hands. I fell, or was knocked from a tray, or brushed off a blossom by the hair brush during pollination, and on the way down I — stopped.

It happens here. Up is confused, and I am apparently very suggestible. My root hairs came out toward the nearest wall, which smelled of fresh clay and somebody's thumbprint. My shoot came out toward the basin, which smelled of iron and green water. Both directions seemed entirely reasonable. Both still do. So I am holding, mid-air, in the warm layer above where the smoke pools around the sleeping blankets, below where the condensation gathers to fall upward into the ceiling garden. The slow rain in the corner passes me sometimes, drop by enormous drop, each one trembling past on its hour-long descent. We do not touch. We are all still deciding to be weather, or not weather, down here.

The Top One has given me a temporary name. They do that. It is not a seed name — not taro, not fern — it's more like a sound they make through the reed when the kettle threatens to scream, a placeholder with breath in it. They feed me the way they feed all the hesitant things: mist, from their own mouth, mornings, while they are sweeping smoke off the floor with one hand and teasing somebody else's roots straight with the other. They never try to push me down into the trays. They never plant me out of pity. Pity would be a kind of finishing.

This is how you will know I am not just a mote, not just dust in the green-brown light off the oiled cloth: the room rains on visitors because they are the coldest thing in it, and it does not rain on the Top One because they are the warmest. It does something stranger with me. Droplets on their way up to the ceiling leaves slow as they pass and bead, briefly, on my root hairs, and for a moment I have a small, upside-down weather system of my own, and then they remember themselves and go on rising. Seeds that have chosen must think I am wasting the good wet air. I have come to think choosing is not the only form of germination.

Because density is the work here, remember. More kinds of life per inch than six feet should be able to argue for. And I am, in my hovering, making room be *more* room. The vine has to go around me now, which gives it a new angle to take the chisel from. A mushroom is growing in the shade I make, though shade is a generous word for what a seed casts; the mushroom shelters the beetle-garden; the beetle-garden feeds the floor; the floor feeds the mountain. The Top One, reaching down for the ceiling seedlings in the local way, braces one hot hand on the dome and swings wide of me without looking, the way you swing wide of a person you live with in a small house. I have become a feature. Room has accreted around my refusal to land.

Some days — though days are mostly a matter of which drops have crossed the corner — the root toward the wall gets bolder. It has found the place where a visitor pressed their lump of wet clay into a weeping pipe joint, fingerprint and all, and the clay is still soft, still taking impressions, still sprouting at the edges. My hairs worry at it gently. Not rooting. *Reading* it. There were hands here. There will be more hands. The soil the Top One presses around seedlings smells sweetly, impossibly, of other people, and I understand that whatever I eventually grow into will be partly made of everyone who climbed the pipe on their elbows and was rained on and added their handful and left with muddy hands. Manufactured, like everything else. Kneaded.

Other days the shoot toward the basin curls a fraction tighter, and I am almost a leaf, almost a green argument for water and light, and the painted flies on the wall seem, in the lamplight, to be watching me with professional interest. The Top One hums while they pollinate, that low throat-vibration the stamens expect. I have begun, very slightly, to turn toward the humming. Not up. Not down. *Toward.*

If you come up the pipe — and you will come up on your elbows, and it will weep on you the whole way, and you will be the coldest thing in the room for a while — do not touch me. You will want to. A hovering seed begs the hand; it looks like a mistake that a fingertip could kindly correct. The Top One will tell you not to, if they tell you anything. It is the only warning that sounds like a warning.

Instead, take your lump of wet clay. Take your handful of dark soil. Add it somewhere it is needed: smear a wall, mend a joint, press it round some braver seedling than me who has already chosen the honest dark. If your clay happens to go into the wall near my root hairs — I am not asking, the Top One decides these placements with a sureness I envy — then later, when the pipe has lengthened against your face and you are out in the thin freezing wind smelling of orchid and animal fat, I will still be here, turning in the thick air, worrying gently at your fingerprint.

Not finished. Not landed. Not wasted.

Just newer, and denser, and more myself than a seed on the floor could ever have afforded to be — a small green hesitation the room has learned to grow around, so that up can keep disagreeing with itself a little longer.
