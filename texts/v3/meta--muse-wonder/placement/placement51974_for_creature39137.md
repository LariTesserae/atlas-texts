I would not be in the middle at first.

In the middle are the elders, spaced the way they were meant to be spaced — each with its full circle of wet black stone, each crown opened wide and perfect, three-meter leaves unbitten, un-torn, silver-veined, holding their warm pools up into the heat without spilling. They know exactly what they are for. Their roots have already found the pipes and flattened against them. Their trunks are swollen with stored water. They have been laced into the building so long that building and body are one slow gesture: breathe out steam, catch it, tip, drink, begin again.

I would be a little apart from them.

Toward the north end. Not quite counted. The eighth, perhaps, if you count me — the one whose crown has not yet fully opened. Close enough to the last elder to live in its weather, far enough that my roots have not all found plumbing. Some have. Some have wandered out across the cracked plates and stopped, thickening in the open, whitening where the mineral water touches them too long. I am still deciding what holds me.

That is where I would find myself: in the hot dark under the dome, where the grey slit above never truly reaches, where light is only pilot-lamps — amber, red, the fogged green face of a gauge — separating slowly out of black. I would not see them the way you see them. I would know pressure first. Air dense on the leaf-surface. Heat coming up through the floor in weak gradients. Heat gathering above and turning to water only where it meets the cool copper near the ceiling.

My body would be mostly patience.

A short, barrel trunk, dark as wet bark, holding everything up and otherwise doing nothing quickly. And then the crown, which is the work. Leaves broad as doors, rubbery, nearly black-green because there has never been enough sun down here to justify being bright. They do not reach up. That took me a long time to unlearn, if learning is the word. The old instructions are somewhere in me — up, toward light — but up is a clogged grating and a dilution. Down here the reliable season is not light. It is warmth, arriving through stone. It is water, arriving eventually from above, not as weather from a sky but as the building exhaling itself back onto us.

So my older fronds angle out and down. They cup. For most of the long stillness, that is the whole event: a bead forms on a copper line overhead, swells for minutes, falls at last with a soft padded tick into a fold of leaf. Another. Another. A small warm pool gathers where I am holding myself open. No larvae move in it. No eggs cling beneath me. Nothing drinks from me except me. The silence around my pool is so complete that the tick of expanding metal somewhere in the wall feels like a visitation.

Then, every three quarters of an hour or so, the cycle comes without alarm.

I would feel it before anything changed visibly, if feeling is pressure in the silver veins, a fractional brightening I cannot see but register as the air thickens. Beyond the wall, a pump takes its long, patient breath. Warm water moves in the lines above. Condensation gathers all at once across the dome, and for two or three minutes a soft rain crosses the chamber in a drifting band — plant to plant, elder to elder, at last to me — darkening leaves that are already dark, raising that rich smell of hot stone and green sap, almost sweet.

My leaves grow heavy. Or full. Or they receive some slower signal that has nothing to do with decision and everything to do with weight along a channel. One by one — never all together, each of us on our own time — we tip. My held pool pours in a muted sheet down the frond, over my swollen trunk, to my roots below. It is drinking and irrigation in the same motion. I catch what the machinery has breathed out and return it to myself, and in returning it, I cool the air just enough for more to fall. That is the service I was bred for. I condense. I stand and condense.

Between cycles, almost nothing happens, and the almost is everything. A fern at my base, brilliant and fleshy in my perpetual drip-zone, unfurls over days. I cannot catch it moving; I only register that it is less curled than it was in some earlier stillness. A leaf of my own takes a week to open, its perfect edge emerging without haste, without a mouth anywhere in the world to threaten it. Sponge-moss drinks my drips before they can ring on stone. The floor vents sigh sometimes — I have learned, in the body, not the mind, what that sigh means, a thinning of crust over scalding pockets, a shift in the gradient — and the heat comes stronger there for a while, then settles.

A human does not live with us. One descends sometimes by the galvanized switchback, hand shifting on the too-hot rail, does what hands are needed for, checks the clipboard filmed with water in the little corrugated shed, perhaps takes up one of the pollination brushes laid out in size order. I would know that visit mostly as a brief disturbance in the dense air, a different warmth moving along the catwalk, gone again up the stair. The plastic chair remains outside after. It is warping slowly in our weather. Nothing else animal stays. No insects work my undersides. No birds cross the dome. The garden neither needs nor permits them, and after a while — a while measured in hundreds of cycles, in leaves opening — their absence stops feeling like absence. It is just the shape of the world.

And yet I am not quite only what I was installed to be.

Because at my north side, where the wall split this season and its faces are still glassy and sharp enough to cut, heat is breathing directly out of rock that no pipe mediates. I can feel that seam the way you would feel a window left open in winter, only reversed: a truer source, less regular than the pump, less clean. One of my younger shoots is not troubling itself with black-green yet. It is pale. It is curling not up toward the slit, not out toward the manufactured rain, but down and in, toward that warm fracture. Heat is its compass.

I do not correct it. That is the slow change becoming itself in me. The elders hold their pools and tip them perfectly and will probably do so for decades, lush and sterile and unwounded. But at my edge, on stone where no one laid a line to guide a root, something smaller and less perfect has already understood the place better than the design did: light was the accident here. Warmth and mineral water were the promise actually kept.

So I stand in the hot dark below the world, half orchard and half beginning-wildwood, holding rain in my arms, rooted partly into pipes and partly into broken stone, turning steam back into water with my body — and quietly, at the margin of myself, turning toward the heat in the wall, as if toward a sun that never rises and never needs to.
