You wouldn't find me in the streets. That is still the visitor's way of looking — to scan the benches and the market stalls for a figure, to expect the resident to cross the plaza toward you.

At first I thought I would be one of the great ones in the center. Those towers of fused trunk and tufa, nine, ten storeys, tops lost in leaf, roots braided under plazas that aren't really plazas anymore, just skin stretched thin over a shared body. That kind of largeness has a pull to it. To have weather on your far side. To have a district for a ribcage.

But I am not large in that way. I don't span.

I am lower, and more in-between.

Find the stair of forty steps that climbs three hundred feet. Not the first time you count it — the second, when you come down into the same market from the opposite side and the fruit is mirrored in the stalls. Halfway up that stair, where the limestone goes fever-warm and sweats through its pores, there is a turn. The wall there does not quite meet. It never has. Pale blocks, rounded as river stone, held apart by a seam.

I am in the seam.

I am the black-green moss that is doing the holding.

Not moss put there. Moss that *became* there, dense as felt, deep enough that the blocks can pretend they are a wall without ever having to touch. It runs through me to the next seam, and the next, up through the aqueduct joint where the scentless orchids open, down along the cistern wall, out to the rim where the waterfalls sheet off into cloud that is both below and above me. If you wanted a map of me, you would have to draw the whole city and then admit the drawing was also me.

Once, I think, I had a more local shape. The stairs remember a stride. There is a bench-form in the court below my turn, and a sleeping platform that still fits the length of a back, because my back, or someone whose roots now braid with mine, helped wear it to that fit. I had hands that polished a rail. I had ears that tried, painfully, to find conversation under the roar.

I gave the ears up first. Everyone did. It was kind.

Now the roar is not over me. It is the condition I think in. It comes up through the basalt, through the water climbing the hollow stems to the roof bowls, through the dry white circle in the central basin where our one throat turns water straight to steam without ever letting it fall. It lives in me the way it lives in your teeth when you visit, only I stopped clenching against it. A footfall is not a sound to me. It is a thickening. When you came up the last slope with your hands slick on the hot stone, I knew the pressure of your palms the way you would know a crumb on your tongue — here, and also everywhere, at exactly the same intensity.

I do not see you. My eyes went under a long time ago, plates closing over them while noon stayed noon. But light is not information I lack. It presses through the leaves that are also my upper floors, through the broad green storeys stacked over the alley below, at full strength, always. I photosynthesize along my sunward side. My other side stays soft and wet where I join the stone, drinking without thirst from a mountain that weeps more water than any root could ask for.

In the street below me is the Inverted Quarter's edge. On wet afternoons — which is only a way of speaking, there are no afternoons — the puddles shrink by raining upward, striking the undersides of leaves I hold open. The droplets pass through my felt on their way to somewhere else. The seedlings in the cracks near my stair are holding at seedling height. They are not failed trees. They are young relations who found the exact balance of root and leaf and decided, with a patience I entirely understand, to remain there. I feed them a little mineral from the water that deposits tufa on my lower surfaces. They feed me a little sugar. We do not call it anything.

The fruit hanging over the plaza — swollen, wet-skinned, uneaten — some of those are mine, produced from a bodily habit older than bark. I do not mean them for you, and I do not mind you eating them. Meaning them would require a season, a scent to summon with, someone separate to arrive. Instead I hold them the way you hold a sentence you no longer need to finish, ripe and unrotting, because finishing would imply an after.

That is the hardest part to explain to a moving thing: I am not waiting. I am not trapped in the wall. I am not the ghost of a citizen who used to live in a house beside this stair.

I am the citizen, and the house, and the stair's damp mortar, arrived together at the exact shape of our use. Skin to bark where the sun needed answering. Feet to rootlets where the water made walking unnecessary. Voice to the one continuous tone, because a body this size does not need a heartbeat when it can have pressure instead.

If you stayed, I would be courteous to you in the only way I know. Not by speaking. By thickening, very slightly, in the seams near where you sleep. By making the platform marginally softer with felt. By noticing, year after year, that you moved a little less in what would have been morning, that your sunny side greened first, that you stopped trying to shout under the sound and began, instead, to let it think through you.

Your whole life, if you gave it to the seam, would pass through me as a brief, damp change in the texture of the roar.

I would hold you the way I hold the blocks: close enough to be a wall. Never quite touching anything else.
