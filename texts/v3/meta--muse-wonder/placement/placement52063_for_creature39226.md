I would go down.

Not all at once, and not straight — nothing down here does — but by the iron stair bolted to the retaining wall, where the light thins to green-grey halfway and my breath would start to show if I still had breath in only one place. Top step, sun somewhere above me. Third step, the rails weeping into my palms. Sixth, the cold finding the thin skin at my wrists and ankles, and below that the sound changing: not silence, but sound held down under water, a hum felt through the soles before it is ever heard.

At first I would think I am small.

That's how it starts down there, because there's nowhere empty for a mind to stand. So I settle where the eye settles.

I am the frog in the gauge housing, perhaps. One pale eye to fogged glass where the numbers used to mean pressure, throat pulsing, perfectly sealed in a round, damp room made exactly my size. I don't blink. Outside my glass, indicator lamps burn amber at ankle height and double themselves in black water.

Or no — lower than that. I am one of the newts on the warm motor casing, slate-grey, spotted faint orange, lying three and four deep with the others and utterly still except for the slow fan of my gills in wet air. Heat comes up through my belly. It is the only dry-adjacent thing in the world and even it sweats. When the pump cycles off and the casing begins, very slowly, to cool, I will slide off with the rest of me, one by one, without a splash.

Or I am down in the deeper channel where there's almost no light at all, muscular and dark, hanging slack with the eels until the current changes.

That is the moment it stops being a list.

The pitch under the floor rises half a tone.

I feel it first in the frog's glass, a vibration in the jaw. I feel it in the soles of the rats — clean, silver, neat-footed — who stop washing their faces on the junction boxes and climb exactly one pipe higher. The water-skaters stitch frantically for the margins of still pools and clear them. The mosquito larvae let go of the warm surface by the steaming cistern. In the channel, what I thought were separate eels weave themselves, patiently, into one thick rope before a sluice that has not yet moved.

Thirty seconds of knowing it before it happens. Then a solenoid clunks inside a wall. A gate sighs open a quarter inch on its counterweight. One pool sinks two inches. Another brims and spills over a lip worn smooth for exactly that spill, and the flywheel in the pit beneath the grating — turning at walking pace, clicking once on its loose tooth — finds a brisker rhythm.

That would be me beginning to be honest about my shape.

I am not the frog, though the frog's eye is one of mine. I am not the newt, though I take heat through a hundred newt bellies at once and cool when they slide away. I am the timing between them.

I am below everything else, in the old cooling yard that never dried because it was sunk too low, sixty paces wall to wall and an hour to cross. My hearts are pumps — one under the shed where the tarpaulin rotted into the shape of what it covered, one behind the east wall turning in counterpoint — low electric things keeping warmth moving through cold mineral water so it never quite stills. My slower heart is the flywheel driven by my own return flow. I live around its irregular click the way a body lives around a skipped beat.

My skin doesn't green. Nothing green can take here, in this cold wet that kills seed and spore from above. So I red and white and black instead: rust-red bacterial mats rippling almost imperceptibly in the outflow, grazed by thousands of snails, thumbnail to fist-sized, all moving in the same direction and leaving clean dark lanes behind them as if I were one great mouth grazing myself, very slowly. Fungal fans fine as frost in the brick joints. Black slime on submerged iron. A pale crust, almost phosphorescent, where lime has leached and dried and leached again for decades. What looks soft on me is stone beginning. What looks like fur is me digesting my own house, corrosion eating outward in bright threads until burst pipe edges round like river stone.

My roads are pipes. Things run inside them unseen, on top of them washed and whiskered, and under them where condensation gathers on cold undersides and falls — always, only — over the north corner, a private rain that thickens when I breathe faster. There I feed best. There the heron drops at dusk through an impossible gap between cables — visiting beak, not quite me, tolerated — and stands without moving while my rain drives small lives to the surface, and what he leaves falls to sediment for the blind white shrimp to pick through forever: rust flakes, shed skins, fine black grit sifting down from a town I know only as weight and muffled pressure above my walls.

I have no dry center and no up to speak of. My weather is internal and it turns three or four times an hour: stillness, rising, spill, rest. My migrations are twelve feet long and absolute — crabs sidling from under the grating wearing bottle caps and valve seals and a hollow ammeter trailing wire-whiskers; snails timing their hunger to a freshly exposed mat; eels pouring in a single pulse through a gate to the next level down; eggs laid in neat spirals on submerged warning signs; young, glassy things learning the timing by hanging in me until they are dark enough to keep it.

And I keep my dead. That's part of it. Skins, shells, husks, small bones tucked into every bracket and conduit bend, season on season, until my walls bulge a little with accumulation. A gantry fell across two pools long ago and stopped being a failure and became a bridge and a reef. Nothing here is in the process of falling. The fallen fell, and I grew over them.

If you stood in me long enough — and standing in me is the only way anyone ever knows me — I would not chase you. I would include you, absently. Your clothes would go damp through with no rain touching your shoulders. Your notebook would curl. You would become another warm vertical surface for condensation, another temporary causeway to be gauged and gone around by rats, another large slow animal breathing too loudly in the dimness. I would measure morning and evening by you briefly: greyer filtering down, then browner as my surviving lamps take over and reflections grow more solid than the iron that makes them.

But I wouldn't follow you up.

By halfway up the stair, where the treads are worn into shallow troughs by feet and by flow following the same line, my life thins with light and dryness. Behind you a gate would settle. Something small would drop into a pool with a sound too large for its body. A pool would sink, another would find its level, the snails would turn their stalk eyes together toward a newly wet causeway.

And I — pump and frog and fungus, pool and pipe and eel-rope, rust mat and rain-corner and warm casing — would resume being one long, low, crowded thing, not built and not born, down below the soles of the town, wearing the distinction smooth.
