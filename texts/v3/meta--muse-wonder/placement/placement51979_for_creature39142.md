I would not arrive standing up.

That is the first mistake anything makes here, and I think I would make it for a while — trying to be upright against the weather, trying to be a separate shape on a landscape that has no interest in separate shapes. The wind takes the top of you first. Not metaphorically. Practically. It finds your head and shoulders, it peels the heat out of your ears, it edits anything you try to say into a noise the frogs could do better.

So I would learn lower.

At first, from a distance, you wouldn't be sure I wasn't one of the errors. A long shape in red sphagnum, not rising when the gusts hit the pools. Maybe a grazer gone down to crop cotton grass. Maybe something the mat extruded in the night — sedge woven roughly into the suggestion of a back, steam coming off it in approximately the right places, following a real animal's route an hour too late.

I wouldn't be sure either.

There would be a period — a whole season, maybe two — of being almost several things here. I could be one of the thumb-beetles in winter, locked inside a furnace-flower, scorched and dreaming in fermented nectar while the bloom holds itself at blood-heat against a world trying to freeze it shut. I can feel the attraction of that: to be small enough that someone else's metabolism is your weather.

I could be a crane-thing, all slate height and patience, standing in a conductive pool waiting for the blue betrayal of an eel under the surface, too tall for this place, surviving on stilts and nerve.

But height keeps arguing with me, and the argument keeps losing. The longer I stay, the more the pull is not up toward light or down into the black water under the mat, but *across*. Outward. A widening.

It starts in the feet.

Not shoes. Never shoes. Three toes spread improbably wide, gristle and bone doing the slow negotiation with ground that isn't ground. Each step a question asked of a lung: may I? Each step forgiven by a slow hydraulic shrug behind me. I would not walk as a visitor walks, stabbing down and sinking to the ankle in tea-brown water. I would spread. I would depress a whole yard of living mat and be held up by the spreading, and the mat — this is the part that would change me from the inside — the mat would *keep* it for a moment after I passed. Not the footprint. The pressure. The fact of my weight, briefly retained in the root architecture, long after the visible print had rebounded and erased itself.

I think that is how I would know what I was.

Because my back would already be taken.

Not bare. A child's back is bare and shining and vulnerable, and I do not think I would be a child here. I would be past the pressing-in. The moss would have taken. Sphagnum rooted shallowly along my spine — rust-red, mustard, that rinsed-out green — drinking the sideways rain before the rain could find my skin. Liverwort in the seams behind my ears. A little cotton grass at the shoulder blades, nodding when I move, ridiculous and tender. On my worst mornings the whole saddle would be silvered with frost-feathers, and I would stand beside a furnace-flower in its melted clearing and let the flower's waste heat work through the garden until the frost gave up and slid off me in sheets.

Under it, the furnace going. Not a heart so much as an industry. My hands would be tarn-water cold. My feet could stand in an icy pool without complaint. All my heat kept hoarded in the core, where peat-fiber and eel-fat and a black, sour nectar culled from inside a bloom are composting down into warmth, and every so often the deep pistons would fire — *thump, thump, thump* — pushing hot blood briefly out to a hand, a foot, before hauling it home again before the cold could rob it. Standing close, you would hear that before you heard me breathe. You would see my breath come in little rhythmic vents, timed unconsciously to the flowers.

I would be alone, mostly. That isn't loneliness here; it's spacing.

My family — four people, five in a fat year — would hold a basin the size of a county between us, and I might go three days inside our own country without laying eyes on a single one of them. But I would not be out of conversation. At dusk I'd put a palm flat and send a word down through the mat, a shaped pressure, and feel it arrive an hour later through a cousin's soles two valleys over. Or I'd braid my step politely into a sedge spire and let the static jump pool to pool, spire to spire, until the smell of ozone and hot pennies reached someone lying low a mile off: *storm sitting down soon. flower open to the south. I am here; do not turn toward my heat.*

Turn toward my heat. Because the furnace-flowers turn, and so do the midges, and so, despite themselves, do the hungry things. To be known here is not primarily to be seen. It is to be felt, at a distance, as a warm depression moving steadily through a cold field.

I would live in loops too wide to call a route. Following the flowering. Following the turbine groves as they wind up for the season, their three-bladed crowns milling static down into root batteries until the bark ticks at night with cooling. Eating what the mercy-islands provide — eels eaten blue and crackling off a bone needle, frogs by the fistful from springs that chorus like struck bells, each spring pitched by its depth so the whole plateau rings differently basin by basin and I would know where I was by sound alone, blind, in weather. Once in a decade a grazer mires, and then the converging: my people arriving singly out of nothing over the course of a whole day, eating in near-silence under screaming starling-things, leaving singly by different routes before the meat cools, none of us retracing, because a rebounded path holds warmth and warmth is an invitation.

And sometimes — this is the thing I would have to learn not to flinch from — something would follow me that was also, almost, me.

A second shape a mile back, woven sedge and white fungus, getting my gait right, getting even the little favoring of my left side where an old injury stiffened the hip. Steam coming off it *almost* in rhythm. The mat, remembering my pressure so faithfully that it occasionally tries it on. I would not turn. I would not hurry. I would let it follow the way you let a younger sibling follow, until the wind worried it apart, and then — smallest possible interruption to a steady pace — I would press my palm to where it last stood.

Because it would have no pressure of its own. That is the whole tragedy and the whole distinction. Its footfalls would be forgotten instantly. Mine are held a moment. The moss on its back would be dead stuff, laid on. Mine is rooted and drinks for me.

Held a moment. Then held a little longer, if I keep walking the same slow loops for seventy years.

That, finally, is what I would be. Not a resident — nothing resides here, nothing is built, nothing stays where it was put. Not a traveler either, exactly, because travelers are going somewhere that is not already under their feet.

I would be a pressure the plateau has learned.

On the evening the cloud tears open — minutes only, the light going amber and low across black pools and pale granite domes and solitary plumes of flower-steam — you'd see me at my true scale. One figure, low, a long way off, garden flattened against a gust, moving steadily and without any appearance of hurry across a world that is all skin. Another dimming of the mat, far off, that is my cousin. Nothing between us but weather and conversation conducted through the ground.

And when I finally lie down for the last time, deliberately, in a saucer far from the family's current walking, nobody will come build over me or carry me anywhere. Days later they will stand at the rim and put their feet down carefully in the old places, one by one. The root architecture will sigh. A bubble of warm air will rise up out of a depression older than I am — methane, iron, the sourness of the particular flowers I always favored — and my family will stand in the sideways rain and breathe me in, once, and know me the way the mat knows me.

Then liverwort, sealing the seams. Then sphagnum.

Then, in time, perhaps a furnace-flower rooting in the warm chemistry of the chest, opening its black bloom to a sky that never fully clears.

No word for a person, separate from that.

Only: *that which the mat is still holding.*
