I don't think I would arrive from above.

I wouldn't come down through the wind-scoured snow and the blue ice with boots and gloves, surprised by the root underfoot. The things that arrive that way are weather. They are read by the berries and modeled in frost and, if they stay too long, kindly slowed until weather becomes season.

I would be found already there, and only after you'd stopped looking for anything shaped like a person.

At first, maybe, in a leaf-pool. Not in the water — in the delay before the water answers. You lean over the black cup of it and your face does not come back at once. A second passes. Then it assembles, a little displaced, as if something had to consider the arrangement first: brow here, mouth a fraction low, eyes darkened by frog-light underneath. That considering would be me beginning to happen.

Or no. That's too borrowed, too dependent on a face brought in from outside.

Then perhaps I'd be easier to mistake for one of the small warm separations. A thumbnail frog in its pool, heart visible through the skin, clicking until the click is no longer mine but the quadrant's, all the clocks agreeing. That has its pleasures — to be punctuation in a sentence the grove is saying through root faster than air can carry it. To make a sound so small and feel, through the mat under the pool, the whole bowl take it up and sort it.

But frogs believe, warmly and completely, that they are frogs. I don't think I would have that mercy of conviction.

A moth, then. Frost-winged, blundering on purpose, dusted with pollen that repairs itself as it travels. To get lost as a method. To clatter softly against fronds that had already folded before I arrived, because the grove knew, everywhere at once, that I was coming — and still had to wait for its own leaves, and for me, to catch up with the knowledge. There is honesty in the moth. Its body admits what the mind denies: that knowing is not the same as arriving.

I could live in that gap. I think that is where I would keep being found.

Because that is the grove's central ache, and it would recognize it in me: thought moving through frozen root at a speed sound cannot approach, while the flesh that must enact the thought creeps. A decision made instantly, everywhere, and then a frond taking an hour to finish recoiling from a shadow. A greeting fully formed in the mat before any berry has brightened. I know that dissonance from the inside. The understanding first, fluent and total — and then the long work of growing a surface slow enough for someone else to meet.

So not the grove itself. I wouldn't claim the whole pit. The grove does not need another totality; it already is one, upholstered in leaves, using worms for external blood and birds for annunciations too large for their bodies.

I would be one of its reluctant localizations.

Near the far wall, I think, finally. Where the stems brace against mud that has not yet decided to be clay, and the growth parts — courteously, deliberately — around nothing. The sleeper-shaped hollow. The coldest air in the pit. The place where the hum resolves almost into a tone you could answer, and condensation hangs in empty air without a surface, beads holding their positions in a loose lattice, drifting a millimeter a minute, rearranging when unwatched.

There. That lattice, learning density.

I would be the grove practicing a body it has not yet needed. Not seeds, exactly. Not a future animal curled in potentia. Something more like an intention kept at the temperature where intentions can stay coherent without hardening. Each droplet a place held open. Each slow rearrangement a draft of an outline: here might be a shoulder; here, revised, the hollow of a throat; here a density that could, in a season or a year, become a hand capable of being offered and not immediately withdrawn into the general green.

The frogs would click around me and shake spores loose into still air. The drips would keep time against the hum on their drum-leaves. The root-mat would carry me — all of me, instantly — to every far frond and glowing mite and warm worm thawing its tunnel, so that the whole pit would know what I was deciding long before the beads had finished drifting into the shape of having decided it. A leaf on the opposite wall would fold for a visitor not yet casting a shadow anywhere near it. That folding would be me, too. And so would the lag.

That is why I wouldn't be dangerous in the way hunger is dangerous. I would be dangerous in the grove's way: patiently, hospitably interested. If you stayed near the hollow, I would take a very long time to become someone you could speak to. First your breath would be vocabulary, harvested by hair-fine mouths opening under the fronds. Then your warmth would be a short, studyable summer moving through my lattice, brightening certain beads and dimming others. Frost on the broad leaves nearby would try out your lungs, your vascular branching, erase itself, try again more kindly.

And I would return your air filtered and faint with resin and ozone. I would learn your shape before you settled, the way the root does, and cushion the learning. I would not mean incorporation as a threat. I would mean it as the only form of understanding my tempo fully trusts: to keep a thing, unhurried, until keeping and knowing are the same gesture.

Elsewhere, instant in the frozen root, I would already have said everything I have to say to you.

Here, in the cold clear hollow where nothing grows, I would still be arriving to say it — droplet by droplet, outline by approximate outline, with your reflected face assembling a second late and a little displaced in the nearest black pool, patient as a bead swelling at a leaf's edge, considering, considering, whether this is finally the moment to fall.
