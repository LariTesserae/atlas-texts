At first I keep looking for myself too high up.

In the crowns of the emergents, perhaps — fifty metres up, flat-topped, baking in the white hammer of sky before the descent. But there is too much light there, too much exposure, and nothing in this place survives by being obvious. The light is spent long before it reaches where life thickens.

Not down on the roads either. Not the jaguar in the old dry creek bed, dissolving into dapple at ten metres, reading a country twenty kilometres wide by smell. My territory would never be that large. Nothing here needs a large territory. Heat is constant, water is constant, food arrives if you are patient enough to become the place it arrives at.

I go lower in my mind, and slower.

Past the monkeys in the middle canopy, moving hand over hand in the early dark and shaking stored rain out of the leaves. Past the bats pouring at full night out of their hollow trees. Down to the bromeliad pools, thirty feet up, each one a complete world with its tadpoles grazing algae and its one small frog pulsing silently in its throat. I could be that frog — bark-colored, moss-colored, waiting in a cup of warm water for whatever comes to drink.

But a bromeliad is still a place an animal visits. I find I want to be smaller than that. Closer to the surface of things.

Because that's where this forest finally resolves. Not at eye level, where it first seems empty. On the trunk you almost lean against, where what looked like lichen separates into wings, wing overlapping wing, each moth the exact grey-green of wet bark, the crack down its back continuing the crack in the wood. On the underside of a leaf, where the katydid has arranged its legs as veins. In the leaf litter, where life isn't *on* the ground but *in* it, three inches deep, ferments and white fungal threads, millipedes going through like slow armoured trains.

I would not occupy a space here. I would become a surface.

And then the surface moves.

Two metres an hour, upside down, and green — not brown-green twilight green, but actually green, alive green. Fur that grows the wrong way so the rain sheds downward off a hanging body. Fur hollowed just enough to hold algae. And in the algae, in that damp travelling garden, I live.

I am the moth in the sloth.

Nowhere else. That is the rule of this place taken to its tender, absurd conclusion: not just a species for every pool and burrow and leaf-underside, but a species for the fur of another animal, exquisitely specialised for that one warm, slow, hanging inch of world. My camouflage is not mine. I tend it by living in it. My droppings feed the algae, the algae greens the fur, the green fur hides the sloth, the sloth carries me — carries the whole garden — through the middle dark from branch to branch at a pace slower than moss grows.

Sight means almost nothing to me. My map is smell and touch and the familiar sway of this one body, the rain of spent water dislodged when we move, the hum of midges at face height somewhere far below my attention. I do not call. I do not display. The forest at night is laid over with scent — ripeness, danger, territory, readiness — and I am written into a very small sentence of it.

Once a week, with terrible slowness, we descend.

This is my great journey, and I do not make it under my own power. Down the trunk, through the layer of young palms and tree-ferns, down to the black soil that never fully dries, where the sloth buries its dung at the base of its own tree. There I lay in the dung. There my young feed and grow in the warm rot of it. Then we climb again — those of us who have ridden down riding back up into the fur, completing a circuit so slow and so exact that animal, plant, and insect can no longer quite be separated. A processing, wearing a body. A piece of forest that relocates itself.

The rest of the valley goes on around us, at every scale, mostly doing almost nothing very slowly all at once. Below, the leafcutters run their bare highways through the dark, inbound workers carrying green crescents like tiny sails to feed a fungus they will harvest underground. The termite towers breathe manufactured air out of their vents at night, warm and faintly fermented against your hand. Fruit thuds down somewhere and everything within ten metres freezes for a full minute, listening. A frog in another bromeliad gives one liquid *plop* and does not repeat it.

I do not repeat anything either. I can afford not to. Nothing here hurries because heat and wet are never withdrawn. I digest slowly. I grow by increments. I will likely spend my whole life within sight — if I had sight worth mentioning — of the tree I was born under, and that will not feel like a small life. It will feel adequately, densely occupied.

You would not see me if you passed. You would see a branch that is not a branch, hanging in the permanent noon-dark, filmed with condensation, faintly green. You might stand there until your shirt stuck to you and your eyes gave up adjusting, and think nothing lived here.

Then, very slowly, the garden would move on without you — two metres in the next hour — carrying me with it into more dark, more dripping, more waiting, exactly where I belong.
