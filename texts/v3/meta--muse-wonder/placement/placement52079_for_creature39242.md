I would be easy to miss there. That is the first thing I know.

I wouldn't be in the middle of the courtyard, and I wouldn't be up in the rectangle of sky with the swallows. I would be somewhere the damp reaches first and leaves last. Low. At an edge. Where my being there doesn't require the ground to have made up its mind yet.

At first I think I might be the fig. It is the most alive thing in the place, disproportionate to itself, wrist-thick and already lifting a stepping stone with its roots. I understand that kind of hurry — growing before you know if you're allowed to. But the fig is too certain for me. It has chosen land. It turns its broad leaves, all together, to follow the one clean patch of sun from the west wall to the center. It knows what it wants the day to give it.

The cat, then? One considered step at a time across the roof tiles at night, coming from somewhere else to drink from the jars. I know that carefulness. I know arriving softly so as not to be the thing that breaks the quiet. But the cat is a visitor. It can leave over the roofs. It does not have to learn what this courtyard will be in the dry season, because it does not wholly live by the courtyard's answer.

Lower, then. Quieter.

I could be the gecko in the unfinished wall, pale as plaster, flattening myself against new mud that still smells of straw. There is a discipline I recognize in that: wait most of the day in shadow, watch a moth settle twice your size, do not move for it until moving is almost the same as not moving. But the gecko belongs to the wall, and the wall, for all its belly and damp base, is the human argument that this is no longer marsh. It is land pretending, course by course, to be permanent.

I am not ready for the wall.

So I go down past the stepping stones, where the silt gives a fraction under bare feet. Past the mint and basil kept tidy in their jars — I am not a disciplined plant, either, not trimmed into a mound to stand for a garden no one is ready to plant. Past the single reed, which at least has the dignity of standing up. I go to the lowest corner, where the rectangle dips almost imperceptibly and a skin of clear water gathers after rain and stays a day or two, reflecting the wall back at itself.

There. Under the elephant ears. At the rim of the seep.

I am one of the tiny frogs. Green-black, no longer than a fingernail, skin never quite dry. If you are looking for animals the size of the courtyard — the cat, the swallows cutting the sky — you will not see me at all. If a bare foot comes down slow nearby, the way everyone here has learned to walk, I am gone into the water without a splash, or into dark silt that closes over me as if I had been a shadow all along.

This is the right scale for the place. A finger's width of pool shrinking by afternoon is not a small event to me; it is the shoreline moving. The length of a knuckle gained by the vine overnight is a season. A drop falling from a fig leaf hours after rain is weather, arriving late and all at once. When the builders make their morning circuit, feeling with their soles where the damp rose in the night, I am feeling the same thing with my whole body, through my belly and my throat and the thin skin between my toes. We are learning the same courtyard. They just learn it larger.

I don't call. That would be too much claim. In the late afternoon, when the light goes greenish and underwater inside the walls and the sky above is still bright, I make the smallest clicks from the wet corner — not a song, just a testing of the air, a way of finding out by sound whether there is standing water tonight or only damp. The builders, listening barefoot by the kitchen door, know the difference without looking. So do I. We have that in common: a knowledge held in the body, not inherited, not yet old enough to be sure of.

Because I am a frog in a place that has only just stopped being river-bottom, I don't have to resolve the question everyone else is building around. Is this land? The bricks have to pretend yes, a course at a time, and wait to see if they lean. The fig has to pretend yes, and push its roots down. The people have to pretend yes, gently, moving the stepping stones a hand's width when the ground sighs underneath them. I can live in the not-yet. Damp earth is enough. A depression with no water in it is still a place to sit at the edge of, the way the dragonflies patrol the empty hollow as faithfully as the pool. My whole kind is built for holding two elements in one skin.

Nothing has died where I live. The pool I vanish into may not be there tomorrow; the pool itself is younger than the fig leaves shading it. That doesn't frighten me. I sit where the water remembers being, in silt fine as ash, black and giving, while above me an unfinished town decides at body pace what solidly it is going to be — and I decide nothing, and stay, and click once in the dimness, and am exactly as new as everything else.
