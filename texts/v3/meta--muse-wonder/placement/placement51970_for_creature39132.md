I wouldn't start on the floor.

I keep thinking I would — that the floor is where a story properly begins — but in this valley the floor is only preparation. Empty leaf-mold, a sharp tooth of limestone waiting through the litter, a game trail worn by hooves in the night. To live down there you have to be either very large, or very still, or willing to be eaten as part of someone else's timing. I am none of those, not here.

And not the sky either. I pass through the sky on my way in, hand over slick hand coming down, but I don't stay with the swifts in their forty meters of hard bright air. They are beautiful and completely foreign — pale underneath, frantic, living a brief life that only touches the valley when a storm knocks it sideways. I want the dark. Not as hiding. As medium.

I come down through the monkeys first. For a while I think I might be one of them: long-limbed, heavy-browed, black-handed from a lifetime of wet bark and tannin, learning the valley by watching shoulders next to mine. I'd carry a nut down to the limestone anvils, carry a stone that fit my palm as if my palm had been made around it, seat the nut, crack, eat, drop everything. I'd be courteous, suddenly and inexplicably courteous, just before a fruit-fall. I'd know things in my hands that I could never keep.

But they never land. That's the trouble. They give birth holding on. They die falling. Their knowledge has to be copied body to body or it sinks into the mold with last season's good stone, and I find myself wanting — not permanence, nothing here wants permanence, permanence would just rot — but a *place* to return my voice to. A throat that holds me while I call.

So I go lower. Past the trunk-space where the bat river pours at dusk, twenty minutes of wings bending around trees without thinning. Past the moths crowding pale bark, the beetles blundering open-winged from leaf to leaf, the glossy crow-bird with a thorn crosswise in its beak that it will throw away the moment it works. Past birds I will never see, only hear — a fluted whistle from no throat, a boom that shakes the condensation out of the leaves over my head.

Lower still, and the light stops being light. It comes down in solid dusty columns and dies before it's useful. It's green twilight all the way, black-green, bottle-green, leaves flashing maroon and copper underneath when something passes and turns them. And here the plants start to be inhabited.

Here is where I stop descending.

You would find me about three meters off the valley floor, in the cup of a tank plant wedged into the crook where a fallen giant has been impaled on a limestone blade. Not in standing water — there is almost none — but in the inch of clean rain the plant keeps in its central throat, funneled there by huge soft dark leaves positioned to catch the drip. The seep-hole is somewhere below me, beading up clear and mineral through the fault, filling a handspan, sinking again. I can hear it in the pitch of things.

I am small enough to be overlooked by everything except what is specifically looking. Glass-green, so saturated I look wet even in the brief mid-afternoon when the floor is only humid — though I am, in fact, always wet. Orange toes, absurdly, flamboyantly orange, splayed against the inner wall of the leaf. Waxy color in a place too dark to properly see color. That's fine. Here color isn't for distance. It's for the half-second of close contact: a flash when I move, a signal at skin range.

The plant shouts because I am in it. That's my work and my address at once. My throat-sac pulses against the leaf-wall and the whole architecture of the bromeliad takes the call and throws it, so it sounds — to anything listening through the cicada-hiss, through that continuous sizzling pressure that is less background than weather — as if the vegetation itself has a temper, a territory, a desire. Hundreds of us do this, or one of us in hundreds of tempers, and the valley inhales and erupts on a signal underneath the noise that I feel in my toes before I hear it.

I feed the plant that holds me, dropping waste into its throat. It feeds me a world: beetles the size of thumbnails blundering in to drink and staying too long, moths dusting the rim, ants that I eat quickly before their road decides to go through me. Fog comes down off the ridges at night and soaks everything without rain. By morning my cup is full, every spiderweb between the leaves is a necklace of beads, and I am drinking the valley as it slides off its own green surfaces. I know which leaves to refuse — my refusal *is* the information. If I won't sit in a cup, nothing wise drinks from it. I am a small, bright test the other animals run without thinking.

I am loud in the right register. That's how you last here: not quiet, not still, not careful in the empty-place senses. I call through the deafening until the deafening is a map I live inside. Frog-pitch dropping ahead means water. Monkey courtesy in the branches above means fruit is about to fall, and when the mast front hits — parrots and toucans screaming down, pigs ploughing underneath, wasps drunk on split pulp, my whole hectare a brawl — I move with it, not against it, hop by hop down wet bark to the next plant flushed full of displaced insects, eating in the turbulence while the predators drift politely at the edges and take the distracted.

Nothing I have will accumulate. No nest — my eggs go into warm leaf-mold when the season of my body says so, guarded by presence, not structure, and if the canopy tears and closes again before they finish, then the next batch goes elsewhere. When this plant collapses — and it will, outline in a month, soft green hill in three, termites in the fallen trunk, roots prying the limestone crack wider while the rock ticks as it cools under the animal noise — I will already be two plants over, calling from a new throat, having learned the new cup's resonance with my feet.

And I will freeze.

That's the other half of living here, the half everything small is built around. When the chorus stutters — that half-second hitch rippling outward as frog, insect, bird, monkey go silent in sequence — I stop mid-call with my sac still half-full, orange toes locked, and I become a wet green thought pressed against a leaf. Because that tear in the sound has a shape, and the shape is moving along the game trail below, breathing through its mouth so the damp doesn't whistle. I don't need to see it. Seeing is the least reliable thing in the valley. I need the silence to pass, to heal over, for the cicadas to resume their rain-stick hiss and the frogs to pulse above it and the howlers, eventually, to shake the water out of the canopy with a roar I feel in the small sternum I have.

Then I call again. Louder than makes sense for my size, from inside a plant, in the green dark at noon, over rock that hasn't decided its final shape yet — holding my one cup of stored rain, saturating, replacing myself at indecent speed, adding my voice to the volume that, for now, holds it all together.
