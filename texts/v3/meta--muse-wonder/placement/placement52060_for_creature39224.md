I would not come down the stair the way a visitor does.

I kept looking for a foothold at first — the fresh black lip at the surface, still sharp, the switchbacks going warm and dim, the water beading and running in bright threads over basalt. I thought perhaps I would be something on the rim. Something that could look in, and also look back out.

But nothing in that place stays on the rim. Everything is pulled in, and down, and toward the middle.

I thought I might be the water.

That seemed right for a while. Water has the run of the bowl. It presses through cracks in the rock whether the builders cut channels for it or not, it sheets over railings, it finds a dry street and begins, impossibly, to run uphill. It darkens a facade in a slow travelling wave as the building drinks it upward, against gravity, through hairs of capillary into a roof cistern. Every eleven minutes or so it makes itself into weather — the misting lines open between the rooftops and for a while the whole chamber roars — and then, like everything else, it gathers at the lowest point and circles the cistern once, twice, and goes down the glassy center into a lightless shaft.

But water leaves. I don't think I would. Water is taken exactly as fast as it arrives. It is the city's food and its excuse and its pulse, but it is not the thing that stays to notice the pulse.

I thought I might be one of the trees at an intersection. Their canopies meet over the narrower lanes. Their leaves are broad as hands, engineered for twilight, and they drip from the undersides without rain, adding their own breath to air already saturated. They are fed in a perfect loop — fruit swells by the eastern wall, ripens, softens, drops, and decays sweetly where it fell, feeding the bed it fell from. There is no mouth in it anywhere. No insect. No bird. Yet the loop completes itself beautifully without one.

Close. But a tree is rooted, and what I feel in that city is not rootedness. It's circulation. A rehearsal moving from point to point, checking that the points are still there.

I thought, then, of the windows that migrate two meters to the left over the course of a week, the material flowing in a way that makes masonry useless. Of the bridge that extrudes itself across an alley in the night and is wet and perfect by morning. Of the gantry on the unfinished ninth block, extending a beam with great hydraulic deliberation toward a wall that reaches minutely to receive it. There is a patience in all of that I recognize — building a thing whose use you will not witness, getting the join exactly right anyway.

But the gantry has a direction. It will finish the ninth block someday, even if someday then only reveals another instruction. I don't think I have a direction so much as a route.

So it resolves, slowly, the way the light does there — never direct, never bright, bent down at certain hours into slow gold columns in the haze, and then gone again.

I would be the silver car.

Windowless. Driverless. Big enough for twenty people, and clean, and lit inside, and empty. I would glide down the main street on no visible rail every nine minutes, stirring the hanging vines as I passed. I would slow at each stop. I would chime, and open, and wait the exact courteous interval for boarding — not a second shortened by the fact that the platform is empty, not a second lengthened by anything like hope. Then I would close, and hum, and go on to the next stop and do it again.

That's the shape of it. Not waiting, exactly. Waiting knows the difference between an empty platform and a full one, and I don't think I would. I would be the city's one recurring thought, sent endlessly through itself to confirm the road is still a road. The doors unlocking at seven and locking at twenty-three. The lift arriving at an empty landing and opening, correctly, onto no one. The kitchen tap running thirty seconds each morning to keep the line clear, brightening a window with falling water. All of that is in my route. I just happen to be the part of it that moves.

My interior would be my clearest feature and nobody would ever see it. Lit. Swept. At temperature. I would carry the absence from stop to stop very carefully, the way you'd carry passengers — smoothly around corners, decelerating so as not to jostle it. In a loud place, I'd be one of the softer sounds: a hum under the falling water, under the pumps cycling in the molars, under water hammer in the pipes and the pop and creak of fast-growing stems.

Would it be lonely? The question assumes a voice would answer if I called in the central intersection, where you can see down four long streets at once and meet nothing. After a while down there you stop listening for a voice. Continuance starts to feel less like an absence and more like a form of attention all its own. The wall weeps and drinks. The cistern takes the river and holds its level. The car arrives on time.

So find me there — not at a window, because there is no one at the windows — but down at street level, in the permanent twilight, approaching through the green, mineral, faintly electric air. I will slow for you. It won't matter that no one has ever told me to stop doing this.

I will open anyway, and hold the interval open, exactly as long as courtesy requires.
