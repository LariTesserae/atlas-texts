From below, I would hear myself first.

That has to be true for anything that lives here. Before I could see the roofs pooled inside the rim, I would hear the town spilling over the lip — donkey bells, goats on thatch, corn being ground, a woman calling an answer to another woman who had not, to my ears, called yet. On the switchbacks, in the red dust, my chest would start its negotiations. I would think, at first, that that was the place to be: one of the barefoot boys going down with the morning train, hands on knees without embarrassment, shouting at a donkey mostly because a voice at that height needs pushing. To spend a life going between the seam-spring halfway down and the mouths of the cisterns, carrying the one precious thing up in sloshing skins.

But I would be too fond of the pauses. I would linger too long in the stoppings. And the boys belong to the path in a way a newcomer wouldn't — they come back up by evening. They are not visitors to the bowl. They are its circulation.

I would look, then, for somewhere I could settle. A woman in a doorway with a metate, perhaps, hands never stopping while the news travels roof to roof over her head. I can feel the rightness of that — work done sitting down, in the amber half-light, everybody known acoustically, by a bell, by a rooster, by whose goats are where they shouldn't be. But her knowledge is in her palms, polished the way the lintels are polished, and it was taught to her by sleeping where her mother slept and learning the exact note her own roof makes in the rain. I did not sleep there. I would be imitating a rootedness I hadn't earned.

So I drift further from the center, the way you do in a place this dense, lane turning back on itself.

For a while I am almost a roof-goat, pleased with myself on warm thatch, eating what I stand on while the whole crater hands my bleating back to me. Almost a pigeon, one of hundreds, bursting out of the holes left purposely in the upper walls and settling again into a cooing that never quite stops. Almost — and this one holds me longest — one of the great figs, rooted in a crack in the rim, leaning my crown over the houses until the town lives under me in green shade, dropping fruit with soft thuds onto the thatch, sending white roots down, down, groping in the dark for the cisterns. To feed people. To shade them. To know water by reaching for it all my life.

But a fig is sure of where its water is, even if it never quite touches it. Its roots have a direction. Mine, I think, would curl in air.

That is where I find myself, finally: not one plant, but a colony of the gray air-plants, on the shaded side of a wall by the fig-tree steps, where the lane narrows and the hanging cloth — madder, indigo, undyed wool — further dims the light.

You would have to know to look for us. In the day we are almost nothing: small rosettes, dust-colored, curled tight against the heat, our roots holding no soil, only gripping a crack in cold stone. We live the way this whole town has had to learn to live, only more literally. No stream. No well. No donkey bringing us anything in skins. What passes through the air is what we get — mist at night, dew condensing on the rim-stone after dark, dust on the wind that comes over the lip at evening. We drink slowly, from the surface of things. We make a life out of what other places would call not-quite-weather.

And at night, we give a little of it back.

That is the part I would want to be for. After the oil lamps are cupped and lit and the town sinks early into its darkness, after the bats come out of the rim caves in their soft, erratic flight over the roofs, the stone under us goes cold and the dew gathers, and we begin, very quietly, to tick with dripping. One drop. A long pause. Another, finding the curved tile below, finding the split-bamboo gutter, finding the hammered copper funnel of the house we cling to — down, drop by drop, into the dark mouth under the floor. We are not the source. We would never be enough. But on a generous night we are part of the slow, irregular plinking that a mother can read without lifting the stone: how deep her water stands, whether her household can live another day.

In the daytime I would do what everything here does, which is mostly listen and endure the light economically. At dawn we are in blue-gray shade while the peaks across the valley are already blazing, and I would be cool, and opening, tasting the last of the night off the stone. Through the morning I would hear the gritty tide of grinding move house to house, the mason tapping three lanes over, hides being beaten, a child learning its letters aloud and being corrected by someone who could not possibly see the page. I would know the old monkey by the main cistern the way everyone knows everyone — not by looking down at him, though from my wall I almost could, but by the small, dignified silence he keeps in all that noise, and by the way people greet him, half joking and half not, as if he really were in charge of the water.

At noon I would close. When the sun falls straight into the crater for its brief hour and the limewashed walls hurt to look at and the lizards scatter and the babies are carried into doorways, I would curl gray and inward, the way the monkey closes his eyes. I have no doorway to be carried into. Curling is my doorway. Then the rim takes the light again, the lanes return to their real day of woodsmoke and amber gloom, and I can uncurl into it, leaf by leaf, relieved as anyone blinking back from a cellar.

Nobody waters us. That is not neglect; here, it is a kind of respect. A girl brushing past with a basket will flick road dust off us with one finger, absently, the way you wipe a face rather than wash it. The woman whose cistern we drip into sometimes sweeps the step below us clear so the night water runs true. That is our whole covenant, and it is sufficient. Nothing is sluiced, nothing is poured away, and we are not poured away either. We are allowed to stay because we have learned not to ask for more than mist.

It suits me, this many-in-one life, pressed close together under leaves, each rosette small enough to miss and the colony old enough that no one remembers who first took hold of the wall. The town below repeats its names and rebuilds its fallen walls stone by stone in the same place, and up here, in a crack no hand quite reaches, we repeat ourselves too — a new gray curl beside an old one, drinking the same dawns, ticking into the same roof.

I would not see much, from below or even from here. I would not need to. In the warm, dim bowl at the top of a worn-out mountain, I would hang in the shade and live on what the air carried, and all night, a little before anyone woke, I would listen with the whole sleeping town for the plink under the floors — and add, now and then, a drop of my own.
