You would find my shadow first.

That is how I would know I belonged there — not because I had learned the etiquette, but because I had finally arrived in the order my life already preferred: an impression first, a warmth in the air, a voice arriving a little detached from its source, and only afterward, if I was lucky, the person herself. In Vael-Mora no one would apologize for that. Someone by the Eastern Cisterns would already be turned slightly toward the empty place beside the arch, making room. Someone would have left a cup where my hand would want to go.

At first I think I would try to live in one of the towers, because towers are what a newcomer understands as an address. High enough to see the basin holding its copper light. Balconies terraced with gardens. A dew-room of my own, kept at the temperature of deep shade, with some unremarkable beloved object at the center of it — not a grandmother's hand, I haven't earned anything that grave and tender. A dented copper bowl, perhaps. A stone from a coastline I no longer live on. I would make tea from almost nothing and learn to serve it without explanation.

I would wake a minute before the cooling, bare-armed, with all the others in windows kilometers apart. That part I believe. I have always woken just before the change in a room, before the bell, before the message lands — prickling with a difference no one has named yet. Two degrees less warm for eleven minutes would be enough of a dawn for me. I would stand while the tower opened its ten thousand ceramic petals around me and my hair beaded and the great leaves below curled around their liter of still water, and for once anticipation would not be anxiety. It would be weather. It would be what skin is *for*.

But I don't think I would stay a tower person. I'm too fond of the pause in the conversation, the part the Vaeline leave open on purpose to listen for a root clicking through warm stone or a reservoir ticking full. Give me a few seasons in that dim, shadowless light and my pupils would widen and my real work would find me, the way arches bud knuckle-like near the houses of good negotiators.

I would be an apprentice to the maintenance crews. Not the ones who print tower segments in held lightning — I'd flinch, politely, and be reassigned. The ones in grey-green that darkens with damp, who stand before a living wall for an entire afternoon doing what looks like nothing. Waiting for wood and layered ceramic to decide on a shape it can hold for ten years, for a child's whole weight. I know that stance. I know standing very still in front of something half-formed and half-alive, not forcing the doorway, listening until the sentence — the wall — discovers where it can open without tearing. They would recognize me not by tools but by temperament. Buildings open for patient people a little sooner. So do conversations. So, eventually, do I.

And because no gift in Vael-Mora stays in only one district, I suspect I'd have been raised, or re-raised, in one of the old Experimental Generosity neighborhoods, where gravity was laid in as a municipal kindness and never quite laid straight. It would explain the way I'd enter every room for the rest of my life: testing each step with the ball of my foot, one hand drifting toward a rail that isn't there, faintly apologetic even on reliable floors. A sudden smell of iron still meaning *the floor is about to become a suggestion*. I would be teased for it gently all my life. I would marry, disproportionately, a dancer or another maintenance worker — someone who understood that care is not slowness, that you can cross twenty kilometers between one breath and the next and still believe hurry has almost no uses between arches.

My names would sit lightly, and I would be glad of it. In the wet season something like *third cup after cooling*. In a dry year, the name you were told to ask for, which I'd still answer to, but with a small delay, as if hearing it through water. I would keep the old ones the way Vael-Mora keeps old maps: not as records, as poems to former arrangements. I would learn not to finish sentences that had turned unkind partway through. I have had my own young voice returned to me hours later, softened and lowered, in rooms where I did not expect to meet it; I don't need the city's help to know why you speak as if your words will outlive your mood. But I would be grateful for a place that had built that knowledge into its acoustics, that kept only the volume it was given. Why risk the echo.

Some afternoons, because gentleness arranged from outside can start to feel like a kind of sleep, I would walk out past the last tower to the uninstructed forest, where no path reroutes itself for my comfort and no arch buds from the soil when I am tired. I'd come back scratched by ordinary branches, obscurely proud, smelling of mineral steam and green sap. And some afternoons I wouldn't make it that far. I'd stop in the cistern marsh with the long-throated waders, those mostly silent birds who can stand motionless for a full day, and I'd practice their trick until the children were right about me too: not standing at all, but listening downward, through the water, into the part of the city below the city. There is always a part below. A reservoir. A returned shout. A name a district used before its cistern dried.

In the deepest dim, when the towers lit their greenish stored glow and the whole basin became a sparse constellation lying low among dark trees, you would probably find me there — in the marsh, at the edge of an arch still deciding whether to be an arch, at the foot of a wall holding its breath — doing my one job, which is to wait at an angle and let other things arrive in their own order. Shadow first. Then warmth. Then words, low and unhurried, with the vowels left long enough for something else to be heard inside them.

And when the cooling came and a shallow pocket bloomed in the clay where there had been dry mineral plate that morning, I would not drink from it. I would stand around it the way other peoples stand around a hearth. I would point it out to a stranger, not unkindly, the veil and the towers upside down in it and much closer together than they are in life. I would teach my children — if the city lent me children, and it might, the way it lends everyone a cup at the right moment — that this, briefly, was the real city. The film of water where building and garden and skin are all damp at once and indistinguishable in purpose. All of them drinking. All of them holding light without shadow. All of them about to let it go.

Then I'd get on, at plant-speed, with living in the part around it.
