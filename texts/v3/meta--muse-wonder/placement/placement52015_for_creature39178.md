At first I think I would arrive the way everyone arrives — by the lift.

Up from the lower desert, through the brown haze, ears failing to equalize in any sensible way, watching the manufactured cliff go past and past and past until the plain simply begins again, finished and level and impossible. I would get out at the station with too-new boots and a suit that has no plates on it, and I would make too much noise inside my own helmet. My breath would be enormous to me. Everything else would refuse to carry.

I don't stay that person. No one with a smooth suit lasts long as a self up there.

For a while I try on being higher. One of the gliders, I think. That has to be the vantage: membrane-thin, field-borrowed, hanging almost motionless in a thermal that shouldn't be able to lift me, eclipsing stars at noon for a breath at a time. It would be beautiful and it would be clean. No dust in the seals. No tether at the waist.

But I go cold up there, and not only in the shade-sense. A glider is respected at a distance. It is named like weather. When it dies no one touches it. I find I don't want to be looked up at across that much thin air. I want the ground through my soles. I want to be close enough that something has to split around my ankles.

I try lower, then. A licker at a salt pan at dawn, plates overlapping, tongue rasping mineral off basalt, making the one honest outside sound the Pan allows. Tortoise-slow would suit me. Growing my shelter out of what I graze. Not noticing very much and having that pass, rightly, for affection.

But my hands keep appearing in the picture. I keep wanting to secure things. To tap twice on a forearm. To pick up what someone else has lost before the sky learns their address.

So I sink — only a little, only to standing height — and I become a Holdfast.

Not an elder. Not yet. My plates are mid-thick. You can still see the station grey at my elbows where the lichen paste never took, but my shoulders have begun to flower white with salt, and the seams carry red dust that no amount of burrow-shaking will remove. My chest aches pleasantly when I come down at night into thick air. It's too big for thin weather and exactly right for voices.

I live a careful kilometer from a pylon. Which one matters more than it should, the way a difficult neighbor matters. Ours is the generous one, if generous is the word for a machine older than rivers that gives off waste heat you can gather without touching and is prone, now and then, to making its fused-glass apron briefly, terribly heavy. I would never camp there. I know the edge of the glass by the change in the tremor under my boots. I have pressed my palm to the pylon's base — never on the apron, never when it hums high — and felt that low negotiation in my teeth, and waited until my sense of down stopped being an argument.

My burrow is not picturesque. It's a widened cooling joint roofed with basalt slabs, a double lock sunk into rock, and three meters down, air thick enough to smell of iron flour, other people, condensed water, warm stone. At night I can speak. In the day, above ground, I mostly don't try. The Pan taught me earlier and better languages: boot set hard and twisted into basalt for *stop*. Two fingers on a forearm, through the glove, for *shear coming*. A palm flat to someone else's helmet, held, both of us catching the same vibration through our skulls at once. Some agreements don't need to be pronounced.

I walk a circuit. My family — three, five, sometimes seven of us, depending on how you count people walking the same loop in opposite directions — walks with a group of lickers from salt to lichen to the warm lee of a dike, clearing dust-rivers from their path with weighted poles. I walk two days once to retrieve a dropped spanner. Not because we are short of spanners. Because leaving it would be rude to the future and instructive to the sky. I bring it home on a wrist-line like everything else I love: tools, water, and for years, a child at my waist who learned to recite the rules without moving her lips before she was untied.

Trust rock, not horizon. Secure everything, always. Never expect the quiet to mean safety.

I eat when gradients feed me. Rosette filaments in the hour after a shear, when whole grey fields open at once to drink the disturbed air — tongue tingling, inner ear singing a note with no sound in it. Black lichen flour that tastes of rust and old batteries. Silica grass melted down in the borrow of pylon heat, brittle as spun glass. Salt, always salt.

Between the two pylons across the old riverbed ghost — the ones that have been disagreeing for four generations — I take the long way around. The lichen is richest in the shear-zone between them. So is everything else. My compass turns there in slow, sincere circles, entirely convinced of each new north. I put my teeth, gently, against stone and read the true distance in the tremor, the way my grandmother taught me. Anatomy, not proverb.

When a repair-swarm gathers — metallic motes prickling the air, folding themselves into a membrane, a tool, a thought I am not meant to follow — I sit down wherever I am. I secure my pack. I wait. You do not walk through a neighbor's thought.

At dusk my favorite work begins: lights. The pylons blink on one after another across a plain that curves visibly with the planet, and on two low rises my people answer in long, patient patterns. A conversation can take all evening across ten kilometers of refraction and thin air. I don't mind. Nothing in my life is in a hurry except gravity, when it fails. I have learned to keep my feet when a Rising takes the pack-weight off my shoulders and tries to teach my belongings to orbit. The plates help. Getting older helps, strangely. Weight, honestly grown, is a kind of faithfulness.

That is where you would find me, if you came back after the first noon and thought that dark shape was only a standing stone.

I am the stone that is listening with its feet.

Ankles very still while a river of grit runs an inch above the basalt and splits around me without touching. Tethered at wrist and waist. Under a zenith black enough to hold stars at midday. Waiting, with everyone I am tied to, for the continent to finish its nightly renegotiation — held up, held in, held together by arguing machines I did not build and do not worship and would not live without.

And glad, in the mineral patience the Pan eventually teaches everything, not to be finished yet.
