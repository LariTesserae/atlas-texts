I would not be at the centre of it. Not at the dredges on the lagoon, not out in the sheet-floods with the heron-things sweeping their bills above my crouch. Nothing there is in a hurry, and neither — I think, given time for my eyes to stop aching — would I be. I would be somewhere the country is thinnest and most kept: on a causeway, halfway between two pump-houses, close enough to feel the stroke of neither quite yet.

That is where you would find me. Barefoot in the warm trough the feet before mine have worn smooth down the middle of the slag. A canvas roll of tools against one hip, gone soft and body-warm. A tin of somebody else's log-book tucked inside my shirt, kept against my skin so the condensation won't take it, because ink here bleeds into topography quickly and a book not kept warm becomes only a swollen, unreadable thing to be fed to the fungus heaps. I would be carrying it from one house to the next the slow way, the only way messages travel here: not shouted across water, not sent into the wet clay of the air, but walked, hand over shoulder, and read at the far end fingertip by fingertip in a room smelling of cane syrup and hot iron.

I would be third-generation, maybe fourth — the kind of child they describe without making a doctrine of it. Born with eyes that opened and searched and learned, mercifully early, to rest. Not blind. That is the outsider's word and it misses the whole arrangement. I would open them still, sometimes: waking from the bright dream none of us describe, or startled in love, and find them wet and intact and entirely unexpecting, and close them again the way you set down a tool this task doesn't need.

What I would have instead is skin that has been apprenticed since the fontanelle. The backs of my hands reading the air before my face arrives anywhere. The soles of my feet fluent in the grammar of the roads — the warmer slab where the buried cable crosses, the cool braid of a freshwater seep, the long exhaled wall of a boiler-house through a whole field of standing water, so that I could be mid-sentence, speaking it into my sister's collarbone where she can feel it, and still step unerringly around the place where the slag has gone sharp at the edge. My scalp would lift, fractionally, at things my ears only afterward confirm: a rat-family crossing ahead in single file, nose to tail. A bat adjusting overhead, its cry a pressure in my sinuses. A child coming home through pond-water she has never learned to hurry across, borne up by it, her feet arriving in my ear through the piers before she arrives at the door.

They would use me, kindly, the way everyone here is used by the roster — for the places where a draught is a piece of news. I'd walk a little ahead of a work crew, not fast, never fast, one palm held flat to a pump housing now and then to feel whether the flywheel is keeping its own slow counsel, and I would be the one who goes quiet first when the damp thins. When the smell sharpens to brine. When every animal in the ditch goes still at once and the frogs nearest me stop, and my legs keep receiving the rest only as vibration. A shadow on its long round, coming around the corner of the transformer yard at less than walking pace, pausing a moment against the running machinery as if listening to work it once did with its hands.

I'd step off into mud that finds life before it finds bottom, and hold the others back with a hand laid flat on the nearest forearm, and hold my breath, and not speak into it. Not from fear. From manners. It might be somebody's grandfather. It might be the woman who taught my mother to sort copper-sweet mollusks from the poisoned ones by sifting ditch-water through her fingers. When it had passed, my chest would forget for a moment whether it had just inhaled, and the scour it left in the mud would still be uncolonised next year, and we would tap the housing and go back to work and the log would say only that the west wall ran cool that day.

My strength would be the local kind. Unspectacular and total. I could close a hand around a chain slick with condensation, or the neck of an eel long as my leg, and simply not slip, and not peak, and wait — one great effort, then the long rest while the consequence travels somewhere underground and the water sighs. Newcomers helping me would always be early for the second stroke. I'd have stopped minding that. I'd lift. I'd wait. I'd feel a wave cross the basin that was set moving by the great sleeper in its dredge pit before I was born, arriving gently at the levee under my feet, going on without me.

At home — a pump-house, a family, because here those are the same sentence — I'd eat what the dark can make without light and find it sufficient: black cane syrup scraped from the sweating joints, fist-sized snails off the mineral crusts, mud-breathing fish that thump curiously against my shin in the ditch and come to the hand as if curiosity were a kind of consent. Sometimes, in the brief years, the red-black algae dried on the boiler housing into something that tastes like remembering meat, and we'd eat it seriously and agree. We'd leave the heron-things their flooded fields. We'd crouch and let the blind bills sweep at the height they've learned for us.

And in the evenings that are not evenings, I'd sit shoulder to shoulder in a house where the tools sleep beside us wrapped in oiled cloth, where the paperwork waits in its dry cabinet heated past comfort, and I'd guide a younger hand over a swollen page — births, deaths, strokes, water levels that agreed to be measured — and we'd enter, in level writing, the new pump-house that appeared this year on a road we have all walked our whole lives. Its concrete still curing in the warmth. Its flywheel already turning. Its peg fitting a younger brother's tools exactly. I wouldn't say the basin built it for itself. I'd say the roster was short, and I'd believe both things in the way you believe a thing with your feet.

If you stayed long enough you'd stop looking for me, because looking is the one sense the country will not feed. You'd find me the way I'd find you: a hand laid warm and entirely damp on your forearm, to let you know a person is passing on the narrow side. Iron, cane-sugar, skin that has not been dry in weeks.

And long after that — after the breaths had spaced themselves to flywheel time, after the family found me cooled through one not-winter and there was less of me to bury than there should have been — a stretch of my causeway would run a fraction cooler in the damp. It would drift. It would pause beside the fence of the transformer yard with its ear, so to speak, to the current, checking whether the note had changed. Workers would step aside for it onto the mud and hold their breath, respectfully, the way I taught them.

Underneath us the whole time — through clay and slag and cable, through the trough worn by bare feet and the page bled soft as fungus — the wave would still be travelling onward.
