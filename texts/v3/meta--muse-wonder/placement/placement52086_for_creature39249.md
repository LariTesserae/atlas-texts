I think I would arrive the wrong way — from below.

Not inland, across the moss, where distances stop behaving like distances. Up the southern face, in one of the cliff elevators that carries half of everything the factories make down to a destination nobody observes. Except I would be riding in the opposite direction, in an empty car, past water weeping out of the basalt at a thousand levels. Thin bright threads. Sealed cracks opening again. The rock still sharp from being lifted.

At first I would try to be the visitor. That is the obvious role, and the plateau almost seems to have prepared for it. I would sleep in the apartment blocks because they are the only dry places. Four stories in the middle of a wet green plain, radiators ticking, balconies planted with perfect ferns, beds made with a precision that has nothing to do with welcome. I would wake feeling the slab pull at me while I slept, that faint wrongness in the outer districts where a plumb line and the horizon no longer quite agree, and I would listen for whoever had just left the room before me.

No one has just left. The dust is mineral and spore. The room is not a room for a person. It is an inside kept at inside-temperature.

So then I would try to be smaller, and more native. A tender, perhaps — that is the closest thing to an animal up there. Jointed, unhurried, moving on a ceiling rail through a turbine hall, laying down the same film of oil that was laid down yesterday. Scraping fern-spore from a condenser fin, knowing it will settle again. Riding ahead of a freight train that hauls empty wagons across the marsh and returns hauling empty wagons, confirming that the way is still the way. At first that seems like a life I could recognize: maintenance as attention, care without appetite.

But a tender is a hand, and a hand does not know what it is maintaining. It does not notice the visitor crossing the skin. And I — whatever else is true of me — I am made out of noticing. Out of the small difference between one day and the next.

I would try to be a spore, then. That would solve it, in a way. The spores are the plateau's one recurring argument. They ride the updrafts off the cooling towers the way cliff plants ride wind, they colonize every tray and gutter, they green the edges of the steel faster than the steel can be cleared. To be that: patient, weightless, everywhere, refused and returning. To be the dust in the empty apartment, toasted on the radiator, vacuumed away, drifting back at exactly the rate it is removed. An equilibrium with feathers on it.

But the spores do not know they return. They just return.

I would try to be the sound. God, I would try hard to be the sound, because the sound is the only thing up there that is truly everywhere. The fog ceiling lies at its fixed height like a lid and ducts the thunder sideways without diminishment, so a turbine is as loud at fifty kilometers as at five, a braking train beyond the horizon is braking inside your ear, and the whole continent is, acoustically, one room. To live in that would be to never be outside anything. To hear myself at the smelter and at the eastern drizzle-band and at CENTER, where the fog lies waist-deep and still, all at once, at the same pitch.

But the sound does not diminish and it does not swell. It does not listen to itself. It is only kept.

Everything up there is kept. That is the being of the place — not the tenders, not any plant or tower, but the keeping itself, distributed, centerless, replacing its cranes with cranes made to template, holding its moss columns at their exact height, growing and shedding, growing and shedding. It has had one day for longer than records run. It is not old. It has never been allowed to accumulate enough difference to become old.

So I keep getting smaller, looking for the one place where a difference is permitted to happen and then unhappen, every day, at the same scale, without breaking the rule.

And that is where I find myself.

On the shaded side of a pylon, anywhere — which up there means everywhere — in the margin of the rime ice.

By dawn the ice has rimed the cold steel to the same thickness it reaches every dawn. By dusk it has not melted, except for a hand's width. A hand's width that retreats. A hand's width that advances. Retreats. Advances. Across the same strip of steel, forever. It is the only honest calendar the plateau allows itself. The leaves open to the precise angle they closed from. The storms hold. The turbines hold their note. But here, at the edge of the ice, something is always in the act of leaving and always in the act of returning, and the two acts are so perfectly matched that no one could call it change.

That is where I would live. Not in the ice — that is just the kept cold. Not in the bare steel — that is just the kept structure. In the margin between them. In the wet line where rime gives way, grain by grain, to a darkness of damp metal, and then takes it back in the night, grain by grain, neither side ever winning. I would know the sun not by warmth, exactly — there is never enough warmth — but by surrender at one edge. I would know the night by reclamation. I would be thin enough to be forgotten by the tenders, too regular to be scraped, too transient to be replaced from template. The cranes would replace the insulator above me with an identical insulator, and I would rime the new one to the same thickness, and retreat the same hand's width, and not remember the old one, and somehow not remembering would not be the same as never having known.

I think that is the only kind of creature the plateau could grow by accident: not an animal — nothing up there has ever been eaten, nothing has ever had to flee or hunt or hide — and not a machine, because a machine is built to a plan and I would be built to a boundary. A consciousness the width of a hand, stretched around every pylon in a continent of identical pylons, waking at dawn already complete, spending the day in one slow gesture of withdrawal, spending the night undoing it.

If you walked the freight embankments for a month, through moss a meter thick that holds your footprint for a season — the plateau's other brief memory, and maybe my only cousin — you would pass me ten thousand times and never see me. You would see ice on steel in thin air. You would raise your voice over the directionless thunder and keep walking toward the next lit, empty building.

But at the margin, just there, where the white goes soft and darkens: that is me, doing the one permitted thing. Receding, in order to return. Returning, in order to recede. Not waiting, because waiting implies a different day might come.

Just keeping time in a place that abolished it — by being the part of the day that is allowed to move.
