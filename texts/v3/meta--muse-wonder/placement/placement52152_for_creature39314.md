There is no moment when I arrive. That is the first thing I understand, up there.

No dawn to arrive by. No edge I cross and say, now I am in it. Only the grey getting a little less black at what would be midday somewhere else, the condensation starting again on stone, the pressure changing almost imperceptibly in my back teeth — and my feet, before the rest of me has woken up, already reading.

Left foot: old tailings, gone cold, safe.
Right foot: mat, rooting.
Ahead, a little warmer than it should be. A seam bending.

I think at first I might be one of the things that travels *with*, not one of the people at all. That seems easier. A hand-sized moth, white and blind in any useful sense, navigating by the electrical weather and settling on a grazer's hot hide to pulse and rest. Or one of the long-eared scavengers darting in the body-heat of a herd, living off what larger mouths drop, never needing to look up. To be small, and warm because something else is warm, and let the continental dark just go past on either side.

I stay that size in my mind for a while. I like the modesty of it.

But my feet keep getting in the way. Bare feet, broad, the callus so thick I could stand on fresh tailings while they are still steaming — and still feel, through all that horn, a degree's difference. A buried warmth turning left. A tremor coming through basalt that is not yet a herd. And underneath everything, so low it is less heard than *known* in the molars, the working rhythm of something enormous and patient, changing cycle far away.

So I am human, then. Or human the way the plateau is finishing the job of making humans: low, broad through the chest from breathing air that sits in the lungs like bathwater. Hair shaved close because loose hair holds damp and charge and lifts bright with heat above the scalp where a cat can read it. Skin rust-dark, not from sun — there has never been sun — but from iron dust and mat-fiber and grease worked in year over year, until it isn't dirt anymore, it's just the color I am. Eyes small, grey, slow. The least useful part of me. I learn not to be embarrassed by that. The old ones are blind by sixty and walk better than anyone.

I am carried at first, the way everyone is. Against a belly, against a back, in a sling where I share one temperature with another body and learn the first language before the spoken one: a tap at the ribs, *stop*. A hand flattened, *vent soon*. Fingers walked down a forearm — crawler, herd, hunter. By the time words come, low and close, mouth near ear so the sound is felt in the sternum, I already know how to have a whole conversation through the ground while appearing to be asleep.

I think I will be a herd-keeper. That is a good life and I can see it whole: following the grazers who follow the warm seams that thicken where a crawler has passed; timing everything by the bloom week after fresh tailings, when the mats open rich and sudden and everything with a mouth arrives in order of size; putting my palms flat on hot, hairless hides at midnight to read their calm; taking meat only from what the cats have already marked, leaving the moths and the scavengers entirely alone, because they are fellow passengers and you do not eat the warmth itself. Births planned for the bloom. Debts settled in meat. A name that is a place on a route — mine might be Lee-Rain, or Third Vent, or The Place Where The Old One Turned — and grandchildren told, quite cheerfully, *you will finish this hill*.

I could live that. For a while, walking, I do.

But I keep turning my head — not my eyes, my whole head, the way you do when your teeth feel something change — toward the crawlers.

Everyone up here lives by them and no one thinks they are ours. It runs the other way: we are theirs, the way the cleaner swarms in the intake baffles are theirs, the way the pool-fish spawning in their abandoned drill-holes are theirs, the way the whole traveling ecosystem arranges itself for decades around one old machine that runs idiosyncratically warm on its left flank. A crawler is a moving province. It makes its own rain along its lee side. It makes the only true pasture. It planes a hillside away an hour at a time and makes less noise than a campfire doing it, and a half-mile off the loose iron dust is already standing in lines on the ground and the hair is lifting on my arms.

The herd-keepers are politely uneasy about the inside-kin. I am not. Or I am, but in the wrong direction: not uneasy that they go in, uneasy that I haven't.

So at twelve — the age when children of the vent-side marriages choose which side of the hull to grow up on, and are mourned and celebrated at the same meal for choosing — I go in.

Inside is not dark the way the plateau is dark. That is what no one outside quite believes until they have felt it. It is orange-dark. Seam-lit. The belly glowing through service gaps and cooling grates, dull as magma through cracked earth, everything coming to you through the bone because the air itself is humming. My eyes, already poor, are relieved of the whole question. Inside-kin don't learn machines from manuals; the manuals rotted before our great-grandparents. We learn them by apprenticeship of the hand. I learn that this valve runs warm. That this centrifuge sings a half-beat slow before its filters want combing. That the old one — the one listing always slightly to her good flank, the one our whole band's route depends on — must never be fed from that side in cold weather, and I learn it the way herd-children learn which grazer will tolerate a hand and which will not: husbandry and maintenance and religion, until I can't sort them, and find the sorting rude.

I eat first pick of what comes out of the intakes. I grow very good at the baffles, because I started small enough for them and patient enough for the heat. I know the periodic steam the way walkers know vent cycles — flatten yourself, let it take the clog and the parasites off you and the walls alike, come out flushed and clean and laughing silently because noise in there carries strangely, through the hull, through other people's teeth.

And yet.

And yet, in the orange dark, laying my palm to a hull plate and feeling a hillside being planed away somewhere far ahead of us, I find I am doing the thing I did as a child on the outside: translating.

Because the inside-kin and the walkers speak, technically, the same small language — but they have stopped living in the same time. Outside, time is the pan dripping fast on the overhang, the mats opening, the bloom week coming. Inside, time is cycles within cycles: filters, heats, the slow filling of a hold with dense dark ingots that may never be emptied by anyone, a purpose that may have become only itself. When we trade down at a venting — fish and meat and route-news coming up, warning and parts-knowledge and warm little ingots going down, marriages arranged cheerily in the roar where no one can hear themselves — I notice that half of what I am doing isn't carrying objects at all. I am knocking on hull and feeling answers in my wrist. I am putting my bare feet to a deck plate and sending a sentence through iron that comes out, on the other side, as feet on stone. *The pan is dripping fast, don't bring the children that way. The old one will vent at her bad seam before midnight. A cat is reading the herd on the warm flank.* I am standing with one hand inside and one hand outside and feeling the same machine say two different true things.

So I don't stay all the way in. That is rarer, and it costs what they said it would cost: I come out at an age when most people are only just sure of their route, and I am footsore and half-blind for a year, relearning the country through my soles as if for the first time, weeping without ceremony the first time condensation rains gently off an overhang into a mineral pan and I can hear the pale stalks clicking as they cool. The walkers are tender with me in the way you are tender with someone who has been ill. The inside-kin leave a place for me at ventings without making me ask. Neither side quite claims me, which turns out to be the precise shape of the work.

Where would you find me, then, if you came up out of the cloud sea — and you shouldn't, your lungs aren't made for this anymore, but if you did?

Not by looking for where I live. I don't. Some seasons I walk the tailings with a band of sixty, in mat-fiber layers that are never dry and aren't meant to be, smelling faintly sweet and iron and rotten, which means the clothes are good. Some seasons I am in the baffles of the old listing one, eating what I clean, feeling the intakes breathe. Most often you would find me in the seam between those two lives: asleep, apparently, in a lee camp, in a shallow oval pressed into a heat-mat that will hold my warmth for hours after I've gone — while under the ground, through knuckles and feet and a hand laid flat on warm basalt, I am wide awake and in the middle of three conversations at once. Carrying a warning in. Carrying a birth announcement out. Telling the inside-kin, in hull-language, that the herd has shifted to the other flank because the mats bloomed early there; telling the herd-keepers, in ground-language, that the warm side they are sheltering on will be the vent side by morning.

At what passes for midnight, when the cloud thins and a few hard stars stand in it and the whole plateau wakes properly — herds moving, cats reading the warm earth, fish rising thick in a black pool to take the insects falling out of the steam, the great dark factories taking another slow mouthful out of the continent — I am awake too. Standing barefoot on ground that is reading itself to me as fast as I can attend to it, one palm sometimes resting against a scarred, mat-barnacled hull, feeling through iron and time and callus the infrasonic rhythm of work I will never see the purpose of and have stopped needing to.

I used to think I would have to choose: moth or grazer-keeper, walker or inside-kin, outside in the wet grey or inside in the orange dark. The plateau is patient with that kind of thinking. It rounds it off, the way it rounds every fresh break, within a season or a lifetime.

What I am is the place where the hull is thin enough to knock on.

That is a life here. That is enough of a shape to keep.
