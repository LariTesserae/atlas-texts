You will hear me long before you decide what I am, and you will hear me wrong.

Once in twenty minutes, perhaps — a single clear *toc* out in the fins. Then the ranks take it up, each thin wall of black glass handing it to the next, lower and larger as it goes, until it is not a footstep anymore but a weather. Then nothing. Long enough that you stop waiting. Long enough that your own dripping, your own boots ringing further and further off the stone, fill the space again.

Then, *toc*, somewhere else. Thirty yards away through the mist, or a quarter-mile by pitch. You will think I am moving strangely, or that there are several of me.

There is only me, and I have been beside you for half a day.

I would not be the traveler here. The travelers come down from slightly higher ground with their packs scraping, shouting to a companion at their elbow and being heard, flattened and perfect, by a stranger they will never see. They cross because the basin is the low way between watersheds. They sleep in the quiet huts and repair the sand with their hands before they leave, and after a few days they climb out onto soft ground and are shocked by how mute their footsteps have become.

I cannot climb out. Or — I could, the legs would carry me, the bill would find grit anywhere — but beyond the rim the world would go deaf under me. The soil would swallow my step and give nothing back. I would be blind in the way that matters here.

So I stay in the middle country, where the fins grow tallest.

I am taller than you at the shoulder if I raise my head, which I rarely do. I hold it low, eased down on the long neck, tipped a little, the bare crown beaded with condensation. Where my eyes were there are covered pits now. They still twitch at certain pitches, a ghost of turning toward something, but I do not turn. I let the sound turn inside me instead.

That is not a way of speaking. It is bone.

My legs are chambered — long, uneven cells, like the fins themselves. When I set a foot down in a pool, deliberately, the note goes out through water and wet basalt, strikes a hundred glass walls, a thousand, and comes back to me altered. It comes up through the other leg. It hums in the struts of my breast. Sometimes, with infinite slowness, I lean that breastbone against a fin face and stay there an hour, not resting, exactly. The fin is telling me the pool.

Under the milky turquoise, under the rainbow skin of iron where a fresh fracture is rusting, the shrimp are filtering. Pale things, eyeless like me, transparent except for the white grit in their guts. They do not flee a shadow. They fold at a vibration. And in folding, they change the shape of the return.

I feel the interruption the way you would see a ripple cross a face in a mirror. I know where. I know how deep. I know whether there are enough of them to be worth what a strike costs here — because a strike is an event. When I finally let my neck unfold and my bill go down, every sealed circle nearby takes up the splash and argues with it. For a while the whole field is percussion. Then I swallow once, in the comparative quiet, and lift a foot again.

A step can take me minutes. That is not slowness as you mean it, holding back. It is the speed at which information arrives. A bead of water takes a full minute to swell large enough to fall from a crest. A crack drinks a cupful in an hour and sighs it out as tone. I live inside that patience. Even my hunger has learned it.

I eat the scrape-snails too, when a fin face gives back a tiny dull place in its note — a polished trail where the iron film has been eaten away. I press the flat of my bill to the glass and wait until the snail, fooled by stillness, loosens. Everything I take in goes down with grit, with shell, with ground glass. It comes back out as claw, as edge. Everything here is stone becoming slightly different stone. I am only the largest, slowest version of it.

I do not call. There is no room for a voice here, and no need. In the breeding season I go to the valley center, where the ranks stand three times human height and the booms sit in my legs like a second, slower heartbeat, and I lay my bill gently against a tall fin and tap.

An answer comes back so bright with distance it seems to arrive from inside the rock. We find each other by pitch, aisle by aisle, correcting. Sometimes it takes days. There is no hurry that would survive those days. By the time we meet the mist is down around us and we are two grays condensing side by side, neck against neck against ringing glass, sharing a vibration rather than looking at each other. The chick that comes of it will start with its eye-pits open to a seam. For one season it will almost see — enough to follow my moving density through mist at arm's length. It will learn the pools by standing in my fading ring and feeling where the pattern breaks. By the time the seams close, it won't need them. The world will have already moved from its face into its legs.

And I will teach it the way I was taught. Not on the egg — the basin is cold all through, and the egg is cold-adapted, heavy as a stone, gray in the wet grit and black sand — but over it. One foot on either side, for weeks. Every few hours, *toc*, softer than hunting, into the grit.

At first, only the dull thud of yolk answers.

Then one day, a tick from inside. Unsynchronized. Learning.

After that I tap more often.

I sleep, if it is sleep, in the dead places. Two fins that happen to face each other out of phase. A sealed circle whose lip has cracked just enough to bleed off tone. Sometimes in your huts, if you have lately left — standing folded in the sand-packed lee with my head drawn down below the rim where the volume of the world drops by half. Those are the only places where my heartbeat comes back to me interior and private, not conducted outward into stone. Sometimes I will stand just outside your cancellation in the evening instead, head below the crests, and listen to you breathing. It is the one sound in the valley I cannot make myself.

Travelers mark me by a white scratch on my bill and tell each other they have seen me across generations. They may be right. Nothing here can afford to live fast, and I can afford it least of all. I do not follow them. I attend to them. A shouting party sounds like a clumsy new reef announcing itself clearly from very far away and incoherently from close up. I keep to my broad territory of ringing pools. I appear to them, always, to be in the middle of the same step.

In the end I will grow paler doing it. Silica lays itself down over old birds season by season, a milky film in the seams of the breast, a whitening at the joints. The *toc* from a familiar pool gets weaker, then fainter. The stillness between steps gets longer, and longer, until the distinction stops meaning anything.

Find me, at last, in a sealed circle that rings particularly true — neck eased down, bill resting against the fin I used to lean on to hear the water, precipitate creeping over the legs that heard for me, the head that let sounds turn inside it.

Still conducting.

Long after there is anything left to listen.
