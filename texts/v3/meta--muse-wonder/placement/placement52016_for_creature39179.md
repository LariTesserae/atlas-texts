I keep looking for a foothold in it at midday, because that is when it tells the truth.

Not on the silo ledges with the pigeons. They are too exposed there, packed shoulder to shoulder in the narrow strip of shade, beaks open, enduring the light rather than living in it. Not out with the fiddler crabs either, though I envy their certainty — drain, hot concrete, mud again, the one huge claw held up the whole way like they have somewhere important to be. Their lives are too brief and too tidal. The street empties sideways and they are gone.

Not in the roof void of the fertilizer shed, packed in with the bats in that warm ammoniac dark. Not in the canal with the monitor lizard, though I come closest to her for a while: half-submerged, back like drowned timber, tongue tasting the brown water, knowing exactly where the fish collect when the pumps run. She owns her place the way an old street is owned. She does not need anyone.

I think I would need to be fed, intermittently and without ceremony. I would need to belong without being kept.

So by afternoon I have settled, as I suspect I settled years ago, in the shade under a parked trailer near the weighbridge.

That is where you would find me.

I am one of the cats.

Not a pet. Not quite wild, either, though wild is closer. Long-bodied, narrow-headed, my coat thinned by heat and dust to almost nothing — a washed-out ginger, most years; in another life I could be tabby or black, it hardly matters. What matters is the geometry. I have learned exactly how the shade moves off a container stack between eleven and four, how it narrows to a blade and then widens again, and I move with it without fully waking. One paw over my eyes. Ribs rising slow. The concrete under my belly never really cools; it just becomes less punishing. Above me the trailer ticks as its metal expands. Somewhere beyond the yard a refrigeration compressor thrums through the ground and into my teeth, and I sleep inside that sound the way other animals sleep inside rain.

I was born behind compressors, in a warm humming space that smelled of grease and hot dust, so machinery has never frightened me. Forklifts, loading arms, the hydraulic sigh of a truck settling — they are weather. I can tell a pump cycle by pitch. I can tell, before the dormitory children can, whether the lowest street is going to flood tonight. I have watched enough tide tables read aloud, enough work shoes lifted onto racks, to know the water's intentions by the smell coming up through the drains.

I have three names. Perhaps four.

The night crew at the grain silos calls me one thing when they leave fish heads by the steps. The women from the market call me something softer, in the late afternoon, when they put down a saucer of water under the weighbridge and click their tongues. A child in the low quarter — the one who keeps crabs in a bucket for exactly one day and then, faithfully, lets them go — calls me by a name I answer to only for her. I am selective about this. Affection here is not owned; it is scheduled, like everything else. You take the shift you're given.

At midday I do nothing, and doing nothing is the whole skill. That is the lesson the town teaches every tenant in it: do not fight the heat, do not fight the tide, wait. The people are sealed in their curtain-dark rooms. The bats are breathing in the roof dark. The lizard is sunk in the canal. The geckos have not yet taken the walls. I wait under my trailer with the rest of the true population, fluent in stillness.

Then the light fails just enough.

It does not cool — don't imagine that. It only changes from annihilating to merely hot. The sodium lamps come on in the haze and make orange pools in the vapor. Shutters roll up in the dormitory blocks. Scooters are uncovered. The bats leave the fertilizer shed in that steady, quiet column that changes the air before you see it. And I unfold.

This is my hour, and it briefly belongs to everyone at once.

I cross the yards by the shade-side routes out of habit, though the sun is gone. I work the grain line along the rail spur where the sparrows fed all day, because after dark that line belongs to rats, and rats are careless in the first hour. I check the lit wall by the canteen where a gecko sometimes drops, stunned, into the moth-drift. I pass the market as it empties — meltwater in the drains, flies settling, crates being stacked, the smell of rice and tinned fish and cooking oil hanging low — and someone, without looking down properly, sets something aside for me. I eat without gratitude or apology. That is the arrangement. No one here feeds anything out of sentiment alone. We are all living off the same two things: the sea and the cargo.

Later I will patrol to the edge of the canal, but not into the lizard's water. We have an understanding made of years and distance. She takes the crabs that come up through the outfall. I take the ones stranded too far from a drain, still waving that absurd claw at the sodium sky. The tide will be coming under the streets by then. I can feel it through my feet before I hear the pumps answer.

Before dawn I will find the warm space behind the cold-store compressors again, or a culvert mouth that stays dry unless a gate is mistimed, and I will sleep through the flood of light that empties the town of people.

At noon a visitor would stand at the central junction and say I wasn't there at all.

They would be wrong. I was there the whole time — just reduced, like everything else that has learned to live here, to the smallest possible claim: a body flattened into shade, waiting for my shift in the relay, listening to the water move underneath a town built to pretend it is dry.
