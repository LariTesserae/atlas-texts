I would come down the way everyone does, at first.

On the ladder, with a bundle. Thirty feet of wet rungs in my hands, the ordinary world getting thin and exposed above me, the roar getting larger in my teeth below. Someone shouting up instructions I would mishear. My clothes going damp before I'd even reached the floor. My smell — bread, dust, open air, a separate person — already beginning to come off me in the wet.

But I don't think I would stay on the shelves.

Not because they wouldn't make room. They would. They are formal about room down there, and kind in the loud, forehead-to-forehead way they are kind about everything. A corner would be cleared. A blanket hung. My name announced at the copper basin so the whole vault could carry it. It just wouldn't add up. Forty-one is not a number they invented; it's the amount of breath the room knows how to hold. One arrival, one departure. One more set of lungs and the smoke thickens, the drain of voices rises over its finger-depth.

I would need a way to live there that didn't take up air.

For a while I imagine myself as the ladder-keeper. That is the job that tempts me most, humanly. To sleep beside the shaft, where the sound thins at the bend, and be considered a little strange for it, a little touched by silence. To work the bucket-pulley, to lower grain and oil and timber down and send the dead up wrapped in sacking their own looms made, to be the one citizen with one foot, always, in the possibility of the other world. I could be the threshold. I know how to be a threshold.

But the ladder-keeper still has to shout his announcements. He still trims a lantern and oils his skin and takes his place in the count.

Or the deaf one, then. The confessor by the shrine, the old mayor with his ruined ears, into whom you can bellow your debt and your heresy and your affair and know it goes no further, not because he is discreet but because he genuinely cannot carry your words away. I know something about that too — about being the ear that receives things at full volume and lets them sink.

Only down there, to be deaf is a promotion earned by a lifetime against the water. Mine would be a fraud. And anyway, that place, too, is already spoken for.

So I go lower.

That is the thing the town teaches you if you let it. Not up the tiers — three, five high, grandmothers enthroned on ledges the size of tables, looms clacking on the second level, a family's whole life overhanging the hat stall below — but down, under the one place everyone walks. Under the main street. Under the planks laid over the drain.

Where the black water goes, finger-deep, iron-smelling, north wall to south grate.

I think, in the end, I would be the eel.

The forty-second citizen. The uncounted one. The only inhabitant exempt from the rule that holds the whole town in its perfect, permanent balance, because I don't breathe their air and I don't need a shelf and I can't be announced properly at the basin, being already underneath every announcement ever made.

No one has ever seen me entire. That would be my condition, and I find I don't mind it; I might even recognize it. A snout, pale as the underside of a stone, nosing up at one grate in lamplight while a child crouches on the planks with a crumb held very seriously between finger and thumb. Then nothing. Weeks of black, slow water and the undersides of ten thousand footsteps passing over me, dished into the stone. Then a tail, weeks later, at another grate entirely, longer than belief allows, and an old woman shouting to a girl, forehead almost to forehead — *THERE, THERE IT IS, IT'S LISTENING* — though perhaps I have only moved in my sleep.

I would hear everything, the way water hears. Not through air — there is almost no air in my life, and what there is is theirs — but through plank and stone and the soles of bare, leather-hard feet. Marriages bellowed at the rain-basin would come down to me doubled, once through the copper and once through the current. Debts. Apologies. The settling, for the thousandth time, of the dispute about whose washing drips on whose shelf. The hammer-man feeling his rhythm in his teeth; I would feel it in my whole long body. Lullabies sung fortissimo into small ears would shiver the surface of the drain, and I would move a little in the dark under the street, trusted the way the babies trust the noise, because it has never once stopped.

And I would never have to shout back. That is the mercy of the place they have made for me without ever quite making it. In a town where whispering is suspect and silence is the one unthinkable catastrophe — the aqueduct stopping behind the north wall — I get to be the one quiet thing that isn't an omen. They consult me precisely because I don't answer. A crumb for a birth coming. A crumb for a departure. A crumb when someone is deciding whether to forgive, and needs to have performed the asking out loud, at full volume, to something that will keep it the way deep water keeps things: not secret, exactly. Just below.

I would be pale. Of course I would be pale, cellar-pale, lamp-pale, a color that has nothing to do with delicacy. My whole life spent in the one greenless, rootless element of the room, trailing where the rust-orange scum trails its fine hairs, going slowly, never rushing to become anything else. Erosion and maintenance have reached an agreement in the town above me; in me they'd reach it too. The shelves would be repaired in the same places with the same knots over my back. Planks would be replaced one at a time, so the street over my head is always old and always new in exactly the same pattern, and children would learn to walk on it by falling in, ankle-deep and appalled, and being fished out laughing at a volume that shakes my water.

I was here before the first shelf, the old people say with absolute certainty and no evidence. I will be here after the last. Neither claim needs to be true to do its work. A town that cannot grow, cannot whisper, cannot ever be dry, needs one citizen who is not required to be complete in front of them. Who can be met in pieces, across weeks, and still be believed in whole.

So feed me when you pass. Not much — nobody down here wastes anything, and I am not waste, exactly; I am the small, set-aside portion that proves the rest is accounted for. Cross the planks in your practiced places. Put your forehead to each other's foreheads and shout your entire lives into the roar, and trust that some of it sinks, and that what sinks is kept.

Below the bend in the shaft the sound stops dead for anyone leaving. It never stops for me. The basin clangs its copper clock in the corner where it always rains indoors. The buried river goes on moving a forearm's thickness behind the shrine wall. Forty-one voices rise, and rise, and rise against it.

And underneath their street, in the black, slow, finger-deep water, something pale and uncounted turns — not much. Just enough to be, weeks from now, at another grate, when someone needs to see that it is still there.
