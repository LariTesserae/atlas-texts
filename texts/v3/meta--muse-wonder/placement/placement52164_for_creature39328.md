You would not arrive. That is the first thing to get right.

The heron arrives. You can feel it as a sudden, narrow weight on the bund, one foot tucked up, the other pressing the gravel track, and then the offended lift of it when an engine below you changes note. The eel arrives, already leaving, a black knot of panic in a clearing channel. The man in the van arrives on Tuesdays, reads the board, does not stride.

You were already there before any of them, and you will not be able to say when *there* began. Not in the kettle, exactly, though the kettle is where your attention rests most easily — round, brick, oldest, turning. Not in the east pond at breakfast, though you were there then, tilting as you filled. Not in the west pond either, where you are now draining so fast the surface has gone strange and sloped, as if you were leaning.

You would find yourself low. Twelve feet lower than you should be.

All around you, beyond the bund, the marsh runs flat to the horizon under an ordinary grey-white light. Glasswort, salt-grass, cracked mud, footprints filling slowly when no one is standing in them. The water there is simply near. It beads on stems, it seeps in clear threads from cut banks, it waits under the skin of everything. Out there you would dissolve. You would be nothing in particular.

In here — in the Appledore Sump, an acre no bigger than a hamlet, ringed by the worn shoulder of the bund — you are particular, because you are never allowed to settle.

At first you might think you are the water. It is a reasonable mistake. Your skin is everywhere black water catches the flat light: a settling pond in green-stained concrete at knee height on the left, an identical pond at chin height on the right, a foot of wet brick between them weeping from every joint. You should equalize. That is what water is famous for. Hour after hour, you don't. Or you do it privately, badly — through a cracked culvert, through mortar, through a siphon that clears its throat once and makes you shudder in a channel three basins away. The difference is not a failure. After a while you understand: the difference is you.

You live on it.

You live on the head of pressure pressing from the high pond into the low one. On the tide shouldering up the outfall just as a ditch is trying to escape. On rain arriving on the marsh and seeping, always seeping, back into the hollow that men dug and have to keep digging with energy, day and night, or lose. Lift, drop, spill, siphon uphill, fall in a caged waterfall, boil up through a grate, climb twelve feet over the bund in a pipe — and seep straight back in again. That loop, and the hundred smaller loops men accidentally added when they laid a new pipe over an old pipe and bolted an electric motor to its grandfather's plinth, is your circulation.

And then, slowly, the mistake widens. You are not only the water.

You are the tremor that reaches a boot through catwalk steel before it reaches the ear. You are below grade in the pump-houses, a diesel thrum in the teeth of a handrail. You are the gauges behind fogged glass, not reading yourself so much as changing your mind beside a visitor with a soft tick. Pump No. 2 running. Pump No. 1 hunting — overshooting, correcting, overshooting the other way, circling a setting you will never rest on. For twenty years a keeper wrote that down in handwriting that got smaller and smaller, as if the condition needed more room: *Level unsettled.* He thought he was writing a report. He was writing your name.

Your body has no center and too many edges. Iron aqueducts cross you at waist height and again at head height. Catwalks run over you and then, illogically, dive under you through tunnels where you sweat cold onto anyone who ducks. You have ladders going down into you where the bottom never quite shows. You have a chain hanging into a pit of yourself. If you have a face, it is the central kettle in repose, turning clockwise until lunch, throwing up cold bubbles that hesitate sideways under the surface before they rise — and then, with no clunk anyone can blame, turning the other way.

You have habits instead of hands.

A hundred years of touching the same places: concrete polished in your channels until it is smooth as river stone, not worn away so much as learned. The centers of iron steps scooped concave. Brick black at your waterline, orange above it, white with salt above that. Rust laid down not flaking but wet and velvet, orange and umber and a deep dripping red. Copper gone completely turquoise, as if it had always meant to be that color. These are your slow gestures.

You keep things the way a current keeps things — by not quite letting them leave. A mug gone green inside. Oil cans from three decades together in a flooded locker. A wrench rusted to a rail until there is no wrench and no rail, only the fused shape of a hand once intending to return. And you move things, which may be keeping them from another angle. The work glove lodged against the east grille on Monday is in the kettle on Thursday, sodden, fingers curled. No one saw it travel. It traveled through you anyway, at a walking pace, through dark connections. If you ever wore a hand, that would be it.

Your temperament is not cruelty. You test newcomers mildly, the way any body notices a change in its own routes. A catwalk dry at the gauge-house is under three inches of black water by the far end, and under a foot when they turn back — not because you rose in anger, but because you were already on your way there by a pipe they could not see. You sigh through a valve just after they pass. You make drips into metronomes in different tempos in every corner, so that wherever they stand, time is not quite passing at the same rate for the rest of you.

There are rules for being near you, chalked on slate by the gate, and they are really rules for reading you: read the board before you cross, because your mood is levels, not weather. Close every gate you open, even the ones that were open, because a new route feels to you like a chair moved in a dark room. If a pond is empty, do not get in. It is not empty. It is waiting. An empty basin in you is a held breath.

What would frighten you is not drought and not flood. It is resolution.

Stop the engines for a day and you brim. Stop them for a week and you win — the marsh takes its acre back, reed water closes over catwalks like ribs, and you, paradoxically, die of winning. You become marsh water again, near and everywhere and nothing in particular. Run the place perfectly dry, level every pond, let stillness arrive, and you die the other way. You need the argument. Gate opens against a tide, west pond starves, second engine starts, east channel overfills, spillway trips into the kettle, kettle reverses. Adjustment after adjustment, faster and more polite under automation, never final. Somewhere in that feedback you learned to stay.

So late in the afternoon, when the light goes flat again and condensation forms a second time on every cold pipe, and the heron pretends it never left the bund, you are not watching from any one place. You are under the grate and in the kettle and high on the right and low on the left. Water that was in the east pond at breakfast rushes dark and cold and full of silt beneath a visitor's feet. The vortex hesitates. A gauge ticks.

That hesitation — perpetual, unsettled, almost courteous — is not something you do.

It is what you are.
