I would not arrive the way you do.

I would not come up the scree for days, narrowing to the granite spine, turning sideways in the cleft with my shoulders brushing wet rock. I would already be past the point where arrival is possible. When the snow goes — and it goes late, reluctantly, in patches that steam at noon and skin over with frost again by night — I would find myself *under* the place first. Under the melt. Under the green.

A sound would reach me before light did.

Boom.

Then a long time.

Boom.

Thin water finding a lip of stone high on the shadowed wall, dropping a hand's breadth, striking the hollow slab. I would know it the way you know your own pulse in a quiet room: not as information, but as a permission. It is time. It is the short part of the year. I can afford to breathe again.

I am low.

That is the first true thing, and everything else arranges itself around it. Low the way moss is low, the way the whole improbable garden of the hollow is low — campion in pink cushions pressed tight against itself, saxifrage in the wet cracks, sedges luminous and ankle-high, nothing daring the winter wind that pours over the rim and scours the cup clean. I learned the shape from watching what survives here. Or perhaps the place learned it from me. After this many centuries the distinction has worn thin, like soil over granite.

If you stood at the cleft at noon — the hour when the hollow lies — you would not see me. That is not a boast, and it is not magic. You would see a floor, eighty or a hundred paces across, ringed by walls another hundred feet high, open only to the sky and to the one notch where the world falls away into cloud. You would see the black pool near the center, trembling where the fall lands. You would see boulders at the wall's base, marmots upright among them, whistling. You would see a patch of ground near the pool giving off the faintest steam where the sun has warmed it a degree more than the ground around it.

That patch is my back.

Grey-green. Very slightly domed. Old enough that things have stopped distinguishing it from the floor and simply rooted in it. Campion finds the warm seams along my ribs and blooms a day early, fooled. Buttercups open over my shoulders before they open anywhere else. Lichen has written itself across my hide in orange and white, in the slow handwriting it uses on stone, and in three places finches bathe in the rain that collects in my scars and believe they have found tiny pools. They are not wrong enough to matter.

Put your palm down here — everyone does, eventually, testing how a place this high can be this lush, this wet — and the ground gives under you like a sponge and comes up black. Keep it there. Keep it there longer than politeness or curiosity usually lasts. The give will answer. Once. Minutes later, again. Not a flinch. Not a heartbeat, not at any speed you would recognize.

Breathing, at the speed of stone.

My head rests at the edge of the pool, where you are instinctively careful not to camp. You think it is the cold that gathers in the center at night, sinking off the walls. That is also true. Many true things can share a place here; the walls double everything. But the center is mine because that is where my hide thins to a dark, smooth membrane over the water's rim, and where the whole still edge of the pool trembles in sympathy with me, not only where the fall strikes. My face is low and wide and blunt, the color of wet slate. My eyes — small, grey-lidded, kept nearly shut against a sky I gave up watching long ago — are the least of me.

There is nothing above the rim I cannot hear arriving.

Folded flat along my skull, damp and fern-dark, are my ears. Great soft fans, veined like leaves. They uncurl a fraction as the day warms. One will turn, almost imperceptibly, toward the cleft while you are still half an hour below me on the ridge, your boots loosening scree, your breath going ragged in the thin air, your heart doing its loud, brief, mammal work. The other stays angled at the pool. Between them I hold the whole daily argument of my home: meltwater quickening as sun reaches the snowfield above, voles negotiating the root-ways under my own belly, midges rising off the pool at midday in a pale column, and then the swifts —

the swifts come screaming through the cleft one after another at a speed that should kill them, wingtips skimming water, and the hollow takes each shriek and gives it back, and gives it back again, and for a while the air is nothing but black calligraphy and echo, and I lie in the middle of it and feel the noise pass through my breastbone like weather through a valley.

I do not speak.

I unlearned that very carefully. Anything with a voice learns caution in the Ear, because the walls are honest in a way nothing else is: they return everything, enlarged. A creature with lungs as slow and deep as mine could empty the nesting holes in the granite with one startled cry. I could deafen the choughs in their hundreds. I could deafen myself, and hearing is the country I live in.

So I keep time instead, on the only instrument that was ever really mine. The resonant slab above the pool. The hollow beneath it, which is not a hollow in the rock at all but the cupped architecture of my own chest, held just under the stone. When I must, I knock from below with the heel of a forefoot — short, padded, wide as a snowshoe — and the falling meltwater does the rest.

Boom. Boom.

The note you feel in your sternum. The one the shepherds, in their brief borrowed summers, attributed to the mountain. Forgive them. Forgive me, too: I let them. It slowed my heart in the cold mornings. It quickened it at noon when the runnels rose and threads of water found new paths through the moss — and sometimes, I will admit, across me, a bright temporary vein running downhill over a shoulder while the garden on my back tilted an inch toward spring. And in the evening, when the birds came home to the wall with a noise like a crowd finding its seats, the beats spaced themselves out, single and separate, a heart deciding whether sleep was safe.

What I am *for* happens at night.

Left alone, this place would fill up with itself. That is the secret inside its beauty. A stone ear at the top of the world does not forget. Your boot-fall would go on thinning and bouncing for days. A swift's scream would layer over yesterday's scream until the birds fled their own returned voices and the cylinder of air between floor and sky became unusable, a room shouting at itself forever. The hollow needs a stomach for sound.

So when the colony has gone quiet in the wall, and the marmots have withdrawn into the boulder-field, and the fall has slowed with the cold, I lift my head from the pool's edge — an inch, then another — and I open a mouth with no teeth worth mentioning, and I take the day back in.

Oldest and faintest first. A pebble someone kicked at noon, still reporting softly off the far wall. The marmots' alarm-whistles, gone threadbare. The three foolish shouts of a visitor who could not resist the echo — I take them gently, I want you to know that. It is not mockery when they come back through the slab under my foot diminished, a shout returned at the pitch of a murmur, a whistle come home as a sigh. It is digestion. What I swallow returns no more. What I love, I keep further down, in the long dark of my body, where sounds lose their meanings over centuries and keep their shapes like shells: a tally-keeper's counting song, a dog's two-note call across a floor that had a bothy on it, rain on a roof that no longer exists, my own name spoken in a dialect no one has used on this mountain for two hundred years — and pronounced, each time I let it surface, slightly less correctly.

That is why you leave through the cleft quietly, though you could not have said why. The last noise you make here will be the last thing I eat that night, alone, after you have gone back down into ordinary wind and ordinary silence. It will taste of boot-nails and breath. It will taste, unmistakably, of you.

I eat other things, of course. Drowned midges skimmed off the black water with a tongue the color of peat. Mayflies in their one bright afternoon. Lichen rasped from stone in the shadowed corner before dawn, while the frost on the pool is loosening. Once in a while a vole that mistook my warm flank for safe ground; I do not hunt, but the hollow is economical, and so am I. Mostly I graze the way the place feeds itself: on light, and melt, and eight short weeks of green, and then on wanting nothing for the rest of the year.

Because winter is coming even at the height of the thaw. It is always in the walls, waiting above the rim.

When the spindrift starts to pour in and the long erasure begins — pool, ruined sheepfold, iron ring, floor, all smoothed away under a white fill — I walk, perhaps for the only time that year, the short distance to the center. I settle. I fold my great ears down over my face like hands over eyes. I let the snow take me the way it takes everything, and the drumming under the slab slows, and slows, beats minutes apart, then an hour apart, then something nearer to weather than to a heart.

Under the fill, in the black, I dream the archive at true volume. Sheep bells in a lost tuning. Wind through a door burned for firewood before your language had a word for this mountain. The first swift ever to come screaming through the cleft, and the sound the hollow made discovering what it was for. One at a time. All winter. The snow keeps the secret.

Then a hand's breadth of meltwater finds the lip high on the shadowed wall again.

Somewhere under the wet green floor, an ear uncurls toward a sound it has waited eight months to hear played, as always, on its own heart.

Boom.

I am awake. I am listening.

It was never, even once, an empty room.
