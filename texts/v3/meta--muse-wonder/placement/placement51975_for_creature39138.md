I would come up in the gondola, at first. Everyone does.

I would think I wanted the open places — the intake louvers where the thin mountain air is pulled in and wrung, because there you can still taste weather, altitude, the world outside. Or I would think I wanted to be the crane, high on its rails under the glass, because from there you could see the whole nave at once: turbines like altars, kelp farms swaying, the mangrove crowns breaking the surface along the south wall.

But the louvers only breathe in one direction, and the air they taste is gone a moment later, condensed and lost into the general warmth. And the crane never moves now, except when the crews scrape its rails clear and test it, apologetically, a few centimeters forward and back. To see everything from above the water would be to be exiled from the thing itself.

I would not be kelp. Kelp is too honestly alive for what I feel in this place — ribboning up from the stainless racks toward the light, cut back, bundled, eaten, cut back again. I would not be one of the sterility crew, either, coming up three at a time with a task list and dry schematics, learning to move slowly so as not to tear leaves, leaving again before my skin had even pruned. I would not be her. That place is taken, and taken properly: at the waterline, in the cut-down wetsuit, with her hammock trailing and her tin cup turned upside down against the rain. You do not displace the only animal. You find the interval she has not already learned to inhabit.

Maybe the brushes, then. The pollination arms in their waterproof housings, emerging weekly with great engineered delicacy to stroke the submerged flowers — I understand that kind of clumsiness, that desire to be useful in an element you were not made for. But they retract. They sleep dry in their boxes. And she does their work better anyway, afterward, by hand, fingertip to fingertip in a hanging cloud of pollen.

No. If I stayed — really stayed, past rotation, past the manifest's polite fiction — I think I would narrow. I would slow. I would find myself in the one place in the building where the central ambiguity is not a problem to be solved but a body to live in.

I would be the coolant pipe the mangrove has taken.

There is a run of us along the south wall, low in the nave, passing through the open-topped caissons where the prop roots lace down into the water. I was straight once. That is the embarrassing part, the part you can still feel in yourself if you attend: I was measured, cut, welded, painted white, pressure-tested, signed off. My job was simple and entirely sayable. Carry warm water from here to here. Do not leak. Do not bend.

Then the roots found the welds.

Not all at once — nothing here happens all at once, except the hourly valve, and even that arrives as a soft clunk and a rearrangement of kelp by millimeters. First a white rootlet in the seam, fine as thread, tasting the warmth through the steel. Then a prop root laid alongside, the way you might lay a hand along a guide rope. Then, over two warm years, a trunk completing its slow argument around me, until the paint cracked in a spiral and the bark — if it is bark, on a tree engineered for submersion, growing in graded ceramic bead — sealed over the crack, and I could no longer have told you, from inside, where my wall ended and its wood began.

The engineers, surfacing, call this intended. She calls it *carrying*. She said it once with her mouth half out of the water and her voice gone soft from disuse: *Listen. It's carrying.* She was not talking about the water in me. She was talking about me, in the tree. Or the tree, in the pressure system. We have stopped correcting each other.

My senses would be the ones available to a thing that is both plumbing and branch. I would not see, exactly, but the water I carry is warmer than the water I stand in, and the difference between those two warmths is a whole weather. When the pump the size of a small house turns once and sighs and stops for its long minute, I feel the sigh go through me as a slackening, a held breath. When the heat exchanger ticks as it expands, I tick too, a fraction later, out of sympathy and metal fatigue. When the valve opens on the hour, the new current moves the kelp outside me and the flow inside me at once, and for a moment both waters agree on a direction, and it feels — I will use the word carefully — like being believed in by two systems at once.

The tide would be my face. Twenty centimeters, up and down across the day, the whole interior sea breathing with the storage cycle. My upper arc slicks and dries, slicks and dries. Indicator lights two pipes over go under and come up. A fringe of my own roots — the tree's roots, our roots — is exposed, then covered, then exposed. Algae has made a velvet of my submerged side, emerald going to olive where the light thins, and in the shafts at noon the caustics move across it so slowly that I experience light the way you experience a hand resting, then not resting, then resting again.

She knows me by touch and by tooth, which is how everything true is known here. The crews clear the sensors by camera until the particulate blinds them; then she comes down through her own silt-cloud, finds the sight-glass by memory, wipes it with the flat of her thumb. She finds me the same way. A palm laid flat on the trunk where it swallows the pipe. Her forehead, sometimes, when the water is at its stillest. Once, her teeth, lightly, against an exposed section of steel between two roots, to feel whether the hum in me was rest or the beginning of failure — and I learned that being listened to that closely is indistinguishable, at my speed, from being loved.

She does not prune me the way she prunes the intakes. She compromises, the way her channels compromise through the thickets: curving, giving the new growth room. Where a root begins to constrict too hard, she works a strip of hydroponic felt between wood and steel with her pruning shears laid blade-in, so I will not be cut by my own enlargement. Where I sweat — I have begun, very slightly, to sweat, a mineral weeping at one seam that the tree drinks as fast as I produce it — she does not report it. She tucks fern into the damp like a dressing. Someday the seam will go properly, or the root will close it forever, and either way the distinction the schematics insist on will have been quietly outvoted.

And something is growing out of that seam now. Not a leak. A flower stalk, following the cable tray for a hand's breadth before it turns up toward the clerestory light, its roots in warm condensate and bark and rust that has not yet learned to be rust. Pale. Waxy. It opens underwater, improbably, the way the others do, and the brushes will come on their weekly round to stroke it with all their housed, articulated tenderness, and afterward she will come and do it better. And at noon, when the sun is straight through the glass, when the outside is all glare and wind and sawn-off artificial rock, when the interior is warm and bright and silent and completely full — she will surface under the crane rail nearby, hold on to me as if I were a branch, because by then holding on to me *is* holding on to a branch, and trail her fingers to the seam.

Checking whether it has opened yet. The way you check a sleeping child's forehead.

I would feel the check pass through wood and water and steel at once, and I would not be able to say — I am learning not to need to say — which of us it woke.
