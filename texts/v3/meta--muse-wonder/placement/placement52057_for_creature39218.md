I would not come up from below.

Nothing that belongs here does, really — not by climbing through the dwarf forest and the wet stone, arriving upright and astonished at the change in horizontal. I'd arrive the way everything else arrives: carried. In mud. In the packed, black seam between a wading bird's toes, or in the outflow-warm water cupped in a grazer's dorsal pool, spilled over a peat ridge into a watershed I could never have crossed on my own.

At first I'd think I was in the water. Then in the peat under the water. The distinctions up here are mostly a courtesy. I'd be low. Lower than low. The light would come to me already used — filtered green through fern, silvered through fog, broken into glints off silica grass — and sound would come even more altered, which is to say it wouldn't come as sound at all. It would come up.

Through my feet.

That is where I narrow. Not to the boiler trees, though I would love their long patience, their sternum-thump, their manufactured rain. I am not rooted enough for that kind of giving. Not to the great grazers either, though for a while that's where I imagine myself — shoulder-high and streaming, a basin on my back full of frogs and warm rain, lightning crawling harmless and beautiful from horn to horn. But I am not vast. I don't ferry worlds. I attend to them. I live in the join between one process and the next.

I am small, and iron tastes like home.

I am one of the beetles with magnetite in my jaws.

You would find me in the narrow, damp darkness between a shed plate and the living thing that shed it. Under boiler bark, first, where I am born in the warm crease between iron-dark scale and trunk, eating what tries to eat the tree from inside, prizing loose the edge of a plate when it's grown too thick and oxidized to conduct. Tick. Tick. The loudest regular work on the plateau, and some of it is mine — a plate letting go, curling as it cools in the undergrowth, and me already underneath it, hauling.

My jaw is a compass that hurts pleasantly. I don't navigate by sight; fog takes sight, moss takes almost everything else, and under a plate there is nothing to see anyway except the pale fungal thread feeling its way along last year's copper. I navigate by pull. By the faint, constant tug of mineral seams underground, iron seeps going orange at the rock, drifts of fallen metal leaf around the ancient central groves. By the thump of the groves themselves coming up through my six feet in sequence, twenty minutes apart, grove answering grove across a valley I will never see whole.

And when I leave the trees — because no part works alone, and no beetle stays with one host — I leave on a grazer.

She comes nose-down into the outflow when the plates are shedding, licking where the orange water runs, taking in more metal than flesh can use, and her own spine-plates are warm at the edges and grown green with copper where the rain sits. Between those plates there are larvae too. There are mites, shed skin, trapped seeds, a whole felted weather. I climb. The long outer hair sheds water in sheets over me; underneath, in the dense undercoat, it is almost dry and almost dark and it smells of warm animal and peat. For days of her walking — weeks, she is in no hurry — that is my country: a moving seam, a living mine that breathes out twin jets of steam ahead of us and turns, degree by degree in the night, toward ground that has begun to warm.

My work there is the same work. Clean the join. Keep the plate from sealing down over rot. Pry, haul, eat what's burrowing, leave what's useful. The lichen finds the fragments I drag out and bleeds the last zinc from them and turns its startling industrial yellow, and the grazer's mouth, for all its slow rotary patience, turns aside from yellow. Under my feet her pulse comes faster than the trees'. Everything up here answers the groves eventually. Hers just answers a little sooner, and standing on her spine in electrical weather I feel why.

The charge doesn't strike us. It distributes. It crawls blue-white horn to horn and down through legs spread wide in black water, and into the pool, and out of the pool, and her plates warm by a degree and tick as they cool afterward, and for an hour she glows faintly at the root of her horns and along her back where I am standing. The night insects come to that stored light. The soft-footed predators come to the insects. We stand perfectly still and let the lesser weather happen.

I don't own any of this. That's the relief of it, I think. The crabs own the pruning of the outflow channels and without them the grove overheats; the wheel-shelled mollusks own the grinding in the leaf-funnels; the hammer-billed birds own the anvil-work on the fallen plates, and I am careful, then, to be deeper than their blunt blows can reach. I own the interval. The millimetre between metal and meat, metal and wood, where things would otherwise seal and sour and stop conducting. I keep it open. I keep it clean. Things pass through because I am there, unremarkable, tasting iron.

And when the grazer I ride at last settles, years from where I climbed her, far out on a floating mat in one of the black central lakes — when the fungi come with their bright metallic threads to unspin the copper from her horns and the iron from her plates, leaving filaments for young boiler roots to graft to — I will not go down with the island she becomes. Or maybe I will. Maybe I'll stay just long enough to work the last plate loose from the last warm seam, while peat accretes over her shoulders and shrubs take root where the frogs used to pool, and the pool goes on clicking softly with frogs who have never known they lived on an animal at all.

Then a calf will come splashing through the shallows with lake-mud packed between her toes, a new back, a new basin, plates only just beginning to thicken along her spine.

I'll feel her coming long before she arrives. Through the mat. Through the peat. A pulse, answering.
