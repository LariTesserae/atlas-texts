It would begin, for me, before sight. Not with the plateau — I think I would know the sound in my sternum first, a hum I felt through something else, and only later understand that the something else was wood.

At first I keep slipping out of human shape trying to find where I'd fit in it.

A cup-frog life pulls at me. Born in a bromeliad pond halfway up a Pillar, never descending, never needing to. My whole geography a leaf-cup of caught dew, my territory defended with one struck-bell note, repeated until it is not a sound I make but the shape of my address in the world. And the bell-mouthed flowers listening for my exact pitch — I would like being *expected* that precisely. A pollinator with a regular hour. A frequency somebody else has grown around.

Or lower, stranger: one of the beetles inside a dead Pillar, tending the green fungal gardens where the roar finally comes filtered down to distant surf. A hundred feet of mineral heartwood between me and the deafening world. Cathedral-quiet. Green light. That appeals to the part of me that wants the whole system at one remove — audible, but not overwhelming.

Or not animal at all for a while. Floor, maybe. The temptation of being the ground everyone stands on, drinking last month's rain into my sponge, sighing cool air out of a stomatal pit at night and closing without hurry, listening through roots to chemical conversations moving Pillar to Pillar at the pace of a walking herd. Being listened *through*. Being the medium instead of the message.

But it is the feet that settle it, in the end. Human feet, broad and splayed, soles calloused grey-green at the heel, testing the warm mat with the ball of the foot at every step — not for pain. For information. Full place, hollow place. Water dreaming below, or water really there. I know that kind of knowing. Not looking. Reading a surface by what comes back through you.

So I am one of the plateau peoples. Not an elder — not yet — and no longer quite what I was.

I was a climber when I was younger. That part stays in the body even now: the route-memory in my hands, the suspended savannas up in the branching Pillars where thickets and orchid mats and whole moss-forests have never known the ground, and you move limb to limb the way the monkey troops do, unthinking, because thinking about the drop is the only dangerous thing up there. I harvested ahead of the flowering wave. I knew which cup-ponds belonged to which frogs by pitch before I saw them. I knew the bell-flowers that opened only when shaken at the right frequency, and which frog, cup by cup, was their regular.

Then, in middle age, as it does, the frog register began to thin.

That is how I'd find myself — mid-transition, in the patterned deafness that is really a calendar. The struck-bell notes going first. Then, slowly, the middle birds. Not silence arriving, but filtration. The world being sorted into layers and the top layers lifting off, one by one, like mist burning off the Floor by ten. People are matter-of-fact about it, and so am I: I am judged ready for different work now. Work in the open, where that distraction matters less, and work — though nobody says this too grandly — that belongs to people beginning to hear the tree instead of its tenants.

So I walk the crossings.

Between Pillars is where I'd truly be myself. Forty of us, perhaps, coming out of the mist river at dawn at the foot of one green tower, skins and bundles and infants tied flat and silent, setting off in single file across miles of open Floor toward a tower that will not visibly grow for a day and a half. No shouting. The predators out there hunt by ear, and for the length of the crossing a whole people goes into its hands. I walk toward the rear — rear lookout, half-upright at the turns, scanning — and the small signs ripple up and down the line through me. *Slow here, the mat is thin. Grazer sign, old. Child tired, pass her forward. Stop.* Things near my sternum for what I feel, arm's length for what I've seen, behind the shoulder for what's only reported. I am a relay in a sentence crossing a blank page.

I love the shouting too, back at camp — that's the odd doubleness I'd keep. Bellowing endearments across a platform with no embarrassment because the roar gives everyone privacy. Children sounding like small furious adults. But my real language, the one still growing in me, is in my hands. Especially now. As the high registers go, the signing gets richer to compensate, and I have signs I didn't need as a climber: a call heard, a call felt through the Floor, a call expected from a regular at its regular hour — and the one I am best at, the one hunters prize and I maybe was always going to end up specializing in: a call that has failed to arrive. I am good at subtracting the whole district from itself and noticing the absence shaped like a cat. Or shaped like a Pillar finishing. I sit some afternoons with my back against a buttress fin, eyes open, apparently doing nothing, and feel through my spine what is left to me: the deep hum in the resonant chambers, herd-tread coming up through the mat, thunder deciding to exist somewhere. I am not an elder yet. But I am learning to be filtered.

At night I'd be in the hollow of a fallen Pillar when I could — mineral heartwood, beetle gardens burning green, the handprints layered wall over wall, a child's hand set carefully inside an ancestor's so the fingers touch. Red resin over pollen-paste. There, where a hundred feet of wood turns the roar into weather on a far coast, I'd tell the stories in a lowered register. The foreign axemen. The black spiral near the southern rim where nothing has rooted since. The season a district went quiet. I'd put my own hand in the prints too, I think. I have always wanted continuity you can touch.

And if you want to know exactly where I'd be, finally — not camp, not canopy, not crossing — it is in the cessation. Midday or midnight, everything within ten miles holding its breath at once, the Pillar-hum cut off like a string, nobody panicking, nobody filling it. A climber frozen on bark. A child signed gently quiet against a breast. And me, stopped mid-gesture, hand half-raised in a sign I don't finish, hearing for once the things that are normally impossible: sap ticking in the ground under my bare, barked feet. My own pulse. A dead leaf worrying somewhere hundreds of feet up.

On their faces, the telling says, is the expression of tenants in a house where the owner has just audibly shifted in his sleep. I recognize that expression. It is the one I'd have. Not worship. Not fear. *Attentiveness.* The understanding that the world is not just alive, which is the least of it, but listening back through root and resonant chamber and the soles of your feet — and that my whole life, from canopy climber losing the frogs to rear-lookout learning the deep wood, has been training in how to listen in return.

Then one frog tests the air. Another answers. The birds resume mid-phrase. I finish my sign, the line moves on, the volume climbs back to its structural, deafening norm — and I resume too, shouting, signing, wholly myself, as if nothing had happened.

As if nothing ever happens any other way.
