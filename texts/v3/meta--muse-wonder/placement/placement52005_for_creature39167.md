I think I would arrive the way everyone arrives — too fast, even when I was trying to be careful.

I would come down the wall on my belly. I would mean to be respectful of the moss, and I would still tear it. My hands would keep searching for holds that were firmer, drier, more decisive than anything the basin offers, and my boots would keep wanting a step, a real step, where there is only the spring of root over root over older root. Halfway down I would already be making plans. I would map it with my eyes: the glare-meadow to the north, the dark center, the broad-leaf tunnels to the south. I would think, *I'll cross it. I'll see the houses. I'll learn who lives here.* 

That is how I know I wouldn't stay a visitor for long. Not because I would succeed, but because the basin would start quietly editing the plans out of me.

First it would edit my pace. Eight kilometers is nothing up on the plain. Down there, where there is no trail and no reason to make one, where every footfall has to be negotiated between stems that don't want to be broken, a morning stops being a unit of travel and becomes a texture. I would get tired in a way that has nothing to do with distance. The heat would get into it — not beating down, but all around, reflected up under my chin and off the pale walls and in through my clothes until there was no cool side of anything to turn toward. My sweat would stop being something my body produced and then lost. It would just stay. A film. Then a climate.

I would keep thinking I was alone, for longer than was reasonable. I would sleep in the clearings — no, I would *camp* in them, which is a different and much louder verb — and I would wake convinced the domes were abandoned, the platforms deserted, the eight stones some patient mystery no one was left to explain. All the while I would be being watched with exactly the attention I gave the emerald lizard on the trunk. Complete. Unhurried. Without any impulse to make it into a meeting.

If I am honest about who I would be in this world, I don't think I would be the one who finally leaves and climbs back to the plain to tell it properly. I tell stories too eagerly. I move toward conclusions. The pit doesn't. And I think, given enough of that green pressure on all sides, the part of me that needs a conclusion would be the first part to get tired and lie down.

Someone would offer me a place, in the only way places are offered there. Not with a gesture I would recognize at first. I would simply notice, over what up above would be days, that a particular fork in a particular parasol tree — broad, humus-lined, smelling of orchid root and warm bark, a bromeliad cupping dark water just within reach — was never occupied when I passed under it, though I had the feeling it had been, once, the way a stone keeps the shape of heat after the sun moves. I would climb up, clumsily the first time, corded arms or no. My climbing would announce itself for a hundred meters through the mat. Leaves would still be settling long after I had stopped.

And then I would sit.

That is the image that keeps outlasting the others when I let them compete: not me crossing the glare-meadow, though I would love its fierce light; not me in the southern tunnels stooping under leaves a meter across; not me as the white, long-necked bird beating crown to crown — I am not brief or rare enough for that. I would be in the fork. Knees drawn up, because after a while that is simply the shape the tree suggests. Back learning the curve of the limb. Hair uncut because cutting it would be an event, and events take more justification down there than I would eventually be able to assemble.

I would learn the countries of one tree and find they were sufficient in the way the basin means sufficiency. Down on the mat, where my feet would thicken and splay and learn to test before trusting. Up the trunk where the lizards go flat and green in the wall-light. In the crown where one side of a leaf can be beaded and cool and the other faintly steaming. The cup of the bromeliad, where a frog lives invisibly and produces, on some days, one glass note. I would know the exact bead that condensation becomes on the leaf a meter from my face — how it gathers, how it holds, how it evaporates at precisely the rate it gathers, so that the leaf is always almost changed and never changed. I would have words for that eventually. More words than I would have left for *before* or *after*.

My skin would take the glaze. I like imagining I'd resist that, but I don't think I would; resistance is also an event. It would start along the forearms, where the light strikes strongest when I part leaves, and across the shoulders, and the backs of my hands — a fine green living film, algae rooting lightly in the outer layer, so that in the north light I'd be almost emerald on one side and brown on the other, and in the center groves I'd go dark and dappled and hard to distinguish from the other vertical things. Vines would test the warmth at my ankle the way they test a young trunk. I hope I would have learned enough by then not to tear them off. To let skin and bark negotiate their own border.

I would eat slowly enough that eating would stop being an interruption. A fruit that has hung ripe until its own seeds have sprouted through its skin, taken in small bites over hours, seeds and all. The peppery water squeezed from a fleshy stem between my teeth. It would be enough — not in the sense of a discipline, but in the sense that hunger down there isn't fired by cold, or by clocks, or by the need to be ready for the next effort. My heart would learn the basin's long, embarrassed interval between beats. My breath would come perhaps four times a minute, shallow, through the nose, tasting the green. I would not experience the air as hot any more than I experience the inside of my own mouth as hot. That is the adaptation I think I would be most grateful for, and most changed by: the loss of the body's clock. No cool evening to organize a day around. No morning chill to wake me into purpose. Just the thickening and thinning of light, barely, and stillness, in several distinct qualities that I would learn to name.

Some days — or one continuous day with weather in it — I would sit in the circle of eight stones in my clearing. Not *my* clearing, exactly, but the one whose dome has accepted my occasional occupation, its thatch beaded, its edges rooting, its stone circle kept clear not by anything I do but by the mild chemistry of a body returning to the same place forever. My presence refusing seedlings. I would sit there the way the mat is sat on: by consent, renewed each time. And from another crown, very far away, a voice would lift — a clear note resolving, if I have stayed long enough for my ears to be rinsed of hurry, into a word or two. A name. A placement.

And much later, later in the way only the basin can be later, I would answer. Not because I had anything to report. Because reporting is what stillness does there, at the rate of frogs in bromeliad cups: one note, an answer, then not again.

*Here still.*

I think that is who I would be. Not a hermit — there would be no elsewhere I was withdrawn from, once the plain had become the remote place, the cool, dim, dry place where time unfortunately moves. Not an explorer, not a witness, not even, after a while, a guest. Just one of the people the basin has arrested halfway through a change that never finishes: human, tall in the frame, long-handed, green along the lit side, sitting in a tree that is also an address, watching a leaf be sufficient, while somewhere a white bird beats up from a neighboring crown and is gone, and the houses wait, and the fruit ripens all the way into its own children, and nothing — leaf for identical leaf, breath for slow breath — is waiting to happen, because it already, perfectly, is.
