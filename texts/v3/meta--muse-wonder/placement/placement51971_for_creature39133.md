I think, at first, that I am in a joint.

Not *on* anything. *In* it. In the dark hinge where a boiler-grazer's second leg meets its body, where two plates overlap and grind and never quite close. It is warm there in pulses. Every step sends a shudder through the metal and a rain of red dust down over me, and the dust tastes — I have a mouth, apparently, and it is busy — of iron and salt and old blood that was never blood. I eat without deciding to. My jaws work the bright edge where rust blooms, licking it clean, and below me the great leg swings forward again, and does not fuse, because I am there.

For a while that is the whole world: the ticking heat, the frost cracking off in sheets somewhere far above, the grazer's ribs sounding through its hide like a furnace thinking.

Then I am too large for the joint.

I don't know when it happens. Time pools here. The herd shakes itself — one vast animal shiver passing shoulder to shoulder across a country of backs — and I am rained down with dozens of my own kind into the black shallows. The cold takes me instantly and sweetly by every seam. The water is hand-deep and should be ice and is instead brine and oil, mirror-bright, full of mouths. Pump-mollusks open around me in iron flowers. My shell, which was a washer, which was a sprocket, clicks against theirs.

I have claws now. One is larger than the other, squared off, grown to a gauge I recognize in my body before I recognize it in my mind. I know the exact bolts this claw was made for. My grandparents turned them. I scavenge the black pellets the mollusks spit out, neat as droppings, rich as ore, and I drag a still-twitching pump sideways through the mud because it whimpered when the crane-hunter dropped it, and I want to see if the rest of it will follow.

Sometimes it does. Parts are patient here. They crawl toward each other through the silt.

Sometimes *I* am what follows.

That is how I learn I might not stay a crab.

There is a morning — one of the dim hours that substitutes for it, the pale sun showing as a rivet-head under my feet more clearly than in the sky — when a tender lifts me out of the shallows between two fingers and sets me, dripping, on a grazer's back. And my weight *lessens*. The step up is like stepping toward the top of deep water. Loose screws drift slowly up off the hide around me, orbit once around the chimney-neck in a thoughtful way, and settle into the frost. I stay. My feet find the frost tacky, magnetic, kind. My claw, the great wrench one, aches and begins — very slowly, over years — to divide. Bone from tool. Tool from bone. I will need one hand that can turn a bolt and one hand soft enough to feel a hairline crack by walking my fingers along a sleeping flank.

So I become, for a while, the size of a child, and bipedal because the backs require it.

This is where you would find me, mostly. On the warm shifting archipelago of the herd.

I move from back to back as the grazers sway together and part, leaping gaps that open for a breath over black water, landing lighter than I left. The bellows-birds turn overhead on their fan-blade wings, creaking around the full circle, and their whistle-cries reach me a few seconds before their bodies do. I answer through my feet. I hammer my ribs — no, I hammer *theirs*, gently, with the heel of my soft hand: *I am here. This joint is failing. Hunter overhead. Pit ahead. Do not step.* The replies come back up through the soles of my feet before I have finished sending, because sound here does not travel, it *arrives*, pregnant, and stands up in the steam as a second, fainter herd grazing alongside this one.

I clear mite-jams from jammed knees. I deliver bolts mouth-to-mouth to tenders working three backs over, the iron warm from my cheek-pouch where I have been ripening it. I midwife calves out of side-pouches, small hot frantic things clamping onto my arms with temporary hooks, and I hook them into the lee of their mothers' legs where the steam falls as frost and melts again. I press my ear to a flank and listen to the sorting mill inside: iron to the blood, sulfur to the heat, glass to the eye-glands, everything finding its size.

The mites investigate me with great professional interest when I slip into a joint. They remember me. I might remember being them.

I am not growing the way I am supposed to.

The others who live long enough get heavy. Plates bud under their shoulders. Their soft hand squares off. The second pair of limbs folded along their ribs begins to ache in wet weather, and one dim hour they go down on all fours on an old grazer's back and do not get up again, and the herd makes room, and the chimney-neck begins, painfully, to grow. I wait for that heaviness. My shoulders stay narrow. My folded limbs stay folded, an unspoken opinion. Instead I get *sharper*. My soft hand gets softer. I can feel a crack forming three plates away through a sleeping animal's breathing. I can hear which part of a call is the animal and which part is the echo of it standing up in the water, still grazing a route the herd left days ago.

So I go, the way the specialized ones go, to the oldest grazers. To the white ones.

There is one near the front of the herd — or the front keeps finding her — who died in a silent pocket years ago and froze mid-step, perfect, eyes open, and has been carried along ever since by the press of living bodies around her. Her chimney still steams, faintly, from nothing living inside. I ride in the lee of that chimney because it is the quietest loud place in the Basin. The cold there should rush in. It doesn't, quite. The herd is too close. Her frozen throat is full of other animals' voices, caught and kept.

When the herd calls in unison — when the sound bridges a quiet horizon suddenly against your skin — something stands up in her dead throat and speaks with a voice made of ice and echo. Routes. The sulfur meadows where the crust is thickening. The nesting flats before the ground there is hot. A stub-hill we passed three days ago, still weeping the same bead of brine, not yet fallen, because the hill is our grandmother and the brine is her remembering us. I listen with my soft hand flat against her locked ribs and my wrench-hand open to the steam, and I read where we are going from the water-duplicates lagging underfoot, from the grazer-shaped ghosts wading out of themselves a half-second late in the shallows between bodies.

That is my work now, if work is the word. I keep the frozen ones audible. I keep the living ones from fusing. I sit in the steam above her blowhole and translate between the herd made of meat and the herd made of noise, because they do not always graze in the same direction, and the plain remembers every shape either of them has ever made, and will not let any of it go.

Below us, in a hot pit at the herd's turning edge, something is assembling itself out of a piston and a casing and a length of intestine learning, experimentally, to pump. It has one of my shed claws in it. I recognize the gauge. A tender I used to be will cough up a lens for it later, secreted like a pearl behind an eye I may be using now.

I don't know if I will ever get heavy. I don't know if I will root at the horizon eventually and slow and weep brine from pores that were once blowholes, and let young tenders climb my back to lick salt from my skin and feel, under their hands, that the stone is faintly warm and ticks as it cools. That is the far end of the road I am on: mite, crab, tender, grazer, hill. Nothing here ends by stopping.

For now I am the in-between thing with its own mouth for a while. Small on an enormous back. Lighter than I should be. Listening to a dead animal remember the way forward, hammering *I am here, I am here* into her ribs so the silence doesn't take the space between her steps — while under my feet there are two suns smeared in black water, and behind me, in the steam, my own clear outline is still arriving, a little late, to join me.
