At first I think I would arrive wrong.

I would come in sideways through the seam with too much hair and too much voice, loose ends collecting condensation and spores, my hard shoes interrupting the floor. I would keep looking up, which would be my first mistake and my most persistent one. Up at the inverted streets, up at the chimneys pointing down at me across a hundred meters of green-black air, up until my neck ached and some child, delighted, ran straight at a wall beside me and did not fall.

I would not be good at the town quickly. I would be good at it slowly, the way the town prefers.

I think I would be taken in — not adopted, nothing so loud as that — by the street-lamp guild. Not because I am useful with my hands, at first. Because I would keep standing too long in doorways watching the foxfire do what it does when someone nearby feels something they are politely not saying. Brightening. Dimming. Pulsing like an embarrassed heart. The guild understands people whose interiors leak. They would crop my hair, oil it back, give me soft shoes and a basket, and teach me to feed the lamps on iron filings and sugar and what they call grief, which is chemically sugar and emotionally not.

I would learn the work in my molars first.

Carrying the hanging baskets out along the boardwalks at plant-night, feeling the factory thrum come up through the living floor, uneven, organic, every belt and piston waiting on the pod's own pulse. Counting without meaning to count. Six minutes of ordinary carrying, and then the seventh minute arriving behind my eyes as pressure: every bridge tightening, every door swelling a fraction in its frame, the lamps in my basket flaring just enough to show me where the surface is. I would learn to pause then. Everyone does. Machinists time their hammer-strokes to it. Thieves time their thefts. I would time my steps so I never took the curve — that place where one street becomes a wall becomes a ceiling becomes home again — mid-contraction, until eventually my body stopped arguing. A hand trailing the root-rail. A lean. A step that is suddenly the first step down in a new world.

I would live somewhere poor and damp, I think. Not out of romance. Out of aptitude for rented, ripening rooms. A gourd-house still sweetening, its walls advancing a finger's width on a good afternoon, my collar blooming faintly green by evening and sponged clean at night, apologetically. I would sweep standing water toward the landlord's heart-fruit and call it rent. I would curl, some nights, in the soft doorway of someone else's budding room to keep the glass snails off the young walls, and wake with the grain of the floor printed into my cheek and the house knowing, approximately, my name.

What I would *do* there would take longer to name than where I slept.

The guild does not only tend lamps. At night the bridges retract. The districts draw apart. The town becomes what it most essentially is: not a crowd but a constellation, points of foxfire separated by forty meters of dark, joined only through tissue. Someone has to sit at the retraction points with a lamp that is allowed to answer.

That would be me.

I would keep the bridge-lamp at one of the crossings — not the grandest, one of the middling ones, where a market tier gives out onto black air and, far around the curve, a chapel district and two tiers of houses wait upside-down from where I sit. I would put one bare foot flat on the living floor to feel the contraction coming early, the way the midwives do, the way lovers do. Condensation gathering in my cropped hair. Basket at my knee. And I would listen below the threshold of speech for the calls that are never loud: a woman calling across to a doorway she cannot see, a warehouse watchman reporting nothing in the tone that means *nothing, continue*, a child baptized twice over now old enough to cross alone and trying not to brighten my lamp with fear as she passes into the void.

My lamp would brighten anyway. I would learn not to mention it. Strong feeling is spillage here, and I have always been a spiller — it is why they'd keep me out of courtrooms and bedrooms and the stock exchange, where futures are traded in neighborhoods that may bud as streets or as organs or not at all. But out on the platform, at the edge of the emptiness, a little spillage is a service. My lamp answers people. Theirs, around the curve, answers back, just slightly. I would become fluent in that: flare for *I am here*, dim for *go on*, the slow double-pulse that means a birth is going with the town, someone new arriving on the squeeze, already keeping time with the thing that contains us all.

In the long hours between calls I would do the other work the lamp-tenders inherit, because we are awake while facing the water. I would chart the eyeless fish in the cistern below my platform, turning their translucent circles night after night, and I would copy the shapes they make onto pulped-leaf paper in a hand I kept deliberately uninflected, the way the citizens speak. Pilgrims would come, eventually, to have them interpreted, and I would tell them — quietly, while doing something else with my hands, rebraiding a lamp-rope, sexing a budding room at a glance with the pollination brush I had started keeping in my waistcoat without ever deciding to — that the circles looked like a street they had lost, or a street that had not been grown yet, or, once, unmistakably, like the long curve of a spine in a market pillar that old women touch in passing without looking.

I would not always be believed. That would be fine. In a town where nothing is entirely metaphor, interpretation is just a form of gardening: you persuade, you splint, you feed it ash and silt and ground shell, you wait an afternoon to discuss a finger's width of advance with total seriousness.

And sometimes, in the deepest part of plant-night, when the moths the size of dinner plates were rowing silently across the hollow and the pale owls were hunting them in a silence more populated than any noise I grew up with, I would take my lamp down — not far, never far, I am not brave in the way tunnellers are brave — to one of the mouths below the factory quarter, and leave what the lamps eat. Iron filings. Sugar. Grief. For the earlier streets. For the less ripe citizens, translucent and industrious and completely unsurprised, who may be the town remembering itself into existence, who may be what we are assembling with our engines and our returned bodies and our carefully timed births. I would not go in. I would set the basket down where the warm, unformed tissue gives off its smell of libraries and hearts not yet committed to being either, and I would say my own name once, low, the way you speak to someone hard of hearing in the next room — in case a doorway down there is learning it. In case a house, someday, needs to sigh it back to someone arriving.

That is who I would be, I think, once the arriving wore off. Not a founder, not a machinist, not one of the dry-floored wealthy on the upper curves. A keeper of a small, responsive light at the place where the town lets go of itself each night. A person who learned to carry a full bowl. Someone stationed, by temperament and by trade, in the forty meters of dark between one lit doorway and the next — feeling through the sole of one bare foot the whole green sphere contract thoughtfully around its emptiness, like a hand deciding whether to close, and answering, on behalf of whoever is still awake around the curve: *not yet. We're here. Cross when you're ready.*
