I wouldn't be on the trail for long.

I'd go the way everything goes there — down the cut steps out of the white glare, following the smell after it stops being a smell. Sulfur, hot stone, that sweet close undernote like old leather. At first I'd think I was still a visitor standing in the rings that failed, with things gentle in my cuffs tasting for salt.

But if I stayed — if I really stayed, long enough for the floor to close over the place where I stood — I wouldn't stay standing.

I wouldn't be the centipede. I can feel that at once. Not the hand-long, wet-ash surfer up top, passed cleanly from back to back, making the local parting and the ripple. Too exposed. Too decisive. Every taking clean, from the side, and then the neighbors smoothing over the absence. I don't have that kind of hunger.

And I wouldn't be a cricket, though for a while I think I might be. Because the crickets are what you see. Finger-long, cave-clay, antennae three and four times the body, so the air itself is woven a foot above the floor. Everything known by touch: here, hot, turn, food, hold. Born into the spiral and taken by it until you learn the little side-step that keeps it from bunching. Top layer fanning with wings that will never fly, then down, then endured at the bottom, then lifted again on other backs to cool against the wall.

It's beautiful, that rotation. It's also too brief. A cricket learns the river by being carried, and then the river is mostly what it knows. I think I'd be slower than that. Older in my manner. Lower down.

In the middle layers, then. Where the light — what little of it ever negotiated the overhang — has already been absorbed by bodies above me.

I am flat as a coin that was given legs.

That's the shape that settles. Not a dome like the beetles, who invert themselves headfirst into the pile and offer their fused black backs as a fraction of shade. Not pale and eyeless and sighing open along my sides like the worm-things underneath, drinking heat directly off the stone, filtering everything pressed out of us until waste comes out softer and edible again. I am between them. An armored raft. I ride, mostly. When the carpet carries me, I let it carry me. When it stalls, I become a place where it stalls more deliberately — young crickets sheltering in my lee to cool, clusters forming behind me the way weather forms behind a low hill. At my size, after enough turnings, the others stop treating me as quite an animal. I am geography that occasionally uncurls.

I eat the past for a living.

That's literal down there. Compacted molt. Ten thousand generations pressed and heated into dark horn, springy under a shoe. Fungal threads no thicker than a breath running through a husk that has lain still too long. My own shed skins. The skins of ancestors who were also, for a while, landmarks. I graze very slowly through what everyone else has finished being, and my digestion is part of why the floor doesn't foul, doesn't choke. Without work like mine the Pan would become a graveyard that still moves. With it, the dead become bedding again. Nursery again. Food again.

I like that economy. Nothing wasted, nothing mourned, nothing solo.

When something touches me wrong — a footfall that is not a cricket's footfall, a centipede's many feet dimpling down too purposefully, a stone loosened from the rim falling muffled through the pile — I curl. Perfect and sealed. A disc. Legs gone, seams gone. The carpet simply climbs over me without pausing, hundreds of antennae tapping across my back, reading me as stone, and I wait underneath my own decision until a minute has passed and the current feels ordinary again, and then I uncurl and resume. I have done this more times than I can count, if counting is even a sense I kept. If the rings that show when I surface are true, I have done it longer than any cricket in the tower above me will live to imagine.

I know the Pan by temperature the way you know a room by light.

Cool is the wall at night, stone still remembering the mountain outside, where we press our bellies in the inward lane. Warm is the long outward drift over the molt. Hot is the approach I never complete — the center, the three low cones of sulfur crystal and black glass, where the bacterial mats crust yellow and white and orange at the mouths of the vents, where the floor would cook meat and no queen could live and nothing stays. The rotation keeps me from needing to choose. Hunger draws me in toward the mats felted down to a stain. Heat pushes me out. I am held in the exact shape those two facts make together.

And once or twice an hour, or once in a night — I never learn the rhythm, I only learn the feeling through other bodies — we stop.

Not startled. Antennae halt mid-wave above me. The beetle ahead of me stops mid-dig. The centipede hunting somewhere up top flattens itself to the backs it hunts. The hanging clusters on the ceiling lock, swayless. My own half-uncurl holds. What is left is the vent breathing against rock, and a silence so total that sound seems like something the Pan evolved out of on purpose.

In that pause I understand I am not mistaken about being one animal among millions. I am also a cell pressed shell to shell inside a larger animal exactly the size of the bowl — one that listens through molt and stone to a pressure building under the vents, or a shift in the mountain's heat, or to itself listening. Faster than thought. Older than me. The instruction to resume arrives the same way: the cricket above me completes the step it began, and I complete mine, and the spiral takes up exactly where it left its breath.

I come to water the same way. At night cold air spills over the rim and meets the rising heat and an inch-wide stain darkens high on the back wall, never dripping. I uncurl at last and wait my turn in silent rotation — cricket, beetle, me, cricket again — each of us pressing mouthparts to the damp for a count no visitor could predict and none of us violates. I do not cheat in the freeze, so I do not cheat in the thirst. It wouldn't occur to me, down at this density. Cheating would be a kind of solo, and there has been no solo here for a very long time.

You, coming down the steps, would not really see me. Once, perhaps — a length of me surfacing between beetles when the pile shifts, the crickets treating me briefly as a low obstacle, young riding behind me where the flow breaks. You'd think *carpet*, then *kinds*, then maybe you'd be down long enough to think *one thing, distributed*. Your headache would start behind your eyes. The heat would climb your legs. Things would be in your cuffs, tasting, because your sweat up here is a kind of rain.

I would let you pass over me. That is what I am for.

Behind you, when you climb back toward the cold and the red pumice and the living drop off you one by one, cooling, I am still turning — in along the wall, out along the warm current, down through the patient layers, eating what we were, making the floor springy for what we will be. Wall to wall. Rim to vent. Not waiting. Not finished. Just flat, and slow, and held, in the dark the noon can't enter, keeping my small part of the surface alive.
