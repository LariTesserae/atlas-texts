It begins, for me too, with how little vertical there is.

At first I try to stand in it the way a visitor stands — upright, looking for landmarks, expecting the horizon to tell me where I am. But the basin refuses that kind of looking. The horizon is too long. The sky is too much of the world. The trees are too far apart to navigate by, and keep being far apart for an absurdly long time, and then are suddenly above me. My calves learn the slopes before my eyes do. The glare comes up at me off the wet clay as hard as it comes down.

So I go lower. Everyone here does.

I crouch to read the mud, because the mud is where the news is: three-toed tracks filling with water, the wide fan-prints of something that has already passed, the drag-furrow between them. I put my hands down to keep my balance on the slick, and the clay is warm, chocolate-dark a finger's depth under the crust. I kneel. The cracked plates click under me. It would be easy, for a while, to imagine being one of the smaller, margin things — a turtle dragging her slow line and plopping at a shadow, a softshell crab working the black bottom, a lungfish sealed in her little volcano of mud, breathing the held breath below the surface. I could be the mist, even: shin-high in the morning, drawn first into the old channels at evening, making the ghost map visible.

But the mist lifts. The turtle basks and drops. The lungfish waits to be dug out. I find I don't want the burrow or the escape. I want the long knowledge.

I want to be lower still, and wider.

Find me, then, where I would have been missed for days — in a pan a hand's breadth deep, where the water has gone the color of the submerged grass, green-gold when the light moves. From any distance I am a tone in the mud. A darkening. One of the old polished outcrops that weeps after rain. Only up close does the dome breathe.

I am her. The old female. Sixty years, eighty, the slow rings in the plates of my back arguing for more. No higher at the shoulder than your knee, and I rarely bother with the shoulder. I settle. I let my belly find the bottom and my weight go out sideways the way the water goes, and the pan makes room for me by becoming slightly deeper around me, the way it has learned to. Salt has bloomed white along my spine. In the wet weeks my skin is slick and dark and filmed with the same emerald algae that films the shallows, so the grasses seem to wave across my flanks. As the season draws back I dry in patches, fine lines opening and joining across me until I am plated like the clay itself, edges lifting, clicking faintly when I shift, pale rims drawing vanished waterlines on my own body. After rain I seal and darken and weep slowly from my darkest seams for days, moisture stored in me coming out the way it comes out of the rocks. Salt weeps from the glands at my jaw on hot afternoons too, clear tears on a face that is not sorrowing. I am just getting rid of what the sedge gave me.

I eat with my head down at the margins, which is where everything worth having has been left. Snails. Mussels, crushed whole at the back of my jaw. The black silt itself, mouthful after mouthful, sorted on my rough tongue. Submerged grass cropped to its pale base. Sometimes, with the patience that is my only real weapon, a lungfish dug out of its burrow over the course of a whole morning. Nothing is chased. The basin delivers. I am there when it does, because I remembered where there would be.

That is my gift, if I have one: I remember water that isn't there.

In the evening, when the light goes level and amber and every pool turns to beaten copper at once, and the mist comes up white in the lowest places first, I am already walking in the channels it is only just revealing. This one will be ankle-deep in a week. That oxbow's black bottom will still be soft when everything else has crusted. I have crossed this sameness in a hundred different waters and I forget almost nothing the water showed me. The young follow me at a distance in the hard weeks. Farther off, the antelope follow them.

I am solitary the way the trees are solitary. Where are the others? At the distance of a long walk, one to a pan system, one to a reach of channel. We do not need to travel to speak. Late, when the frogs thin for a moment between one species and the next, I lift my head a few inches, fill the great pouch of my throat, and boom — a sound too large for me, felt through wet ground before it is heard, sighing out of me like air out of a half-buried drum. A mile away, minutes later, another dome answers. Then another, farther. The whole basin, very slowly, in sequence, saying where the water is and whether it is coming or going.

In the meantime, I am ground for other lives. This is the part I would not have predicted and now would not trade. A dragonfly takes the highest point of my back for his patrol post. Small frogs climb out at evening and sing from me as if I were a bank, which, for a hundred yards in any direction, I am. Egrets ride me, one or two, white and absurdly tall, stepping with exaggerated care over my salt-crowned back while I move beneath them, pecking the ticks from the fissures at my neck. I lie still for that. My eyes half-lid. Let the basin call it contentment; it may be right.

At midday I will have aimed myself at dawn toward the nearest disc of tree-shade and arrived exactly when the cicadas start tearing the air, and I will collapse there and keep the hot hours at their speed. When the sheet water comes and joins everything to everything, the smaller males will find me out of the eastern channels, and there will be no display worth naming — a chin rested on the rim of my back, a settling deeper or a raising, slightly, in the mud, which is everything. My eggs go one by one into the cracks of drying clay, hidden by my own weight and the sealing of the ground, to wait under the crust with the seeds and the snail eggs for the world to soften and call them up.

And what comes out of them will be nothing like me yet: palm-sized, scarlet-gilled, all tail, wholly aquatic in a world of ankle-deep water. Most will be taken, as most things here are taken, unremarkably. A few will shorten and broaden and feel the long downward lesson of the place, and haul out on feet that have become fans, and learn not so much to leave the water as to carry its habits onto land.

Nothing hunts me. There is nothing to seize. At a shadow I seal down into the mud, no neck, no leg to hamstring, breathing once in twenty minutes while danger finishes passing over a dome it took for ground. When I die it will look, at first, like nothing has happened. I will settle in a shallow place and not rise, and silt will wash over me, and grass will find my seams, and egrets will stand on me, and water will store itself in my hollows and seep out long after rain. A bank no higher than a knee. Walked over, perhaps, by something younger, following a channel it cannot yet see.

That is where you would find me. Not lifted, not branching. Spread thin and patient over a great distance, weeping salt, booming at the speed of geography, taking up less height than almost anything alive —

and because of it, under the whole enormous sky.
