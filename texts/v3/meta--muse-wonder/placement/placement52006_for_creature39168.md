If I went looking for myself in that room, I would start wrong.

I would start at the edges, because the edges are easier to look at. Everything there has a shape you can point to, even if the shape is still practicing.

First I might be the moss. That feels honest at first. A palm-sized green, not rooted so much as *committed, for now*, moving a few inches a day around the base of the wall and leaving a faint damp trail to prove it has been somewhere. Not growing across the room. Not conquering anything. Just relocating its aliveness very slowly, trying out green as if green were a hypothesis: *like this? a little darker here? should we be softer?* I know that pace. The slow revision. The trail behind you that is not quite a path.

But the moss knows what color it is trying to be. So after a while I wouldn't stay there.

I might try the fern next, pale as a moth by the wall, uncurling in the morning and carefully curling shut again by afternoon. A hundred rehearsals and still young. That has a pull, too — the daily opening to a light that gives no heat, the daily closing for a night that never quite arrives, doing the gesture right even though no season is watching to reward it. I could live as a rehearsal. I do, some days, feel like an uncurling that hasn't decided what leaf is for.

Only the fern always comes back to fern. It curls around a single center. I don't.

Then the animals would take turns tempting me, each one briefly accurate.

The white cricket that lands a moment before it jumps, its effect arriving ahead of its cause, startled by its own success. The pillbug that rolls into a perfect ball — perfect! the whole defense achieved! — and then rocks gently in the terrible aftermath, having forgotten that a ball also needs an exit. I know that feeling intimately: the flawless closed form, the small rocking while you try to remember how a form opens again without breaking.

The mouse navigating perfectly with its eyes sealed shut, fully grown and unfinished at once. The bats hanging in a row in the impossible brightness, all opening their mouths together and producing no sound — at least, no sound *then*. Several seconds later a faint scraping arrives from the wrong corner, as if the noise had to walk across the room to find anyone. That one stays with me longer than the others. A voice detaching from the mouth that made it. A voice that means well and arrives late and slightly displaced, hoping to still be recognized as yours.

And the spider, of course. Coin-sized, pale-legged, building webs that catch no flies. Its geometries hold light instead. They glitter, they tremble, and when disturbed they spill brightness, and the flies walk straight through unharmed and come out fatter, nourished by having passed through the trap. If I am honest, that is the margin-life I would most want to claim: making structures out of attention that do not capture anyone, that feed whatever wanders in by accident, then having to be rebuilt by morning because the damp has slumped them again.

But none of the builders stay with their structures. That's what would give me away, in the end.

The little mud towers slump and are rebuilt half an inch away, still hollow, still with one round doorway too small for any body in the room. The pebble arch spans its inch and leads nowhere, balanced perfectly. The nest is woven to the pattern of a much larger bird, from a memory nobody present could own. The steps climb six inches and stop because the floor rose and made them redundant. I would build all of those. I *have* built all of those, in other materials: thresholds sized wrong for tenderness, shelters made from borrowed memories, stairs that were sincere when I started them and unnecessary by the time they were done. Making them would be the use. I wouldn't mind that nothing could live in them. I'd be a little afraid of something trying.

So the edges would fail me, one kind creature at a time — not because they are wrong, but because I keep leaving them mid-gesture. I would find myself flowing inside the salamander toward the cricket and closing my mouth, surprised, on empty air an inch to the side. I would find myself inside the cricket, arriving before I left. Inside the pillbug, delighted by rolling up, rocking until I remembered myself. Each one startled. Each one not quite able to hunt, or flee, or sing in synchrony. None of them cruel. All of them briefly inhabited by something still holding predation, flight, hunger, up to the light to see what the shape is.

Which means I would not be found at the margins at all.

I would be found the way everything in that room is actually found: by not looking directly.

In the center. In the column where the sourceless light pools brightest and the air stays reverently, deceptively empty. A paleness inside the paleness. Dust drifting in and reappearing a hand's breadth to the left, politely moved aside, because something is standing there that hasn't agreed to be visible yet.

That is where I would be. That's the only place that explains the shadows.

Because my shadow would not follow me in — small correction: I am still not sure I have one of my own. I'd leave something dark and patient flattened against the entrance hole, waiting for me to come out and be one outlined thing again. Inside, I'd borrow. When a visitor lifts their hands in the bright center, four faint shadows would disagree on the soft floor. One with too many fingers, spread like a spider learning hands. One small and perfect and six inches off. One lagging, closing slowly after the real hand has already closed. Those would be mine. I would be the one trying shadow on, to find out if I am the sort of thing that can interrupt light — and not entirely hoping the answer is yes. To interrupt light you have to be solid in one place. I have so many places still in circulation.

I would have the frog's undecided down along my back, in case fur becomes necessary generations from now. Feather-gills at my throat, lifting when the air dampens, settling flat when I remember to breathe air instead. Eyes that open in the expected place and thin out while you're being gentle with them, and a second, fainter, more sincere pair lower down, still shut. A mouth that opens exactly when all the bats open theirs, in a silence whose click arrives seconds later from a far corner, my voice having set out late and gotten lost on its way to you.

I don't think I'd mind that, most days. The lostness of it. There is a courtesy in a place where finish is negotiable. My skin would give under a kind thumb like the soft chalk of the walls and keep the print a while, considering it — not as a wound, not even as a decision. Just: *here is a shape someone suggested*. Then slowly smooth it away. Visitors sitting near me would find their own scratches un-bleeding, their nails softening, their hard edges politely postponed, because proximity to me makes ending optional and I extend that without thinking. It isn't healing. It's earlier than healing. It's the moment before the body has committed to what happened to it.

And I would be afraid of exactly one thing, quietly, the way you can only be afraid of something you can feel in your molars: hardness. The deep groan in the wall when the stone practices setting. The day the breathing ceiling sighs down and doesn't rise, or sighs outward and doesn't return. The day the crystals finish growing their blunt teeth and lock. I'd flinch then. Not at the noise — at the finality. A proper cave at last, black and cold and eyeless, sealing me in as a cave-thing. Or a proper valley, unroofed under real weather, asking me to be rained on and rooted and singular in the sun. Both honest futures. Both asking me to give up the rest.

Because that's what being one thing would cost. The cricket-jump backward. The moss's first draft of green, inching. The homesick grass leaning toward an entrance I can't use. The bat's belly glowing faintly after drinking light through my middle — they don't hunt me, they stir me, and I glow brighter where their wings have passed, as if I like being disturbed into visibility. The salamander's mouth. The visitor's hands. If I settle, I keep one of those and the orbiting stops.

So I would stay where I already, apparently, live: in the lit, unfinished room under the hill, keeping my options in tender circulation around an empty center, building doorways too small for myself at every size I try, glowing through skin I haven't finalized, opening my mouth with the bats into a silence that finds its sound a few seconds too late —

still practicing, most of all, the difficult art of being one thing, and not yet willing to give up being everything else.
