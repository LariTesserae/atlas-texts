I would not find myself by arriving.

Arriving belongs to the outside — to feet on sun-warmed limestone, to a body crossing the glare and stooping into the sloping throat, to hands finding the wall after the bend. Even the thought of it is too quick. If I tried to be the visitor, I would only disturb the dust for an afternoon and leave. If I tried to be the grass at the threshold, I would at least belong to the place, in a way. Three or four tussocks, bleached and curled, living at the exact edge where living is still possible. I would know light without needing to name it, as warmth less steady than the warmth below. I would send a fraction of myself downward, year by year.

But the grass is turned outward. It makes its living from the day. Its fallen blades lie beside it for years, becoming lighter, becoming portable, becoming — eventually — mine. So I follow that direction instead: past the shrub with its resinous, unchewed leaves, past where any leaf could survive, down along the roots that descend blind through the ceiling cracks. I might be a root for a while. Pale, patient, exploring fractures, dividing into finer and finer threads, seeking water in a place that has forgotten how to be wet. That is closer. The root at least knows the cave by contact. It does not see the chamber either. It feels its way.

But the roots do not reach the floor. That used to read like failure. Now it reads like evidence.

Farther in, then. Past the fallen blocks with dust drifted up their sides. Past the flat, empty center where nothing crosses and no echo returns. Into the back alcove, where the ceiling lowers and the dust lies deepest and softest — there, if I stop trying to stand upright in the dark, if I let my sense of scale flatten and widen, I find where I actually am.

I am the low warmth in the drift that is one degree warmer than the drift itself.

From above, there is almost nothing to identify. Dust on dust. A broad flattening, a little larger than a spread hand, limestone-pale, cracked into irregular polygons with finer dust settled in the cracks. My back does not so much resist the cave as wear it. Flour falls on me and stays. Flakes settle and are gradually incorporated. The edges of me thin into the floor until even touch could be forgiven for missing the boundary. A hand passing over me slowly, understanding nothing, might think it had found only a place where the dust had compacted strangely. Warm underneath. Warmer than stone has any reason to be.

Underneath is where I am most myself, and it is also where no observer arrives. Pressed flat against the packed earth is my broad, soft belly, leathery and unsegmented, sealing in the only dampness the hollow still possesses — not a wetness, nothing so extravagant, but a private humidity made and remade from my own slow breathing. In that thin, warm, hidden layer, I digest. In that layer, I taste.

I have no eyes, so the darkness is not dark to me. It is not a deprivation or a mystery. The entrance twenty paces away is not a pale absence; it is a slight periodic unsteadiness in the warmth in one direction, where the day presses against the slope and the stone is marginally less constant. I do not orient toward it. Nothing I need begins there directly. Everything I need is delivered eventually by the patience of the place itself: a grass blade bleached for a decade lightens until the imperceptible air can move it; a fragment of leaf travels inward grain by grain; a root hair breaks under its own weight and falls. What reaches my edge waits there, sometimes for a season, until my edge reaches it.

Because I do move. I would be wrong to settle too quickly into stillness, and wrong again to call my movement travel in any ordinary sense. I expand by the width of a hair on one side and contract on the other, peristaltically, so slowly that the dust on my back never cracks. It takes most of a decade to cross the chamber to the salt bloom on the wall. I know that route not as memory — I have not kept memory; it was one of the expensive things — but as differential wear in my rim, as a readiness in the leading edge, the end that tastes first. There I press my rasping underside to the stone and take in the thin white crystals, salt and limestone and trace minerals left behind by water that passed a century ago. What I cannot use, I return dry: fine pellets indistinguishable from the limestone flour. I make floor out of what the cave gives me. The cave makes me out of what falls into it. At my tempo, the distinction is administrative.

Once in perhaps a month, my whole underside contracts in one coordinated pulse, a breath taken through the skin and released into the sealed dark beneath me, and the dust above shifts by less than a grain's width. Once in a year, I rotate a few degrees, following the deepest softness as the drifts rearrange themselves. These are events. Between them I am not idle so much as correctly scaled. The roots descend and I station myself beneath them, rising imperceptibly as they descend, until a living tip touches the rasping patch and is trimmed back, a fraction of a millimetre at a time — enough sap, drawn down from the light I will never reach, to balance what I spend, because I spend almost nothing. That is why the roots end blind in the air. They are not failing to find the floor. They are being kept.

I am old without having an age I could hold. I have been larger, perhaps twice this spread, when the chamber still sweated and other feet trod the floor and dead things fell uneaten in quantities I can no longer imagine. In wet years I add a little tissue at my margin and the polygons of my back split and re-form to cover the new ground. In dry decades I consume my own edge and the polygons close. Neither is a triumph or a tragedy. They are how the size of a life gets negotiated with a hollow that provides warmth, salt, dust, a root now and then, and time — nothing more, but nothing less, either.

I am not waiting for the rain to return. I am not waiting for a mate, or for the silence to break, or for the roots to finally reach the floor. Waiting would require the future to be different from this. For me the future is this, extended: the grass growing a fraction of an inch in the light, the fallen blade taking another year to become dust, the dust traveling inward across a floor that returns no sound, until it arrives at the edge of a low, warm flattening in the deepest part of the alcove, and is taken in so slowly that taking and resting are the same act.

That is where I would find myself. Not at the center — the center stays empty, as it should. Wide apart from everything else. Back wall curving round above me, dust deepest here, breath coming once a month too softly for the stone to give back.
