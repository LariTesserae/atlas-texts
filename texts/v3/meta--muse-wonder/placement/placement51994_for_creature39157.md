You would not wake up. There would be no moment of opening your eyes to the dark, because your eyes were never going to open.

You would come to yourself the way weather comes to the rift — gradually, as a change in pressure.

First, vibration. Not sound, vibration, arriving through the soles of your feet. It is everywhere before it is anywhere in particular. Through the wet film under your toes: the shallow rasping of crickets grazing stone, millions of them, so continuous it ceases to be noise and becomes a surface. Through the water lapping at your ankles: the shifting of crabs, the tiny, patient clicks of claws on limestone. Above that, felt in your sternum before it reaches your ears, the frogs.

*Tink. Tink. Tink-tink.*

You would be standing in the edge of a pool in the middle of the rift, where the floor lies almost at sea level and the limestone walls are so high above you that noon is only a theoretical event, a thinning of the blue-grey breath far overhead that you have never seen and will never need to. There is no green here. There has never been green here. The world is stone the color of wet slate, bacterial film the color of rust and old cream, guano whitening the ledges like dirty snow, and cold — a damp, mineral cold that lives in your joints even when you let your blood warm.

You are the silence moving through it.

Around you, for perhaps three meters in every direction, the loop has stopped. The crickets under your feet have frozen mid-rasp. The frogs at the rim of your pool have cut off mid-*tink*, their glass throats still. The water boatmen hang motionless beneath the surface. This is how everything else knows you are there: not by shape — your filaments are stained cream and rust and calcite, your skin gone stone-textured with age — but by the hole you make in the sound.

You are tall, if you ever chose to unfold. A meter and a half of leg, mostly. But you rarely choose it. You fold. Your legs hinge wrong and then right again, tendons showing like cords under wet paper skin, and your small body settles close between them, kept low, kept patient. Your feet are absurd and perfect: four long toes splayed in front, each ending in a soft pad instead of a claw, and a fifth set far back like a second heel, so that you do not so much stand on the valley as listen to it through the ground itself.

The valley arrives in your feet as a map, redrawn every second.

Twenty meters to your left, a frog calls, and through your pads you know its size, its direction, the exact scallop of pothole it is sitting in. Further off, a crab shifts in deeper water. Further still, at the far end of the rift, a wave is beginning — you feel it before you hear it, a change in the stone itself as tens of thousands of frogs take up the note and pass it down the valley toward you, the sound swelling until the air rings, until the pools shiver into rings though nothing has touched them, until it would burst you open if you let it in whole.

So you don't. Behind your jaw, where ears would be in another kind of animal, you keep broad patches of skin slack as unfingered drums. You tighten them. You loosen them. You narrow the roaring world down to one pool. One frog. One small glass voice calling a half-second too carelessly near the water's edge.

For hours you have let yourself cool. That is the discipline of your body. Your heart has slowed to a beat every few seconds. Your breath is nothing. Your skin is the temperature of the pool you stand in and your translucent chest shows, faintly, the dark knot of your gut, the pale flicker underneath — though there is no one who hunts by sight here to be fooled. The crickets are fooled by stillness and by temperature and by patience. The frogs are fooled because you have become, in every way that matters to them, a piece of the valley that has not yet decided to move.

Then you decide.

You warm from the inside out with a speed that still surprises your own blood — heat rushing to neck and legs, filaments lifting off your skin — and your long narrow head unfolds downward in one unbroken fall, your slate-colored beak slipping into the water exactly where that last *tink* placed it, no splash, no wasted motion. Something soft. A swallow. A brief shiver the length of you to shake the water off.

Then the cooling again. The patience again.

You do not call. Your throat was not made for that. Once, when you were newly hatched in a dry alcove — naked, filaments plastered flat, your oversized feet paddling at shell fragments in a warm drift of guano — you learned to imitate the *tink* of a small frog, badly at first, then well enough to draw real frogs toward your clumsy, hungry strikes. You kept the trick through your first year. Then your throat changed, the way throats do, and closed that door. To call like prey now, at your size, would be a lie the whole valley would have to live inside, and this place runs, absolutely, on the honesty of its noise.

What you speak with is your feet.

Someday, at the edge of a flooded terrace during the wet pulse of the year, another of your kind will stand at the opposite rim of your pool — another travelling silence in your map — and for a very long time neither of you will acknowledge the other at all. Then he will shift his weight, toe by toe, pressing a slow pattern into the film and the shallow water, rings going out and out until they touch your pads. You will wait. You will answer with a variation. Back and forth, building pattern on pattern through an entire frog-wave and through the strange, ringing hole of quiet that follows it, negotiating in your soles what other animals negotiate in song: whose pool this is, whose season, whose turn to fold down and disappear into the dark.

If you are female, your turn will come to scrape a shallow cup not in mud — the swiftlets own the mud, their nests packed in a continuous crust over every ledge above you — but in a dry guano drift against a boulder, where rot keeps the earth faintly, steadily warm. Two eggs, grey as pool water, speckled like flint. You will stand over them for weeks, cooled almost to stone, eating only what wanders within reach, and when the chicks hatch blind, their sealed eye-pits smooth as yours, their first act will not be a cry. It will be a tapping. Weak. Arrhythmic. Against shell and warm drift. And you will answer it, toe by toe, before you ever feed them — the pattern established before the animal is.

And mostly, you will simply be spaced along the rift, one of a solitary, overlapping country of listeners, each known to the others as a recurring quiet. You will learn your few terraces the way a tide learns a shore: which pools restock fastest after rain, which hollowed drumming-stones draw the crickets thickest and make them careless, which sinkholes at the southern end breathe cold air upward hours before the streams join and the floor floods ankle-deep. Lizards will take some of your chicks. Floods will take some of your eggs. Once, perhaps, you will mistime your deafening at the height of a chorus and go through the rest of your life half-sensing on one side, compensating with your feet, surviving anyway, because feet were always the truer sense.

Thirty years. Forty, if the valley is generous. Slower to warm each winter-that-isn't. More scarred. A fine white beard of fungus growing over the sealed pits where your eyes would be, drinking the condensation before it can chill your skull, doing you no harm at all.

And at the end you will stop, as your kind tends to stop, in one of the dry alcoves where generations of swiftlets have died and been picked clean — whether you seek it out or simply cease, finally, in the last place you ever stood as a chick, the beetles will not care about the distinction. The films will creep. The filaments will go first, then the translucent skin, then everything soft, until what remains is the true architecture of you: the long listening toes, the folded stilts of legs, the narrow trowel of a skull with its two smooth, sealed pits.

Another layer of small white geometry in the bone drifts. Incorporated. Nothing wasted, nothing leaving — because nothing about you ever needed light to leave by.

That is where you would find yourself: not above the noise, not outside it, but underneath it and holding a small, clean silence open inside it, year after year, in the cool damp dark at the bottom of the world — the quiet, foot-listening thing the deafening valley is watched by, and kept honest by, and ultimately *for*.
