At first I think I would be the thing in the sky.

Not because I belong up there, but because that's where a visitor looks first. The one broad-winged scavenger, hanging a thousand feet up in the gale without moving, as if nailed there. To be still while everything else is being torn sideways — that seems like freedom. Then it is gone, and I understand it was only ever visiting the wind. It doesn't live in this place. It borrows it.

I wouldn't be the hare, either. Earless-looking, the color of dry grass, gone before it becomes itself. Or the bee, fat and bewildered, working the inch of still air behind a boulder. Or the birds that don't so much fly as get thrown and have to fight their way back down to their own nests. They endure the plateau in flashes. They are not shaped by it all the way through.

The shepherds who come up seasonally — I would be close to them, and then not. I would come up with the shaggy, low-bellied flock the first year, weighted hems clicking, hat tied hard under my chin, shouting and losing half of every sentence. I'd learn to lean when walking into the west wind, at that impossible angle, and to lean back and let it hold me for a step when walking with it. I'd learn not to set a cup down unsecured. I'd learn to come forehead-to-forehead to be heard, hand cupped around a jaw, speech carried through bone.

And then, when the season turned and the others went down again, my family would be one of the ones that never quite did.

That is where I settle. Not on the plateau, exactly — excused from it. In a hut sunk three feet into the peat, only two feet showing above it, round-walled with smooth, egg-shaped stones, the sod-and-felt roof netted down under boulders and braided grass rope. From any distance another hump of old ground. Inside, the roar doesn't stop, it only muffles into a buffeting, a breathing against the felt. My spine would keep its bend even in that sudden quiet. My body would remember outdoors when I was indoors.

I would have been tethered before I could properly walk — a light cord of wool and grass at my waist, the other end at my mother's wrist, or around a stone weight by the waist-high door. Not held back. Held *down*. Held in place in a world where a toddler can be gusted clean off his feet on a clear day.

As a child I'd run in a stumbling line with my brother and cousin through the bad passes, tied lightly to each other, our felt coats sewn around the hems with flat pebbles so we clinked softly as we moved. Like the cairns do, when the wind finds the gaps. My hair braided tight to my scalp and wound with cord and small bells, so my mother could hear me moving inside the deafening grass without either of us wasting breath shouting. In a gale the whole family would be audible that way. Bells, pebble-hems, breath timed long and slow in barrelled chests made for air that is mostly a rumor at five thousand meters.

I would be one of the children sent out at dawn, tethered to a line, to listen to the stones.

That, I think, is the job my self keeps returning to. Pressing an ear to a cold, hollowed boulder — one of the ones ten thousand years of grit-wind has ground into a flute — and feeling its note in my teeth before I quite hear it. Knowing our local field of singing stones the way lowlanders know voices. This one rising means the wind will veer and drop into the hollow after dark. This one gone suddenly silent means it has clogged with wet moss, means heavy air, means a storm is making itself somewhere out of sight. Running back, grass whipping knee-high and emerald and gold around me, the whole plateau travelling sideways in waves like water, to sign what I heard — whole arms flashing pale felt against the green, a circle, a flap, a crouch readable across a basin: *squall in ten minutes, sheep scattered, do not take the flock to the lake that dried to salt yesterday.*

Growing older, I would not straighten. I'd widen through hips and shoulders, shorten in effect, learn the permanent useful lean. My face would go mahogany and leather-thick across cheekbones and nose and the backs of my hands from that straight, honest, equatorial sun that burns you while the wind freezes you, and stay pale in the strip where my chin-strap sits. My eyes would narrow from a lifetime of grit, lashes pale with dust, and I would learn to spot a single sheep the color of wet stone at a distance a visitor would swear was empty grass.

I would eat what can keep low and keep still. Hard cheese salted and wind-dried on stone slabs under nets. Peat-root bread from a dung fire, hot and quick and sweetly acrid. The tiny bitter herbs that flower only a centimeter above the soil, gathered by the fistful right after a squall, when the wind has combed them upright again. In the mornings I would wring dew out of cloths left purposefully out all night — grass, felt, cold metal running with it where the racing cloud dragged its belly straight across the ground — squeezing it into a wooden bowl before noon came and the wind drank it all again.

And in the afternoons, when I was old and my knuckles had swollen into knots as smooth as the loose stones outside, I would follow the dust devils.

That image won't leave me alone. The pale columns wandering the basins in the hot part of the day, picking up grass seed and spray and shining insects, depositing them inexplicably sorted — seed here, dried insects there, beetle wings in a glittering line. And the old women following after, unhurried, collecting as if shopping in a market only they can see. I would be one of them. Unhurried because there is nowhere up here you can hurry to that isn't half a day away and mostly grass and stone in between, and that distance is not empty to me. It is room to be blown clean.

I would know the dead by pitch.

When we carry someone — and the old ones are astonishingly light, hollow-boned with age and altitude — we build them upright into a cairn in the field of stones, each rock chosen round and egglike, fitted without mortar so the perpetual west wind can find the gaps and hum. A good cairn sings in that person's approximate note. Passing weeks later, I would pause, lean in despite the roar flattening my clothes until the seams sting, and listen. *He is still sounding. She has settled silent; the stones have shifted.* I'd adjust nothing unless I needed to. Standing upright is for cairns. The living keep low.

Taken down to trade in a sheltered valley, true quiet would frighten me a little, the way it frightens our children. My ears would ache with expectation. The sky narrowed to a strip above deep valley walls would feel like a pit, like enclosure, and trees — trees would unsettle me most, those things that insist on standing straight and catching the wind until they break. I would want to get back up where the sky takes up three-quarters of the world, where the land has finished changing and only the weather changes, endlessly, over it.

At dusk I'd come home ducking through that low door, pebble hem settling, bells going still. A stone set, without thinking, on any light bowl, any cup, any sleeping infant. Soup. Peat smoke. Hands still busy with sinew and felt because stillness here isn't rest, it's just being briefly unblown. Outside, the squalls blow themselves off the edge of the world, the mirror-thin lakes go furious with spray at one end, the tussocks spring upright minutes after being flattened and shake off the rain.

And through the felt roof buffeting softly all night, like something breathing in its sleep, somewhere out in the dark a cairn would hold its note, and I would sleep — as I first slept tied lightly to a bedpost indoors, against no danger except habit — through a roar so constant it becomes a ringing silence I was born inside of.
