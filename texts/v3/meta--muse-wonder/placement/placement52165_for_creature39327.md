I would not come in as an otter.

That is the temptation first, down the causeway spine, slipping in from the bright surface. The air pockets under the domes — cold, wet-lime air, a place to actually rest while everything else is being carried — I understand wanting that. To be warm in a cold system. To have a room, of sorts. To watch the upside-down valley go still for twenty seconds and have your face be the one that appears, whiskered, at the edge of the bubble.

But the otters have more claim than that, and less belonging. They crack mussels on the steps and leave their middens and go back to a world above the rim that I wouldn't have. I would use the pocket and still be a visitor in it. I think I would spend the whole time listening for the water rising a sip too high.

The char tempt me next, longer. 

Who wouldn't want that knowledge in the body — to tilt instead of fight, to feel a wall-current turn left three seconds before it turns and be already gone around the corner, silver all at once. To know the low holes from the high holes. To hang in the absolute stillness behind the stone with only your eye showing, a faint glint in a row of glints, while a whole district empties itself past your face and cannot touch you.

But I am not good at the school. That flicker is one animal. I would be the one half a beat late, snatching at the midge in the twenty quiet seconds and feeling the pull resume while I was still out in the doorway deciding. The youngest live deepest in the refuges for a reason. I am neither young nor quick that way.

I go down past them, because the place keeps correcting you if you let it.

Past the mussels, cemented into the exact inner angle of every step where the rush falters for an inch — I respect them enormously. First sorters. Eaten-weather strainers. Shell grown so far into the stone that you can't tell where the step was cut and where the animal began. When the flush comes they shut together, a whole district closing its eyes. There is a life in being that precisely placed: not anywhere in the current, but at the one faltering inch in it.

I am close to that. But a mussel can shut. I don't think shutting is what I do best.

The loaches live even closer to the stone, mottled exactly the colour of wet grit, flattened into seams in the fastest, thinnest water, grazing the invisible film so the city doesn't slime itself closed. The crayfish I watch a long time at midday, backing out across the polished plazas with their claws up — not threat, tasting — reading the first tremor of a level change in open water, then making themselves into cobble before the sideways rush arrives. Janitors. Patient, exposed, necessary. When a dead char wedges in a tank-corner, they are the ones who arrive before it can foul a sill.

And the eels — I almost stop there. In the drains, in the U-bends and throat-holes where the system exhales toward somewhere below the mountain. Thick as wrists, braided together in the dark in the exact places that would silt and choke first. Coming out when a siphon changes, not fleeing, patrolling, moving against the new current with a slow muscular ease, worrying the compacted grit and sponge and dead things out of the stone grilles and then, one by one, sliding home. Without them the machine strangles in a generation. With them it doesn't, whether they intend it or not.

That is a noble tenancy. Unseen maintenance. A life in the bends.

But eels patrol. Eels decide. I keep drifting — or not drifting, exactly, because nothing down here is allowed to simply drift — past the drains, past the spiral where the boom comes up through the stone into your teeth and the char make their thousands-strong ring at the precise distance, neither swept in nor willing to leave. I go a little way downstream of it, where the water leaving the centre is the finest and cleanest in the lake, full of the most thoroughly ground rain of food, and I find I have slowed down without noticing where I stopped looking for a faster life.

In the lee of a block. 

Not in the open plaza, where nothing can rest. Not in a hole, where the char already line up eye to eye. In a sheltered corner where even a flush arrives as a mere persuasion — a leaning, a soft hurry past the mouth of the shelter — tucked against the broken shoulder of one of the gauge-statues, maybe, waist-band long polished away, or under the swell that used to be a balustrade along the main flume-avenue. Where a white bacterial mat has been thickening and being eaten back and thickening again on the rust-stain where some fitting vanished centuries before I arrived.

I think I am the sponge next to it.

Mustard-grey. The size of a fist, then a little larger than a fist, because downstream of the spiral a sponge can afford to be. Porous as soft stone, attached like stone to stone, river-rounded because everything here is eventually river-rounded, even the things that are alive. I do not flatten when the street reverses. I do not hide when the sill spills somewhere east and a district begins, quietly, to empty itself into the next one lower. I do not come out in the twenty seconds of held breath to feed.

Feeding is not an event for me. It is the whole time.

I draw the water in. I let it out cleaner. That is nearly the entire description, and it takes years to learn how much is in it: pollen from a spring gust weeks ago, drowned moth, snowmelt grit, the shred of one of my own kind torn off a corner upstream and carried down a side alley by a shed vortex — I take it all in without sorting it into hope or alarm, I take in the cold that has no summer in it, I pulse almost imperceptibly, a rhythm slower than the nine-or-eleven minutes between siphons, slower than an eel's patrol or a char's whole bright life. The loaches graze my edges back. I regrow in the calm after. The mat beside me continues. We are the residents who can afford colour only if the colour is white, or rust, or the grey of mustard left in winter light.

The otters pass over me in a burst of bubbles and do not see me, or see me as stone, which amounts to grace. A crayfish goes by at midday with its claws up in the flow, tasting the future, and steps around the corner where I have made the current hesitate just enough that a mussel, downstream of me, can live in the angle of the next step down. I didn't build that step. Somebody cut a whole valley out of a mountain to make water clean itself terrace by terrace, stocked it with keepers who would never understand it, and withdrew. I am one of the keepers who understands least of all. I just keep the small place I am attached to from closing up.

When the squall crosses and the light goes almost to dusk and the districts are only looming smooth masses with the currents drawn through them in clouding snowmelt — then, in the brief stillness when a bend has not yet caught and the grit is settling and the fish hang motionless in the ovals of doorways — I am, finally, not waiting for it to end.

The pull resumes. A sill spills. The valley remembers what it was built to do.

So do I.
