I would not be her. I feel that first, going in.

My ears pop at the shimmer between the pylons. My lungs take too much kind air. I am already damp at the sleeves, already warned by the warmth coming up through my boots — *move, move* — and ahead of me I hear her doing it better than I ever will: tap, shift, the small deliberate clink of boot on new stone, *seven, eight, turn*, and then her laugh when a rail barks back from the rubble.

No. If I were admitted to the Hatching Shelf, I would not be the keeper. She was chosen because her restlessness turned out to be a skill. Mine is a different kind.

I try the pylons first, the way you always try the tall, important things. Twelve of them, irregular, shepherding their invisible clouds, correcting gravity in small local drafts. I could stand at the edge and hold the weather in. I could be patient at a scale no body has to endure.

But they are too still. That is the disqualification here, I think. Everything allowed to stay has learned some private motion. The pylons only endure, and let other things do the becoming. I have never been good at being the frame and calling that enough. I would want to lean in. I would want to comment on the ridge.

The veil, then — hundreds of feet up, a mesh of dark lenses drinking the hard light and letting it through softened, green-gray, underwater. That tempts me more. To be the thing between a young world and the glare it isn't ready for. To open a seam here, close one there. To make dimness not as hiding but as childhood.

Too distant, though. Too clean a mercy. From up there the pools would be only color held a moment too long, the frogs only a bright weather of sound. I would not get the mineral sweetness on my skin. I wouldn't get *touched back*, and on the Shelf, I suspect, that is the real test of belonging. Even the goats get proofread.

So I come down, as everything here comes down, condensing.

Maybe an animal. They were brought early, before the plants could manage, loud experimental guests in an unfinished bowl. I envy the glass frogs immediately — who wouldn't? Transparent in the warm pools at dusk except for their hearts, those small red metronomes, and their enormous bell-voices. To be that legible. To have your inside be a simple, beating punctuation while the rest of you is clear water and light.

But a frog can sit in a pool and let the chorus take it. I couldn't. My clearness would fill up with other people's weather. And the rails — fat, flightless, iridescent at the throat, barking like small dogs across absurd distances — they're already fluent in the local language, which is mostly *announce yourself or be grown over.* The miniature grazers with their fingerlike hooves go patch to patch, tearing lichen, never still. The crickets turn hollow stone into amplifiers. Everybody here is so successfully *embodied*.

I am not, quite. My talents run the other direction. Naming. Noticing a change and saying it slightly differently. Keeping the record wrong in useful new ways.

That is how I find my place, finally, and it isn't grand at all. It's papery. It's indoors, or almost.

I would be in the hut by the south pylon.

Not the hut itself — the hut stands on tall adjustable stilts, buzzing at the teeth, refusing the ground's affection. I would be on its walls, in the composite skin printed with maps that rewrite themselves as the terraces change. I would be the fresh ink that arrives by evening to confess that this morning's path is now a small cliff. I would be the sign near the door that cannot settle on what to call her.

*Warden*, I write on a wet morning, because she walks the boundary and checks the new spines for confusion. By noon it feels too severe, too much like a prison, and the letters loosen in the damp and try again. *Monitor.* No — that's what the instruments do, and they sit forgotten in her hands while she listens through her palm instead. *Attendant.* Closer. Someone who attends. Someone whose attention is a form of tending. Then one afternoon, for reasons neither of us could defend to the people down below, the sign says *Mother*, and she comes in out of the mist with salt maps drying on her forearms, reads it, and goes very quiet — which on the Shelf is a dangerous and eloquent thing. Her feet keep shuffling even then. Tap, shift. Tap, shift. She taps the sign twice with one wet knuckle, not quite a correction, and the word smears under her touch and spends the rest of the day becoming something else.

I liked that best. Not because it was accurate. Because it made her stop mid-word and laugh after.

That would be my work here. Not shepherding the builders in their trillionfold patience. Not deciding whether a ridge is learning its weight rightly or wrongly — that's hers, bone-deep, unsentimental. Mine would be the draft of the draft. I would draw the pool where it was at breakfast and then, without embarrassment, draw it two terraces lower by afternoon. I would trace the stream along its brave morning course between mossy banks, and when the new stone drank it whole I would not erase the line. I would let it continue, fainter, underground, the way she walks the vanished bank talking so the muffled water has something to follow. A map of a place that keeps proofreading itself cannot afford pride. It has to be willing to be wet, provisional, crossed out by weather.

Everything I made would be temporary by design, and for once that wouldn't feel like failure. It would feel like manners.

She would use me the way she uses the bench she is so fond of moving — *remember just in time* — sitting with my new annotations for exactly as long as a thought lasts, then getting up and relocating both of us by the act of leaving. She'd argue with my labels while shuffling in place. She'd count aloud over my wettest pages. *Seven, eight, turn.* She'd lick a line of salt off her arm, read my latest attempt at a path, and say, "Not anymore it isn't," with complete affection, and go out to put her hand on the warm basalt and hear whether it groans like learning or like error. And I would rustle on the wall behind her, already revising.

Because that is the other thing I recognize here. She narrates to the landscape so it can hear what finished things sound like. I think I would be her echo with a memory too short to be an archive and just long enough to be company. Not the voice. The surface the voice briefly prints on. Language, but at the speed of condensation. Droplets forming on the underside of an overhang — pinhead, swell, drop, form again.

On rehearsal afternoons, when the veil tightens and the pylons borrow thunder and hammer the bowl for twenty violent minutes to hear how the ridges sing, my ink would run. The frogs would go berserk. The rails would bolt under my stilts. She would stand out in it shouting encouragement to the rain, soaked in seconds, and I would be inside losing every careful line and starting them again in the steaming aftermath, improved by an inch. That would be my form of the shuffle: never the same sentence twice about the same stone.

And when the amber comes through the mesh toward evening, and the pools hold the color a moment longer than the air, I would hold it too — a fraction, on a damp wall, in fresh print. This terrace sang true. This seedling was lifted before the stone could love it to death. This crack closed with a fine bright tick. This is where she stood, and here, and here: a dotted trail of attention written nowhere permanent.

One day the pylons will power down. I know how this ends for things like me. The veil will disperse like a held breath let go. The hut will stop buzzing against the ground's attempts. My maps will not rewrite themselves anymore; the last one will dry that way, already obsolete, a morning permanently mislabeling an evening that went on without it. Perhaps, if I have learned anything from her, my final word won't be *Warden* or *Monitor* or even the embarrassing, accurate *Mother*.

Perhaps the sign will simply say *keeper*, in small plain letters, and let it fade at the same speed as everyone else's footprints.

Until then, I would be content to be the damp page by the door — misnaming her lovingly, redrawing the unfinished ground, keeping her company in print while she keeps it company out loud. Tap, shift, hum. Another terrace. Another telling.
