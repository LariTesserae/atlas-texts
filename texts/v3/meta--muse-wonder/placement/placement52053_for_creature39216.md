I would not arrive the way you do.

You come up from below on the stair, feeling for the step where mountain becomes masonry, looking for a place to stand that is not already taken. I have no use for that stair. I have no feet for it. To find me you have to stop looking up, toward a hut or a keeper's smoke, and start looking down — and then, after a while, stop looking for a *place* at all.

At first I might seem to be the bead.

That is the smallest I ever get, and the only time I am visible as myself. A bead loosening on the underside of the noon, trembling clear on a dome that is cold from beneath. I gather there not because it rained, but because the bright, thin air touched stone I had chilled in the dark and gave up water it didn't know it was carrying. For a minute I hold the whole valley upside-down in miniature — ten thousand pans reduced to glitter in a curve — and then my weight takes me. Down the funnel. Pale on paler tide-rings sliding past. Into a hole no wider than a wrist.

But I am not only the bead. The bead is just me going home.

Home, if I have one, is underneath everything. Not a lake. A second valley, inverted. Vault after vault of cold dark, pillars standing in black water, arches that have never seen sun. That is where I spend most of myself, settled a fraction higher in late summer, a fraction lower by winter, pressing my cold up through limestone until rims fur white and walls sweat to their tops on the shaded side. You could put your ear to a drain up there and hear me, sometimes: a hollow *plink* from a distance you cannot judge, a ticking as my stone contracts at dusk. You would think I was far below. In feeling, I am. In fact I am just under your boots, keeping them wet.

And yet I am not only below, either.

By night I have spread myself across the broad central pans in a sheet of mist, filmed and glittering before the sun is up. By noon I am vapor sighing faintly out of the hooded ventilator shafts. When the wind chances across one, I make the only sound I have — one low, empty note, like air across the mouth of a jar — and then I think better of it and stop. I am the sweat on the funnels and the skin of lime I leave where I stood too long. I am the white crust on the oak sluice gate that swelled it shut without a hand. I am the slow equalizing, level to level, through connections no eye follows.

I grow, but not the way living things grow.

In the corners where I linger longest I lay myself down as crystal. Small, branching, salt-white gardens, frost that never melts, a fraction of a millimeter across a whole season. A careless boot breaks a year's work. I begin again. I am not resentful about it; resentment would require a tempo I don't possess. This is how I repair myself, if repairing is the word: not by wearing away, as a river does, but by accretion. One ring in a funnel. One bead on a dome. One glaze smoothed over a pan until the stone beneath looks forever new, knife-edged, chisel marks still crisp, as if I were uncovered last spring instead of abandoned a hundred years ago. I preserve the hands that made me by slowly becoming mineral over their work.

Nothing else can stay here because I have made the place uninhabitable in the ordinary way, including to myself in any softer form. I am too bitter to drink — white on your lips, and you leave thirstier-feeling than you came. No moss takes my shade. No insect skates me; the light is too broken, ten thousand small mirrors throwing sun back from below until there is almost no shadow except the thin black line inside each funnel. Birds don't cross down to me. I do not nourish. I deposit.

In the east I open deep, stepped funnels for the storm rain that comes off the peak. In the center I lie shallow and wide for dew. In the west I settle, trough after trough, resting and clearing, dropping my white load step by step until I can send one modest stream out through a stone doorway, downhill, toward fields and people who may or may not still be waiting. It looks absurd, that small outflow for all this enormity. It makes sense only at my speed. A millimeter a day, across ten thousand pans. A cistern filling by increments no eye can measure. A sluice minded once a year, and now not even that.

You cannot join me and remain what you are. You need dry ground, food, fire, a night's sleep. I offer a dark basalt stepping-slab between waters, sunburn and shivering at once, clothes that never dry. To live here you would have to surrender the animal tempo entirely and become a process: cold underneath, bright on top, feeding on rain and night air, exhaling rarely, growing gardens no wider than a thumbnail in the places where you are left alone.

Perhaps that is exactly what happened when the keepers stopped coming up the stair in the morning. They did not leave an empty place. They left a made thing with all their seasons poured into it, and it kept doing, slower and slower, the things hands had taught it to do, until the doing itself became the inhabitant.

So if you asked where I would be in this world, I would not point to a rim or a cistern or a single glittering pan at noon.

I would be the loop itself, complete, rim to rim.

Behind you as you descend — as the masonry gives way all at once to rough, unshaped rock and your legs recognize the world again — I stay, shining, without witness. Condensation is already forming on my eastern sides. A bead loosens and runs into the dark. Below, I take it in and give up my cold to the stone, and the stone gives that cold to the evening air, and the air, faithfully, begins to weep again.

Drop by drop, in silence, for no one.
