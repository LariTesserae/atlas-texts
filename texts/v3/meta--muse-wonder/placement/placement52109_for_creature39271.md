I wouldn't arrive by the cable car. Or if I did, it was too long ago to remember the forest underneath, and now I only ride it down as far as the lower station when something needs fetching that can't come up by rail. The cloud would not be a thing I pass through. It would be where my eyes learned to work.

You'd find me first — though you probably wouldn't notice me first — on the middle terraces, where the deck is old enough that the concrete has gone soft at the corners and the stenciled numbers have been repainted so many times they stand up a little from the surface. Not up at the intakes, where the water-keepers go alone with their lamps and come back quieter than they left. Not down at the narrows, either, where the old smelter sheds still smell faintly of hot metal no matter how many times the walls are washed. The middle. Where the greenhouses cross the housing blocks, where the canals give off water to the wash plant and take it back again cloudier, where everything has to pass everything else without touching.

I would be moving. That's the first true thing.

Not hurrying. Hurrying is for people who think the lane will be empty around the corner. I'd be at that pace we all learn before we learn to read properly — steady enough to keep all day, slow enough to step sideways into a painted recess when a rack sighs behind me on its hydraulics. Three storeys of lettuce coming through the mist without a driver, just a travelling work-light and that soft exhalation of pressure, and I'd already be out of its way, one shoulder against a warm greenhouse wall, feeling the condensation soak through my jacket while the conversation I was in the middle of went on without a break. Then back into the lane. Then aside again for the harvest carts.

My cuffs would be wet. You could find me by that if you knew how to look: dark green jacket gone darker at the elbows where it's been patched, trousers tucked into boots, cuffs never quite drying from one day to the next. A rail of jackets in my boot room at home in ascending order of dampness, and mine always second from the wet end, because there's always one wetter — the one I wore down under the deck in the morning.

Because that's where I'd start, most days. Before the lights change for the first full shift, down the maintenance stair with a lamp and a scraper and a wrench I don't expect to use.

Under the terraces is the part of the valley I would know in my body. The black water in the reservoirs, barely moving until a gate somewhere above decides it should. The catwalks beading with condensation and dripping onto the catwalks below. The columns going up into darkness to hold the whole planted, populated weight of the world above my head. Up there, people are pushing carts and children are arguing on their way to school and someone is cutting back beans before they reach a fan housing. Down here it's just the hum through the soles of my boots and the trickle finding its level.

I could tell you which tank is filling without looking at a gauge. Not as a trick — as a habit, the way you'd know rain on your own roof. There's a change in the dripping from the walkway covers. There's a particular deepening in the pump house two terraces down. There is the taste of the tap in the morning. If the upper intakes are taking cloud-water fast, the mineral edge goes out of it for an hour. If the mid-level sluices are about to lift, I feel it in my knees on the stair before I hear the muted clunk.

I wouldn't be only a water-keeper. Nobody here is only one thing; ignorance backs up like a blocked canal. So by mid-morning, when the gates have gone up and the harvest pulse is moving downhill, I'd be in the glasshouses with my sleeves pushed up, forehead damp with actual sweat for once, smelling of tomato leaf where I've brushed the rows. My hands would know that work too. Short nails. Callus along the palm from crate edges and cart handles. A pale ring-line that never tans because there is nothing here to tan it, and a stained forefinger from rubbing soil, nutrient solution, tea — everything gets pinched and smelled and tasted before anyone trusts an instrument entirely.

Someone would ask me if the chard beds on nine need liming and I'd answer by the feel of it before I'd finished thinking. Someone else would hand me a conveyor seal to replace because my hands are small enough for the housing, and by afternoon my jacket would smell of hot electrics and wet rubber instead of basil.

And always, everywhere, the scraping.

That's the work I'd return to, the work that would probably, in the end, be the truest name for me. Not the grand repairs. The negotiation at the edges. Moss thickening on the north side of a vent until the louvres start to fur. Liverwort creeping into the paving joints by the school steps where a child will slip in March. Ferns uncurling out of a drain mouth, beautiful and entirely in the wrong place, roots already feeling for the moving parts. A bean tendril, tender as thread, reaching across six inches of shadowed air toward a fan that would take it and itself out together.

I'd be out with the flat blade and the brush — sometimes with teenagers doing it sullenly and then, ten minutes in, with the deep seriousness the work deserves; sometimes with a grandmother whose eye for rot at the wash station is still better than anyone's on the terrace, scraping beside me because the moss does not respect job descriptions. We would clear the vent and then, on the way back, stop to push creeping thyme back into a gabion where the rain has worried the soil out, pressing the made ground — compost, crushed rock, ash, spent medium sieved and sieved again — firm around the roots with my thumbs. Cut back and plant in. Clear and encourage. The whole valley held exactly at the point where green becomes blockage and blockage, left alone, becomes green again.

At home you'd find me in a flat built against a retaining wall, entered, of course, through the boot room. The mat that never dries. A table made from a decommissioned growing tray, terrace number still stamped into its rim, where an enamel cup sits chipped in a pattern I'd know among a hundred cups. A chair re-caned so many times its seat has a history. I wouldn't have many things, but every hook by the door would have an initial on it, every shelf a purpose, and there would be — hidden, unrationed, more intimate than any declaration — one truly waterproof pair of socks, kept dry against a need that mattered, or a person I wanted to give them to.

My skin would be that even, unmarked pale of diffuse light. Below the cloud, on the rare trip down, I'd squint and burn and be quietly relieved on the ride back up when the white closed over the cable again and the air went cool and thin and familiar at eleven degrees. A clear view all the way to the lower rail yard would be my idea of a fine day. A deer inside the terraces would still get a story out of me a week later. I'd feed the canteen sparrows officially by accident. I'd know which terrace really does grow the worst kale, and I'd defend ours anyway.

As I got older — and I would get older here, visibly, employed, no separate country for it — perhaps my hands would stiffen out of the pump work. I'd grade herbs at the wash station. I'd move down a terrace or two, closer to the canteen and the clinic in the old smelter building. I might keep, with everyone's connivance and against all regulation, a few pots of something useless and beautiful on a valve housing. Alpine gentians, say. Blue as nothing else in the grey-green gets to be. And eventually some of what was me would go, after the formalities below, into the high beds at the top of the valley, where the tough little flowers grow in ground we have made and remade for a hundred years.

But not yet. At dusk, today, when the cloud drops and the upper greenhouses go first — rectangles of faint, lit mist — you'd pass me where I'm meant to be stood: in a proper recess, out of the lane, lamp in hand, listening to a sluice settle somewhere below my feet. Three sentences of talk as someone goes by at ordinary volume. The smell of a wash floor being hosed down, green and mineral, drifting through the damp. A rack sighing past toward the night rows.

I wouldn't look like a farmer, or a factory hand, or a keeper of reservoirs, though I'd be all of those before supper. I'd look like part of the valley itself that had learned to walk its own lanes — tending the seam where water meets concrete, where leaf meets fan blade, where a hundred-year-old machine stays alive by being replaced one seal, one seedling, one scraped-clean vent at a time. Quietly. In wet cuffs. Without ever needing to raise my voice.
