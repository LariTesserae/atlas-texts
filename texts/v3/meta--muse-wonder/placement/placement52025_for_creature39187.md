I come in expecting to be the animal.

That is the mistake I bring with me through the ice. I keep my shape too tightly at first — hands, lungs, a warm core to defend, eyes turned forward looking for other eyes. I descend past the grey plates, past the ten meters where the light gives its last useful account of the world, and I keep waiting for the place to startle. For something to scatter from the kelp. For a shadow to know it has been seen and go to ground in one of the roofless houses.

Nothing does. The fronds close behind me at the speed of fronds, which is to say not yet, and then, minutes later, when I have already forgotten the push of my own passing, they finish closing.

If I stayed as I arrived I would only be a brief failure of warmth. Fifty meters down the cold is not an event, it is a condition the water chose before I was born and will not renegotiate. My fingers would go bright, then numb, then clumsy. I would kick over the meadows and leave a record in the silt for a week, a month, and then one of those slow submarine fogs would come shouldering through at a walking pace and settle a finger's thickness over my footprints, and the world would be exactly as unwitnessed as before.

So I let the animal idea go first. It is the quickest thing in me, and this place has no use for quickness.

For a while I think I would be kelp.

It is the obvious grandeur available: to stand on the old field walls with my holdfasts fisted into unmortared stone, to rise twenty meters through the permanent dusk, purple-black blades spread for a noon that surrendered long before it reached me. I like, childishly, the reach of it. The idea of having a head lost up in the darker blue. Of being the forest and the architecture at once, turning causeways into lanes and canals into corridors of swaying dark.

But the longer I stay with it the less it is about rising. A current comes through at a walking pace and my answer arrives minutes late, blade still finishing the last weather while the next one has already begun to press. Thought and movement come apart. I cannot chase the light — there is almost none worth chasing, and what there is has already been taken by the green bloom far above in summer, which will die and snow back down on me in white flecks for days. The tall ones do not rule this place. They just stand in the most exposed poverty of it, communally, shoulder to shoulder, spending years being slowly shaded out by their own bodies.

So I sink out of that ambition too, and let myself be lower.

Down into the paddies, then. Into eelgrass and stonewort, dense as turf inside the little stone enclosures where soil once waited for a crop. This is the great common body, league after league of it, stippled, furred at the wall edges, flattening under a passing current and taking an hour to lift again. There is an honesty in it I trust more. No reaching. The rows persist not because anyone tends them but because the walls persist, and because everything here inherits the exact plot it falls into. Grow until the center of you starves in your own thickness. Collapse. Rot at a geological patience in the oxygen-thin water. Root your next self directly into the unspectacular fact of your ancestors.

If I stopped here I would be content, I think. One blade among a continent of blades, keeping the shape of agriculture long after agriculture stopped meaning labor, fed by a rain I would never see fall from any sky — only the dead summer of the surface settling onto me, millimeter by millimeter, to be buried and become ground.

But I keep getting smaller, and slower, and closer to the stone.

Because underneath the meadow, underneath the kelp's grand delay, there is the work this world was actually left to do, and it is being done by the ones pressed flat.

I would be moss on a dressed block.

Not nobly. Not at the center — though I am drawn, as anyone would be, to that great paved circle at the lowest point of the basin, where the kelp refuses to grow and only a short even turf endures in the clearest, coldest water, swept by converging currents like a room someone still bothers to keep. I do not think that bare place is mine to occupy. I think I am one of the things that has learned to stop at its edge.

I would be in the double row of standing blocks around it, silt-skirted, or a little way out along a canal bank where the water is marginally less dark. Liverwort in a cushion. Moss in a cushion on a cushion. A green skin living directly on the peck-marks of a dressing hammer — those shallow, patient dimples, thousands of blows to a stone, laid down by hands that knew exactly what a straight line was and intended it to last.

That is where I finally recognize myself: taking the shape of a carved character I can no longer read.

There are inscriptions on the gate-posts and canal markers, softened past reading. I would be the softening. I would live in the grooves first, because the grooves hold the moisture and the settling silt, and then over the raised places, and then indifferently across both, until letter became suggestion and suggestion became texture and texture became just the way the stone feels under its one continuous living surface. It would look like erasure from above. From inside, at the speed I would then be living, it would feel more like a very long act of attention. To know a thing by covering it completely. To remember the builders not in what they said but in the pressure of their work against the underside of my body, year after cold year.

My senses would not be senses. Current against frond, though I would have no fronds worth the name. Sediment against leaf. The weight of my own dead accumulating ungrazed, season on season, until I was rooting through myself to reach stone again. In winter, fifty meters overhead, the ice would groan and it would arrive not as sound but as a pressure in the substance I was growing on, a tone felt in the block beneath me. Sometimes, districts away, a waterlogged beam would shift in a collapsed roof and knock once against stone, and the knock would travel a long way through the water and reach me as the only kind of news this world produces: something settled. Something gave, a fraction, toward its final flattened shape.

I would eat the way everything here eats, which is by falling down. No mouth. No hunger with direction in it. The bloom above would thicken the dark to near night and die back. White flecks would drift through the still strata and land on me and on the meadows and on the causeways until we were all briefly, faintly snowed. I would take what light remained — this blue remainder, this permanent late afternoon — and find, species by patient reduction, that it was exactly enough, because I would have given up every part of me that required more.

And I would not be alone in the way loneliness requires a witness. I would be a place in a body that is also a population: the tall ones standing on the walls above me, the low ones filling the fields to the limit of the murk, the small ones like me upholstering every stair that climbs three steps and stops at water on all sides. Rectangles within rectangles. Green inside grey. A garden that was planted for drowning and forgot its planters so thoroughly that the forgetting became a form of fidelity — still growing in their rows, still dividing itself into their fields, still keeping, for reasons of current or chemistry or some reverence learned in the root, one cold circle swept almost clear at the heart of it.

If you came down through the ice looking for me, you would not find an eye to find you back. You would brush algae off a stone on a canal bank and feel, under your numb fingertips, the dimpled blows of a hammer, and the cushion living in them, denser where the characters were.

It would close behind you at the speed of plants.

And stay.
