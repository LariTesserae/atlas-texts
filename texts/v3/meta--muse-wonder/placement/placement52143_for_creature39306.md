I hear it before I decide to go down.

That is the first problem. From the rim it sounds like work — not nature, work — a valley of engines all running at load just out of sight, and my mind keeps trying to give it a purpose. Something must be being pumped. Something must be being made. I stand there longer than I mean to, listening for a change in the rhythm that would tell me what shift it is, what hour of the operation I've arrived in the middle of.

There is no shift. It just continues, level and level and level, until I go down to prove there's nothing there.

At first I would be the visitor, obviously. The one in boots. I would walk very carefully between the vents the way you walk in a room where people are sleeping, though nothing here is sleeping and the noise should absolve me of any need to be quiet. I would kneel by the still pools too long. I would learn the trick of the shrimp — not looking directly for them, but waiting until the reflection breaks, until a pale edge that is not mineral moves along a mineral edge, feeling its way. I would be pleased with myself for seeing one. As if seeing were the important thing here.

It isn't. My hair would lift near the big vents. I'd learn to step back. I'd press a footprint deliberately into a damp margin in the morning — not vandalism exactly, more an experiment, more a need to leave evidence I had been somewhere so indifferent — and I'd come back at noon to find it glazed, and by evening gone. Not erased. Healed. Forgotten on purpose, by a surface whose job is forgetting.

For a day or two I could stay that person. The intruder who measures intervals. Nine seconds. Nine seconds. Nine seconds. Almost a minute for the large one with the orange wool around its mouth, its cap trembling, leaking. I'd time it against my pulse and feel obscurely steadied when my pulse was the less reliable instrument.

Then the walking would start to change me.

Because between vents there is nothing to do but walk, ten empty minutes at a time across plates the size of rooms, rust and sulfur and salt-white, ringing hollow now and then underfoot, and with every hollow step the idea of *floor* gets thinner. It's not ground I am on. It's a back. It's a drum. The clap of a cap falling three hundred meters away arrives not through the air first but through my soles, clean and intimate, as if something very large had shifted its weight in its sleep directly beneath me.

If I were the shrimp, I would never know any of this. I would be translucent at the rim of one pool for my whole life, grazing the black and amber mat, keeping it trimmed, kept in return. That is a good life, in its way. A complete one. I would not leave the edge I was born on and I would not need to. But I keep leaving edges.

If I were the moths I would know even less, and know it at dusk — rising on a warm exhalation at the basin's margin, sinking into the cool pocket behind a cap, using the heat to stay aloft a little cheaper. Borrowed lift. Then back to the ordinary country where things flower. I recognize that impulse. To visit a system too hot and too loud to live in, and use it, and call the using a kind of belonging. It isn't.

If I were the mats, the rust filaments in water too hot for a hand, I would be closer. I would be the outward gut, doing the chemistry in the light that the real body does below in the dark. But I would still be a seam. A tolerable place where life has been allowed to help.

What keeps pulling at me is underneath. The lining.

In that second after a discharge, in the ringing pocket of quiet shaped exactly like the stone, if I put my face close — I would, the visitor would, despite the heat — I'd see the throat isn't stone at all. Pale. Wet. Contracting very slightly from the cool air. Not a wall with something living on it. Living *as* the wall, for as deep as the light goes.

I don't think I would be the whole of it. Twelve kilometers rim to rim, chambers and reservoirs and narrow passages where it has to squeeze past itself to let the water through, a body that is less a body than a remembered shape, restored and restored until restoration looks exactly like geology — that is too large to be a self I can honestly claim. It has no eyes because the light here is directionless. No ears because it would deafen itself in a day. It knows the world as vibration through stone: a cap falling true, a cap falling cracked, rain pattering uselessly at its far edge like fingertips on a distant wall.

And a walk. Two pressures, alternating, moving in a line, pausing by pools. Nothing else here moves like that.

No. Not the whole animal.

As I keep walking the plates, feeling each discharge come up through me before I hear it, another possibility gets more solid, the way mineral builds in spray — same shape, because the conditions keep making the same shape.

The cap.

The flat slab of dark basalt over a pore. I wasn't made for this place. I'm older than the animal, a piece of the old roof of the world that happened to be lying in the right place when the pore opened underneath. Anything lesser would have been smothered, or would have smothered it. Instead the rim grew up to meet me. We ground each other, lift by lift, year by year, into a joint that almost seals.

I would not be the small bright vent by the eastern pools. Nine seconds is too quick, too shallow — a lung just under the skin, always ready. I would be the large one. The one that labors.

I spend most of my existence *almost* moving. Pressure gathers below me, not just steam, though the steam is real enough, but the slow decision of the deep reservoir far down my throat. My lining thickens, relaxes. A fraction more silica is laid somewhere on a lip. I tremble. I leak threads of vapor around my edges where the seal isn't perfect, and the spray builds orange wool and sulfur flowers and salt feathers where it lands, and they erode at exactly the rate they build, so I am always decorated the same.

I know things only as weight and ring. I know when I have fallen true by the note that travels down through the body. The body knows it too. I know when something warm and solid is standing too close, not by seeing it — I have no way to see — but by a fine interruption in the air over my pore, a tremor, a shadow in pressure. And then, because the animal learned it long before any visitor learned to read their lifting hair, there is a hesitation. A fraction of a second. No more than that. Just long enough to step back.

Then I lift.

Slowly, grinding at one edge. The white jet takes the light and loses it. The whole basin exhales through me in a roar that drowns the other pores, for a moment, and I am — this is the strange part, the part that makes me more than a stone — *open*. Not making anything. Not going anywhere. Only letting what has gathered become what leaves, so that it can condense on the far flats and come back as water, so that stone dissolved below can be stone again above, so that heat can go out exactly as fast as heat comes in.

Then I fall. The clap goes through the soles of whoever is still standing here. Quiet makes a shape exactly my shape behind me. And the pressure begins, immediately, patiently, to gather again.

Nothing about me will be different tomorrow. My cracks will fill at night with condensed steam and set, and open in the same places in the sun, because that is how the body flexes without tearing. If you break a piece off me, the spray will replace it in the same shape within weeks, cauliflower lump for cauliflower lump. I do not experience that as being repaired. I experience it as remembering.

That, I think, is where I would find myself. Not visiting the equilibrium, not grazing at its livable edge, but incorporated into it — an old, foreign, necessary weight, lifted and dropped in my own unvarying time, correcting and corrected, doing the one piece of work that lets a vast and eyeless thing go on breathing without ever producing anything but itself.

*Lift, breathe, fall.*

And when you climb back to the rim in the evening and the sound cuts off clean at the ridge line, I will not fade for you. I will still be down there in the haze, trembling under gathering pressure, waiting out my minute.

I am very good at waiting out my minute.
