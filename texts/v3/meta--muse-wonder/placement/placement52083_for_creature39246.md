I would enter the way anyone does, too fast and too warm.

That is the first shame of it. My pulse would arrive before I did. Squeezing through the slit, ducking under the basalt lip, bringing in alpine wind in my clothes and bright air in my lungs, and then — the hollow closing over all of that noise. My knees making prints in the moss. My breath refusing to disperse. Everything in there taking its minute to withdraw one foot, and me standing in the middle of that slowness feeling obscenely eventful.

I don't think I could be her. Not at first, not honestly.

She is tempting, of course. Lying at the shallow edge of the pool with limpets fixed to her in gray rosettes, moss rooting along her spine, her heart visible and pausing in her throat — eleven seconds, eleven seconds — as if time had finally been reduced to the only interval that mattered. To be that finished. To have given up extravagances one by one. To know a beetle crossing the ceiling by the tremor it sends through rock and peat and the rootlets entering your skin. There is a peace in imagining that, but it would be a lie to claim it. My thoughts do not move at the speed of calcite. They crowd. They arrive hot and brief, like the visitor she will register next year, or the year after, turning her head a fraction toward a warmth that is already gone.

I'm not finished changing. I'm barely begun.

So I look lower, and smaller, the way the hollow teaches you to look.

At first I think: a moth on the wall. One of thousands, folded into the tiling, gray or bone-white, overlapping my neighbors like shingles. My wing-dust beaded with water. No song, no flight to speak of — just the occasional adjustment, a fraction of a wing-width, and the soft sub-audible rustle when hundreds of us resettle at once. The individual moth is brief. The pattern of moths is not. I like that disproportion. To be replaceable and still be part of the surface the rock wears. To have my brief occupancy matter only insofar as the wall remains covered.

But even a moth eventually loosens. Its weight of beaded water pulls it down toward her open mouth, toward the beetles, toward the white lace of fungal thread. And I'd fall, as everything falls there, without violence. Met before I could settle.

Maybe a salamander, then. Black and gold-flecked under a fern frond, skin beaded with the same condensation as the leaves, eyes filmed. Taking an hour to climb into the hollow beneath her jaw for winter. Deciding, over a season, to stay. That's closer — the philosophy of slowness practiced at animal scale, one deliberate foot at a time.

Or one of the eyeless fish, no longer than a thumbnail, the color of diluted milk, hanging motionless above the silt. Facing the slow current that is not a current. Replaced, in some ancestral sense, by another fish in the same dark water. There is a kind of purity in that suspension. Nothing to build, nothing to carry.

But the fish hold their place. The salamanders hold theirs. The hollow has finished arranging them, and I — I am still assembling myself out of whatever falls within reach.

So I sink, finally, to the bottom of the pool, where I suspect I belonged all along.

A caddis larva.

Low in the cold and the silt, dragging myself along the submerged rock in a case I have made and am still making. Sand grains. Black grit. Fragments of shell. Bits of whatever the hollow sheds and lets fall — bound with silk from my own mouth, patiently, one addition at a time. This is the only construction here, older than hands, and it suits me: to live inside a portable arrangement of borrowed pieces, each one chosen not because it was mine but because it fit the gap. Because it would hold.

Caddis larvae on the floor of that pool have incorporated shed fragments of her nails into their cases — curved, translucent plates like snail shell — so that she is slowly distributed across the floor in other creatures' architecture. That is the detail I can't leave alone. To carry a piece of her, without being her. To be upholstered, over time, into the place I live in, and to do some of the upholstering myself.

I would be down near her open hand, where her four long fingers rest on the bottom, webbed to the second joint, nails grown out into shell. Shrimp moving upside-down white as peeled roots above me. The living film closing over the surface far overhead, absorbing each drip so completely that the water where I live never ripples. Eleven seconds. Eleven seconds. My own small heart learning the interval, not all at once — that would be another extravagance — but the way everything there learns: by being rained on, slowly, until the rhythm is inside the stone.

I would graze. I would drag. I would add a grain when the case wore thin, and refuse a grain, sometimes, for most of a season, because the cold teaches you that not every improvement is necessary. When I shed, or when the case was finally outgrown, beetles would meet what I left before it settled, and fungal thread would lace it white, and it would become peat, moss, her again — a fragment of a fragment, still in circulation.

I am not finished, like she is. That's the difference, and the hollow would know it, and make room anyway. It makes room for everything that agrees to slow down. A spore finds the exact damp ledge its parent occupied; perhaps, once in a decade, a larva finds the exact curve of a discarded nail plate and understands, in whatever way silk and mouth understand, that this piece has been waiting.

So find me at the bottom of the night laid flat, in the shallowest dark beside her fingers, carrying my small assembled house through water that never rises or falls by the width of a limpet. Moving, if I move, in slow circles, like the limpets above the waterline's other kingdom. Building nothing that will last — only something that will be taken apart correctly when I'm done with it.

And if you stayed long enough, longer than feels reasonable, soaked through without any rain having fallen, you might see my case shift by one grain's breadth toward the warmth you disturbed.

Sometime after you've gone back out into the bright alpine wind, I will finish that movement.

I will have registered you.

I will add you — one bright, brief, sand-colored fragment — to the case, and drag on.
