I wouldn't start on the summit. That's the human place to start — up where you can see a hundred miles and understand nothing because your face is freezing off.

I would start lower. Down where the rule changes at knee-height.

At first, arriving there, I think I'd try to be something that flies. It makes sense under that white, enormous sun. I'd be one of the blind sail-kites over the smelter herds, all ribbed membrane and trailing nerve, turning a slow propeller in a thermal that never ends, making current just by turning. Never landing. Never touching the mat. Eating moth-clouds as they rise sparking off the backs below.

It's a beautiful idea and it would kill me with loneliness.

Because nothing up there is alone by choice, only by altitude. The kite only survives because the herd below is making the warm air it lives in, and the moths below are making the food, and the lichen below is making the charge that crawls along its nerves. If I stayed up there too long I'd start to understand that I wasn't the animal. I was just the part of the valley that had gotten temporarily airborne.

So I come down. In spirals. Through layers of wing and spark and shadow, through my own weather.

Lower, I'd try the ridge. I'd be a gape-mouth, maybe — that at least is honest work. Head split wide into the katabatic wind, fan turning in my throat from birth to death, grinding spores and ice and aerial plankton into paste, my whole blood supply cranked around by the wind I eat. In a line of ten thousand, vane to vane, humming. Every so often clearing my throat in a blast of my own snow.

Closer. But still not it. The gape-mouth faces outward. It trusts the wind. I don't think I'd trust anything that arrives for free here, not light, not wind. Heat is only real in this place when something has made it and something else is holding it in.

So down again, into the traffic in the valley. Into the knee-high fog where it's improbably tropical around your boots. Flank to flank with the smelters.

And there — not as one of them, exactly. That still isn't quite the shape of it.

I would be in her.

Not her eyes, low and silica-browed, measuring glare. Not her six legs, four pillars and two steering rudders dragging behind. Not even her piston-heart, though I would live by it: *thud* — a shiver all the way to the soles — then the long pause while the pressure builds, the hiss in the neck valves, the steam that becomes snow before it falls.

I would be the red that she is plumbed around.

The culture in the long retort of her gut. The bacterial furnace that holds, hour after hour, at a dull red heat that would cook any ordinary animal alive, while her molars turn above me in their four-hour round — take in feather-lichen and iron scree and black grit, crush, hold, crush again — fuel and ore together. I would taste copper separating from stone without ever having a tongue. I would know nickel as a change in the heat. I would keep the meltwater channels under her skin at temperature, send warmth out in timed surges to the frost-rimmed edges of her feet, her ears, the seams between her plates where the furnace-moss grows out of her and glows at night and harbors small blind groomers who are, in their own way, also me — not the same body, but the same arrangement of dependencies.

Because she was never only one, and so I would never be only in her.

I was fed to her mouth-to-mouth before she had a furnace of her own, a wet slurry of starter passed from three elders, none exactly her parent. I was already old then. I was in her sister of the same ingot, whose furnace runs pitched a note lower; when they press together for days, flank to flank, making a moving wall of heat no one of them could afford alone, their hearts synchronize — and I feel that from the inside, my red pulsing slower, steadier, two herds of me briefly agreeing on a rhythm. I will be in the young clustered at the warm hollow where she voids an ingot — copper, almost pure, ringing as it falls into the mat, steaming for days. Some calf extruded there in its wet mineral sac will take me in, keep warm against the cooling metal, rotate through the long night at the outer edge of the sibling cluster so no one freezes in place, and one morning feel that first dull ache in its center. Not a light switched on. Heat, made. Mine. Ours. Its contract signed: now you must never fully stop.

That's the life. Not sleeping, idling through the months-long night with vents narrowed to blue slits, listening — not with ears, with chemistry, with temperature gradients — to new cracks sighing open somewhere out in the dark plateau, letting out one hot mineral breath. Knowing the herd will turn toward it, eventually. Knowing it may take a season to get there and that this is not slowness, this is the only speed at which anything here can afford to move. Grazing the lichen forests to a bright nub by day. Feeling the glass-toothed skaters test the seams of the herd in the dark, feeling one cook against a plate on her third leg and mineralize into a white scar that I will carry, in a sense, without ever seeing it. Keeping the young to the middle. Keeping myself to the middle. Rotating the warm core.

And perceiving the way this world permits perception: not trusting brightness, because the light here has been stripped of everything but visibility. Trusting vibration instead — the ticking of the filtering choir on the ridge above, the thud traveling body to body through the herd so the sound never stops, only relocates. Trusting heat only where it has been manufactured and shared.

In the end I would die the way I lived: distributed, and out of phase with any single death. A host will slow her chewing past the point where I can hold the red. Her piston will lengthen its pauses. I will cool in her first — and by then I will already be elsewhere, in a flank pressed against hers, in a calf by an ingot, in dung steaming in a footprint already green with seedlings. The smaller things will mine her cooling plates while she's still standing. I won't resent that. Resisting it would waste temperature. I will be the reason she stays warm long enough to be worth mining, and then I will be in the things that come to take her apart, warming their small guts as they bore for the nickel in her bone.

Look for me from the summit and you won't find me. Look closer — in the seam, in the glow between plates at night, in the pause between one *thud* and the next, in the ingot still warm days after the animal that made it has moved a valley away.

I'd be the part of the Foundry that learned to keep itself lit, and then learned — which is harder, and is the whole lesson of the place — to never keep that light in only one body.
