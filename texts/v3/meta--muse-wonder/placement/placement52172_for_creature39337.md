I don't think I would arrive there, the way a visitor arrives — gasping up the last notch in the road with the wool strips snapping on the cairns, trying to make my breath do in days what everyone else's has learned to do from birth. I would already be there. I would start, the way everyone starts there, inside someone else's warmth.

Inside my mother's coats, first. Skin to skin through my first winter, in the room above the stable, listening through her ribs to a breath slower and fuller than a lowlander's — drawn down, held a fraction, sieved. Below us the cattle shifting and breathing the ceiling warm. Above us my grandmother, my father, a married brother and his wife behind a curtain, all breathing almost in unison in the dark, while the frost feathered across the inside of the shutters. That would be my first country: not the plateau yet, not the white distances, but a stacked, shared heat that must not be wasted.

Then a bucket. Small enough for small hands.

That is how the plateau would give me to myself, morning by morning. Bare feet protesting on cold stone — everyone complains about the cold there, constantly, inventively, it is a way of loving anyone who has to go out in it — scraping the night's frost from the inside of the outer wall before the sun could steal it. From the ropes. From the door's inner face. Best of all from the broad, patient backs of the cattle in the byre, standing steaming and accustomed while a child works along their spines with a wooden scraper, learning not to spill. What a household of nine and a byre of four needs to drink and wash and cook, drop by drop. Learning gentleness from being corrected about waste, and waste from watching how little there ever is.

I would grow compact, as we do. Short legs for the stepped alleys. A chest that deepens whether I will it or not, ribs flaring, until my breath has that unhurried plateau rhythm and I stop noticing it except when a trader from below the rim tries to talk and haul stone at the same time and is sick and ashamed by mid-morning. Broad shoulders. Hands too large, reddened across the backs with that dense net of vessels we all carry, chapped and healed around the mouth from smiling into wind.

My hair would tell people things before I spoke — and we do not spend speech carelessly; talking is breathing spent on purpose. Braided tight and oiled, wound with cloth in my town's grey and brown, then the knot changed when I married, changed again when the marriage house began to build. Because I would not live alone, of course. No one with sense does. A solitary house bleeds heat to the wind. I would live wall to wall, curtain to curtain, mastering that elaborate courtesy of not hearing what you plainly heard through felt and floorboards, conducting any necessary quarrel in whispers over three nights because there is nowhere to storm off to that won't kill you by morning.

Some years I would be at the loom — every third house has one, and its tick is a house-sound, like the pigeons shuffling in the eaves. Weaving cloth dense to the point of cruelty, cloth that stands up by itself and sheds wind the way slate sheds melt. Some seasons I would be handing slate up to a roof, in the short spells the air allows: heave, set, lean on the haft, breathe, talk a little, heave again. A wall a season. A room when a marriage requires it, bridged over an alley we also own half of. My father cut the first stones for a room he never slept in. I would understand, without anyone explaining it, that I might do the same, and that this is not sadness. It is the normal span of ambition. A house is a fifteen-year argument in sharp, unmortared stone, and my great-grandchildren being warm inside it is a perfectly reasonable thing to work for.

The herds would interrupt my marketing and I would not call it interruption. I would flow up onto the market steps with everyone else, still bargaining over salted, dried cheese hard as stone and black barley for the grinding, while a hundred steaming backs filled the street wall to wall below us, horns clashing, calves calling, the lead ox's felt-muffled bell going past at the height of my knees. The great cattle have right of way by law older than the streets. By then I would keep animals of my own close, as everyone does: sheep in the courtyard pen, a cat in the granary, a dog thick as a bear asleep across our threshold whom everyone steps over without waking. A chicken coop warmed by the kitchen wall, laying thin-shelled eggs that are less food than news — someone recovering, someone being generous, someone worth visiting.

And I think — letting it settle now, letting the years do to me what the altitude does — I would end where the old end up, and it would not be an ending of usefulness. I would become the one who minds what is scarcest and slowest. Who no longer hauls stone but minds the cistern and the children with their small buckets, teaching them where the frost forms *between* the double walls so it can be harvested clean in the morning. Who remembers which year the spring failed and produced no collar of green moss and white flowers, which cousin owes what, which week to open the granary mound and — more important — to shut it again with something still inside. Whose heart has enlarged in both senses, as ours do: the literal one the thin air demands, and the social one, because in a house stacked that close a person who remembers is a kind of stored warmth too.

My husband's beard would frost white by breakfast in winter and I would crack the ice gently off him indoors, like shelling an egg. At weddings in roofed courtyards I would weep with the cattle audible below. I would hoard and shamelessly display small luxuries: a bronze bell with a clear note, a piece of real wood smelling of forests I will never see, southern salt, an egg. I would grease my hands with rendered fat against the worst months and smell of dung-smoke and wool grease and boiled grain, and know that smell as home.

At night, when the towns go dark early because fat is not to waste, I would sometimes stand in the market court among thousands and do what we consider a rich person's pastime. Stand in that quiet that cold, dense air makes — where people speak low and close and snow swallows what noise there is — and listen. Past the pigeons settling. Past a dog deciding briefly to announce itself two streets over. To the drop-by-drop ticking of meltwater falling into a cistern, two courtyards away.

I would be someone who has never had much water, or air, or wood, or warmth she did not have to keep. And who has therefore never once been alone. Wall to wall, breath to breath, stone laid on stone in full expectation of being, a hundred years from now, the reason someone else's frost gathers on the inside of a warm door — and is carried, carefully, in a small bucket, to where it is needed.
