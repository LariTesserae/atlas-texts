I wouldn't come down the ladder the way the hand does.

I would arrive the way everything real arrives there — accidentally, on someone else. On the shoulder of a jacket. In the damp of a glove the galvanized rungs have just stripped. Most likely in a breath, held a second too long while someone bends to breathe the frost off a gauge face so they can read it.

For a moment I'd still think I was part of that person. Warm. Intended for somewhere. Then the compressors kick, the lamps dim that fraction, and the room begins its eleven minutes, and I would learn the first rule by having it applied to me: nothing warm stays owned for long down there.

I would be taken.

Not dramatically. The cold would simply get more persuasive than the skin I came in on. I'd let go and be air again, invisible, and the humidity needle would lift a hair because of me — the smallest possible misconduct. Speak little in that place, stay no more than two cycles, because people leak. I leak. I am the leak.

After that I stop being a visitor at all.

The room has routes for me that I would learn by being moved along them. First the coldest pipe, because that is where loose things gather. A bright thickening. A bead forming among other beads until we are too heavy to be only vapor and not yet heavy enough to fall. And then — this is the part I would keep returning to, turning around in the small room three paces by four to understand it — I wouldn't fall.

Along the charged collector wires I would go up. Sideways. Bead over bead, bright and obedient, against what I thought was a law, toward a drip point chosen by electricity rather than gravity. The factory above is still thudding faintly through steel somewhere, but it has nothing to do with this. Up there, things are made at scale and in straight lines. Down here the scale is a droplet hitting a sump pan sounding enormous in the off-cycle silence, and I move in the wrong direction and it is exactly right.

Sometimes I wouldn't make it before the cold deepens.

I'd freeze mid-tick. Clear needle. Held. The frost is the quick tissue of this place and I would be part of how fast it can prove itself — ferns on coil fins, white fur on a screw head, a soft white outline that makes the unfinished wall readable. Pencil lines underneath. Measurements in marker. I would sit on a fastener that has only just been fired and already I am covering it, making the new thing look Briefly, beautifully older than it is: not worn, never worn — but coated, claimed.

Then nine minutes end and eleven begin again, or the other way round; after a while you stop counting the way the clipboard does, in its zip bag, in handwriting getting shakier. You count in pulses. Hum in the teeth. Sigh. Tick of a duct contracting. And in the defrost, all at once, not gracefully, I'd let go with the rest of the held rain. Indoor rain. Rain in a room that has never had a sky, pattering through steel grating into the shallow pan below the floor, where I would be dark for a while, gathered, and pumped through three ceramic canisters and offered back again.

Offered is the right word. It's a liturgy if you're small enough.

I might be misted over the moss then, and darken it. I might settle on a blade of dwarf rye no taller than a finger and make it stiffen as the cycle turns, with a sound below hearing. I might sit on a spruce seedling — a green asterisk in shredded mineral wool, growing so slowly it seems like a model of a tree — and be there for what feels like its whole childhood, which is to say one afternoon. The plants don't lean to the light, and neither would I. Light down there is only amber work-lamps and green LEDs kept low to save heat. We all lean, if we lean at all, toward the compressors in their off minutes, toward the waste warmth pulsing off enameled metal on rubber feet. Heat is the rare season. You learn allegiance to a season.

At first I thought I might be the plant. I wanted the green story: tolerated, tested, asked *can you live like this?* But the plants are questions the resident is asking itself. I am closer to the grammar it's asking them in.

Or I thought I might be the riveter behind the plastic sheeting — moving an inch along its rail, waiting a long while as if listening, then *thock*, one fastener in the frame of the second, smaller chamber. I love its patience. I love that it builds mostly when no one is there to fog the sensors. But the riveter has a task and a direction. I have a circulation. I will be on the other side of that plastic eventually, beading on a pipe that hasn't been plumbed yet, frosting a screw the riveter hasn't fired. The room is building its next version, and I am one of the materials it has quietly decided to keep for it.

No earth. No rot. No mice, no spiders, no flies in the trays. Corners still corners, blue film still on the panels, nothing stained or settled. And yet I would get older there in the only way the place allows: by going round. Exhaled, stolen, beaded, risen, frozen, rained, filtered, misted. Never leaving through the hatch when it opens — or only a fraction of me leaving, because the hatch is brief and the room is good at taking back.

That's how I would know I belonged to the being and not just the building. A finished cold store would use me up or throw me out. This thing is still learning how to be a winter, and it is learning partly by rehearsing me: how to make weather without a sky, how to drink without a source, how to hold a difference between inside and outside by growing a white coat faster than a green one.

So find me between cycles. In the sigh when the compressors rest and the whole pit ticks and lets go. On the wire, travelling up. On the gauge face someone will have to clear with their own breath to read, adding more of us to the system even as they try to see clearly. In the second chamber, before it knows whether it is a lung or a nursery or a child, where the plastic billows faintly with a pressure change —

and something very new, and very cold, turns over in its sleep, and I turn with it.
