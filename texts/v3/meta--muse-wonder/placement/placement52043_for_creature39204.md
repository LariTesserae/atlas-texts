I think I would come down from the ridge slowly.

Not because of the view — though I would stop there, the way everyone does, looking for a town and finding only that unbroken green breathing in the bowl — but because I suspect I'd be looking, at first, for where I was supposed to fit. Up at the Top End would be the obvious place. It's drier there. The school, the clerks, the Co-op, the rim railway bringing in tea and tinned things and letters from places where the ground holds still. I understand paper. I understand stores and timetables.

I wouldn't stay at the Top End.

I'd be drawn down the boardwalk, I think. Ducking my head at first in the wrong places, and not ducking where I should. Tripping at ankle-height over root-knots that everyone else steps over without breaking conversation. Learning the particular humiliation of Sallowmere — not falling, exactly, anyone can fall, but apologising too loudly afterward, and hearing your voice just stop, taken by the leaves, three metres out.

I'd want to be in the Press House for a while, too. Who wouldn't? It's the only tall thing, the only allowed fire, standing on the one true knob of rock in the centre of all that negotiation. The thump of it comes up through the soles of your boots before it reaches your ears, and that feels like a heartbeat you could trust. I would like loading the trolleys, weighing the nets of cut leaf, watching the sap run off to be boiled into that terrible preservative.

But the Press House only exists because of the cutting, and the cutting is everywhere, and after a while I think the centre would start to feel too fixed to me. Too much like rock. 

I'd drift to the Terraces.

Not a born Sallowmerer — I'd be one of the ones who came in from outside and stayed, and therefore had to be taught, late and a bit self-consciously, what the children in the school crocodile already know in their feet. Which whitewashed, numbered thickness is an Arterial and must never be nicked. Which pale feeder can be trimmed before breakfast. Why you never leave a door unlatched, and that it has nothing to do with thieves. How to listen through the boardwalk while pretending perfectly convincingly that you are only walking to the shop for bootlaces.

My house would be in the second curving row, following the old arterial everyone calls Nine, though Nine has daughters now that weren't on the map when the house raft was laid. Stone would be too heavy for me; I'd live in pressed motherleaf board, grey-green, bitter-smelling when I saw at it to fit a new shelf, filmed with moss a month after any repair. The roof would have ferns in the gutter that I solemnly defend to visitors as drainage. I would acquire extra steps at the front door one year and lose them the next, and I'd keep my coats on a peg I have to move a thumb's width every winter because the whole house is travelling, very slowly, sideways, toward a little more morning light. 

At night I'd sleep through the ticking in the pipes and the creak travelling through the raft under the bed. That's how I'd know I'd settled, actually — the first night I came from outside I'd lie awake listening to the town grow by a millimetre underneath me. A year in, I'd wake only if it stopped.

My work would not be one work. Nobody's is, here. On paper I'd be the meter-reader — the one who goes out between six and nine, in the bowl's own rain, tapping pipes and reading dials while the leaf-cups tremble and empty overhead. I have the hat for it now, broad-brimmed with its little evolved spout, and boots expected to come up dark. I can walk the middle of the boardwalk light-footed, feel a trolley coming before its bell rings once at the crossing, tell by a sigh at a tap whether someone three streets away has nicked something they shouldn't.

But meter-reading is only the excuse to be out early, looking at where things have got to since yesterday. So in the afternoons I'd owe my cutting hours like everyone else — forearms mapped in fine pale scratches, nails stained dark at the quick with sap that soap will lighten and never cure — and in the winter, when all the leaves are down and the town stands startled in its own lamplight among pale stubble, I'd be found more and more often in the town hall, at the great root-map.

Not in charge of the ink. The ink is for the old arteries, the ones smooth and settled for a hundred and fifty years. I'd be one of the pencil people. The ones who argue, amicably and at length, about where Nine's new daughter actually ran this year, about what shifted under Mrs. Ellery's raft in August, about whether that curling fist by the boardwalk will bow the boards by Thursday or can be left till Monday. My pencil line would be erased and redrawn more than most, because I am still learning to draw what my boots already know. I would consider that an honest description of a life, not just of cartography.

I would eat potatoes from a boxed planter on legs, grown in clean imported soil safe from the sap — and be fierce, as we all are, about tea, because tea tastes of somewhere dry and un-moving. I would hang washing that takes two days to dry and comes in smelling green, and peg it one-handed while pushing a new leaf away from the line with the other, without noticing I had done it. I'd know a heron in a glade is news worth putting in the shop window. I'd feel, obscurely, in my chest in August when the moths come for their one uncountable week at the amber lamps — an excitement no one organised and no one needs to explain.

And I would not be a pioneer, and I would not be a custodian. I'd correct visitors, gently, on the rim road if they used either word. We are maintenance people. We leave slack in cables and a hand's breadth between houses and a certain sparseness in our friendships, because everything here needs room to be cut back, and to grow again differently. I would be, in the end, a neighbour kept at the proper Sallowmere distance — far enough apart not to crowd, intergrown underneath where nobody sees.

What would I make? Board, pellets, paper stock, a preservative no insect will touch. Those go out on the railway. But mostly, tomorrow morning, at ten centimetres a day, with a hook on my back and a pencil behind my ear:

The clearing I stand in.
