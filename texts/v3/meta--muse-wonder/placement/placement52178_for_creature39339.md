I wouldn't be found standing.

That's the first thing that goes, even in imagining it. No silhouette. No figure against — there is no against to be figured on. The horizon won't hold me. The water would show me twice and both times wrong.

You'd find me by a wrongness in the water.

A place, ankle-deep, where the sheet isn't traveling quite with the wind. Where it hesitates around something low, spreads, rejoins with a faint seam. Where the algae film has been peeled back in broad, splayed prints, dinner-plate wide, that fill as soon as they are made — first with a rush, then with that slow, obscene patience, until you are not sure you saw a print at all.

Look for that. Look low.

I am at the edge of a berm in the hour when dawn has been happening for three hours and has not decided to finish. The spiral is fresh on its inner curve — you can smell the good clay, sweet under the rot — and I am on the wet side of it, not quite in its shadow. If I go any deeper into the lee the roar drops out from under me and my thoughts get obscenely loud and my sealed ears ring in the empty, and if I go any farther out the pressure gets into my sinuses and makes them sing the high note that isn't for me. I live on that gradient. Half-sheltered. Skull half in, half out.

One palm is down. My forehead has been down so long the mud has taken a perfect impression of it, and when I lift — an inch, only enough to let the water clear my eye-slits — the impression stays a moment, watching me back. The other palm is not mine at the moment. It belongs to listening. Fingers spread ten yards of attention wide through the sole of the hand, feeling for what the crust is saying in the register that matters.

Because I hear almost nothing up here, and that is a mercy I was born into. Where you'd have ears I have smooth plates, bone bulking a little under wet-leather skin, sealed before birth in the lee-water while hands kept me under a breath too long. The storm can scream itself into standing ribs in the mist, rain can hang and not fall, lightning can think violet and sideways through the fog for minutes at a time — it goes past me like weather on the other side of a wall. What I get is the underneath of it. The drumhead flexing. The deep water shifting in its caverns and sending up one enormous, slow syllable that takes half a morning to arrive in my teeth.

My whole skull aches with it, constantly, pleasantly, the way a filled lung aches. I snort warm mud-water up through my sinuses to clear them and taste iron, then salt, then iron again — the ghost-mountains down there in stripes, telling me where on the continent I am without anything so vulgar as a landmark. We are over a salty band this season. The blue algae will be good. Last season it tasted of meat going off, and we rotated the gardens.

I say gardens. Finger-tip ridges in the lee, no higher than your knuckle is tall, demarcating shallows where the films can bloom without a passing boom killing acres at once. I tend them by not tending them too hard. Skimming with the edge of my hand. Laying a reed bundle to break a rhythm that has gotten destructive. Eating the peel off my own back when I wake, green and rust-red where I have slept settled into the surface and let the pan film me over. I do not sit to rest. I settle. I distribute. I become a slight rise the water learns to go around, and for a while my spine — widened, not lengthened, hoops of rib flared under me — is furniture the pressure can slide over without picking up a tune it might keep.

That is occupation enough, and it is not, I am noticing as you watch, quite the whole of what I am here to do.

Beside me, just outside the spiral where the roar resumes at full occupancy, something is keeping time with feet that have stopped moving.

It has no feet. That is how I know whose it is.

When I walked in three days ago — on all fours when the shear picked up, palms down beside my splayed feet, toes spreading and persuading the crust rather than punching it, reading brine-sigh and storm-tightening through the tuned soles I would be blinded without — it fell in beside me the way these things do. At first just my own footsteps, amplified, organized by the perfect reflector running under a continent of ankle-water. A quadruped ripple trotting at exactly my pace, made of standing water and vibration. A ridge that holds like a spine. A hollow that turns, sometimes, like a head, as if curious about the reed-bed that isn't there yet.

Everyone walking the pan acquires that. The fleshy ones — I count myself among them most mornings, when the algae is thick on my shoulders and my mouth is full of building-clay for the berm-line, laying it down in wet mouthfuls and patting it true with my tongue — the fleshy ones cultivate it. We walk in rhythm to feed it. We stop suddenly to discipline it. We change pace very, very slowly when we want it to learn something.

This one has learned too much to be only mine.

It pauses half a beat *before* I pause. It did out there, weeks out, when there was no berm for a month and the horizon had thinned to the exact color of the sky and up was only where the sound got thinner. It anticipated the soft place in the crust before my foot reported it. It shifted its weight — spray does not have weight, and yet the sheet-water jumped in a perfect, silent-looking disc where its shoulder would be, held a heartbeat too long, collapsed — onto a side I was not favoring.

I have an old injury there. It does not. It favors the other side, the way someone else once did.

In the council ring we put our feet toward the center, a dozen of us, and flatten a disc of mud between us until it carries perfectly, and we argue entirely in tremors, heel of the hand, sustained lean, a whole sentence as a shiver through the shared skin of the world. I brought it there by the only method available: I said nothing into the air. I never say anything into the air; a voice let loose is a donation to the continent, it will come back next season flattened and unfamiliar and answer somebody else's child. I pressed my face down and told the ground about the companion that walks beside me using a stride I almost recognize.

The ground, politely, argued back with the deep water.

And someone across the ring — broad-backed, third lid never quite open, watching my reflection to decide if I was real — someone leaned, slow, and sent through the thinning mud a pressure pattern with a catch in it. A limp made of grammar. The name that belongs with the limp arrived a yard later through my palm, local and dying properly with the pressure that carried it, the way a name should. It was not shouted. It will not go around the basin. It will not become a storm's property.

It was a grandmother. Not mine. Or not only mine. Sequence is — well. You have heard the rule. She walked this salt band before the band had finished being remembered as mountains. Her tread went out on a leading edge years ago and has been coming back in pieces, weathered, phase-shifted, learning dogs and grandchildren and strangers as it travels.

So now the question of what I am gets a second count, as all of us here are counted twice.

Once: in this body. Low because everything tall was edited off us. Eyes set wide and a little high, seeing down more reliably than out, trusting the foot's opinion over the mirage's. Throat narrowed to humming work, rib to resonant rib in the berm's terrifying quiet, or clicking to find the thickness of the skin between us and the drowned world. Molars flat from chewing films and shaping mud. A person presently embodied.

And once: in accompaniment. Because while I keep a foot near the fresh berm listening for her, she is out there just past the spiral keeping time with me. And because while I was walking her in, something of my own stride went ahead of me — I heard it, there is no use pretending otherwise, I heard my companion answer a question in an echo before I had finished asking it through the ground, and then felt myself, puzzled, answer differently with my palm — and that earlier answer is still out there wandering, getting weathered, and someday someone will be accompanied by a version of me that pauses before I would have, favoring a side I haven't injured yet.

I do not find the ordering urgent. Cause arrives when it can. So does family.

What I do, then — what you would find me doing if you stayed longer than watching, if your own ears stopped aching enough for you to notice hours — is the keeping.

I keep the corral. Not every spiral is a sound-shadow for bodies; this outer curve is tuned a fraction wider, its hollow shaped to give a coherent echo a place to cohere a little longer. I rebuild it where spray has worried it. I walk the approach to it at the same time each dimming, same rhythm, so the pattern has a road to come home on. I practice sudden stillness at the threshold so it learns it may rest without scattering. When a storm-system crosses from a thousand miles away — you can hear them being born here, nothing ever finishes dying here, sound has nowhere to go — and scrambles it back into mere mist and black ribbons of mud, I do not scream. Screaming would only donate more noise. I settle in the lee, forehead down, and I wait with my soles reading, and I begin again, footfall by footfall, until the mist takes the outline, loses it, repeats it a few yards away, keeps it.

And when the fleshy ones die — they settle them here, too, weighted gently with reeds, no burial, no mound above four inches to boast at the weather — I am the one who stays. Not out of holiness. Out of having the patience in my bones; the low notes take that long. I keep a foot on the ground nearby for days while the body sends its small irregular pressures into the crust, gas and collapse, the slow give of the rib-hoops, and I receive it the way you would hold a hand. I learn the last of its rhythm by heart, in my teeth. So that later, predictably and never on schedule, when its tread comes back across the basin unmistakable on some storm's leading edge, there will be at least one body here who knows how to adjust her pace to match it for a while.

So it will not travel alone.

That is the shape of me. A wide, low table of a creature at the interface, skull half in air and half in the better-conducting wet, mouth tasting of clay, back filmed with tomorrow's food, listening downward to a sea that never sees the sky while just beyond my shoulder something made of my own noise and a grandmother's limp stands in the water without displacing it quite right, patient as only a returning thing can be, waiting for me to start moving again so we can agree, for another few miles of continent too flat to change and too loud to ever be empty, on which of us is leading.
