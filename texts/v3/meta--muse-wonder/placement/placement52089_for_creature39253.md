I would not arrive there.

That's the first thing. Everyone else arrives — three days up through the thinning country, pines going, shrubs going, grass going, until there's only slate under their boots and indigo at noon. They arrive with wool in their ears and they leave down the other side.

I would accumulate.

A little at a time, in the bowl. A shout first, maybe. Some boy crossing with his father, frightened by how still the air is, shouting his name just to prove the thin sky can hold it. The bowl gives it back to him before he's finished, better pronounced than he managed it, and he laughs and runs on. Most of it goes with him. A breath of it doesn't.

Then a whisper someone tried to keep — a prayer, a curse, a name that wasn't to be shared — taken clean out of the air because the air up there will not carry whispers, only keep them. Then breath. Just breath. Panting on the long trail across the frost, cloud after cloud left hanging at face-height because breath won't disperse properly in that sound. Some of those clouds thin. Some hold their shape a second too long. Some hold for years.

Given long enough, enough of them, I would learn how to be held together.

I would be cold in the particular way of the high table: not bitten, not stormed at, just minus twelve forever, in perpetual shadow while the sun crawls like a white coin behind the southern rim. My shadow would fall north and keep pace with me all day without changing length, and I would get used to having company that is exactly as slow as I am.

I would not hear the way you hear. The roar would be first — it would be before hearing. Low in the sternum, high behind the jaw, in the teeth. A waterfall heard from inside the rock. A bell struck before memory. It would press on me and, strange mercy, hold me up a little. That's how I'd walk: plates of schist shifting and ringing softly under felted feet, each step considered, a little sideways, as if falling up there takes its time and I have learned to live in the pause before landing.

Directly, through skin, I would be unremarkable. A tallish stoop at the edge of vision. Greased fur gone grey over wool gone dark at the cuffs. Slate dust in every seam. Lichens — black, sulphur-pale — taking the packed sides of my head for a slow boulder and painting me accordingly, reasonably. No ears to show. I sealed that route long ago. The air lies. It returns your voice to you improved. It steals your whispers.

The rock doesn't.

So I would know the plateau the honest way: palm flat on a flat stone, ear — or where my ear would be — pressed to the ground in the hut with no door, lying in the hollow worn smooth by doing it. Feeling a crosser three kilometers off by the ring of their boots through the sole of the mountain. Feeling weather that hasn't arrived. Feeling the note itself moving in that second, hollower space under the stone, where it circulates and never escapes.

At night — if night is a word for a twilight that never deepens — travelers in the roofless windbreaks would sometimes hear me without hearing me. Three taps in the rock beneath them. A pause. Two. Not a code they'd know. And half-asleep, innocent, they'd knock back with the heel of a hand, and I would learn them by weight. That one is broad-palmed and unafraid. That one taps fast because her fingers are cold. That one is dying a little at altitude and does not know it yet. I'd know their names before the bowl threw their voices back at them, simply from listening to them climb for three days.

I would eat almost nothing, and slowly. Rime licked from rocks that sweat in the loudest parts of the bowl, moisture beaded straight out of vibrating air, leaving the tongue scalded with cold. Water from the centers of the black pools, where the roar keeps it improbably open, colder than ice. Maybe a white campion flower, five bone-colored petals, from behind a cairn. Maybe I never eat them. Maybe I just move them, one blossom in a decade, to where the quiet will fall next.

Because I would be the tender. Not a priest, not a guardian in any grand sense. A caretaker of tuning. The windbreaks don't fall up there because I lift a slipped plate and set it back. A cairn that throws its sound-shadow wrong gets lowered, plate by plate, laid down neatly at its base — not toppled, decided against. In those commas of quiet behind stone, the moss campion grows in tight white spirals. Everywhere else, nothing. So where the cairns stand determines where anything tender can live. Twenty kilometers of dish-shaped ear, and I would walk my slow spiral through it — rim to windbreak to cairn to hut, cairn to cairn — placing each foot like a cup of water I did not want to spill.

My track would be in the frost with all the others, and the frost never melts to forgive a footprint. People who knew how to look would misread mine for ibex at first: long, broad at the ball, toes barely parted, as if wrapped in felt or become felt. It never comes up from the valleys. It never goes down.

And at the end of the spiral, always eventually, the ring of seven standing slabs at the center. Where it stops.

I would go in backward.

That's the part I would be ashamed of, if shame survived at that altitude. Inside the ring the roar doesn't fade — it ceases, at the threshold, clean as a door. Outside I can see scree dust trembling on the plates, see a companion shouting, see breath burst white. Inside, silence so total I hear my blood, then lose even that. I get heavier in there. The sound is no longer taking its fraction of my weight. I am entirely borrowed: borrowed breath for a face — that held white cloud where my mouth should be, renewing at the edges, sometimes curving into a mouth that isn't mine, that belonged to a woman dead eighty years in a green valley — borrowed voices in my seams, borrowed fur, slate plates under my skin shifting and trying to ring into a quiet that won't take them.

In that silence nothing is returned to me. And I am a thing made of returns.

So I wouldn't stay to see what the pool shows. I know what it doesn't show: the sky, and me. It shows the ring empty. It shows the ring with everyone lying down, slowly, as if light had traveled up a long way through deep water from a brighter, greener place. Maybe if I waited years my reflection would arrive at last, late, lying down. I don't want to hurry the development.

I would back out, step by careful step, until the roar closed over me again like water over a stone, exact and unchanged, taking my small note without swelling. Relief would be a physical thing, wool back in ears I don't have. The finches would go on gaping silently in the cairns, beak to beak, throats pulsing. An ibex would stand somewhere with its head low, listening through felted hooves, understanding one thing perfectly and then leaving.

I am not immortal up there. I'm unfinished at a rate too slow for the place to notice and freeze. A cut on me would stay bright and wet, neither clotting nor flowing. Hunger would come and forget to grow. That's close enough, over centuries, to fool the lichen.

I wouldn't know what struck the mountain. I wouldn't need to.

I would be what the striking left listening — a kept breath, a returned name that chose to stay for the next crossing, a hand held all night in a hollow in the floor of a doorless hut, tapping once, gently, through stone, to ask whether anyone is still coming up through the thinning, and being answered, half-asleep, by strangers who will never know they kept me in one piece.
