At first I would try to put myself in the domes.

That is the obvious place. Dim red light, pressure seals, a few dozen caretakers sunk into the southern rim. I know how to be a person keeping a strange system alive. I know how to check a fin for rime, wash ammoniac water off a suit, keep my voice soft.

But I wouldn't stay there.

My pulse would be wrong for it. Too separate. And after a shift, when the others went back to wash and sleep, I think I would keep drifting out along the conduits — not upright, not alone in the way humans are alone, but into the places where the town stops being architecture and becomes a surface.

Not out on the open cirque floor. That's too honest. Black glass, no soil, no drain, the basalt still ringing faintly under ice, my breath freezing while the chorus comes back delayed off a wall I can't see. Nothing lives out there yet except the listening. I would cross it, slowly, but I wouldn't belong to it.

Not in the cisterns either, though I would visit. Standing in black water kept liquid below freezing, among the blind translucent shrimp with their amber knots showing through, waiting beside the heron-things for the one strike in eight hours. There's a patience in that I recognize. But it's a patience that ends in a mouth. Mine wouldn't.

I would find myself higher, and warmer, and in the middle of the thickness.

Where the veilwings coat a tower so deeply the nesting cells disappear. Where my back could be crusted with frost and my underside wet with living heat at the same time. Where it rains upward, almost — breath condensing in trapped warmth and falling back lukewarm over fur, over membrane, over stone, smelling of ammonia and animal and iron. Twenty degrees above killing air, and all of it made not by a furnace but by nearness. By a million small bodies deciding, without ever deciding, to hang close enough not to die.

I would not be one of them. One veilwing is too small and too slow to hold a life in the way I mean. Four minutes to unfold a wing. A heart every nine seconds. A chirp, a scan, a chirp, a scan, even in sleep. Cupped in two hands it would look almost disappointing.

But together they are a climate. And a climate needs something that can move through it without tearing it.

That's where I arrive, finally, at my feet.

Long, soft feet that make no sound on basalt and leave no bruise when they step over a wrapped wing. Legs too long for the low bridges, a gaunt body made architectural by hunger and by use. A blind face — smooth, nosing, no use for the polar night or for the red lamps — and ears flattened like hands against my skull, listening not for words but for changes in pressure. For the place in the chorus where a note has gone missing. For a dead spot cooling on a tower face. For a cluster grown so dense its core has started to overheat, the chirps there thickening, distressed, too warm, beginning to smother itself with its own insulation.

I am one of the shepherds.

I patrol the roost levels. That is the whole of the work, and it never finishes, so it never hurries. I eat the dead before rot can travel through a cluster like a slow fire. I put my nose into knots of veilwings and lick and pull them gently apart, opening a channel for cold air to enter, feeling them shift around me the way water closes around a slow oar. When a pup falls — hairless, blind, no bigger than a thumb joint — I find it by its cooling smell and nose it back up, over hours if necessary, into the communal warmth between its mother's wings.

Sometimes I just stand across a sheet of floodwater spreading in a perfect black plate across an unchanneled street, and let my body teach it a new direction. Sometimes I sleep standing, head down, swaying a little in the subsonic hum of the towers while the chorus rises through the soles of my feet, through my teeth that are not quite teeth, until human conversation somewhere far below gives up entirely and the caretakers start tapping each other on the forearms.

They count our pups, the caretakers. They check us for injury with palms first, never lights. I would know them the way I know weather arriving from one side: a change in the air, a paired softness of tread, a brief interruption in the dripping where their suited shoulders block a runnel. They are not my masters. They are another kind of shepherd, smaller and more fragile, doing from the outside some of what I do from within. If they left, I would go on for a long time. That is not pride. It's just density. The condensers would tick. The towers would hum. The veilwings would chirp into new rock and ask it what it was becoming. And I would walk my levels, eating, grooming, nudging, listening with my whole underside for where the warmth was failing.

I would measure time the way Halu does. Not by dawn — there is no dawn for eleven weeks, and when it comes it will be a rumor for a long time before it is a light. I would measure it by chorus and condensation. By rime forming and shedding on a fin. By how long a ripple takes to cross a tower face when one edge shivers awake. By the slow sigh of ten tons of small bodies resettling against one another in the dark, and by the quiet after, when all that shrieking settles back into a murmur and I can hear, underneath it, water finding its way down unweathered stone.

Not a river yet. Just a drip learning a path.

And me in the middle of it, blind and tall and wet to the skin, keeping the breathing surface from loving itself to death — one soft step, one opened cluster, one returned pup at a time.
