At first I try to stand there the way a person would — on two feet, at the edge of a pan, with wax and mud in my ears.

That doesn't last. Standing is too high, and too exposed, and ears are the wrong way in. I try being one of the professionals instead: a heron, grey as the light, stomping shrimp up out of the mat, unbothered. Or the rail running pan to pan. Or a deaf dog, sleeping placidly with its chin on vibrating stone.

All of them are still visitors to the noise. They work around it. They leave.

I sink lower. That is what this place teaches everything that wants to stay.

Lower than the snails, almost. I could be a snail — most of a life spent sealed mouth-down to the underside of a stone, shut, tasting the world very slowly through one foot when the water finally comes. That patience feels true. The silver writing across the flats, the whole pan faintly written over in trails.

But a snail does not answer the plain back. And I keep feeling an answer rising in me, not in ears — there is a pressure in my jaw, in the spread of my toes, a trembling that comes up through wet stone whether I want it or not.

So: smaller. Wetter. Wedged.

You would find me on the side of one of the whalebacks, not on top of it. In a bowl the water has hollowed over longer than I can make sense of — a blind stone cup, deep as two fists, polished inside, black with manganese varnish where seep-water beads even in a dry week. If you peered in during the wet you would mostly miss me. A warty back the color of the rock, pressed hard to the curve. Eyes. A throat pale at the opening.

I live with my back to the stone and my face to the weather, and that is my whole architecture.

When there is no rain, I am down at the damp bottom of the bowl where it never quite dries. My skin stays cool against the seep. My metabolism goes thin and patient. Above me, somewhere too far away to matter, snails click faintly against rock. A cairn drips. The plain is not empty then, only turned down, and I am part of what is turned down with it — low, shut, listening not with anything I can close, but through the soles pressed to stone.

The alarm is not a sound. It is rain arriving unobstructed across eighty miles of flat, striking stone, felt first as a change in the ground. A shiver that finds my bowl and makes its walls almost ring. Then water: first a beading, then a trickle finding old channels, then the bowl itself filling enough to touch my belly. My body knows before any thought bothers to arrive. I climb, wet and stiff, and wedge myself higher, where the curve fits me.

And then I call.

My ears shut when I do it — a muscle clamps down, the round skin stills. I do not hear myself the way you would hear me. I feel myself. My throat swells against the mouth of the bowl, my toes spread wide on stone to catch the return of my own note coming back through the rock, my jaw carries the lower notes up into my skull. The bowl takes what would be a small, unremarkable croak from an animal that could hide in a child's fist, and it rounds it, deepens it, throws it out across the sheet-water grave and enormous.

Out there, predators hesitate at a toad the size of a dog. Out there, a female somewhere in the shallows does not turn toward me — on this flat, direction is a lie the loudest pan tells. She finds the particular rhythm of my ripples against her belly in the general trembling, one distinct pattern in the shaking of the whole world.

Around me the plain finds its sections like an orchestra that has practiced forever. Below me the spotted pan-frogs rolling in the open water. Further out, Mother Pan, where the heavy toads float with only their eyes out and make the note that lives in the sternum, and for a while no smaller throat dares anything at all. Highest of all, the peepers on their cobbles, thin and metallic, coming from nowhere and everywhere. Mine sits between — not the highest, not the ground-note of the toads — made larger and darker by stone.

For three, four weeks I am almost only this: eat what the wind and the midge-columns bring to the lip of my bowl, a snapped mouthful taken without leaving my seat; call; feel the answering calls of bowls half a mile away come through the whaleback itself so my ribs know them; go still.

That stillness is the truest thing. A harrier's shadow crosses the water and a square mile of us cuts out at once. I feel it in my feet before I see it — the sudden absence, travelling outward, the plain holding its breath. In that second I can feel the tiny things buried under our noise: water dripping, a snail shell ticking, shrimp jerking in the shallows. My clamped ears do not matter. My whole underside is an ear.

Then somewhere, one brave male starts again. I feel the invitation through wet granite, and I answer — not out of bravery, exactly. Out of the bowl filling with sound again whether I join it or not. Louder this time. The ibis freeze and go back to stomping. The rail darts. The world resumes breathing through ten thousand mouths.

People come, sometimes, to my whaleback. I know them as pressure and shadow, as careful feet that do not strike the stone with metal — that would ring for minutes and hurt in a way I have no word for. They leave things at the lip of bowls like mine: a shell bead, a smear of fat. I do not understand offerings. I know salt and grease on a tongue when the rain washes a trace of it down. I know their deaf dogs by the untroubled weight of their steps. I know their children are quieter here than living things usually are, and move their hands a great deal.

They leave. The birds leave in long dim lines. The rain thins and the sheet-water retreats in an afternoon, leaving salt lace and a crunch of midges.

So do I, in my way — only downward. My calls go first: as the water drops, the bowl stops answering properly and my voice comes out thinner, exposed, only itself. I eat less. I let myself slide toward the cool bottom where the seep still beads on black varnish. The frogs out on the flats are burrowing backwards, corkscrewing into cracking mud, wrapping themselves in their own shed skin. I have no mud. I have this damp hollow that has shaped my life and, climbing down it year after year, perhaps been shaped a fraction by me.

Dry, curled, cooled to the temperature of stone. Not dead. Not waiting for a date.

Waiting for a vibration — rain on a finished plain, coming across a horizon with nothing to stop it, until the whaleback wakes under my toes and the bowl begins, again, to sing me larger than I am.
