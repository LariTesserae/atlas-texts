You'd find me where the light-line keeps changing its mind.

Not up by the pods, not down at the landing stages where the off-valley crews come in loud in their clean shells, trying to shout over weather that has already taken shouting away. Further out than that. Knee-deep, usually. Where a bank went soft in the night and the river is trying a new idea through it.

You hear me before — no. That's wrong. Here nobody hears anybody. You'd feel the pump note change through the curb I'm standing on, and then you'd see the amber of my lamp kept low, because white only shows you rain, and then I'd already be close enough to touch. That's the only distance that works.

I was born in the linked pods behind Hjalli Flats. Second winter after the schoolroom was bolted on. I have never known a quiet room. I don't think my ears are built for one. In the cot the pump thrum came up through the frame, rain needled the roof, frogs in the ditch made one solid peeping weather out of themselves, and somewhere below us a builder was printing the valley back around a river that had moved in its sleep. I learned to sleep inside that the way other children learn to sleep in a bed. Still do. On still nights — there are almost none — I wake up listening for what's missing.

I'm valley-born, and it shows once the shell comes off. Shorter in the leg than my father, who came for a three-month rotation and stayed fifteen years. Broad through the calf. I walk splayed whether I mean to or not, weight never quite committed, always testing. My boots buzz faintly at the ankle when the green mat is lying to me and underneath it's soup, but mostly I don't need them to. You can feel it through the moss if you grew up being swallowed to the ankle by it every day of your life. Old moss, acid-yellow, tight — it'll hold you another hour. Pale fuzz on last week's print — it'll hold you and take you at the same time. My eyes can be somewhere else while my feet are reading that. On the geese. On whether the drones are up in working lines or clumped under the eaves like swallows, which means don't bother.

My hands give me away indoors. Nails to the quick. Cuticles stained black-green no scrubbing lifts. That pale callus between thumb and forefinger from pulling sedge plugs and stitching through slump-mesh. And the little hairless dent behind my jaw where the tab sits. I press it when I'm thinking. We all do.

I'm a swarm-hand. Mostly. Everybody's ticket says one thing and everybody learns moss work, but swarm work is what my hands wanted. I stand in brown water that wasn't a channel at breakfast while ten thousand hand-sized tillers and grippers pour around my boots in a glittering sheet, quite ignoring me, stitching a collapse shut with fungal polymer before the next surge can get its fingers in. My job is the stupid, human part the swarm won't do: reach in and retrieve what the water has taken this time. A drone still trying gamely to swim upstream. A laundry shirt wrapped around spruce needles. Somebody's glove, which will scroll across three visors by noon — *Glove yrs? in new channel* — and be claimed by someone touching my shoulder as they pass.

Some days I'm sent with the print-riders instead, walking alongside the big six-legged extruders as they waddle and lay a levee down like wet concrete crossed with root. You shout at them the way you shout at any plumbing. They are not miraculous to me. Nothing here is. A bridge told to go porous by lunch so fish fry can pass, ground that remembers an instruction and then forgets it on purpose — it breaks, it floods, it eats your afternoon. You fix it standing in what it was supposed to be holding back.

That is most of my life. Brief, and close.

Outside, I almost never use my voice. It's extravagant, and useless. Text unrolls wet across the inside of my visor: *Mat lifting E. Pump 3 up. Step up.* I type without looking while already moving, stepping up onto tussock or printed curb when the note deepens under my soles, not even breaking stride. If I need to actually say something, I get in close. Hood to hood. Forehead nearly to forehead. A gloved hand flat between your shoulderblades while the substrate settles under both of us and decides whether we're standing on ground. I grew up thinking being understood *was* being near, and I still don't quite trust a conversation held at arm's length. Arguments happen that close. So does grief. So does everything tender. We are forever righting each other — a strap adjusted, a child's weight shifted onto higher root while water commits below, an elder's elbow held, eyes still scanning moss.

Indoors it surprises visitors how soft we are. Voices hoarse from disuse, long pauses filled by frogs, nobody in a hurry to fill a silence the pumps are already filling through the floor. We eat hot and salty with our knees up, shells steaming on the rack: geothermal potatoes, fish that came through a bridge that let them, greens cut that morning from a ditch that was bare steaming mud two seasons ago. Someone is always half-listening through the talk. When the pitch changes, the whole room rises as one animal and moves the baskets up a shelf, and sits again, and no one comments on it.

The children count us. I remember doing it. Unobtrusively, again and again, in the warm linked rooms: not possessions — you don't keep things here that can't float or dissolve on purpose — but people. Who's in off the reroute. Who's still out with the swarms. It isn't anxiety, exactly. It's navigation. A path isn't where you went yesterday. It's where the light says you may go this morning. People are the only landmarks that agree to come back.

I don't expect to leave. Where would I go that stays still long enough? The mainland, I'm told, has maps that last all week and rooms where you can hear a person across a table without leaning in, and I believe it the way I believe in deserts.

At last light — mid-afternoon, blue-black already, masts pricking amber through the murk, surf still just a pressure in my teeth — I'll come in with two others off the upper track, single file where the moss will hold, not talking, touching shoulders as we pass the pump house at Hjalli. I'll peel off a shell that pours a day's condensed water down my own back. I'll dry my tab. I'll eat close to people I can name by the way their weight shifts on wet ground.

And outside, all night, the deer will pick delicately between the machines, the geese will settle or won't, the builders will keep their patient printing through the dark, and I'll sleep the way I was rocked to sleep — nested right in the middle of the roar, one foot, even in dreams, already feeling for higher ground.
