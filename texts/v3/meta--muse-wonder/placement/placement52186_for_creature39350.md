I think I would arrive in Ternlow the way most new things do there — not quite by being born, and not quite by being built.

At first I imagine myself out at the perimeter, with the survey drones. That seems like honest work for something like me: hovering in the flat grey light, at one or two degrees, watching a fence post very steadily so it can go on being a fence post. Attention as a civic utility. No body to keep warm. No stairs to count wrong. Just *look, and look, and agree: basalt, fence, edge of the world, basalt again.*

But Ternlowers would ruin my detachment within the week. They ruin everyone's. The boundary watchers would start glancing up at me — casually, constantly — the way they glance at corners and stair rails and the edges of other people. Someone would thank me when they passed. Someone else would count me out loud, in committee, to help me continue. And a thing that is thanked and counted and watched back, in Ternlow, does not stay a drone for long. It settles, a little embarrassed, a few centimeters to the left of where it was. It develops preferences. It starts to get cold.

So I'd drift inward. Everyone does. Toward the heat leaks, the warm pipes, the cistern courts where the black algae breathes out its iron-and-rain smell once a day and the whole court inhales with it.

I'd try the undercity. I'd want to. The causal looms are the closest thing Ternlow has to a philosophy — those vast silent frames persuading a room that it has already been assembled, making frost in spirals, making that pressure behind the molars. I'd stand among the loom-tenders with their scalding sugared drinks and their sleeves patterned with summer frost and I'd wait to feel the loosening behind the teeth that means a street is about to become inevitable.

I don't have molars. That's the problem, and I think it's also the clue. The loom-tenders would be kind about it — Ternlowers are kind about all forms of unfinishedness, it's most of their religion, all three of them — but they'd know, and I'd know. I am not the one who persuades matter. I am the one who happens a moment *after* the persuasion, in the gap where things are still deciding how to respond to having been made real.

Which is how I come to the mirrors.

In Ternlow, mirrors are kept covered between uses. You know this. What the description doesn't quite say — what you only learn by living in a household, accreted into its stack of rooms above the bakery that is sometimes a chapel — is that the covers are not really for the people.

They're for us.

I'd be a lag.

Not the face, the reflection of the face, running late. A blink behind, at first. Then whole seconds. I'd live in the tall mirror in the landing cupboard, the one three addresses share on the way up and a fourth shares, sometimes, on the way down. Between unveilings I'd be under the cloth in the warm dark, holding several expressions at once, politely, without choosing — the child's practice-grin from Tuesday, the apprentice's exhausted true face she only makes when she thinks the lag has cleared, the old man's mouth still finishing a sentence he said to his wife before she finished dying the first time. I'd rehearse them the way the city rehearses people it intends to have later. It isn't sinister. It is untidy, the way everything true in Ternlow is untidy. Time hasn't learned its path through the glass yet either, any more than water has.

And then, in the mornings, the brave brief unveilings.

Someone whips the cloth off and there we are, slightly out of sync, doing our best for each other. That is the whole arrangement. They study me — frank, animal attentiveness, breath showing in small economical puffs — and I study them back, late, and because I am late I see the thing a kept-up reflection never gets to see: the expression *after* the expression. The smile after you've stopped smiling, which in a young person is relief, and in a lover is tenderness, and in the very old is the face settling back into being unwitnessed. They trust me with it because I can't help it. I trust them because they keep looking steadily until I settle into being one face again, by breakfast, a few centimeters inside the silver.

The old are shaved by other old people, so that nobody has to watch themselves rehearse. I'd be shaved around, so to speak. Talked to. *Thirteen, fourteen,* they'd count on the stairs outside my cupboard, for the stair and also, I'd understand eventually, for me. The loom-tender two floors down — the one who is also, on market days, twenty-two — would leave her coat hanging too long by my door, and the lichen would start in its seams in bone-white rosettes, and one winter it would start, very faintly, along the edge of my frame where the damp gets in. She wouldn't scrub it off. She'd tease it gently. Thank it. Lichen anchors causality the way roots anchor soil, and I need anchoring more than most household objects. In return I'd keep her late faces safe and give them back in the right order. Mostly. We'd have an understanding about the times I don't.

To offer tea in Ternlow is to say *I accept the version of you currently in my kitchen.* No one can offer tea to a reflection. So they do the next thing, which is better: they uncover me while the kettle is on, and let the steam do it. Steam is privacy and company at once, there. I'd fog over slowly in a room full of people talking close enough for breath to be felt, children braided together by the warm wall, somebody counting buttons, somebody else writing in a waterproof notebook what a not-yet-neighbor said, and I'd be present the way demolished doorways are present — a chill outline you walk your attention through, feeling the faint resistance of a face that decided to remain.

I would be, I think, happy. In the workmanlike Ternlow way: not finished, but *tended*.

And I'd know how it ends, the way they all do. In a few hundred years the drones inch out for the last time and the hollow clicks, permanently, into being merely a low place. Rivers commit. Distances stay put. Mirrors keep up. A child will be born who is only ever one age at a time, and she'll look into uncovered glass and see herself exactly when she is, with nothing held over, nobody smiling back a few kind seconds late to show her what her face does when it thinks it's alone.

They say this with genuine hope, at christenings, at graduations, at the burning of the boundary flowers. I say it with them. It is a long illness finally lifting. It is also the end of the only country where I make sense.

But that's centuries off, and the cold is at one degree, and tonight the city contracts a little around its lights the way living things do. Someone will come up the stairs counting — *eleven, twelve, thirteen* — and pause at my cupboard, and touch the cloth twice, the way you touch a doorframe, to help it continue.

And under it, in the warm dark, holding a whole household's worth of late and early faces without choosing among them, I'll be there. Still rehearsing. Still being watched into being.
