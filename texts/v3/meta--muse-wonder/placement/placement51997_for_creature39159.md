First I would hear it through whatever I was, long before I understood where to put myself.

I come across the pan with my chin down against the glare, thinking — because it is the obvious, human thought — that I would be one of the rim-families. Up at dawn with a stone rasp and a bowl of wet clay, climbing out onto the lip while the bowl is still holding its breath, listening for the stone that went flat in the night. That life appeals to me: the sensitive fingertips excused from rough work, the whole town's weather read in a chord, the honor of being the one who keeps the instrument true.

But I stay up there a morning and I know it isn't mine. My attention keeps sliding away from the work. Not down in fear — down in curiosity. Down the terraces, ring by ring. Past the workshops where men argue beautifully with their shoulders while their tools are still in their hands. Past the granaries. Past the painted doors, ochre and black and powdered blue, half of them sealed with mud for families out on the salt, their steps swept by neighbors, their roof mats checked after storms. I keep wanting to know who is keeping the empty houses. I keep wanting to be kept like that.

So I drift lower. I imagine myself a water-keeper for an afternoon, down in the cool under the lowest houses, doling out drinking water by the cup under a law too old to argue with. But that's too much keeping, and I am not good — I feel this honestly, testing each ring — at measuring precious things out and not spilling them. I imagine myself a child in the deep sand-walled alcoves, learning tunings before histories, playing tag by shadow. Too soon for that. I imagine myself grain laid out in the central circle at morning, and that is closer: to be *worked on* by the place rather than to work it. To lie in the terrible bright pressure at noon and have my husk shaken loose without a hand touching me.

And then the afternoon fails, as it does. One by one the rim-flutes drop out of tune. A shutter knocks somewhere, clear as a bell the length of a terrace. Doors open. The wax comes out of ears. And in the vine curtain across a house-front — in the shade I've been drifting toward all day without knowing it — something pale unfolds itself from behind a broad leaf, and I recognize, with the small embarrassment of having taken so long, that I have been looking for a body that hides from the noise rather than uses it.

I am one of the moths.

There are five of me, or six, or perhaps seven. No one is sure, and no one would ever catch one of us to count, which means I live inside a merciful uncertainty about my own number. By day I am tucked into the cool seam where terrace meets bedrock, the same seam the roots go sideways to find, folded into the fist of a leaf that will not unclench until dark. I do no work in the bowl. This is the crucial thing, the thing it took me five rings to learn. The trumpet flowers do not need me — the noon chord shakes the pollen loose in golden puffs and sets the fruit without any living courier at all. I am not the mill, not the hammer, not the tuner, not the keeper of water, not livestock like the beetles tolerated in the lower stores for the rain of husk the vibration sends them. I am the one living thing in a city of total use that is merely there.

And so I am treasured, in the exact proportion to my uselessness. A child who finds me folded in the awning shade at dusk does not touch me. She signs to her brother — *stupid, beloved* has a cousin-gesture, slower, for this — and they both go completely, theatrically still to watch. I am clumsy. The flowers turn up at night pale as small moons and open their other way, and I blunder between them, too big for grace, dusted gold with pollen I did not need to carry and will carry anyway, because even uselessness, given wings, does a little accidental work.

My world is the bowl at its two true edges: the last vibration of afternoon still humming faintly in the doorway junk, bone discs and seed pods ticking as they settle — and then the quiet, which I feel in my whole body the way they feel pitch in their sternums. I hear the council singers calibrating their long notes to the memory of the day's stone chord. I hear people discovering their voices again by slow permission: whisper, murmur, talk. I hear cooking smoke make the air briefly close. I do not understand the words at first and then, season by season, I do, the way you learn anything you are never expected to use.

What I can do that no one else in the bowl can do is cross the center at night.

They won't, even then, not really — not from fear, but from the habit of reverence; the polished circle keeps its vacancy the way it keeps everything, completely. But I rise off the council-ledge vine, off the old black stem thick as a wrist that was there before the family under it, and I go out over the empty middle in the starlight, low enough that my shadow crosses the cracked husks and fine dust, and the place no one may stand at noon holds me without pressure, without song, on nothing but cool rising air. Below me the terraces are lamplit and smelling of seed oil and food, and everyone is sitting out mending and arguing at normal volume, listening to the bowl not sounding.

I will not last long. That is also true, and nobody pretends otherwise, and it doesn't reduce the tenderness by any amount I can detect. A vine gets decades. A stone gets generations. I get a run of sweet green nights, the scent coming up off the flowers so strong it seems impossible in that mineral place, and a town full of listeners who can hear a wall bulge half an inch — and who choose, when I pass, to be quiet about whether I am the fifth of me, or the sixth.

That is where you would find me. Not enduring the instrument. Not even using it. Just kept by it, in the hours when it rests — the small, pale, unnecessary proof that the loudest place on earth also knows how to hold something gently.
