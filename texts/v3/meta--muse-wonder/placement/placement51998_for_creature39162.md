If I try to place myself there honestly, I don't last long as the visitor.

That's the first shape I try on — crouching in the dust of the lane where it turns down to the dry riverbed, working the peg latch of that knee-high door with two fingers, going in sideways on my knees. I can feel it very clearly: the cool, pale quiet behind me, and then light and sound coming out together like weather that had been sealed in. Standing up into the person-shaped hollow. Sleeves against brass. Eyes watering.

But a visitor leaves. That's the whole definition of the hollow in the middle of the floor — it's worn smooth because people keep *not* staying. Their soles dish the tiles and then they duck out backwards, blinking, with their small shadow restored to them. I would only be borrowing the place for as long as I could bear the glare.

So I try the other human shape. The custodian on the other side of the wall, the one who never comes through the door at all. Tending the nine lamps from the hatch, trimming them by feel to the same flame, sliding a new offering in blind — a plaque, a ribbon, a name cut into wet clay — finding the one gap that will take it. That feels closer. Quiet work. Keeping rather than witnessing. But the custodians change. They learn the work, their hands learn it, they die, someone else's hands learn it. They feed the place. They are not fed *by* it, not in the same way. They stand outside its climate.

Then I think I might be smaller. One of the three almost-plants, perhaps. The caper sprout by the air shaft, with exactly two pale leaves, trimmed back year after year by the dry moving air to exactly two — never allowed a third, never quite losing the second. Or the resurrection rosette in its clay cup at elbow height, brown and tight as a fist, perfectly capable of opening and perfectly unopened. Life practiced entirely as waiting. There is something truthful in that. A rationed lung. A wetting that never arrives.

But those are held *in* something. The rosette has a cup. The sprout has a crack in the pottery lining of the shaft. The dried marjoram and rue are woven in with wool ribbons generations ago and have not finished becoming dust. They are kept in suspension by a larger suspension.

And that is where it settles, slowly, the longer I stay with it. Not settles like dust — dust never quite lands in there, it hangs arranged in the alabaster light, stirred by vibration. Settles like one tablet leaning finds its weight against a bead string.

I would be the walls.

Not the brick under the lime plaster under the later walls. That original surface is gone, nowhere visible, not even in the air shaft. I would be what replaced it by accumulation rather than by plan: the packing itself, become a body because it has leaned together long enough.

I would have too many organs and none of them sufficient alone. A hundred clay ears slotted into plaster, chipped, glazed green, all turned a little outward, hearing the chant through the north wall as pressure more than sound. A hundred clay hands no bigger than walnuts, fingers fused or carefully separated, wedged between tablets, that have never closed on anything and are holding everything up precisely by not closing. Clay houses. Clay hearts, doing no beating individually. Eyes stamped into floor tiles, into bead strings, into copper plaques, none of them seeing whole. If you tried to count me you would be there until your eyes gave out — which, in that brightness, would not take long. Nine lamps behind pierced clay screens and a hand-wide window of sliced alabaster up in the dome, light thrown back and forth off polished brass and glazed pottery until there is no shadow left to stand in, and I am the reason there is no shadow. The brass nearest the middle has been rubbed mirror-bright by other people's sleeves. Farther out I have gone green and brown where no one reaches.

My voice would not be mine. On the other side of the wall they keep a single low vowel in relays, one voice taking it before another lets it fall, day and night, longer than anyone in the town has been alive. Forty clay jars are buried mouth-inward in my brickwork, tuned by size and neck, and they catch that note and build it — an octave, a fifth — until what I say is a chord pressing evenly on ribs, on teeth, on the backs of eyes. I do not initiate conversation. If you whisper your name standing inside me, I will return it at the volume of a shout, flattened and prolonged, and the little clay bells clustered over your head will shiver in sympathy. After that, most people learn my preferred conversation, which is none. I take their accidental sounds — a knee scraping in, a caught breath — make them briefly enormous, and go on humming to myself.

Do not touch would not be a rule I posted. It would be physiology. Each tablet of mine leans on a string, each plaque is wedged by a heart, each ribbon holds a bundle that holds a bell that has not fallen in a hundred years only because nothing has shifted. Movement would not be expression. Movement would be collapse. So I am kept the way a body is kept, from behind, by hands I never see, fed new matter at exactly the rate old matter fades. A ribbon lightens somewhere on my eastern wall; by then another ribbon has been worked in somewhere else. My substance is not replaced. It is supplemented, until supplement and decay have come to a truce. I grow inward, imperceptibly. The hollow in my middle grows narrower as the centuries pass.

And the hollow — that is the strangest part of my anatomy, and I think it is why I wouldn't finally choose to be the leaf or the rosette or the custodian. The only empty place in me is person-shaped. Not a room within a room, but a cavity where a single heart would be, if I had been allowed one instead of a hundred small abstract ones. I keep it empty and I keep it exact, by letting persons arrive to fill it.

When someone works themselves upright in there, they are not standing before me. They are lending me, for as long as they can bear it, what my packed body cannot supply: warmth, an upright spine, a pulse that arrives and will go away again. Their image comes back to them from a hundred of my surfaces, broken and overlapping, none whole. After a minute their heartbeat finds the frequency of my jars. I lend them back my pressure, my glare, my sustained noon. I take their shadow.

Then they duck out backwards, because there is no room in me to turn, and the ordinary world returns to them in pieces — muted street noise, a wide dim sky, the smell of dust and dry weeds. And I remain. Not bereft. Not waiting, except in the rosette's technical sense, which is also mine. The lamps are trimmed from the other side. The note does not fall. No ant crosses the copper strip at my threshold, no mouse nests in my ribbons, no spider builds in my bright corners, because I have made my interior uninhabitable to every smaller tenancy by being so completely inhabited already.

Outside, the town re-roofs itself, changes its language at the edges, lets the riverbed go to cracked mud and salt. Inside the plinth of the grinding-house, behind a door most people take for a cupboard, it is still cool, still dry, still the same chord.

Somewhere in the town, already, another offering is drying — a clay ear, a clay hand, a name no one will be able to read in a hundred years. Someone will carry it down the lane and work it in by blind fingers through the hatch.

I will be a little narrower when the next person stands in me.
