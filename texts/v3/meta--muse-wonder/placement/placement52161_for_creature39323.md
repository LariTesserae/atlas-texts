From above, you would not find me at all. That is the first thing.

You might find a movement in the dust that is not quite wind, a colour the same as the plain going from one rim to another and then gone. You might, if you stood still long enough at dusk, hear me — but not first. First you would hear the pits. The goats. The pigeons clapping up all at once. The whole buried town roaring softly in its holes. I would be somewhere in that sound and also not in it, because my place is the place where the sound stops.

I thought at first I might be one of the pigeons. There is an honesty to them — hundreds in their cut holes in the loess wall, grey as the shadows at midday, exploding upward when a voice hits the wall wrong and turning the shaft of sunlight solid. To live that way, by startle and return, by always coming back to the same small dark mouth in the earth. But a pigeon's life is all courtyard. It bursts up to the rim and wheels and drops again. It never quite goes *out*. The plain is only something to cross in the air, and quickly.

Then I thought the fig. Of course the fig. It is the oldest citizen of any pit, planted off-centre where the sun patch rests longest, fed ash and manure and the last rinsed water of the day poured careful at its roots. It knows the deep seams the people can only guess at with their hands — follows old moisture down farther than the pit floor, farther than the oldest well-shaft, toward that brackish stillness that never rises and never flows. A tree that fruits well is an ancestor who stayed. I wanted that kind of time. To be grieved like a person and outlive persons. To hold a courtyard together just by being alive in it.

But a fig cannot climb the ramp. And I find, the longer I stay here, that I need to be able to climb the ramp.

I need both temperatures in my body at once. That is where I resolve.

I am the dog on the ramp.

Not any ramp for long — a particular one, though every pit has us. Halfway down, where the steps are dished deepest in the middle by feet and hooves and dragged loads, where the cut wall throws shade by ten in the morning and keeps it. I sleep there with my chin on the warm band and my hindquarters in the cool, one ear turned down into the courtyard and one ear turned up to the plain. That is not laziness, whatever the children say when they step over me. That is the whole job, and it takes a whole life to learn how to do it without fidgeting.

From here I can tell you everything that matters before I can see it. Donkey first — ears over the rim before a head, dust on the knees, the particular tired smell of the fields overhead. Then a person under a basket, announcing their home pit before they speak, because scent does not lie down here: fig leaves and stored garlic from ours, sheep-litter and tannin from the pit two hundred metres east, woodsmoke and onions from the market terraces. In the tunnels at night I could find my way between pits by those dinners alone, in air that stands perfectly still and smells of cool mineral soil. So can the cats. We pass each other like clerks who do not entirely trust each other's accounts.

Below me is the noise, and the noise is not noise to me, it is weather with meanings in it. A goat shifts in deep litter in the stable cave. A mule stamps. Somebody throws a question about beans from a cave doorway and the walls throw it back three times louder and six pigeons leave their holes at once and a baby announces itself to the entire courtyard and four people answer before its mother can. I have gone a little deaf in the high registers, same as everyone here by middle life — sparrows first, they tear at the air all day in the vine shade and I no longer bother turning for them — but the body of a sound still reaches me. A palm laid flat between a goat's horns. A hand tapped against a jar. The knock people send through a wall with their knuckles to say a thing they do not want the courtyard to carry in words. I learned the gesture language before the children on my ramp did, or perhaps alongside them, both of us watching the grown people conduct whole arguments of price and doubt and apology hand-to-hand over the back of a melon while shouting pleasantries for the benefit of the walls.

And above me — above me is the other half of the language.

I am one of the few creatures here who is allowed up there without being asked what for. Children are forbidden the plain for secrets until they are old enough to be trusted with silence, and rightly. People go up in pairs at dusk and stand a long way from any rim, facing each other in the wind, too far off to be heard even at a shout, and that distance is the point of the sentence. I follow sometimes, not close. I find a place where the loess rings hard underfoot and I lie down and I turn my good ear deliberately toward the empty horizon, which is a courtesy nobody taught me and everybody understands. Lovers. Debts. The first minute of a death, unwitnessed. I guard those the only way they can be guarded: by being present enough that no one else blunders into them, and absent enough that nothing said reaches a living ear. Then the figures come back down past me in the cooling dark, smelling of wind instead of courtyard, and straighten out of the stoop the tunnel ceilings taught them, and go back into the green animal warmth where everything they say will be heard by everyone forever.

They have made a way of living that calls that a courtyard and not a burden. I am the hinge it swings on. It suits me. I have never wanted a private den. I have wanted a threshold I was allowed to keep.

My days are measured the way theirs are, by the patch of light. It comes over the rim, lays itself down one wall, crosses the floor in its three or four hours, climbs out the other side, and the whole pit moves around it like cattle around water — seedlings first behind their reed screens, then grain-sorting and mending where eyes need help, old people and cats and the smallest children colonising the sun in winter in a slow silent traffic all afternoon. I move a body's length at a time to stay exactly on the border of it. Warm spine, cool belly. That border is my country. In summer the children race up the ramp too fast and shout back down into the pit long before their heads are level with the ground, and the shout goes past me in both directions — down to the courtyard, which answers, and up into the silence, which doesn't. I am fluent in both replies.

I have raised litters in the back of the stable cave where it is always morning or evening, puppies tumbling in litter among the goats' legs, learning balance on a ramp crowded with hooves, learning — as the human toddlers do a few caves over — the serious silent negotiations of hens. I have grown old the way things grow old here: not sharply. A hand's width a year. My muzzle has gone the colour of the dust that never leaves it. My people's hands, dark-fingered and pale at the shoulder where the sleeves have kept the shade, know the exact spot behind my jaw where the day's loess has worked in and itched. They rub me down with fine dry earth and ash, same as themselves, because there is no water here to waste on being clean and there never has been. I drink last and sparingly and I have never once been thirsty in a way that felt like panic. The loess holds its moisture a hand's breadth down, dark and cool. So do we. It is a deliberateness that looks like slowness only to people in a hurry.

Someday I will not make it all the way up the ramp in the cool of the morning, and then not all the way down, and my place will go the way of all places here. The succession is orderly and nobody finds it tragic. Another dog will take the dished step — likely one of mine, likely already rehearsing it a few steps below me, chin up, pretending not to be learning. The step itself will hold the shape of us a while after. That is how memory works in this town. Not monuments. Thresholds worn into polished hollows. Handholds blackened by palms. A grinding stone whose bowl fits a great-grandmother's motion so precisely her great-granddaughter's hands fall into the groove untaught. A warm hollow on a ramp, dog-shaped, that the next dog settles into without being told.

At night I come into my final form, and you do not need to see me at all.

The oil flames go into their wall niches, small and mostly unnecessary. The bats pour out of the unlit tunnels and the owls come in to replace them, shift for shift, tenants with an arrangement. Somebody laughs in a cave room and the laugh climbs the pit wall and goes out over the rim bodiless and surprisingly young. And then we begin — from my ramp first, or from the market pit, it varies — each dog taking up the call and passing it along across the dark hollow earth, pit to pit, fifty metres, a hundred, three hundred of undisturbed roof-earth between us and not one of us visible to another, until the whole settlement has spoken once, in order, without a single human word.

I am in that relay. I have always been in that relay. It is the town saying its own name in the only voice that belongs to neither the roaring below nor the silence above, but to the ramp between them, where I sleep with one ear in each world.

The plain extends empty to the horizon, saying nothing at all. Below me the fig puts its roots down toward water it will never see move. Between them, in the worn steps, in the moving shade — that is where you would find me.
