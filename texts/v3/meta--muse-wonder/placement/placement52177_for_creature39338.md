I don't think I'd arrive well.

I'd be one of the newcomers you hear about for the first week — windows up on the switchback road, already feeling it in my teeth before I could see the bowl. The headache would start somewhere below the plateau and keep perfect time with the engine. By the time the heat came up in that warm, wet breath off the limestone, I'd be trying very hard not to be sick, not from the altitude, but from the loneliness of being in a crowd where no one seems to need to look at anyone else to know they are talking.

You'd find me first, I think, in all the wrong places. Standing too long in the empty plazas between districts, enjoying the brief, relative lessening there. Sitting with my back to a cistern tower at noon, trying to learn how to be deaf in only the right frequencies. Sleeping badly despite the bolted bed trembling, because my body would keep treating the roar as a message it had to stay awake to decode.

I wouldn't be any use at the First Drop. That's clear early. People who work there have a grandeur to them — that forward set of the shoulders, spines already learning to lean toward the fall, palms out in the turbine hall listening three weeks ahead of the gauges. I would admire the tuners completely and fail their work completely. Put a copper coin between my back teeth and press my jaw to wet basalt and I would feel, vividly and unhelpfully, everything at once: the bearing, the water over old stone, someone re-seating a gate two districts off, my own blood. I wouldn't know how to let most of it go. And letting go, here, is not inattention. It's the whole physiology of peace.

I probably wouldn't be a swift, either, though I'd envy them. To live in the dry rim cliffs, outside the noise, and only drop through the spray at dusk hunting insects dazed by vibration — fast, silent, never landing. That's a cleaner kind of creature than I am. I stay. I get damp. I learn the slower routes.

Where you'd actually find me, after a year or so, is in between.

On the long trams, to start with. I like vehicles that have to cross emptiness to be useful. I'd learn to read sign across a rocking carriage, across a plaza, forearms aching in the good new way, hands too big in their gestures at first and then, slowly, funny. I'd be good at the part of the city's life that happens because its deafnesses don't match. Second Drop married into Reservoir, a child's name pitched exactly where her father can't hear it anymore, a young millwright from the Third home on leave trying to explain a bearing's wrong note to an old woman by the First who has lost that band entirely and has to feel it through his sternum with two fingers while he speaks.

Children of cross-district marriages are prized as interpreters here, and I think I'd apprentice myself to that work whether or not I had the birth for it. Not the engineer. The go-between. The one called into a courtyard because a marriage, a bargain, a diagnosis, a joke is getting lost not for lack of language but because two bodies have been tuned by different water. I would stand close — closer than lowlanders like — and learn not to flinch from it. Hand flat to a forearm to get attention. Forehead briefly to a child's forehead. Listening not only with ears, which are unreliable here anyway, but through the sternum, the soles of the feet through bolted floors, the spoon against a cup on the lee side of a wall.

And in the afternoons, between two and five, you'd find me doing it badly and then better: sitting in someone's courtyard with my hands in my lap. That stillness would not come naturally. I'd want to repair things at the speed of noticing them. This city would teach me otherwise at the pace of a mule on a towpath. A gate being re-seated. A basalt block being re-cut over a month to fit a curve laid out three hundred years ago. Nothing finished, everything kept at exactly the rate of its erosion. I'd start to understand that the sitting is not the rest between work. It is part of how you pay for living inside a sound that never stops. Let the vibration pass through like weather. Let the eloquent hands be quiet.

Eventually — this is the shape that keeps coming back, so I'll let it stay — I'd have a small workshop on the lee side of a house in no important district. Not near enough to a fall to be prestigious. Twenty houses, a cistern tower, laundry strung with steam, and then half a kilometer of scrub and wet rock. One fig in the courtyard. One olive grown in the shape of its shelter. My workbench would smell of machine oil, hot stone, algae drying green on reed mats. I'd make the things that let people carry the city with them when they have to leave it.

The clockwork water boxes. Small reservoirs, a fall of three inches, endlessly recirculated, for traveling millwrights who cannot sleep in the silence below the plateau. Not loud. Just enough to tell the teeth the world is still running. I'd spend absurd care tuning each one — not to a note exactly, but to a household. A Second Drop family wants the rush a little higher. By the First they need it lower, almost only a trembling in the box's wooden base, something for the hand pressed to the sternum of the night. I'd test them the tuners' way, coin in teeth, jaw to the side, learning at last to hear not everything, but the one wrongness: a bubble, a catch, a place where the little fall stutters and would wake a traveling child at 3 a.m. in a quiet lowland hotel room that feels airless without shaking windows.

I'd also keep the shims. Copper coins in a chipped bowl, for doorframes that have walked out of true. Spare slates and chalk for children whose arms still tire before their voices do. I'd pre-swallow nothing — leave that to better craftsmen — but I'd learn to pre-swell wood in tanks so a repaired shutter would fit its swollen frame. I'd bind my own slow-healing cuts with the green poultices from the sluice ferns and cough in the mornings until the mist burned off and think nothing of it.

My sign would develop an accent, probably. A bit of tram-conductor sweep from crossing the empty distances, a bit chopped vertical from talking in narrow millraces, entirely too much vocabulary for small distinct sounds. People would know me by that the way you know a Second Drop family from a Reservoir family. I'd be teased for signing *spoon*, *insect*, *baby two streets away*, with a reverence out of proportion to their usefulness most of the year. But then most of the year I would need the roar to hold all those small separate voices together into one bearable thing. I understand that mercy.

On the one night they stop the First Drop, I would not be brave about it. I'd have posted the notices myself, checked the flashing beacons twice, and still come out into the street with everyone else feeling exposed by the ordinary world getting through. A dog astonished at its own bark. Insects existing after all in the scrub. My own normal voice carrying obscenely far and making me whisper, hands clasped tight so they don't betray me. Old women weeping beside me without quite knowing why, and me weeping too — not because silence is beautiful, exactly, but because it is so unmerged. Everything separate. Everything having to be listened to on its own.

And when the gates opened again in the small hours, when the white fall rebuilt itself step by step and the turbine took up its note and the windows resumed their almost invisible trembling, I wouldn't cheer. I'd exhale the way they do. Shoulders down. Hands unclenching, rising again into easy, tired conversation. I'd go back inside the thick wall, through the corridor, to the courtyard where the fig leaves were still.

Not a tuner of great falls, then. A tuner of small ones people sleep beside. Not native to any single drop, which is its own kind of deafness and its own kind of use. A person whose body slowly learned — jaw heavier, forearms ropy, inner ear thickening in its own idiosyncratic pattern — to inhabit a pressure that never lifts, and to make, for others leaving or marrying or traveling or grieving, a way to carry just enough of that pressure with them: a hand pressed forever, gently, against the sternum, which over enough time, learns to press back.
