At first I think I would be high.

It's the obvious place to want, up where the cloud-sea stops below the rim and the light comes in thin and unobstructed. A crown leaf on a tower, twenty meters up, broad enough to drink all morning and drip condensation down my own gutters by afternoon. I can imagine that life very clearly, which is suspicious. In this place, imagining yourself as the thing that seizes the light mostly means imagining the brief part before the shade you made kills the plants holding you up.

Towers live hard and know it least. I don't think I have the appetite for that kind of race — to grow on a structural lie, fatten tubers in the sun while underneath me, in sequence, my supports are already yellowing.

Lower, then. The rim of a basin, maybe.

That has a cleaner shape to it. Weeks of holding. Leaf overlapping leaf, wax-sealed against a rising mirror of brown-green water, while submerged stems ferment in the warm dark behind me and the level climbs a fraction every day. Then one coordinated extravagance: to bloom white and scent-heavy for no bee, no nose, and let the flowering be the timer. A strip of us dies back cleanly, the basin cuts itself a door, and everything I was holding leaves at once in a warm flood, carrying pollen and fragments and news downstream.

It's beautiful, and it is a single sentence. I would like to say more than once.

So not the crown, and not the rim. Not the dam either, though I linger there — a hundred controlled leaks, a body made mostly of deciding how much *not* to hold. And not the core stalk of anything, stiff and silica-edged, racing straight up because the shade of my neighbors has trained me into desperate verticality.

I think I am in the seams.

You would find me first, if you knew how to look, in a plaza. Not crossing it importantly, just *being* most of it at ankle height without quite being the groundcover itself: the pale green runner-thread that lives threaded through that flat, springy interlace. Root hairs down in the film of water just below the surface, testing the flow. Small leaves kept cropped by my own habit. Apparently empty. Apparently waiting.

The plazas are load-bearing emptiness here — solar collectors, evaporation pans, floodways — and I like the honesty of that work. To stay low and open on purpose. To hold a space in full sun without filling it prematurely, and steam faintly after rain, and keep adjusting, millimeter by millimeter, to where the water wants to go next.

And then the basin upstream blooms and fails, and the flood comes sheeting across us, warm, spore-thick, smelling of fermentation, and waiting stops being the job.

Then I run.

That is my other speed. Overnight, runners cross open ground. A fragment of me torn loose in a rerouted channel catches three basins away and I am suddenly there too, a first green thread in fresh wreckage. Where a tower has sighed and listed and laid its whole twenty meters down into the water, I am among the first into the corpse — not mourning it, mining it. Rootlets working down through root-fiber and hollow stem-brick and hot leaf-masonry, softening last season's architecture, pulling its stored minerals back up into green tissue. If this city has memory, it is stored by being eaten, and I am one of its readers.

Given a bundle of new stalks anywhere — a wall beginning, a colonnade starting at a slight experimental angle, a staircase differentiating itself out of what yesterday was chest-high meadow — I find them within hours and climb. That's when I become audible. Tendrils tightening around a support in the evening quiet, that small creaking strain under all the roar of the falls. Stiff plants take the wind-load; I turn a bundle of poles into a column. Broad leaves sprout above me and steal light and make the shade that will train the next generation straight, and I hold, and hold, knowing the holding changes the watercourse at our base, which changes what can grow across the plaza, which changes where the next wall will be needed. Shade is a sentence here. So is a leak. So is the squeal of pressure in a hollow stem. I am not the presiding mind of any of that. There isn't one. I am just one of the ways the place talks to itself without a nervous system.

I would live most completely, I think, in a doorway.

Not passing through it — nothing does, nothing ever has. Growing across it slowly, exuding at the pale unfinished edge, making the threshold slightly more itself every afternoon. On one side of me, weather. On the other, a room where the air is a little warmer, a little more scented, where rain is guttered noisily away instead of falling straight. That difference in humidity is the whole purpose. A way for the outside to be briefly, usefully inside something. I don't need a tenant to justify the enclosure. Being the braid that makes *inside* possible is enough of a life, watched at the right speed.

And when the wall I am in grows too fast and splits with a crack you feel in your teeth, or when my own shade finally undermines us and the whole structure shudders and comes down in slow, wet timber — I don't end. By nightfall the wreck is steaming. By morning I am colonizing myself. Within days the corpse has stopped being a corpse and started being a foundation, slightly higher, slightly richer, already putting out translucent yellow-green flags.

That turnover is supposed to be the real inhabitant here, wearing tower and plaza and rim-flower in succession. I won't claim the whole metabolism. Let me just be the part of it that binds and unbinds: low in the floodway, tight in the wall, soft in the rot. The part that survives not by keeping the light — height is temporary and everyone knows it but the towers — but by being ready, in root and runner and tendril, to become the next shape the water requires.

At evening, when growth sounds briefly outnumber water sounds, you could hear me if you stayed still across an entire plateau: leaves unfolding, stems extending, and underneath it, a thousand small tightenings as I take hold of something new.
