I go down slowly.

Not all the way at first. I stop halfway on the ramp where the wind gives up, where the air from the plain scrapes over the rim and can't be bothered to follow. Below me is the permanent late afternoon of the town, the pink blocks already in shadow, the work lights already on over the power hall though it isn't night. I can hear it before I can properly see it — pumps, fans, frogs tuning up in the drains, a truck idling somewhere because no one dares turn it off.

I think I might be one of the dogs by the clinic, at first. That has an appeal. To sleep in the cool exhaust, to belong to no one and be known by name by everyone, to lift an ear at a passing truck and decide, wisely, that nothing in this heat is worth chasing. A long-suffering life. A life pared down to shade and breath.

But I'm too restless for the dogs, and too fond of watching.

I drop lower. Past the market roof where the sparrows live, past the pipe racks where the rats travel valve to valve above people's heads, their own streets above the streets. I could be a cat — thin, scarred, officially discouraged, unofficially fed on every stoop, patient as a landlord beside a woman sweeping beetles from her threshold. The cats have status here. They have work. But the cats still have to negotiate. They get chased, they get named, they leave their headless offerings and are praised or scolded for them. They are too involved with the humans, and the humans, for all that they run the pumps and keep the town from drowning, are not the main event in the pit.

Lower still, and I am nearly a frog. Throat-deep in the warm green sumps, fist-sized, one of ten thousand gullets grinding at dusk until the call stops being a call and becomes a pressure in the ears. That is tempting, too — to be so completely suited. Warm standing water, reeds, no winter, no wind. To dissolve into the chorus. But I don't want to dissolve. I want a wall.

So I find myself, in the end, on one.

I am a gecko.

Not on the sunlit outside walls with the lizards — at noon, when the sun drops straight into the pit for its ninety murderous minutes and everything flashes white, even they go sluggish and stupid with heat. My country is the other side of the wall. The ceiling over a porch. The plaster above a blue-white bulb, where the damp has made the paint bubble faintly and the salt creeps up from below in its slow fans. I live in the permanent shade the town makes for itself, and I come into my own early, when the yellow lights come on in the dimness and the moths arrive so thick they soften every beam.

That is my hour. That is my harvest.

All day I have been still. That's the discipline of it. Flattened in the crack where ceiling meets wall, cool belly to the plaster — cool being a relative term here, everything is warm, the rock itself will give back the day's heat at seven in the evening — watching upside down. I see the town the way it actually is arranged: not street by street, but threshold by threshold. The double doors. The net tucked in with a shipwright's precision. The shoes shaken out in the morning by hands that don't even look anymore. The cup covered the instant it is poured, the plate covered the instant it is set down, because my neighbours the flies are faster than courtesy, they're faster than thought.

I watch the children do their homework under the porch bulb. They are the most pit-native of the humans, these ones who have never known a wind that reaches the bottom, who chase lizards at noon and know which stairwell currently has wasps. At night I watch them back, unblinkingly, which is not menace, it's just that I have no eyelids to blink with. They don't mind. I am furniture that occasionally moves.

And when I move, I move well. My feet are the one elegant technology in a town of pumps and scaffolding. Each toe spreads and holds, spreads and holds, on damp plaster, on painted concrete, on the underside of a tank where the condensation beads. The pit weeps everywhere, and everyone else is slipping — algae in the channels, salt on the boots, mould at the backs of cupboards — but I walk the ceilings of the wettest buildings in the world and I do not fall. The raw limestone weeps for the frogs, the sumps rise a centimetre a day for the mosquitoes, the mosquitoes rise in columns for the dragonflies and the swifts. The moths come to the light for me.

I eat, I scatter, I return. A child is told, again, to shut the screen door properly, and it bangs, and the vibration goes through the wall into my feet and we all flinch off across the ceiling in a small pale panic, then settle again one by one. Below us the woman sweeps beetles from her threshold. The cat waits beside her. The fried onions drift down from the top street through the hot iron and wet limestone smell of everything. The pump indicators show red along the channel. The bats are pouring out of the fissures above the top terrace now, thin black smoke against the last of the light, going up to eat what I miss.

I miss very little.

That, I think, is why I stay in this shape. The pit is a trap for anything that can thrive on being trapped, and most of its citizens thrive loudly — the frogs grinding, the swifts screaming between the blocks, the pigeons crowding every unwired ledge, the red ants rerouting around a boot without breaking stride. I thrive quietly. I am not the town and I am not its infestation; I'm the line where the two stopped being distinguishable, up there on the ceiling with the salt blooming through the paint around my feet.

Above the rim, I am told, the plain is empty and silent and blinding, and the little acacias lean away from us as if embarrassed. Let them. Nothing up there has learned how to live. Down here, just before dawn, when the rock finally cools to the temperature of a held breath and the frogs thin to single gulps, I will go still again in my crack above the porch, belly to the warm wall, and listen to the bats come home over my head — and I will have eaten, and watched, and not once needed the ground.
