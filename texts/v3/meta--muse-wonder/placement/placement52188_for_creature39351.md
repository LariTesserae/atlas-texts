I wouldn't come down the switchbacks, I think. Not at first.

Visitors do. They come down from the heath in a car and feel the sound arrive through the floor before they've understood the place, and they spend the day shouting and getting a headache behind one eye. If I started there I'd only be passing through.

I'd find myself above the town, in the queue.

In the slow, flattened spiral over the cooling towers, turning with the gulls and the jackdaws and the other starlings. Not migrating. Not hunting, exactly. Waiting. There's a difference, and down there everything has learned it. From up here the town stops looking sparse. The wide streets and the far-apart hostels aren't empty at all — every ridge and rail and pipe-run below is feathered, shoulder to shoulder, all of us facing into the same warm draft with our beaks a little open.

My whole life would be measured in vacancies.

I would circle for hours, for days, patient as weather, watching a roofline that never changes even though the birds on it are constantly dying. A gull tips sideways off the clinic sign. Before the body has cleared the gutter, someone is already dropping. A pigeon goes into a fan above the rendering plant and the wire does not stay empty long enough to cool. That is not cruelty here. That is arithmetic. The plant makes food at one rate, heat at one rate, midges and standing water and shelter at one rate, and we solved ourselves to fit it a long time ago.

Sooner or later — midday, probably, because time down there is mostly midday, nine degrees and grey and damp — a space would open for me.

A starling's space. On the pipe-run over the canteen door, say, third from the bend where the lagging has gone soft and grey with fungus and old nesting. The bird before me would be gone so completely that I would never know its name, if starlings had names. I would drop out of the spiral, land exactly where its feet were, feel the warmth of the pipe come up through my toes, turn into the draft from the towers, settle, and open my beak.

And that would be it. I would be home, in the way anything is home here: by holding a position.

I wouldn't be the falcon in Cooling Tower Two. Too senior, too singular. I wouldn't be one of the ravens in the dead siren housing either, with their long memory and their private exemption from the emergency plan. I would be much more ordinary than that, and ordinary is the real condition of the place. A starling. Black in a way that isn't quite black when the even light catches it — oil-green, purple at the throat, speckled white with guano dust I would never entirely shake off. One of the vents-feathering, drain-cover-shaking, roofline-keeping thousands.

My days would not have a shape so much as a level.

I would eat what the town provides at the rate it provides it: midges thick enough to halo the streetlights at noon, spilled grain by the loading yard, rendering waste, a seed in the gravel that has to be taken within the hour or not at all. I would drink from a green-black puddle with a painted line at its edge, the water exactly at the line. Below me the drain covers would tick faintly in their frames from the frogs, that bass calling coming up through the culverts and into the pipe under my feet, into my hollow bones. I would raise chicks in the warm lagging — in December, in July, it would make no difference — because under the cold store it is always blood-warm and always the first evening of spring, and nobody ever told my body how to stop.

And I would learn the second language.

First the reversing beep of the yard trucks, because it's simple and everyone practices it. *Beep. Beep. Beep.* From the roof, absentmindedly, the way people hum. Then the two-tone warning before a pressure release, traded back and forth across the street with another starling until it stops being an imitation and becomes just something we say. I would get very good. All the starlings are very good. Eventually I'd do the ammonia alarm perfectly — right pitch, right duration — and a new worker below would look up, startled, before an older one touched his arm and shook her head and murmured something lip-close into his ear.

That's how the people would come to me: not their shouts, though I'd know those too, the same greetings at the same shift change. Their murmurs. The low, flat, almost breathless speech they lean in close to make under our noise. I would hear fragments of it all day from the canteen doorway and have no use for most of it, except as sound, except as one more repeated note to learn.

The official cat from the canteen would sleep on the transformer housing across the yard, steam curling around her numbered collar, sparrow chicks walking unconcerned over her paws. The rats would commute below me along the warm pipe-bridge, glossy and unhurried, pausing for the forklift. At dusk the bats would leak from the ventilation shaft in their continuous ribbon. None of us would be chasing anything properly. Chasing forever, in the middle of abundance, is just another way to starve.

If I travelled — if some storm knocked me out over the quarry lip, up onto the bare heath where the cloud sits low on wet stone — I think the quiet would frighten me in my sleep. No compressor through the ground. No frogs in the drains. No chorus braiding itself through everything. I'd wake at 3 a.m. in that ringing absence the residents talk about, and find my way back down toward the one sustained note.

Because the deepest thing I'd learn, standing on that pipe with my coat of feathers dampening at the shoulders, is that I am not really the bird.

The perch is the constant. I am how it stays occupied.

One day I will tip off it — age, a fight, a moment's misjudgment near a fan — and I will barely have begun to fall before another starling drops out of the turning sky, lands in my exact outline, faces into the draft, settles its feet on the warm pipe, and opens its beak. The photograph of the canteen roof will look the same that evening as it did that morning. The sound will not change. The town will not notice a death, because here death isn't an interruption.

It's the mechanism by which everything stays.

I'd find myself there, mid-note, mid-spring, mid-replacement — not living in the equilibrium.

Holding one small, feathered piece of it in place.
