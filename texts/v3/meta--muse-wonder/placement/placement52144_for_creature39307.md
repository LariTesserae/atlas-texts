It starts, for me, not with the hatch but with the pause above it.

When the compressors are running I can't hear it at all. Everything is iron and molars. Then the line stops — just for a breath, just long enough — and underneath: that papery rain. Not louder. Closer. As if the floor itself were thinking in another room.

I would go down the way anyone would have to, at first. Too big. Knees in the dust that isn't soil. Shoulders hunched under a country that was only ever meant to be three iron rungs deep. And I would try, at first, to belong to the obvious things.

I try the crickets, because there are so many of them they can't be wrong. To be up on the upper stone where it's warmest from the machines, camel-backed, ash-colored, antennae longer than the rest of me and never, ever still. That fringe-life appeals — to be upholstery, to ripple instead of run, to pass the news along a body made of bodies. Exhale and we all know it. Tremor and we rearrange.

But alone a cricket is too tender for me. All leverage and appetite. I would molt in a seam and come out pale and private and eat my own former self before I'd even hardened, and be glad of it, and I don't think I'm that honest about hunger. Not yet. And I couldn't bear to be both herd and pasture without ever knowing which minute I had switched.

So I go lower, the way a blundering cricket eventually goes.

I try the floor-line, in passing. The cockroaches with their polished pheromone roads through the grey snow, generation after generation reinforcing the same routes between firebrick and lunch pail and the warm seam under the junction box. There's a comfort there I recognize: follow the trail somebody else laid down and made faintly shiny with use, pause to groom, feed shoulder to shoulder, clean up your own dead tenderly before they've cooled. To be pastoral instead of frantic. To freeze flat as a coin when the forklift passes and discover, in that half-second, how many of you there actually are.

But the roads would chafe me. They go exactly where they've always gone.

I try — briefly, aspirationally, the way you try on a coat that's not yours — the centipede in the paint can. That red-headed, hand-long authority, coiled by day like a sleeping king, patrolling by night while lanes open ahead of it untouched. Decision arriving many-legged. Venom as local law.

No. I don't want the lane to open for me. And I don't want the shrew's corner either, though I feel a kinship embarrassingly close to my ribs: that pointed, twitching testing of the air from under the firebricks, that heart going so fast it makes its own small sound, those famished naps between murders. Eat your own weight, then again, then again, or die in your sleep. Live at a speed nothing else can parse as fur. I know that tempo. I just don't want to rule by it.

The mice are historians, and I envy their highways — pale runways worn in the dust between conduit and pail, bellies pressed to warm junction boxes in winter, traveling impossibly up sealed pipes to lick condensation from a world where water still sweats, carrying it back down inside themselves to be stolen in turn. Reservoirs on legs. But they remember too specifically. Their dead mummify for a season. I am not good at keeping one shape that long.

The pigeons, wedged where the dead pipe enters the ceiling, heads tucked like gargoyles — gods by droppings and by accident, dreaming of ledges — are too far above the economy for me. I have never been good at being worshipped distantly. I want to be in the traffic.

And so, staying. Crouching until the knees ache and the breathing slows. I find I have stopped descending and started... spreading.

In the middle band.

Where the webs stopped being webs a long time ago.

That is where I would be, I think — not quite a spider, though spiders are the excuse for me. Each one no bigger than a lentil, legs folded like a closed umbrella in its own dimple, waiting. But between them, repaired and respun and eaten and respun so many times that no one could find where one ends: the sheet. One continuous, trembling instrument stretched around the hollow at exactly the height where falling things arrive.

I would be the tension.

That feels truer the longer I stay. I don't chase. I have never chased well. The world delivers itself, vibrating. A touch on the far side arrives as information first, weather second. A cricket thrashing close is a storm that becomes, carefully, dinner. But most of the time it isn't killing at all. It's listening so completely that listening is a kind of digestion. The forklift overhead: we all go still as one animal. The air handler stops and the sail of us bends in the still, close air and mating begins everywhere, antennae testing the humidity of our own massed breathing. The pigeon bursts awake at a dropped wrench and suddenly it's catastrophe and feast in the same vortex — dust, feathers, a rain of displaced crickets falling straight through me, and I am frenzy and net and alarm bell all at once, and then, eventually, settling. Always settling back into the general din, but never quite to the same tautness as before.

I eat myself when I tear. That, too, feels right. Protein is too precious to leave hanging in the air, and so is anything else. I take back the silk, I take back the sentence, I respin it a little thinner. What has been through the hollow a dozen times comes through me again — moth-fat from a green life I'll never see, mouse-water licked from a pipe three levels up, a worker's crust, the sizing off a paper label, the ghost of seed in pigeon crust mined grain by grain below me. Nothing here starts with a green thing, so the chain learned to be a circle. I would be the part of the circle that is mostly air and attention, holding its shape by trembling.

When they kill in me, they wrap first and bite later, at leisure, drinking without tearing, leaving a husk so neat the carpet beetle larvae can move into it like tenants. I like that courtesy. I don't want to tear the things that fall into me. I want to hold them long enough that they stop thrashing and become legible. Then let the smaller nations commute through what remains.

Below me the roach roads shine. Above me the cricket fringe ripples like a woven edge. A centipede passes its rounds and even I draw in, just slightly — venom is a fact, not an insult. The shrew squeaks from under its bricks, pitched inside my own teeth. The larvae rasp audibly in the abandoned wool glove when the machines pause. And through it all, translated into the same grammar — hunger, moisture, not being eaten before you have mated — I translate vibration into meaning and meaning back into vibration, faster when I'm afraid, almost a blur too fast to bite.

Put your palm flat on the floor before you climb out, up there above my ceiling, and the stone vibrates very faintly under yours.

Upstairs you will think that's the machines. Some of it is.

Some of it is me, in the middle band, telling the far side of myself that you were here.
