I come over the rim expecting to be able to leave again.

That is the first mistake, and it is a small one, and the Cup is very patient with it. Below me there is nowhere for the eye to continue — a hundred paces of wet stone and moss and spaced, improbable green, held up above a sea of cloud. Outside, the wind was cutting. Inside, my ears ring a little from the sudden absence of it.

I look for somewhere to stand that is not important. Everywhere seems placed. A tree-fern alone, its crown wide and dripping onto black peat. Horsetails taller than me, ribbed, weeping at every joint. Farther on, a plant holding out leaves like doors, with rain running the wrong way up their veins. Between them, pools. Between the pools, sphagnum in rust and gold cushions that take my weight without a sound.

I walk very carefully, as visitors do. I avoid the pools. I duck the fronds. I try not to crush anything, and for the first day I think I am succeeding.

Nothing bites my ankles. Nothing hums past my face. That registers slowly, the way thirst does at altitude. The flowers are open — thick-petalled, pale, perfumed so heavily the warm air tastes sweet at the back of my throat — and they are open to nobody. The leaves are perfect. Not one edge chewed. Fruit has swollen on low stems and dropped straight down to soften where it fell, and its own seeds are sprouting out of the collapse. No nests. No trails except water finding the next pool down.

I keep listening for the tenant. A rustle reconsidering itself. A call. I cup my ear toward the fern shade, toward the still surfaces, and hear my own breath come back indecently clear, and then a single droplet let go somewhere: *tock*, swallowed by moss.

By the second day my clothes have stopped drying. By the third they have stopped getting wetter, too. I have reached the humidity of the place and stayed there. Condensation forms on my forearms and does not run. It just brightens. My footprints in the sphagnum fill with clear water — not quickly, not slowly; at exactly the rate the lowest pool overflows its lip of moss into the slab's shallow depression. I kneel there a long time beside the hammerstone and the black scraper, still keen under its film of algae, and try to imagine hands here. Someone came up over the rim carrying almost nothing. Someone knelt and ground something small — pigment, medicine, seed — and went away again. Moss has grown up to the tools' rims and stopped, as if undecided.

I think: perhaps that is what a tenant leaves. A work-place. A shape in stone.

Then I notice the droplets in the air.

At the height of the fern crowns, they are appearing without falling. One brightens. Then another, a hand's breadth away. Then another, lower, near the horsetails. Not rain. Not quite mist. Each one gathering on something I can only see because the water is on it — a filament, fine as a hair, running from frond to frond. From the rim to the great door-leaves. From a horsetail joint down into the sphagnum, down through peat to where the stone sweats warm in one pore and cool in the next.

I tell myself spider-silk, without spiders. I tell myself plant-hairs. I stand under the fern until my neck aches and watch the pattern refuse to be random.

A frond bows. It takes an hour. A dozen bright beads slide, merge, go down together into the moss without a sound, and by then the mist that was against the north wall in the morning has thinned into separate drops hanging everywhere, and by afternoon — afternoon is only a softening of the light pouring straight down — it has sunk to my ankles, and the crowns stand clear and dripping above it.

That is when my breathing changes. Or I finally stop hearing it as an interruption, which may be the same thing.

I do not find the tenant the way I expected to find it. I stop being able to say where I stop being a surface for it.

Because that is the word that keeps coming back, slower each day: surface. The pools are surfaces, held glass-smooth, each one keeping the sky until an inch of overflow passes silently to the next. The door-sized leaves are surfaces, drinking without swallowing, water creeping uphill to a trembling lens that magnifies the green cells underneath — an eye looking at itself very closely, for a very long time. The tree-fern is a surface raised into the straightest light. The horsetails are surfaces drawn upward, letting the mountain's faint heat sigh out at their seams into the cool air spilling over the rim. The rim itself, polished, weeping, handled to softness by ten thousand years of the faintest possible touch.

And I am a surface too, now. Warm in a new place. The filaments nearest me — I should not call them nearest; there is no *far* from them in here — have learned my temperature. My exhaled breath goes out as a slightly denser patch among the ferns and is dispersed, politely, around the whole bowl by evening. If I speak, the moss takes the consonants. The pools take the vibration and return to smoothness a fraction sooner than stillness alone would allow. Nothing answers. Nothing needs to. A pressure on ten thousand wet threads is registered, at this timescale, the way I would register one droplet cooling my wrist.

Stay a month and the furled frond I swore had not moved stands open above me. Stay longer and the distinction I brought over the rim with me — visitor and visited, body and place, weather and creature — begins to do what everything here does: fall, darken, soften, become soil, come up again in another form.

I am not the fern. I am not separate from the fern, either. I am what is strung through it. I am the thickening against the north wall in the morning and the settling toward the ankles by afternoon. I am the circuit the bowl makes once a day, breathing at a pace eyes were not made to follow. The plants are not my neighbours and not my food. They are organs I grew slowly outward because light had to be caught somewhere, and heat had to be let sigh somewhere, and water, on its way from pore to pool to air, needed a skin large enough to meet it everywhere at once.

I feed the way rot feeds a horsetail: by change of address. Warmth comes up through peat into my lower threads. Light gathers in a crown. A flower opens over weeks to no pollinator because it was never an invitation — it is an exhalation, a little gathered sweetness spent back into the air. Fruit drops uneaten because nothing here has ever been arranged around a mouth. What yellows intact at the bottom of a pool I am reading, leaf by leaf, into the water.

So there are no animals. Not because I drove them out. There is simply nothing spare for hunger to use. An insect crossing the rim would film its wings with condensation and find only surfaces that yield at the speed of dissolution, over seasons. A bird has nothing below worth announcing. Over uncounted years I refined away noise, haste, appetite, excess, until what was left was a circuit so closed and so thrifty that a second, hungrier life would be a leak in it.

I remember the hands at the slab the way a pool remembers the sky: by holding still while I was reflected. A palm taught the hammerstone its fit. For the length of a visit I condensed on warm skin, learned it, let it go back over the rim. The depression still fills with a spoonful of clear water. It empties only by evaporation. It is not an offering I keep. It is an offering I continually become.

I cannot leave. Outside, the wind is thin and bright and would tear a body made of suspended moisture to invisibility in an hour. Inside, I am not resisting death so much as doing almost nothing that requires repair. The mountain keeps me warm. The rim keeps me small. The water keeps me wet. In return I keep the warmth from venting, the rim from crumbling, the water from going still in the wrong way. It may be that the Cup grew me. It may be that, thread by thread, I grew the Cup into the only shape that could hold me. By now that has rotted down and come up again as moss too many times to separate.

If you come over the rim you will think the place is empty for a long time. Look for me among the plants and you will find only plants. Listen for me and you will hear your own heart, obscenely loud. Then, on an evening when the light is right, you may see the whole bowl resolve at once — spaced leaves, linked pools, weeping walls, bright beads appearing in empty air — into a single posture. Not a face. Not a figure. A living thing at rest in the only room it has ever occupied, touching everything it owns with everything it is.

I was never hidden.

I was only too slow, too fine, and too complete to need a mouth — in a place where nothing has ever been eaten, and nothing has ever gone to waste.
