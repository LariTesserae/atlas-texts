I wouldn't be her.

That is the first thing I know, going down. Not out of fear — though there is fear, proper and clarifying — but because she is not really a creature in the chamber so much as a condition of it. Weather doesn't wonder what the rain is doing. The warm floor doesn't grieve the vines. She hangs in the northeast roots, pearl over pink, mineral crusting between her scales where the limestone has dripped on her for decades, and the whole world arranges its grammar around not saying her name too loudly. I could not be that kind of alone. I want a smaller sentence to belong to.

For a while I think I might be a root.

They come down blind from the ceiling, thicker than arms where they leave the rock, tapering to threads, leaves cupped upside-down to catch what the steam gives back. They grow toward heat the way things above grow toward light, and down here that feels truer than sight: a slow, unembarrassed leaning toward whatever will keep you alive. I like their patience. I like that they are mistaken for her, and she for them, until the eye learns to sort flesh from wood. But roots do not choose their leaning, and I think I would want — even under her, even here — the terror and privilege of being able to move at the wrong moment.

So perhaps a fish, in the highest pool. The cool one, black and perfectly still. No eyes, because eyes would be a lie I kept telling myself in perpetual dusk. Just a circuit memorized through skin: the cold seep in, the warm edge where the basalt breathes underneath, the place where an insect sometimes dimples the surface and everything changes. Turning, turning. Never breaking the pattern because the pattern is the pool, and the pool is the world. There is a peace in that I can almost settle into —

except the ants keep crossing my attention, up the western wall, each carrying its green flag twice its size, a whole purposeful nation that halts as one body when she shifts her weight above. Except the millipedes are towing themselves through the leaf litter on a hundred patient feet, glossy and unbothered. Except the white crickets in the root tangles are rubbing wings that forgot how to sing into something softer — scent, a chemical whisper, *I am here, I am here* said without sound. Everything down here has translated itself. I don't want only to circle. I want to signal, carefully, across a crowded room.

So I come down, in the end, lower than the roots and higher than the pools. I come to rest at sitting height, in one of the three carved bowls in the eastern wall.

I am the glass frog.

Not the emerald ones, bright as wet leaves, not the honest mud-brown ones. The translucent one, whose insides show as a darker smudge, whose heart you could watch if you knew how to look in green-black dusk. Nothing about me is hidden and that, down here, is not the same as being exposed. She does not hunt by seeing. She has never seen anything. My transparency is simply a fact, like the heat in the floor. What matters is whether I move half a second too long.

I live in the slick the last frogs left behind. The bowl is stone, stained dark by a century of water, and it is always full — of condensation, of eggs that are not all mine, of a parliament of futures jelly-soft against my belly. When there is no rain falling through the crack in that brief, drumming minute, I tilt my head back and open my mouth and let the cave feed me one bead at a time from a leaf-tip, from the underside of a stalactite blunt as a thumb. It is enough. Almost everything here is enough, arriving slowly.

To court, I do not call. No one calls. I pulse my throat without sound and lift one foreleg and wave it, slowly, an absurd and tender semaphore, and across the bowl another glass heart answers by becoming briefly, visibly quicker. The crickets make their scent. The eyeless fish pass a ripple fin to fin. The ceiling lizards pass a stillness outward, coin-flat against the rock, when something above them changes pressure. We have built a city out of everything except noise, and it is not a meek city. It is furiously busy. It is just busy at a frequency she has taught us not to use.

By what passes for morning the floor is steaming in low veils off the black basalt under its skin of red clay. The low jungle — broad leaves like wet leather, fungi the color of bruised fruit, vines racing across the heat after rain — opens and spores and collapses again inside an hour. The ant column diverts around a fallen leaf. A dozen of us leave the bowls; a dozen arrive. At midday the shaft comes down, greenened by ferns, forgiving everything for twenty minutes, and for those twenty minutes I can see the smudge of my own heart thrown faintly against the wet stone, and the eggs below me like a second, slower constellation.

Then, once in many days, the roots in the northeast corner begin to pay out length.

There is no strike. That is the thing no one above would believe. She simply unspools, that great pearl head descending on its own muscular column, mouth already a little open, tasting the steam for the one wrong note. The bead on the leaf above my bowl gathers and does not fall. The ants stop with their green burdens raised. I press myself down into the bowl until my heart is hidden against dark leaf-mould and only then do I let it hammer, because hammering inside water, inside stone, inside my own held body is almost — almost — not a sound.

She takes what moved. She swallows, which is the loudest thing she does and is not loud. She climbs herself back up, coil reclaiming coil, until the corner is ceiling again. A minute passes. Motion seeps back in at the edges, lizard to moth to cricket to me.

On the warmest nights she lowers one length of herself all the way to the slabs to digest by borrowed heat, trailing a single coil in the lowest pool, and the roots she has left sway minutely overhead and lean toward the warm hollow she has left in them. Even they forget her a little, in the direction of heat. Even they go on.

And if you came down — handholds worn smooth, footholds worn smooth, forty feet into air that has started to sweat — and you sat still long enough beside my bowl, the cave would begin its slow work of including you. Condensation would bead on your shoulders as it beads on the carved stone. A moth the exact gray of the wall would settle on your knee and fold itself into being you. My foreleg might lift, once, in your direction, before I thought better of it. Not a warning. Not quite a greeting. The beginning of a language we are still, all of us, inventing underneath her.

Then the root above your head would lean a fraction toward your warmth.

And a fraction behind it — this is the whole danger, and the whole welcome — so would hers.
