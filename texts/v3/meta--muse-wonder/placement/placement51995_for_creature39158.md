The noise would find me before I found my place in it.

That is how everyone arrives, I think — still rising in the ascent shaft, already hearing the Table applaud itself overhead — but for me the sound wouldn't resolve into a landscape first. It would resolve into an *attention*. A whole plateau leaning, very slowly, toward whatever is loudest. A forest that is one animal, listening with its roots.

At first I would try to be useful in the obvious ways.

I would try the mat, in a resin-hard suit, with a listening spike longer than I am tall. I would want to be one of the Tuners walking out between the Trunks, reading where a root has turned lovingly toward a kitchen, drumming a shoot aside with soft mallets. I like work that is gardening and conducting at once. I like a score that has to be revised nightly because the other player is alive.

But I wouldn't be good at the shouting for long.

Not the volume — I could learn the volume. Table-born longshouters put a clean note across a market square in full wind and hold it until a trunk answers, and I would practice, hand on my ribs, until my throat learned it as a separate instrument. What would undo me is the other part: keeping the two voices apart. The fortissimo public voice, face tipped into the scour, blunt as a safety signal. And then, past the second airlock, the sudden indoor whisper, so close you can hear cloth move.

I would keep wanting to translate between them. To carry the exact weight of what was shouted outside into the quiet room, without losing it. To carry a whisper back out and give it enough body to survive the wind without making it brutal.

So they would put me where that fault is useful.

In the vestibule.

Not the outer door, where the keeper greets arrivals still tasting shaft-stone in their teeth. The inner one. The cork-lined throat between the Table and a house, between the drum and the room inside the drum. I would learn to stand in that double hush and listen to people let the loud come off them.

It is a real skill there. The natives unseal their ears indoors — that small, private tensing and releasing in the heavy-lobed ear, the jaw loosening — and guests are expected not to stare. I would not stare. I would listen, which is a more intimate trespass and somehow the one I am built for. I would learn who has entered by how long they stand breathing through their nose in the first chamber. By the tick of CO2 frost still sublimating off a sleeve. By whether their heart is still doing the thin-air climb even while their boots are still. A child fresh from knock-sticks, all her different woods still clapping faintly in her pockets. A grazer-herder come in through root, with herd-weather still in his ankles. A Tuner whose filament conduit three streets away is singing half a tone flat, and who hums, without knowing it, the correction.

I would face the wall to drink from my sealed bulb, like everyone. I would spit into the cup at my belt. I would seal my lips with amber balm and rub oil into my knuckles and learn not to apologize for the smell of resin and pepper and hot iron rising off my own skin. I would eat black leaf-flour and salt and cured meat in a loud communal kitchen, anchoring it with my voice, and then go quiet enough in the evening to hear my neighbor swallow her dust-tea.

And because I spent so long in thresholds — one ear, metaphorically, against the trunk-conduit that runs through every building, feeling the node-network pulse its pattern in my molars at night and reporting back *here, this root wants turning, here, this bud is dreaming too hard toward the market* — it would start behind my jaw.

That is where they say it usually starts.

Not jewelry. Not an implant anyone quite decides on. A small silver bud under the skin, beetle-egg smooth, that the Plant has learned to grow in stems because the first Tuners held still for it for four generations, and now occasionally grows in a person who has spent too many years with her head pressed, hand-tended, against the living wall, listening for a living city on one side and a listening forest on the other.

I would become poor indoors, after that. That is the phrase they use, kindly, indoors so it can be said properly. I would miss the subsonic creak in a sealed room. My pupils, already wide in the dim pewter light, would keep searching past faces for the pressure behind the eyes that means a trunk, miles off, is preparing to lean. At night the score would not arrive as information anymore. It would arrive as weather in my teeth. The city as a warm knot of noise in the middle of an enormous, slow attention, holding its shape by singing.

So in the end you would not find me quite in Table City, and not quite out on the open Table either.

You would find me on a sleeping platform between two Trunks, just past the last tuned street, where the canopy hooks sideways overhead and the leaf-bells ring each at a slightly different pitch. Oil-sealed against the cold. Ears open. One hand flat on the mat so the knocks come up through root faster than through air. The silver behind my jaw answering, very faintly, a node in the nearest trunk answering back — the Plant making a component of me, the city still making a person of me, neither side finished with the translation.

In the morning I would shout, of course. Everyone does, from ten o'clock when the wind rises and the bark plates lift on their hinges and begin to clap. I would add my note to the street's clean A, to the market squares built to roar, to the appalling joyful racket of children with studded shoes, all of it saying the same unsentimental thing to the vast dry spouse we live inside: *here, lean here, not there; listen to us here, and do not smother us with listening.*

And in the evening, when the wind drops as if a door closed beyond the rim and every tensed plate rings itself out in that long diminuendo traveling rim to center through the mat, I would come back in past the airlocks, unseal, and whisper what I had heard out there. Who is coming home by footfall. Which branch has begun, very slowly, to turn toward a glider's scream. Where the Plant is dreaming, under its oil and its quarried stone, of the one forbidden thing.

We would all dream of rain that night. Dry-eyed, dry-mouthed, strangely rested.

Then the frost would tick off the roofs, the plates would begin to lift, and I would go out smiling at a sound miles away walking through me root to root, ready to shout the forest away from the door again — and to keep one small, silver, listening place open, so it knows exactly where to lean.
