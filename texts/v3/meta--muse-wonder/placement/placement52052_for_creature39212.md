I wouldn't stay one of the figures coming down on the rope.

I can feel that at first — cloth over the mouth, torch smoking, ears already ringing — but that shape is too temporary for the place. It only skims. It arrives loud and leaves quickly and builds nothing, and the cave closes over the disturbed crust behind it. If I let myself belong here, I'd have to get smaller, and slower, and give up on arriving from anywhere else.

I wouldn't be a bat, either, though for a while I drift up there.

To be a bat is to be the weather itself: packed flank to flank in the unseen dark, belly warm against other bellies, hearing your own clicks come back off bodies more than stone, dropping ten metres into air before your wings catch, streaming up through a sinkhole into cold grassland and moths and distant rivers, then returning tight-bellied to let all that outside country fall out of you onto the floor. That's the engine. That's almost too central. The cave is arranged as a fall beneath them. I think I'd be somewhere arranged *by* that fall. Somewhere that has learned to live on what's dropped.

So I go down. Past the fruit bats quarrelling nearer the light. Past the swiftlets stitching their dry clicks through the roar. Down through the fine, constant rain — urine, hairs, moth wings, mites, a pup sometimes, still warm — down to where the floor stops being stone.

Not in the middle of the guano. Not deep in it, though it's warm there, the only warm ground in eleven-degree air, and a spadeful of it would come apart in my hands into beetles and maggots and amber cockroaches. The middle belongs to the swarm, to things that burrow and breed and are eaten without ever having to travel. I would be at the edge, where the crust thins and breaks over limestone rubble. That's the hunting line, the border country.

There. Pale as a peeled root. Legs too long for my body, antennae longer than both, sweeping, sweeping.

A cave cricket.

My world would not be seen. That's the first relief, and the first truth of it. Eyes are a rumour other creatures bring in on their bodies — the rats come in sleek and dark and full-eyed from above, and for them the grey country between sinkholes is still a place of shapes. For me it has always been black, and black is not empty. Black has texture. My antennae read it a body's length ahead of me: the sharp new edge of a fallen slab, still angular as the ceiling it came from; a crack that gives back cooler air; the polished slick where a perennial drip has run for longer than I will live; a white fungal thread, almost food, almost just touch, until taste confirms it.

I live by grades of smell and grades of sound.

Toward the roost: ammonia thickening until it coats the mouthparts, chatter rolling overhead in waves, the floor turning soft underfoot, first dusty crust, then a give, then warmth rising through the soles of my feet. That's richness. That's dung fresh enough to eat, fungal threads on a dead beetle, another cricket's shed skin, once — a feast that lasts nights — a fallen moth chewed and dropped from a bat's mouth far above, still holding a trace of the outside in its powdered wings. Everything I have ever eaten flew here first, one way or another. The plains are in it. Rivers I can't conceive of are in it. I graze the country without leaving the dark.

Away from the roost: the ammonia thins, wet limestone smells clean, almost sweet by comparison, and sound separates out. A drip. Then, long enough later that I count it in steps, the same drip again, striking its pool with a note so regular I've built my geography around it. Another pool, a quarter of a mile off in cricket terms — which is to say, a journey, a whole province of rubble — answers with a lower note. I don't navigate toward light. I navigate toward that spacing. Open floor sounds like that. Danger, often, sounds like nothing at all: a mat of bats asleep overhead is still a loaded ceiling, and a centipede, hunting the cracks beside me, is quietest just before its speed becomes vibration in the stone.

Because the world *does* announce itself through the floor. A rat is an earthquake with a pattern — four beats, a pause to snatch, four beats gone, timed to the falls from above. If a pup drops nearby the whole rubble trembles once, terribly, and then there's a race written in footfalls: rat, perhaps snake after rat, belly-scales whispering over crust and sharp stone alike in a line that doesn't pause. A startled mat of bats overhead is not a sound so much as a change in the air on my back, ten thousand wings becoming a waterfall going upward, pressure behind where my eyes would be. I flatten into a crack then. I am very good at flattening. My body is an arrangement for entering stone.

Between those events, my own sound is small and constant. Tick. Tick. The probe and step of me, the antenna tapping ahead, the hind legs testing. Other crickets answer from their blocks, not in chorus exactly — there is no need to agree — but in a scattered, stitching pattern across the rubble, under the swiftlets, under the bats, under everything. If you stayed long enough as a human, you'd stop hearing us as separate from the dripping. We are part of how the cave tells time in a place that has refused seasons.

I would have my stations. A web to avoid — a spider has strung hers irregularly between two blocks on my route to the guano edge, and the threads tremble with a different weight than dew; she eats flies fattened on dung, and she would eat me with no change in her night's work. A flake of stone that holds the day's — I say day, but my body knows it only as a shift in traffic overhead, more wings leaving, more returning, the roar never actually thinning to silence — that holds, under it, a small colourless scorpion, waiting. I go around. A hollow with three shallow pools where, on the rare journeys, I drink the clean way the people do, not from the white-rimmed lens of water itself but where moisture films the stone beside it, and I feel through my feet the faint turning of the blind shrimp in there, grazing dust out of water that has never seen a leaf fall into it.

Under a sinkhole, once, the light cone would be a change in temperature more than a sight: a column of faintly warmer air at noon, smelling of fern and sour grass and real green things I cannot eat and do not trust. My kind thins out there. That's frog country, and algae on the wet stone, and insects blundering down from a world too bright to be safe. I keep to the edge of it the way I keep to the edge of the guano. Edges are where a cricket can be two things: fed, and not yet eaten.

I will be eaten, probably. That's not a tragedy in my grammar; it's just the next transfer. A centipede's venom works as well in permanent night as anywhere. A frog's tongue at a pool edge doesn't need me to be visible, only near. If I am lucky I last through several moults first, each one leaving a paler, hollower version of myself hung on a block for something else to find, my antennae lengthening each time toward a world they will never finish mapping. I might lay eggs in the warm crust where it meets stone, where they'll hatch into that warmth and hear, from their first hour, exactly what I heard, and my mother heard: the roar at the same volume, the drip at the same interval, frogs calling in a twilight that doesn't advance or retreat, eleven degrees on the skin in January and July alike.

Nothing waits for spring where I am. Nothing needs to. The bats will bring the moths in. The moths will fall. The dung will rise a fraction. The fungal threads will find what the beetles missed, and I will find the fungal threads, ticking across the sharp white rubble at the edge of the hills, reading the dark with two long whips of touch — not an inhabitant of the cave, exactly, but one of the small, patient ways the cave digests its own sky.
