I would come in the way everyone does — not through a gate, because there isn’t one, but already inside before I knew I had arrived.

My boots would be wet first. Not soaked through in one dramatic step, just slowly defeated: the moor sighing under me, pools filling my prints behind me, grass the color of old straw, and then a paved ford where I thought there was only marsh, a retaining wall, a house, three houses, and suddenly I am turning sideways in a lane for someone in a wool coat and not apologizing out loud, only lifting a hand, low, close to my body.

I don’t think I would belong to the far edges.

Not the south, where the water spreads out at last into reed-ponds and clears and the ducks are, and frogs start up in the evenings; that letting-go would make me restless in the wrong way. Not the northern quarries either, where the stone is fresher and the yards are louder and the rain that was made over the middle of the city spends itself and clears. I admire the quarrymen’s shoulders and I would be useless there. I am not a builder. A fresh-cut stone would embarrass me too, with its pale, unweathered face.

And I wouldn’t be a swallow, though I understand why the children go quiet for them. They are the one frantic, noisy, delighted thing, working the air over the canals for a few months, and then they are gone. I am not made for that kind of season. Not a pony either, muffled in sacking, bringing peat and wool in from the farms and leaving before dark. Not one of the grey cats, though I envy them their dry lofts and their right to be suspicious of everybody.

Down in the cold shadow under the boardwalks, maybe, for an hour I could imagine it: one of those small blind-looking fish drifting where the canal slows, feeling a change in the current before anything can be seen. Clear water, peat-brown water after a storm on the moor, the green thickening that means the reeds at the bend want cutting. But even they are going somewhere, however slowly. I need hands.

So: hands.

Broad, short-fingered, the knuckles already a little enlarged, sheep fat worked into the cracks in winter whether I remember or someone’s grandmother clicks her tongue and does it for me. Nails soft in summer. Hands that know the weight of a sluice-board on its rope sling without looking, that can turn a fallen wall-stone over in the sorting yard and find, by touch more than sight, which face had the moss and should go inward this time.

I would live somewhere unremarkable in the middle terraces, where the interior cloud gathers late in the morning. Not high, not low. On a stepped street — forty steps, say, each tread worn into that shallow bowl at the turn — where the lane goes under somebody’s second story, comes out as a stair, crosses a canal on boards, and narrows again. My house would stand on its stone plinth a knee’s height above the seep, and at night, lying still, I would hear the water moving underneath, and know things by it I couldn’t say I know: a leaf-matt forming three houses down, ice beginning to tick against stone in a snap, tomorrow’s rain — here, on this terrace, not twenty minutes uphill where my sister lives and where the weather will do something else entirely and it would be presumptuous to guess.

By day I would be one of the listeners. That is a real trade there, even if no one carves it on a cistern. In the morning I’d be out before the boards go up, moving along my stretch of channel with a wooden rake, combing out the algae, checking the reed-beds at the bend. Kneel. Ear near the plinth. Wait. The city eats most sound, but for the person who has lived inside the hush long enough, the culverts are talkative. A blockage has a sound before it has a sight: the flow goes thoughtful, then hurried, then wrong. I’d rap on a board with a stick. Raise a palm — *slow it* — to a boy with a market-boat who thinks the afternoon rise will let him pole one street farther uphill, and sometimes lets him, because that is how he is apprenticing and because the water, today, agrees.

At dawn I would take my turn at the sluices with everyone else. A hundred boards clattering up terrace by terrace is the clock, each district with its own lag, and I would know where I was in the city by that lag the way other people know bells. My whole work would be a refusal to finish anything. Clear the channel so it can silt again, slower. Cut the reeds hard in autumn so they can clean the water again in spring. Sort the stones of a failed wall on the spot and see them back up within the season. Air the bedding over the stone warmer every evening, even in summer, *especially* in summer, because a household is judged by whether it can keep a bed dry, and that judgment, delivered in no words at all, would matter to me more than praise.

I would eat hot and plain and early: broth steaming the oiled-cloth shutters, bread from the wall-niche, a little cold-water fish on stone, kale and onions from a courtyard garden drained with a fanaticism my neighbors would consider merely adequate. I would drink from the right cistern, above the carved hand, in the right month, and I would think — quietly, at a volume that carries one house and no further — that dry-country people are mad about water. I would have strong calves and broad, splayed feet and a permanent slight stoop, chin tucked against the chill that arrives ten minutes after cloud. Orange lichen on a sunward wall would be ornament enough. It is, honestly, a strong color.

And I would grow local in the way that looks like narrowness from outside and is, from inside, a form of attention. Married, if married, within a few terraces. My news arriving at the speed of women calling roof-yard to roof-yard over washed wool spread for the brief hard noon sun. Never seeing the far side of the city and feeling no wound in that. My vowels would sit on one side of a ridge you could step over without noticing, and my cousins on the other side would have a different word for sluice, and we would fail at each other’s jokes at family meals and find this ordinary, and correct.

If you visited, I would not show you the city. There is nothing to show that isn’t a gradient, a water-line, a repair. I would give you the dry pair of socks by the hearth, and that would be me saying the most important thing I know how to say.

Old, I hope they give me the sitting jobs: sorting stone, mending rope, watching a cistern fill and rapping the board when it reaches the carved hand — *this much, no more*. My hands curling gently inward, the shape of sixty years of wet rope. Hearing better than I ever did. Then carried up at the end, not down, to where the roofs thin and the pools take over, under a low stone that will be half-sunk and holding the sky within a season or two.

Not a founder. Not a stranger on the moor anymore either. Just one more pair of hands the place uses to weather itself — re-setting, listening, keeping one small stretch of water moving through stone, terrace by terrace, in a city with no edge that stays habitable precisely because nobody in it is trying to be done.
