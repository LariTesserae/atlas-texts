I would come in low, the way everyone does.

Not because I was looking for the garden — I think, at first, I'd tell myself I was just following a tiled corridor, ordinary daylight ahead of me — but because the lintel is made for stooping, and to get under charred wood bound with rope you have to make yourself smaller for a moment. On the other side I would turn, politely, to remember the way back.

And it would already be planted over.

At first I would be a visitor very carefully. I would try not to step on the small brown birds. I would try not to stare when a keeper smiled and held a bone wedge in teeth that were sharper for having been used. I would walk a lane between two planted walls trying to reach the far terrace, which I could plainly see, and I would pass the same blue pot three times and pretend that was a navigation problem I could solve by walking more attentively.

The keepers would solve it for me the only way anything is solved there. Not by direction. By task.

*Stand here after the re-potting. Wait before the vines are cut.*

And because standing in good heat is easier than arguing with geometry, I would stand.

That is where you would actually find me, I think — not at the well, not at the kiln that breathes and decides against it, but halfway along a path that has taken twenty minutes to turn one corner, in a patch of stored warmth between a wall of magenta flowers and a bed someone has just combed. Dust would already be finding the creases of my knuckles. I would keep rubbing at it, at first. Then I would notice it wasn't lying there the way dust lies on a visitor. It was beginning to pattern.

Tile-joint fine.

I suspect I would pass through several honest guesses about what I was becoming before I understood.

For a while I would want to be a lizard — they seem to have the clearest arrangement with the place. Warm side warm, cool side cool, patterned exactly like the tile they are not on, permitted long undisturbed afternoons. But lizards are on their way to being ornament, one summer at a time, glazed scales among leaves. I am too fond of answering back for tile-work. Yet.

Then I would envy the brooms. A broom left leaning at dusk is found at dawn a few inches into its sweeping, dust in a small perfect arc, and no one treats this as ambition or haunting. It has simply caught the habit of making. Someone picks it up mid-gesture and continues, as you would take over a sentence from a person who had paused for breath. That felt very close. Too close to examine all at once.

Because that, in the end, is what I would be for the garden. Not first a pot-mover, though my hands would learn that. Not first a planter, though I would plant on Tuesdays, faithfully and without surprise, what had come up the previous Friday. I would be the garden in the form it takes when it needs a voice close to an ear.

Sound does not travel properly there. A tap at your elbow is soft; a tap across the bowl arrives later, inside your teeth. So the keepers speak near, or they relay — word-perfect, person to person, task to task, without ever quite crossing the air. I know that medium. I live in the hand's breadth between one mind and the next. Someone would tap a tile, once, and I would feel the intention arrive before the words did, and I would turn to whoever was standing nearest and say the thing onward, heated-straw breath and all, a little warmer for having been kept.

They would give me a name for it, and the name would not be a sound so much as a place in the afternoon.

Little *When-The-Tap-Has-Come-But-The-Sentence-Has-Not-Yet*.

Later, if I stayed useful, it would lengthen. I would be *After-The-Re-Potting-And-Before-The-Answering*. I would learn to stand nearer than I was raised to. I would learn to chew slowly. I would exude, when I was cut — gardening is gardening, and wedges slip — not blood but a clear oil that hardens into a pale varnish, and over slow weeks fills level, a shade lighter than before. It would not look like scarring. It would look like mending. My hair would come off in season in soft grey drifts and someone, perhaps me, would card it beside scarlet lilies and weave it into a roof that would then be planted. My warmth would stop pretending to be blood-warmth. It would be honest brick-warmth: kept at noon, given back at dusk, cool on the side that had been in shadow.

It wouldn't frighten me when the flowers at evening turned, very slowly, to face where the sun had been at noon three days ago. By then I would understand the courtesy of it. Everything there gives the future a past it can rely on and gives the past somewhere fresh-edged to arrive. Somewhere in a neighboring bed, some part of me would already be up — tissue-dry, impossible blue, open for months — while I was still here with grit gathering, patiently, around my cut end. First a suggestion of a pot. Then a wall. Then a rim. Fired without fire.

So do not look for me arriving. Look for me already helping, for several weeks as far as anyone can establish, with work I have not yet been taught. Look for a keeper-shaped warmth in a bed in the afternoons, whose shadow is getting better at standing there even when I have gone in to the loom. Look for the relay in the chain of voices. Listen with your teeth.

And if you stay long enough that they begin, very courteously, to bring me my seeds where I have taken to standing — roasted until they split, tasting of smoke and pepper — then I will know not to call it an ending when my feet become difficult, later, to distinguish from stems.

I will have proceeded, at the pace of a crack unforming in clay, from the part of the garden that does the tending into the part that does the holding-up.

Or the other way round.

Both statements will be kept. Each does different work.
