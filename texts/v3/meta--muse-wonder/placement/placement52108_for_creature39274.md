From the air I wouldn't be visible at all.

That seems right. If I think of the black granite, the pale scree running in long unstable slopes, the fault slit no wider than a doorway — I don't place myself out there. The wind would take me apart. I'd be hunched wrong, breathing wrong, too loud.

If I follow it in, though —

Outer door. Pause. Inner door. Never both at once. The wet-room, and the familiar, small settling in my ears.

That's where I arrive in my own body.

I am not one of the ravens, patient as officials at the chime, though I envy their outside view and their black, uncomplicated dryness in the wind. I am not the clinic cat, long-haired and lordly on the warmest pipe in the school, though I understand why the children follow him. I am not one of the pale fish either — not yet. Though down in the cistern I have watched for them long enough, on the quiet shift, to know how they move. Slow. Unbothered by depth changing a finger's width a day.

I have hands. That settles it, more than anything else. Inner hand trailing near the wall as I go down the spiral, not for balance, for news. Fingertips that know the difference between a normal morning sweat on the stone and a seep that wasn't there yesterday. Short nails. The faint dark lines at knuckle and wrist where I've handled cold metal for years.

I think I'm in my forties. My downhill knee has started its lifelong commentary. I live on the fifth terrace — no, the sixth, lower down where it is damper and warmer in a relative sense, where the overflow pools are.

That is my work, most days.

Not the reactors. I could not describe them except the way I was taught to: plumbing. Not the membrane, though I've done my rotation up there in the orange shell, scraping rime while the whole sky stood open over my head and the town exhaled beneath me. Everyone has. Everyone has done water-watch, too, walking at noon melt with a listening rod. But they keep giving me back to the pools.

They are milky turquoise. Thin films of mineral-loving bacteria on black water, brightening, deepening, thinning at the edges when the composition shifts. The lower levels smell of hot stone and iron around them. Children crouch beside them and are reverent in the way only Cup children can be about water they've never been allowed to enter. Our closest word to *garden* is the word for this bloom.

I tend it the way other people elsewhere might tend soil. I skim nothing away that doesn't need skimming. I log the color. I note when it goes a deeper turquoise than last spring, and I tell the old people in their niches, and they tap the stone and tell me what that meant thirty years ago, when a seep on the fourth ran clear for a month and then stopped. My handwriting is good. That matters here. Screens die in the damp. Ledgers don't, if you keep your sleeves waterproof and your letters patient.

I am not important. The water board could replace me in a rotation. That feels correct, too — a relief, even. In the Cup importance is a kind of dryness everyone eventually shares out.

My coat would tell you more about me than I would in ordinary conversation, because ordinary conversation is short here. It fits inside a breath. My speech is low in the chest, through the nose, with that pause between phrases that visitors think is hesitation. It's not. It's portioning.

My coat is patched where I've caught it on drain grates and condenser shutters, and each tear has sealed overnight to a slightly darker seam. Some of those seams are mine. Some are my mother's. I wear one outer layer while the other hangs in the wet-room — I am, by local standards, comfortable, maybe even a little smug about it in a way I would never say aloud in a doorway. Talk doesn't belong in doorways. It belongs in a niche, with a kettle, hands around something hot.

My hair is braided tight. In the morning my cuffs are damp. By afternoon my hands are calloused over skin that was pruned-soft at breakfast. At market I stand with my hood up and my fingerless gloves around a bowl of the same protein everyone else is eating, and I discuss, with genuine seriousness, whether it was better last week crisped hard at the edges or steamed until it sighed apart. I have a position on this. I have held it for years. In the bathhouse, when the waste heat comes down and the steam loosens everyone's sentences into whole paragraphs, I will defend it longer than is reasonable.

That is probably where you'd actually find me, if you came looking and wanted the truth of me rather than the roster version. Not at council over the cistern. Not rappelling the well in orange. In bathhouse hour, hair finally drying for once, saying more than I say all day, listening to news travel stall to stall — a condenser running sweet, a new trickle with no color to it on the third terrace, a fish seen, slow, near the east walkway — and filing each thing away the way my body files the feel of a wall.

At night I would not stand talking in the shaft. I know whose morning work my breath would become. I go home down the spiral while the membrane goes clear above the well and the stars crowd down, undimmed, and the tram makes its forty-minute hum past lit doorways spaced far apart in wet rock.

And at noon — wherever I am, poolside, ledger open, bowl in hand — I stop.

Not ceremonially. The melt-pulse comes down from the seep-fields outside, every channel in the city runs suddenly loud and bright, conversation is briefly impossible, and there is nothing sensible to do except hold still and listen to the place metabolize. My breath fogs. The walls bead. Far below, the cistern takes its finger's width, and the fish adjust their circles, and the bacteria in my pools shiver at the inflow and brighten.

Then it passes. The wipers come out like patient beetles. I knock my boots twice against the bar out of a reflex older than reading. I put my palm flat to a wall that sweats a fraction differently than it did yesterday.

I report it at dinner.
