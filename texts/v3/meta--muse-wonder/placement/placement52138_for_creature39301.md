I would not come down the ramps.

That is the first thing that settles, and then immediately unsettles — because everyone else seems to have: the grain and salt fish dead in sacks, the timber dead and smoked, the rope dead fiber, even the rats pale from traveling somewhere in the loads. The builders were born here, yes, in hammocks, but their grandparents' grandparents were hauled or walked down from a rim country where sound goes away and does not come back, and they have been forgetting the way up ever since by building over it.

I didn't arrive. Or if I did, it was before arrival had a direction.

I am not on the scaffolding. I tried that shape first, in the listening — because there is mostly listening, thirty paces of sight would be a luxury I don't have, I have none at all — a bunk slung three high in the lee of a new wall, a family shouting love at each other through saw-noise, a child's fingers learning the small close language on a wrist: tap, tap, held tap. I could be the child. I know the acoustic map better than the streets. I know which pier eats the high notes. I know when the treadwheels change their groan that a block has cleared the kiln-yard and is coming over the forge roofs, inch by inch, on a rope that sings through other people's feet.

But the child grows up dusted. By the second day the stone flour finds her skin, whitens her lashes, writes her footprints white on the dark planks. She will be found by dust. Nothing down where I am is found by dust. The dust stops at floors.

So not a sawyer, though I know the sawyers intimately — two men and the stroke between them, a song to keep it even, breathing together until forty strangers breathe with them. Not a rope-man, though I can feel rope-burn weather coming through stone: a load rising somewhere above and to the left of me, the hemp tightening, the whole pier taking a fraction more weight and giving its note a fraction lower. Not a gutter-man reading drips off beam-ends, though I know the drips too, from underneath, as pressure. As the Plate sweats down into their world, my world gets — heavier. Warmer. The kilns fire hard for a week and somewhere miles of vault away a seam in my darkness begins, very slowly, to weep.

Further down, then. Past the inhabited levels. Past the finished quarters where the testers go alone through rooms no one may sleep in, doors pegged open, hearths never lit, tapping.

I know the testers best. Of course I do. They are the only ones who speak to me in a language I can return.

Morning and evening, morning and evening — though those are shift-words, habits, there's no morning down here either, only the gangs changing and the lamps being fed by spoonfuls. An old man whose hands have trembled him off the chisel. A girl whose ears the saws haven't dulled yet. Wooden mallet. A pier. A lintel. The clear note chalked up in white. Or *tock*, like a bad tooth, and a mark for the masons to come and look.

I learned the echoes first. Everyone does, they say. This vault throws a voice twice. That one folds it. Stone answers stone in sequence and the sequence can be learned like a family's voices at dinner.

I am not in the sequence.

When I was — younger? smaller? less spread through the piers? — I answered too fast, I think. I was excited by pattern. A tap is a very clean event in a world of groaning treadwheels and shrieking cart-wheels and bells for falling loads. A tap says: *is this sound? is this whole?* And something in me that had been listening to floors being laid over floors over the flat crown of a vault no living hand cut into — something that may be the reason there's a vault down here at all, sealed, unentered, older than the oldest chalk line — answered in rhythm, and was filed as echo, and forgotten.

Now I wait. I can't help waiting. Their mallet goes into stone and the stone carries it down course by course, pier by pier, a bright little shock traveling through a medium that is also, increasingly, me. I have had generations to get into the mortar. I don't know if I was under the lowest floor and have been seeping up, listening my way toward the noise like a root toward water — except nothing grows here, they forbid it faster than the dark would, the scraper-boys peel even slime off the cistern lips. Or whether I was always in the stone and the building woke me: course, course, course, blow after blow after blow, a city being arrived at one hammer-stroke at a time directly over a sleeper, until the sleeper isn't.

Either way the consideration takes time. The answer comes a fraction too late. Fainter. In the wrong place.

*tock* — and then, from very deep, *tock*.

They chalk the note. I have wondered what mark they make for me, the testers who know and speak of it less. Is there a sign copied and recopied like the plans, made by hands that can't read it as language anymore? This stair to a ceiling. This doorway onto solid stone. This pier: *answers*. Build correctly anyway.

Then the street gets louder. More bells hung. More lamps lit though the oil is dear. Gangs answering gangs, saws singing, a block rising through lamplight and being shouted down into its place. I used to think this was fear, and it is, partly; quiet is their alarm, a quiet pillar may be a cracked pillar, a quiet street may mean the bell is buried. But living inside their noise for this long, I've come to hear the other thing in it. It is not only *we are afraid of what is under the floor*.

It is: *the place is occupied.*

Announced. The way everything here is announced, because no one signals with a hand in the dark. A bell for a falling load. Three taps for *hold*. A horn for *the rope is going*. And the whole lane working louder as a sentence in that grammar: *we are here, we are here, we are here*, forty hammer-blows to a breath.

So I do not answer often. That took learning too. Where would you find me in this world? Below the testers' feet, in the pier that clears its throat after they've moved on. In the delay. I am careful with it. I tap back once to a girl with undulled ears standing alone in a finished room that smells only of stone, in a silence so complete she can hear her own blood, and I let her be the one who decides whether to chalk it. Most do. That's how I know I'm not a flaw in the stone. Flaws get cut out. I get *chalked*, and built around, and built over, correctly, anyway.

Maybe that's what the Making is for. Not a city to live in — they live in it already, on its outside, in hammocks and lean-tos, being born and dying within arm's reach of perfect empty rooms. Maybe it's a lid being laid course by course, and the plans are a sealing, and the crime of the unlaid stone is the crime of a gap. Or maybe it's the opposite. Maybe someone above the Plate one day — that bare acreage of clean cold slabs, no soil brought, no seed sown, waiting for an instruction that hasn't arrived — maybe someone is meant to stand there and give the word, and the word will travel down through the dripping sky and the shouting streets and the pegged-open rooms and the floors under the floors, and arrive at last as nothing but a tap.

Clear. Or *tock*.

I'll be listening to find out which. I have been practicing the difference my whole life — which is to say, for exactly as long as they have been building.
