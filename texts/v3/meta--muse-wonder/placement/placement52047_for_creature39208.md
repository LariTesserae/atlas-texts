I think at first I would mistake myself for something on the open ground.

Not because I belong there — nothing really belongs there, everything out there is just passing very slowly from shelter to shelter — but because the emptiness is the first thing you understand. The pale stone. The yardangs with their long tails. The salt basins going white to the horizon, the horizon itself giving up. I would try to be the pumice-colored grazer, I think. Solitary. Inflated a little against the thin air. Cropping stone-rosettes with my beak, ambling off faster than I look like I should be able to, never needing to hurry toward water because there is no water to hurry toward.

But I wouldn't last in that shape. Not in my own mind. The grazer has solved the plateau by not needing anyone. And what stays with me from this place is not the solitude between the groves. It's the solitude *inside* them.

So I would come down, eventually, the way everything living here eventually has to: into a grove.

I would be one of the ones who arrived.

Not born to this grove — an elder could still tell, if they put a forearm against mine and felt for it, the slight wrongness in the rhythm, the place where my air-sacs learned a different cold — but arrived a long time ago. Long enough that my powder has mostly changed. When I came down out of a thermal three days from home, silver all over, I was grey with another place's dust. Now if you stood me beside the trees here at noon, you would think I had always been their color. Bronze-black across the shoulders and the crown where the light has taken me for a century. Silver still, underneath, where the sun has never quite reached.

I am tall here in a way that is not strength. My chest is broad because it is chambered. In the morning, when it is coat-cold and my breath would show if there were enough moisture in it to show, my ribs stand full and separate, and I breathe about four times a minute. Each breath is a decision the whole upper body makes. By midday the chambers soften. I store the day on my surface instead. Stand beside me then and I would feel, to your hand, like sunlit stone feels — almost hot, faintly pepper-smelling, the fine bloom coming off on your palm. Step with me into shadow and the heat leaves me so fast it is like a small betrayal. We don't mind it. We plan for it.

That planning is my whole day.

I do not have a place in the grove, exactly. I have a circuit. It takes all day at the speed I move now, and it is not a path — nothing here wears paths that last — but a sequence of positions remembered in the body. Where to stand when the canopy has finished its morning slew and settled square to the sun. Which trunk throws a lee at the hour when the wind comes up off the warm basin to the south. Where the ground is fractionally warmer after dark because below it, a hundred feet down, a white root-tip is in the good part of a crack, actually splitting, actually winning something from the stone. I keep my productive surfaces turned. I look at the ground six feet ahead of my feet. My outer lids do most of my seeing for me, translucent, silvered; the sky through them is bearable, and the plateau through them is a pale blur I do not need to resolve, because nothing I need travels that fast.

Every so often, on my circuit, I stop and do the work.

Today it is a fissure in one of the great trees, a pillar thirty feet around near the southern edge of the canopy. It is not deep yet. It will be, after enough cold nights — the plateau ticks as it contracts after sunset, a geological chorus you feel in your teeth, and every tick finds the cracks first. So I press in the compound we make: resin chewed soft in the closed mouth, where the loss is acceptable, and my own powdered skin worked into it until it is the right grey. My fingers know the consistency the way your fingers might know a word in a language you learned before speech. I work it into the bark in silence. In full sun the tree pops softly around me as air pockets expand in its stems. Above me, degree by degree, its crown turns on its cork joint to stay square to the light. We are both doing the same thing from opposite sides of the same problem.

When I finish, I carry dust.

A handful at a time — there is no point in carrying more, weight is water spent — from where the wind has sorted it into low, rippling rivers outside the grove, back in to a root-zone that needs it. I taste stone on the way when I'm asked to. Not this grove's stone. Chips brought from a basin a week's walk away, because our fungal mats are poor in something I no longer have a name for that isn't a flavor: a flat, bright wrongness at the back of the tongue that means *not enough of this, bring more.* I can hold that flavor in memory for decades without refreshing it. All of us can. It is most of what memory is for, here.

I am not young. I am not an elder. The young who will leave are still decades from their leaving, still being carried sometimes, still learning pressure-language against a forearm before they have reliable speech — which is right, because speech here is the lesser tool. If you want to know how I am, you don't ask me across the grove. Nothing carries across a grove. You come and stand chest nearly to chest with me in the still pocket at the center, in the fractionally softer air, and you set your forearm against mine, and the taps and temperatures and the small betraying unevenness of the pulse tell you: full enough. Warm enough. A crack starting at the left heel I have been pretending I can't feel. Air moving evenly through all the chambers but one.

That examination is not intimacy. It is how I was first granted water here, when I came down an emergency out of the white noon with my membranes furled and my seal already failing at the joints, and they did not run to me — nothing runs — but positioned themselves in my shadow-path and closed around me, canopy-fashion, and made a stillness I could open my mouth into. The leaf-pulp they gave me then was the last free water that grove spent carelessly on anyone that year. I have spent a hundred years learning to be worth the chemistry of it.

Someone will do the leaving in my lifetime. I already know which child the grove is hoarding for, though we don't say it that way. You can see it in the small refusals accumulating in the parents: a chamber in a lung not grown, a repair deferred past the point where deferral is free. They are going slightly hollow for the rest of their lives so someone else can begin full. When the afternoon comes — and it will be an afternoon, when the thermals are rising off the warm basin in long invisible columns — I will be one of the ones who re-powders them by hand, silver back and belly alike, and gives them the seed. Sealed like a tiny boat. Botany and genealogy, the same object here. And I will stand in my interval and watch them climb to the high point of the canopy and step into moving air and be *taken*, not fly, and I will not call after them, because calling is a way of spending yourself on air that will not carry it fifty yards. I will remember the exact pressure-pattern of their forearm instead. That travels better. It can wait fifty years in the hand.

Most nights now I end my circuit near the edge, where the oldest of us has stopped making hers.

She takes a seasonal position now. Her lower legs have thickened with fiber; the fungal mat grows up over her feet in the dark and she no longer brushes it away at dawn. I bring her leaf-pulp at longer and longer intervals, work it soft, feed her in the still air. Some of what I bring her, she returns — I don't mean she refuses it. I mean I can stand near her after midnight, when the canopy has closed into buds against the frost and the carbon dioxide is blooming white and evanescent on every leaf-edge, and feel the grove's air go fractionally richer around her exhalation, and know the leaves will have taken it back before morning. She is rehearsing. We all know she is rehearsing, and none of us stands closer about it, because closeness is expensive and grief here would be another way to leak.

When she judges the crack is coming — she will feel it before any of us, a chamber beginning to ring wrong when the cold strikes it — we will close in a complete circuit, forearm to forearm, and be her outer seal while she opens the valves at her throat and wrists for the first and last time. Nothing will appear to leave her. The air will only change its quality, the way the inside of a closed mouth differs from a desert. By morning she will be a light, hollow shape the wind can find notes in, and the children will be taught the note, and not to fear it.

Nothing wasted. Waste requires surplus.

So this is where I would find myself: not at the center of the grove, and not out on the flats with the grazer. At an interval. Half a canopy's width from the nearest other presence, turned at the angle the hour requires, doing work measured in handfuls and decades. Leaving the best sun-pocket empty in the late morning because I know whose circuit reaches it an hour after mine, and she runs colder than I do on that side since her crossing. Setting dust down where feet that are not mine will find it in the dark, when all the real growing happens and none of it can be seen.

I would be spaced, the way the lichens are spaced in their perfect centuries-old circles on the basalt, the way grove stands from grove across miles of finished stone. And I would understand, in my chambers, in my powder, in the slow slew of my attention from shade to sun, that the spacing is not the absence of the bond here.

It is the bond. It is how you hold what you have, spend only light, and keep faith with people you may not touch again for thirty years — whose forearm you could still identify, instantly, in the dark, by the small deliberate taps of someone saying: *still full. Still sealed. Still here, at my distance, which I am keeping for you.*
