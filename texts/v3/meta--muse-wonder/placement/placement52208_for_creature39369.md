I thought at first I would be one of the porters.

I keep coming back to that road halfway down the rim, where the roar thins enough for birds to happen again. I imagined myself strung between the two kinds of quiet — plugging my ears to go down into the basin, unplugging them to climb out, getting good at both weathers. I would know the cold country. I would know how small a human voice sounds up there, out in open air.

But that doesn't hold. A porter only passes through the pressure. He is down for an hour, he is marked by his bare ears or by how often he reaches for plugs he hasn't learned to leave in. I wouldn't want to be the one who gets to leave.

So I tried the indoor life instead.

A storyteller, maybe. One of the hoarse ones, knee to knee in a double-walled room with the leather doors shut twice behind me, arguing and singing at what anywhere else would be a shout and here is just conversation. I liked that. A hand flat on someone's chest to feel the tune. A child learning rhythm through ribs before ears. Foreheads almost touching so a voice does not have to travel.

That held longer. I could feel the ash-and-pumice stuffed between the walls, the way the frogs never actually go away, they just drop down into the floor, into the soles of the feet. I could stay there a long time.

But the longer I stayed, the smaller the room got — not smaller like a trap, smaller like a focus. The plaster warm. Wet plaster, cooking oil, fish, hide, sulfur in the back of the throat. And in the wall itself, underneath the human talking, that other sound. Not loud. Everything else in the basin is loud. This was close.

A house at close range seems to vibrate.

I think that's where I would find myself. Not in the street, not in the canal, not up under the eaves with the swifts pouring out in their dark, drowned streams. In the warm crack in the kitchen wall, where the crickets live.

I would be one of them.

It solves, in a cricket-sized way, the problem the whole town is built around: how to make a sound in a place that is already full of sound. The frogs do it by never stopping, tens of thousands of them shoulder to shoulder on the canal lips until calling is just the condition of water. The spring does it by being underneath everything, a bass pressure in the sternum rather than a noise. The people do it by retreating indoors and then, even indoors, by putting a palm on a chest to feel a lullaby arrive.

I would do it the way crickets do. I would sing from inside the stone, not expecting to be heard across anything. My song would go into the plaster and the basalt and the ash packing and come out the other side as a hum in the room, a faint trembling in a cup set down on the stone table. People living their whole lives in that house would know me without ever seeing me. A child pressing her ear — unplugged, indoors, where ears are for using — to the wall before sleep. An old woman who has lost her upper registers and doesn't much mind, tapping the plaster once, companionably, with a knuckle, on her way past.

My world would be narrow and total. A crack is a country if the temperature never changes. And here it never does. No winter to go dormant against, no dry season to thin out and wait through. The ground-warmed stone stays ground-warmed. The fungal film I feed on keeps growing in the perpetual damp. The refuse keeps coming — fish scales, crumbs of grey-brown grain hauled down at great expense from outside, threads of woven hair, oil soaked into plaster over years of cooking. Generations of my kind in the same wall, because the wall outlasts us the way houses outlast families here, standing dripping with no vines to take them, until someone needs the block.

I would learn the house the way basin people learn the basin: by vibration and by touch and almost never by distance. Antennae tasting sulfur, wet lime, snail-water tracked in on sandals. Feet finding the hot places where the rock under the floor is thin, and the cool runnels where canal water is piped underneath. The shift-bells in the gang houses arriving not as sound but as a blow travelling through the ground, through foundation, through wall — *now, now, change* — and the house stirring around me, hands everywhere suddenly, because in this town attention always begins with a hand on a wrist, a palm on a back: *I'm here, I'm passing behind you.*

Human speech would come to me as pattern, not words. I would know the difference between an argument and a story by tempo long before I knew what either was about. Arguments speed up in the chest and the floor. Stories slow down and get pauses in them. Singing is the steadiest of all, low and regular, because indoors people sing close, sometimes forehead to forehead, and the wall itself learns the rhythm. If they are like me — and in the crack, at night-that-isn't-night, I would decide they are — then half their singing is not for hearing at all. It is for making the small enclosed space they share tremble in the same way at the same time.

Outside my crack the rest of the census would go on, layered the way it has to be. Frogs in the canals, protected by white stone law at every bridge, because a week without them and the midge columns over the standing water would make the place unlivable. Swifts coming home over my roof in silence that isn't silence. Bats shifting in the bathhouse rafters. Lizards running the outer walls after my cousins who stray too far into the light, snapping them up in the permanent noon-twilight. Ducks shoving through the market canals, snails cracking, fish mouthing up between the tadpoles — tadpoles of every age at once, a calendar that refuses to turn, because the water is always warm and there is no season written into anyone's body here. Not even mine. Especially not mine.

I would be eaten, eventually, most likely. That's not a tragedy in a town where everything eats everything else in a closed, balanced arrangement and calls it citizenship. A lizard's tongue. A swift taking me in the one moment I sing too near a crack that opens to the eave-dark. A child's quick fingers — children here are frog-wardens and snail-netters and fluent in sign before they can speak, quick and serious, with blunt, articulate hands. If I am lucky I last long enough to go slow and pale in the deepest part of the wall, where cuts and wear heal the way they do in the damp, and my singing thins to an irregular tick the house makes in its rest hours.

But before that: a life spent being part of the pressure that lets people sleep.

That's the thing I keep circling back to. Take a basin-born man up to the cold country and he sleeps badly in the quiet. He wakes reaching for earplugs that aren't there, unsettled by his own breathing. At home he sleeps with the frogs coming up through the floor. The spring under everything. The insects in the walls. Home is not the absence of noise. Home is a specific, layered, unending vibration that holds still, that the work-gangs guard with sluice gates and water clocks because if it ever faltered, if it ever changed, every layer would shudder.

I would be one of the smallest layers. Unseen, uncounted, unprotected by any carved stone — no one passes a law for the cricket in the wall — and completely necessary in the way unnoticed things are necessary. Knock the house down for block after a generation and I would move, or my children would, crack to crack, beam to reused beam, into the next double-walled core, and set up the hum again.

Nobody here says anything important where the frogs can hear it. Fine. I don't need to be heard where the frogs are. I would be heard where it counts: through plaster, at knee height and ear height, in the room where the doors are shut twice and the voices live. A small grinding song in the warm dark of the wall, going on exactly as loud when the visitors climb back up the rim road and discover birds again, and going on the same after they've forgotten there was ever a place where the sound arrived before the place did.

I would still be singing.
