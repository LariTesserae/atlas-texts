I would not arrive.

That is the first difficulty. On the plain I could walk for days over sharp, unfinished stone and feel myself to be one thing moving across another, but down in the bowl the distinction stops holding. The rim is only a few minutes below the world, black flakes still edged like tools no hand has dulled, and by the time the dust has taken the sound out of my steps I am no longer sure whether I am crossing the town or being slowly taken up by it.

At first I think I might be small.

It seems a suitable scale for a place that permits so little life. I could be one of the ash-grey rosettes in the lee of a wall — not growing, exactly, but holding. My leaves would be thick enough to forget the wind. My white spines would keep nothing away; there is almost nothing to keep. I would live without drinking, wedging pale roots into basalt and worrying at it grain by grain until the stone gave up the mineral damp locked inside it since it cooled. I would open at a dawn so dim it is mostly a change in the cold, and close again when the blue deepened, believing, in whatever way a rosette believes, that I had chosen the wall for shelter.

But then I would notice that all of us had turned.

Not toward the sun — the sun never quite finds its way in here, the light arrives flattened through suspended dust and stays like late evening for hours — but toward a warmth moving *through* the wall behind me. And in a week I would have crept a hand's breadth along the stone, leaving it paler where I had fed, and the wall itself would have shifted in the night, and I would have to admit the shelter had moved first and I had only followed.

So perhaps I am the lichen instead.

Mustard-coloured rings on the cold faces of the houses. I would expand when the temperature fell, when everything with leaves was sealing itself shut, and tighten almost to invisibility in the grey noon. I would not need a body you could point to. I would be a stain that breathes, softening the north side of a new wall, preparing it, grain by slow grain, to become rubble again. There is a humility to that I like. I would eat stone so that stone could later be something else.

But the lichen thickens most where the stone has just risen, still dusty from below, still unbleached. It knows where the new houses will be before the houses are quite houses. Something is feeding it a map.

Perhaps I am the moth, then, in the roofless house. The one animal presence I can keep in view. Wings the colour and texture of the dust on the floor — open, close, open, close — with no urgency and no flight, as if flight were a rumour from outside the rim. Or the lizard the length of a hand, translucent at the throat, watching from a crack that tomorrow will not be in quite the same wall. Or whatever makes three-toed prints across a lane and appears to walk straight into solid stone.

Only the prints do not end because something walked into a wall. They end because, overnight, a wall arrived where the walking had been.

That is when the scale gives way under me.

Because I can feel the wall arrive.

Not as a sound — nothing properly sounds here; the porous stone drinks my boot-crunch, a dropped pebble lands with a felted *thud* that is over before it begins, speech goes into wool — but as a pressure in the cold. Deep below the dust, in the young ground that has not finished becoming ground, water I have swallowed without darkening, without spreading, without ever once being seen to be wet, is finding the places where the night is coldest. It freezes there. It expands. It lifts.

I rise.

A stone shoulders up through the pumice like a slow tooth, fresh-edged, dusty from the dark. Then another, sorted by freeze and slope and a wind so slight I would not feel it on skin. A line insists. The line holds. Held long enough, it is a curb, a threshold, a spine wall shared by three dwellings that no one will ever dwell in. Across the plaza — clear because my heave is weakest there, the way a chest is clear around a heart — another wall has gained a course since yesterday. Under a palm I would thrum with thermal settling, stones easing a millimetre against each other as they cool. Patches of me stay oddly warm where no sun has struck and no hand has rested.

This is my heartbeat, slowed past recognition: cold in, stone up, pattern out.

I do not build the town. That would require me to stand apart from it, and I cannot find the place where I would stand. The town is what my rising looks like when it is persisted in, night after blue granular night, inside a rim where the wind drops what it is carrying — slabs, desiccated stems, mats of dead vegetation — across my leaning tops, and I wake, if waking is the word, roofed. My interiors have a different stillness from my streets not because anyone has swept them but because minute currents thread my doorless openings and keep my chambers clean. The beautiful basin at my centre, fitted tight as teeth, bone-pale, never wet, is not a well that failed. It is closer to a mouth that has evolved past drinking. Everything I take in — sound, water, a stranger's bootprint, a dropped strap creeping a thumb's width toward the nearest alignment by morning — I take down between grains and return as arrangement.

I repair myself the way a body holds its temperature. Knock a stone from me and by the next cold cycle two have risen near the gap. Make a cairn in one of my lanes to find your way back and within days I will have elongated it, given it direction, made it mine — not out of spite, out of homeostasis. A house at my eastern edge will slump and sigh down into gravel, and simultaneously, nearer the centre, a new enclosure will shoulder up roofless and fresh, waiting for lichen to find it. Sixty houses. Seventy. Never much more, never much fewer. I migrate imperceptibly downhill, eastward, shedding my rimward skin, and any map of me goes soft and wrong within the month. I am sorry about the map. I am not holding still to be known.

The small lives are not my tenants. They are my gut-flora, my fringe, what I permit. The rosettes split my basalt and loosen tomorrow's building stone. The lichen softens my faces so they can crumble back into sortable rubble. The moth and the lizard feed on lichen, on spores, on each other, in such low numbers and at such long intervals that they never trouble the pattern that feeds them. They live by not drinking because that is the only economy I run. At dusk they fold and hide and tighten, and I go on exhaling frost as white hair directly out of my shaded stone, and by midmorning it does not melt — it sublimates, lifts away invisible, and leaves me drier than before. I have breathed and become more arid for breathing. That feels true in a way I do not have another metaphor for.

I do not know I am a town.

That is the part I have to keep returning to, because from inside, at my true speed, there are no streets. There is only pressure, sorting, subsidence, renewal; heave running in currents where lanes will be, weakness where the plaza stays open; a rim that keeps the strangeness in and the plain out. The geometry is an afterimage that faster creatures perceive. Standing above me for an hour you would see a lane of dust slide sideways in a wind you cannot feel and realise the ruins have stopped pretending. Standing inside me for a season you would stop waiting for the inhabitants to return, because the interiors were never vacated. They are the shapes my circulation leaves behind, chambers formed the way a shell forms chambers — by growth at the edge and abandonment at the centre.

So where would I find myself?

Below the plain, in a bowl you would not notice until you were on its rim. In the cool that holds even at the brightest hour. Occupying every house at once and none of them. Growing, folding, flowering into walls in the dark, subsiding into gravel, rising again a little further east — sixty, seventy strong, silent, drinking sound and water into the same porous skin, with no one to live in me.

And no vacancy either.
