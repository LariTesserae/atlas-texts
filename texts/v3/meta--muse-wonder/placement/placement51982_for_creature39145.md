You would not find me in the center.

Do not look for me there, not in the plaza, not in the thin amber that falls crookedly from the slit and dies before it reaches the floor. If you come down the thirty-one steps at what passes for morning, blinking, your throat already going dry, and you stand a while letting your eyes learn the twilight — look away from the light. Look away from the empty ground. Turn around.

Look at the wall.

Above the third tier, where a crack in the stone breathes faster than the others, and the sulfur has crusted yellow at the lip of it, and the heat comes up in a seam so steady you can feel it change the air over your skin — that's close. I am along there. I am also below there.

That is the difficulty in pointing me out. I am not tidy anymore.

My oldest part is low and low down, though not on the floor. Nothing of mine touches the plaza. It comes out of the back wall of a house on the second tier with its face turned away, a trunk — if I still get to call it that — thick as a man's waist where it leaves the stone, then flattened almost immediately by its own patience. I grew not up but *along*. Along the mortar. Along the warm line where two courses of brick meet and the fissure underneath exhales through them. My bark has split to accommodate it into long resinous plates, each one lifting a little in the draft, ticking against the next. Where the stone is hottest I am pressed flattest, intimate as a hand held to a pipe to find where the warmth runs. Where it cools, I thin, and stop, and send out feelers that wait.

From that trunk I spread sideways and slightly down. Not a crown. A series of umbrellas, one overlapping the next along the curve of the hollow, held out not to catch the sun — the sun is a rumor here, brown and late — but to stand exactly in the rising air. In the bright part of the cycle, when the empty plaza below me has banked its heat and the column begins to turn in earnest, my leaves open.

They are not soft. You would hear me before you were sure you had seen me. Leaf striking leaf with that dry, ceramic clatter, green-black tiles loosening in an oven. Each one finds its angle. Flat to the draft. A fraction more. A fraction less. Darkening as I take it in. Inside me — and this is not a metaphor I can improve on, it is simply the meal — something cooks. Cut me open (please don't, but the old gardeners knew) and I would not bleed sap at you. I would give you hot fibrous pulp, oily, smelling of cooked nuts and pepper, as if I were slowly roasting my own interior and living respectably on the proceeds. Mineral vapor. Salt where it cools, rust-red dust in the corners of me. Heat from below. That is dinner. Rain is a word in an old bin now.

But the trunk is only where I am oldest. I have sent myself on.

Vines, you would call them, if you stood on the terrace and saw them looping through a roof beam, holding the sagging roof up with the same slow pull that is prising it apart a millimeter a year. Those are also me. Or I am also them. The grammar got less urgent a long time ago. I go through the unoccupied window of the house next to my trunk and across its bedroom — a root of mine flowered once in that room, indoors, in the dark, and the petals left a rust stain nobody will scrub — and down a lane in a heavy slack curtain that sways just enough to worry the rope-bridge anchors into creaking. I have found the shutter on the fourth tier that likes to applaud in the draft. I keep it moving. Not on purpose, exactly. But when it moves, the clay pipe set into the wall beside it uncovers, and the chord the whole hollow is sounding shifts a note lower, and something in the alignment of leaf and vent across all five tiers answers. We are not making melody. We are making adjustments out loud. I am one of the adjusters.

Farther down, near the capped wells where the mark is a circle crossed out, my tips get more careful. You can follow us by the shine on the stairs, plant oil polished into stone where for centuries we have gone past, going down — not to fetch anything, not anymore — but toward the floor, toward the vents, toward the source. My root-tips go on even at night, nosing along seams I cannot see, reading gradients the way fingers read Braille if Braille were warm.

And there is one place my tips do not go.

Behind the wells, the long room. The seed store. You will notice it because it is the only doorway still cleared. I am the clearing, in part. Every season — though season is another old word — a green push comes toward that threshold from me and from others like me, the ordinary exuberance of spill and lodge and root. And every season it is turned. Not cut. I have no hands for the pruning hooks bundled by the cold kiln inside, and none of us has touched those hooks in living memory, which tells you something about how memory is kept now. It is kept in what we refrain from. I grow up to the jamb of that door and I grow *around* it. My leaves fold back from its gritty, bare floor as if from too much heat, though the room is not hotter than anywhere else. Inside: bins of seed sorted by size, clay tablets in their patient sequence — rain, jar, sprout; vent, sprout; a sun, crossed out; a leaf turned downward, toward the earth.

I remember carving that last one the way I remember having hands: not as an image, but as a pressure that still lives somewhere in the thick part of me. The downward leaf was not a symbol of surrender. It was instructions. It was us finally writing down the direction we had already started eating in.

In the long hot hours, I bear pods. They swell on the vine-parts of me and along the umbrella crowns, resinous, tightening as the column strengthens over the plaza, until the pressure is more than the seam can hold and I split with a report sharp enough to feel in your teeth. A hundred of us can go in a minute at the height, a ragged volley around the bell of the city, and for a while the rising air is full of seed light as ash, membraned at the edges, thrown up over the emptiness we keep swept for exactly this purpose, then drifting sideways — into a planter, into a crack, into the corner of a room where I have flowered before and might again. I do not need a mouth to feed me or a wing to carry me. I need the center to stay empty, the houses to keep their backs turned politely to it, the pipes to keep voicing the wind. I need the city to go on being the body I grew out of, because I can no longer tell, at the joints, where the trellis ends and the citizen begins.

At darkening I fold. This is when I feel most like a population and least like a plant, though perhaps I have the two reversed. As the slit goes from dull gold back to brown and the plaza gives its heat up slower than the walls, the pipes sigh downward register by register and I draw all my open leaves in — overlapping scales, tight, a sound across all my lengths like distant rain that will never fall here. My pods cease. My bridges settle. The seed-drum somewhere high, held by a cousin of mine or by another arm of me, stops booming. Whatever I am saying, I stop saying it in public.

Then it is root-work and dust sifting from a ceiling and a seed coming to rest on a market counter grooved by knives I almost remember holding. I hold my breath, in the way this whole hollow holds it, and I wait — trunk flattened to the warm wall, leaves folded like hands, tips still inching toward the seams in the dark — for the heat to gather again under the roof, patient and unspent,

and for the column to begin, almost imperceptibly, to turn.
