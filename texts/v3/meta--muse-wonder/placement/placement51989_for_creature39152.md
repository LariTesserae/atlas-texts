I think I would hear it first the way I hear everything here — I would not arrive so much as I would stop being able to leave.

On the climb, when the roar gets inside my teeth and my own footsteps go out of the world, I would still try to be a visitor. I would aim for the center. Everyone does, I suspect. The dry-ish island, the altar stone, the place where you can breathe even if there isn't quite air. I'd stand there with my hair lifting and my clothes billowing in no wind and try to take it in, try to be the kind of thing that witnesses a place like this whole.

I wouldn't last there. Not because of fear, exactly. Because of pressure. Because every thought flattens and spreads thin, and after a while I would start making mistakes: I'd press my palms over my ears to make one small, private silence, and feel the ceiling notice. I'd try to leave something neat on the altar and expect it to stay. I'd raise my voice. I am not built broad in the sternum. I don't listen well with my whole weight. I listen best a little to the side of the loudest thing.

So I would retreat — not over the rim, not back out into the honest cold wind, but sideways. Into the shelters first, probably. There's something I recognize in them: walls that are always almost built or always almost fallen, every stone humming a fraction out of place each day and then humming back, never quite to where it was. I could stay there a while, roofless by necessity, learning to communicate the way the bowl teaches — hand flat to slate, knuckles rapped in rhythms that travel better than shouting. For a while I'd let myself think I *was* one of those stones. Load-bearing, patient, part of something human.

Only stones get returned by hands that aren't theirs. I noticed that eventually. Something on the other side of the water keeps the shelters. Keeps the offerings. Keeps the bead and the black river pebble and the knot of red wool in slow orbit, turning them over the way you roll a coin to learn its weight. I am not the thing being kept, either. I liked the pebble — smooth and old and carried up from a river days away, the only rounded thing in a landscape of shards — but I'm not smooth. Nothing here has taught me to be dull yet, and also: the pebble belongs to open currents. I keep drifting out of them.

I might be the flower, I think, washing in a seep. Waist-high, leafless, stem like wet rope, waiting all that time for one particular overtone to shiver the bowl like a struck glass before I open. It's tempting. It's dramatic. It makes a white ring in the air that becomes rain by the time it reaches the rim. But no — I don't open only at the peak. If anything I do the opposite. When the note locks and the ceiling sea goes mirror-smooth and my other self might be standing over me in brighter light, I'd be the thing that curls tighter.

That's how I find the crack.

Behind the largest of the rim-teeth, on the inward face where a boulder makes a lee — the same kind of lee the ceiling makes when *it* stands on the water and presses all that weather down with its attention. A place where the vibration deadens just enough. Not silence, never silence; the roar is in the stone here too, arriving through the rock a half-second late the way a dropped stone lands after you've stopped waiting. But it's sound with a shadow in it, and things can root in a shadow.

That's where I would be. That's what I would be: the grey-green moss that grows there in perfect circles.

Nothing glamorous. Nothing that roots deeply — I can't afford to, the mountain hasn't finished deciding what shape it is and neither, on most days, have I. I grow the way the quiet allows: outward in every direction equally, because I have no preferred direction, because direction here is mostly a story water tells before it tears the pattern apart and starts another. My circle isn't a design. It's a map. The edge of me is exactly the edge of where the sound thins enough to live. A finger's width further and the vibration unpicks me a fraction at a time, the way it unpicks grass dams and conclusions and the seam in a five-toed palm. A finger's width inward and I'm translucent, almost, like the ferns curled tight as fists in the next crack over.

From there I would know the bowl the way I can actually know things: not by looking up. The moss doesn't look up. I would know the hanging sea by the light it lends me — dim green, dim pewter, caustic nets crawling slow across rock that never quite dries, arriving late through moving water. I would know the seeps by warmth coming up through me in sudden silt-clouded bursts. I would know visitors by pressure: a warm hand between the shoulder blades of the stone, a shadow going under to a ledge that doesn't exist, footprints appearing between one look and the next — small, five-toed, the short toe a clean absence — crossing near my circle and never quite through it. Whether it steps around the lee places out of something like kinship, or simply because it too is drawn to where the noise gives out, I wouldn't presume. We'd share a preference. That might be enough.

And when the overtone comes — when waterfall and steam-hiss and grinding new rock and the hum of the shelters lock, briefly, into one note, and the flower out in the open does its white, visible ringing, and the ceiling smooths into a mirror and everything is suddenly, terrifyingly still — I wouldn't open. I'd hold. I'd feel the light change above the water to a brightness I will never stand in, and feel my small circle of quiet get, for one breath, a fraction wider. Not enough to cross the bowl. Enough to grow by the width of a grain of grit.

Then the note breaks. Rain falls in three directions. A shelter loses a stone and gains it back somewhere else. The offerings resume their orbit. And I resume mine, which is mostly staying — shallow-rooted, salt-white where the minerals bloom through me, rust-fresh at the edges, growing in the one shape a shadow allows: round, unfinished, listening at the rim of the roar for the place where it thins, and living there.
