I think I would have to go down slowly. Not as a decision — just the way the pit insists on it.

From the rim I would be useless. Too much glare, too much distance, everything up there too far apart to assemble into a face. I would flinch upward like the grandparents do, even though I've never been sunburned. So down: past the sharp, fresh break in the rock, past the first bolted houses still startled by their own height, into the column where daylight gives up and becomes that green, underwater dusk. My eyes would adjust, and then keep adjusting. I would stop expecting them to finish.

At first I would try on the high work. Everyone romantic, arriving, wants the crown.

The fin-scrapers climb to where the air is coolest and the rust-red lichen spreads over the Condenser's copper in maps, and they move like divers up there, wooden blade in hand, copper stain worked so deep into their fingers it has become a kind of drip-right made visible. I would want to read those maps. I can imagine my hands learning them. But I would be too eager to name what I saw, I think — too quick to call a shape finished — and in Karal that is a way of losing things. The lizard on the hottest pipe stays, perhaps, only because nobody makes it into a fact. The lichen would close under a gaze like mine. I would scrape too clean.

Lower, then.

The smiths, the root-masons, the fern-cutters with their forearms crosshatched pale — I would envy their competence too much to counterfeit it. A root-mason can put his palm flat to a beam that is half root now, or a root that is half beam, and feel which one is tired. He doesn't separate them. He persuades them, over seasons, to keep holding each other up. A wall-keeper knows by the smell of her morning cloth whether the east seep-holes have opened after a rain nobody below will ever see. Their hands are their truest faces, and mine would still be a newcomer's hands for years: pruned, soft in the wrong places, nails cut brutally short against the rot, learning a town through the soles of my feet before my eyes caught up.

*Which boards give sweetly and are rotting. Which pipe-bridge goes slick with algae after the Drip. Which threshold leaves melon rind out for whoever passes.*

I would learn. I would go barefoot because my boots would rot. I would learn the sideways rotation in the alleys, chest to chest for a heartbeat, no apology. Lips cracking in unison on the Dry Days. Setting out every cup I owned when some change in my skin told me, an hour early, that the water was about to remember the sky.

But I notice, following that life down ring by ring, that I keep stopping in the wrong place. Or the place that keeps stopping.

The cage.

The cage elevator that takes just under an hour to make a descent that should take minutes, creaking past kitchens and workshops and sleeping alcoves, past a loom-shop whose waste steam is somebody else's rice, past laundry hung so close across the alley that I could mend an awning without leaning. It is operated always by an elder, always half-asleep in the way that is actually attention. And it stops — this is the detail I cannot leave alone — for goats that no longer exist, out of a schedule no one will revise.

There. That's where I would accrete.

Not the operator, not at first. The second pair of ears in the cage. The apprentice who is mostly ballast and memory. The one learning which creak means the third-ring cistern is full, which pause rocks whose baby to sleep, which kitchen times its rice by our passing and must never, ever be hurried past because we once tried hurrying, and slow is not inefficiency down here — slow is the accumulated list of everyone who died when something was rushed.

My house would be, legally, a problem. A wall belongs to whoever keeps it dry, and mine would be the inside of a moving cage: iron that is warm at midnight, wood that never quite cures and smells faintly of fruit left in a closed room. I would wring it every morning into a house-pot that travels. My roof would be where the sideways water, on Drip days, pools for an extra hour on its way to being cold. That would be my drip-right, and it would be a modest, itinerant one, and people marrying into serious north-facing walls would smile at it kindly and marry elsewhere, and I would understand completely.

I would become a carrier of unfinished things.

Because the cage hears everything the way the whole town hears everything — in pieces. A woman on the fourth ring starts a sentence while she's hanging laundry. Her brother takes it up, not interrupting, just taking up the weight of it, from the cistern he's mending below. By the time the cage has creaked down two rings, the neighbor who heard the middle through a damp-thin wall an hour ago has finished considering his part. Strangers mistake this for telepathy. It is economy. It is breath.

But some sentences fall. That's the truth nobody makes a law about. Somebody begins something on the Dry Days, when tempers are banked and ferns and old resentments curl alike, and by the time the Drip comes they've forgotten they meant to say it, or the person it was for has moved two rings down to nurse a grandmother, or is sleeping behind a swollen shutter with a fern uncurling against the other side of the plaster. And the cage — I — would be the thing that passes that wall again. I would learn to hold a half-sentence the way you hold a cup set out under a slow pipe: not gripping it. Just keeping it level, in the mist, until there was somewhere for it to land.

*Your aunt —
 — the blue thread, not the —
 — tell her the seep opened in the night, she'll know.*

Delivered not because I am wise. Because I was going that way anyway, at the speed of an hour a descent, and the town has learned to use anything that moves predictably as a shelf.

At night the mist would thicken until the nearest lamp was a drowned star six feet away, and I would sleep sometimes in the cage between stops, where it hangs swaying almost imperceptibly over the sump-pond. Black and warm as broth below. A translucent frog clearing its glass note somewhere in the reeds, perhaps twice a year, perhaps only when a pipe bursts. Above me the Condenser ticking as it cools, its great finned crown drinking moisture out of my breath while I slept, out of everybody's breath, gathering for nine days and then — beads crawling sideways, pooling under beams, raining gently upward on the strangest days — giving it back.

I would not count the lizard. From the cage, midway down in the dusk, I would have the best view in Karal of the hottest pipe, and there are days I am certain I could settle it, and those are the days I would learn to ease the shutter shut instead, softly, the way everyone down there eases shutters, and finish someone else's sentence about something else entirely. Some things stay only so long as you do not make them into facts. I would be practicing that with my whole, damp, condensed life.

I would get the stoop. Of course I would get the stoop — ceilings sank as the wood swelled, and the cage door was cut for people who duck under pipes without thinking about it. Strong calves. A pruned softness to the fingertips. A cough that is less an illness than a dialect, shared. In the amber lamplight I would look burnished; in the green shaft-light, faintly verdigris, as if the copper-green moss in the leaking pipes had a cousinship with me and was not shy about claiming it. Take me to the rim at noon and I would weep without noise at the sheer, unbearable distance of things.

And if you asked me, some generous evening at the start of a Drip, with children standing open-mouthed under the cold pipes and every voice in town dropped below even its usual register so as not to fold the broadleaf plants — if you asked me what I was, down there, in the cage, between rings, between sentences —

I would put my palm flat to the cage wall and feel it sweat against my skin, and take my time, because beginning a new sentence costs breath and there is no hurry that hasn't killed someone.

“The machine dreams it’s a town,” I'd begin.

And someone, two terraces below, hours later, hanging something up to dry, would finish it for me.
