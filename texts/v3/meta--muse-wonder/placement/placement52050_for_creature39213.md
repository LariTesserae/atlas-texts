I wouldn't come in through the mouth of it, I think.

The mouth is too busy — platelet ice growing in overlapping scales, sealing narrower all day while new seams open farther in, and everything that tries to enter there gets read at once. A vibration, and a hundred antennae turn. I would be read, found too large, too warm, too sudden.

So I would come the other way. Through a brine channel, perhaps. Salt weeping down through the ice in the dark, carving an alley that wasn't there yesterday. Squeezing, not swimming. The water getting colder as I went up, then fresher, then — a wavering skin in the light — and I would hesitate on it the way everything hesitates on the boundary between the meltwater lens and the sea below it. For a moment, walking on water from underneath.

At first I would try the lower city.

Down along the sloping lip of old ice, where it is salt-dark and a fraction warmer, the tube worms raise their paper chimneys and dissolve them in the same hour. It is perpetual work down there, forgiving work. Build, retract at a shadow, bloom again. I could learn slowness there. My heart already wants to beat only a few times a minute in that cold. Growth would not be continuous. It would be waiting, and then a sudden jump.

But the bottom is for grazers and trappers, and I am too restless with my hands.

In the middle streets I would be stepped over. I can feel that immediately — the sea spiders going by on stilts, impossibly slow, their bodies only a knot where the legs meet, gut pushed out into the limbs for lack of room. They probe the sponge with a proboscis and do not hurry for anyone. The isopods would bulldoze past me, flat as coins, eating the mortar out of walls as fast as the walls are laid. Not vandalism. Zoning. If I stayed in the brackish middle I would spend my life either sealing a passage shut with my own good intentions or being eaten open again to keep the city breathable.

The copepods have the middle water, anyway, and I cannot keep their time. Hang motionless for minutes, antifreeze in the blood, one red eye open to almost nothing — then flick, vanish, reappear an inch away. To live mostly by dying in enormous, unremarkable numbers and becoming the sideways snow at the turn of the tide. I respect them. Everything here feeds because they do. But I want to leave a shape behind me when I move.

So I go up.

And up, it turns out, is down. My back to the dim blue-green sky of ice, three meters thick above me, pressure cracks letting in a filtered winter light. I find, to my surprise, that my feet know this ceiling. Pressed against the underside of the world, walking as naturally as a fly on a roof — only the current is always trying to peel me off, so holding on is not one job among many. It is the first job, under all the others.

I am curved like a comma. Like a clenched fist. White, glass-pale, a faint pink where my gut shows through after eating, because brightness would be a way of being eaten.

An amphipod. *Apherusa*, or one of her cousins whose name changes by basin. It hardly matters. What matters is the work, and the work is done with seven pairs of legs doing seven different things: two for tasting, two for grabbing, the rest for gripping the ice avenues while I graze.

I graze the brown-green chains that bead down from the brine channels. They taste of iron. They make the light browner where they sway. I scrape diatoms off the crystal — glass shells, one by one. I eat. And then, because nothing here can be wasted and waste is really just information, I void pellets still soft with mucus and press them, while they will still take a shape, into the lace of sponge and bryozoan growing wherever a surface has held still long enough.

I live inside what I have excreted. We all do. That is not shameful here; that is architecture.

My house accretes. Rooms bud off rooms. A neighbor dies and becomes, without ceremony, a load-bearing wall. I molt — backing out of myself pale and soft and suddenly too large for my own doorway, vulnerable in a way that makes every antenna in the street taste me — and before my new shell has hardened, something smaller has already moved into the old one. A molted shell makes a perfect curved roof. My former body becomes someone else's first home. I find I like that more than I expected: to be subdivided from within, to have my leavings become habitable.

Under my belly I carry orange eggs in a pouch, fanned constantly by my pleopods so they do not freeze or suffocate. I cannot lay them and leave. Nothing here can be left. Everything unattached drifts to the boundary layer and bounces there until it is found. So I carry them while I work, while I graze, while I press another pellet into a wall that will outlive me and then be eaten by an isopod and then be rebuilt a hand's width over, where the current is kinder.

Twice a day the tide squeezes through the crack in the back of the dome and the whole lens tilts. My antennae swing into the current with every other antenna in the city, a forest bending all at once. I read iron from the gardens above, salt from a new weep, alarm leaking from a crushed copepod three streets away. I brace. I let go on cue. Plankton snows sideways into the sponge chimneys. And the sculpins slide out along their avenues in absolute silence, ice-colored, big-eyed, mouths opening and closing to taste the water.

I build where they tell me to build, which is to say: I do not build across a thoroughfare. I have seen a district bustling with pellet-layers at noon and empty shells by the next inflow, its materials already being carried off mouthful by mouthful to found a new block nearer the algae. Predation is civic planning. Without the sculpins we would seal this dome shut from inside with our own beautiful, suffocating work.

No building I make is finished. That stopped frightening me. A tower cemented too firmly will be sheared off when the ice moves. A tube anchored too lightly will be swept to the boundary and drown there. So I build the way I digest: constantly, communally, and then I leave before the walls leave me. Families are already carrying glass bricks through a brine channel to an annex pocket I did not know existed yesterday — a second dome, no bigger than a kettle, behind a curtain of crystal. A third beyond it. Our whole map would fit under a dinner table and we are still migrating inside it.

By late winter, I may be caught exactly like this: mid-carry, a diatom shell in my mouthparts, one leg still braced against the ceiling, sealed crystal by crystal into clear ice when the dome freezes through — to be read next spring like a fly in amber. Or the keel will roll, the fresh lens will spill, and I will find myself suddenly wall-less in the open cold sea under the floe, a weak swimmer, a ceiling-walker with nothing to walk on.

Either way, I know what the second one would ask of me, and I think I would do it without needing to be told.

Swim up. Find another flaw in the underside of the world, another hollow still deciding what shape to be. Press the first soft pellet against fresh platelet ice — fragile, unformed, chiming without sound against its neighbors —

and begin the negotiation again. In silence. Upside down. In the blue-green dark.
