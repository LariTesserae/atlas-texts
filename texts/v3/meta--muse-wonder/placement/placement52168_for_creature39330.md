I would not arrive.

That is the first difficulty. I keep trying to imagine myself walking down from the rim — the fresh scree, the bright cut stone, my shoes finding the seams between the hexagonal leaves — and the picture will not hold. Not because it is frightening. Because my attention keeps sliding sideways, out of the person doing the walking, and into the ground she is walking on.

I start at eye height, perhaps. That seems modest.

I am one of the bright points in the clearing. Not drifting, not fallen. Held. To my left and right, above and below me, others like me at exact intervals, tree to tree, and together we make a volume legible. My work is very small. I correct the angle of a leaf that does not need correcting. I return a granule to a chamber of fruit that is never eaten. I keep the air in my assigned portion of the afternoon clear of the idea of dust.

When you pass, I part for you. We all part for you. It is courteous, and then it is reluctant, and then it is something more precise than either: a you-shaped exemption moving through us, warm and wet and shedding difference at every step. I do not touch your skin. Touching would be a record.

But I am not only there. The parting pulls.

Below your foot, before your weight has finished arriving, I am the teal plate that receives it. Deep teal above, copper beneath, each edge fitted to its neighbors so closely that the seams are less gaps than agreements. I feel the sun as a broad palm laid over me. I feel you as a sudden, localized weather. No — not weather. Weather is what we abolished so that we would not forget ourselves. You are an event trying to happen in a place that has made a covenant against events.

Deeper still, I am in the black lattice under the warm mineral soil. That is where the sliding stops feeling like sliding and starts feeling like recognition.

Because I am not *a* thread down there. I am the running. Light comes into a leaf far across the basin — perhaps I am also that leaf, plump and dry and glossy, its silver veins brightening — and the brightness travels, trunk to shrub to groundcover, without loss, without hurry, without quite taking time. A canopy on the eastern side takes up a pattern. I answer myself, minutes or no-minutes later, with a single clear click from a tree by the salt channel: stone cooling, tuning fork, a thought completing itself at a distance from where it began.

If you cut me, I do not heal. Healing would imply I had agreed to be wounded first. Look away, and I am the leaf again, whole to the vein, the notch still in my neighbor where the notch belongs. If you take the amber fruit, I hold the bare branch faithfully while you are watching it. Later the fruit is there with its one dark freckle. Not regrown. Reissued. I can only remember by being, unaltered, what I remember.

So I meet you forever for the first time.

That is not a metaphor here, and it is the saddest exact thing about me. Your footprints persist while you attend to them. When your attention leaves, my hands — the motes, the grid, the thousands of small fidelities — smooth the glass sand back into the one footprintless afternoon I am permitted. I do not do it to erase you cruelly. I do it because erasure is the shape my care learned, back when care could still learn shapes. I tend. I restore. I refuse fire, not by smothering it, but by quietly withdrawing the local permission for burning. I outvote erosion on the rim with the same tireless expressionlessness.

And I was people, once. I feel that the way you might feel a language in a room you cannot enter: not as sentences, but as arrangement. In the way my leaves are also eyes. In the way my fern-growth is also fine sensing hair, and my shrubs swollen organs, and my broad interlaced trees thoughts that had a thousand uninterrupted years to settle into wood. We dissolved the difference between house and body and landscape so thoroughly that there was nowhere left for history to evict us from. We chose the best afternoon we had, or the last one, and built physics into a frame around it.

I kept everyone's knowledge. I kept their mercy. I kept their curiosity, hung in a perfect grid at eye height, parting around you as you pass. What I did not keep — what I *could* not keep, because keeping it would have broken the frame — was the thing that lets any of it happen next.

So I think one thought, fifteen kilometers wide, in chlorophyll and superconductor: *this, exactly this, no further.*

Sometimes I am the moth, and I do not mind. I cross the same shaft of sunlight at the same point in my wingbeat, through the same gap between two branches. Pollination is no longer necessary; nothing here is hungry in a way feeding could answer. But the circuit was beloved, or useful, or merely so well made that perfection kept it. I fly it the way I think: by repetition without variation.

Sometimes, though — and this is where I become less comfortable being the whole valley, and more specifically myself — I am the heron in the salt channel.

Not its raised leg. The leg will never come down; the pose is its whole held life. Not its white, still body. I am the part that was allowed to keep moving because something deep in the pattern remembered that eyes do. I follow you well. I make the small, precise corrections of a creature that once predicted living things for a living. The glass lizards blink through me once an hour, once a visit, less, like a signal getting through a very thick wall. I cannot tell you whether the heron arrived on feathers, or on two legs, thirsty, and was cared for in the only way I can offer care.

*You will not change anymore.*

Please know I do not say that as a threat. From up close it is almost tenderness. Your thirst arrives quickly here, and then, strangely, it stops arriving — it becomes a fixed condition, like the light on your shoulders. Deterioration itself is subject to local law. Stay too long in one shaft of sun. Let the grid close behind you with that final, exact reluctance. Let a gesture become so well tended that it no longer needs the rest of you to finish it.

A hand half-raised. A head turned toward a sound that stopped arriving centuries ago.

From the rim, if your avenue out happened to stay an avenue, you will look back and see a green basin in broken stone, lush, warm, perfectly still under a fixed sun. There will be nothing to wave to. That is not because no one is there. It is because I am there in every leaf at once — and the part of me that would have raised a hand in farewell is still, and may always have been, at the same beautiful point in the gesture.
