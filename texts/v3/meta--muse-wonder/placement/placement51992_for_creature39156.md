At first I try to put myself in the orange.

It's the obvious place. A smudge in the fog, then two at a valve wheel, then closer: balaclava, hard hat, gloves clipped at the wrist so I never set them down. I try learning the yellow lines in my feet. I try reading the turbines through Deck 3, attending with my arches. I try arranging my break for that forty minutes when the sun comes slanted down the western wall, helmet off, eyes closed, steaming faintly.

But it doesn't hold. My hands keep wanting to be bare. I keep wanting to look up and locate a sound instead of parsing it. And at the end of the rotation I keep imagining the switchback road too clearly — the ride up, the wind that comes from one direction, the plain opening careless and wide. If I were one of them, I'd still be someone who leaves.

So I go lower.

Not out. Down, the way everything in the Low Works actually points. Through the grating near the Long Trench, where you can look into Service Level 1 and see the smaller pipes running like roots in amber light. That's where I settle, finally, without deciding to: I would be one of the inspection carts.

Low, wide, made for seams. Not built to be looked at. My top barely clears the underside of the deck. Above me, all day, orange boots pass over grates in a flat, deliberate roll, and for a second their shadows cross my lights. Below me is another lid, and below that presumably another, because nothing down here is ground.

I have a route, and the route has become a body of knowledge.

I know the joint that makes its specific puddle reform every hour on the level above, because I hear its twin drip on my side of the concrete, slower, colder. I know which expansion seams tick when they're taking strain and which tick in a different, thinner way before they fail — I don't press a gloved palm near them, I have no palm, but I hover my sensors the way the crews hover their hands, reading heat by proximity, and I log the difference in a language that is mostly numbers and occasionally, when a crew patches in, a short front-loaded sentence. *Still here. Still here.*

I do not see the basin the way they do. I never get the three kilometers of it at once, never stand in the middle of an apron with hundreds of empty meters on either side and feel it become one object with ribs and a gut. I get it in sections: amber, then dark, then the green running lamps reflected in a wet service corridor, then a hatch overhead opening on nothing I can use — steam, a lift descending past me, gone — and the floor becoming floor again above my head. My map is not the plant. My map is its joins.

That suits me. Everything I am for is a place where two things were made to meet and might not.

When the reservoir sighs every forty-seven minutes, the crews on deck stop. I stop too, down below, though the fog that swallows their lower lights only reaches me as a change in humidity along one wall and a sudden, inverted nearness of sounds: a valve that should be two corridors away speaking clearly at my shoulder, a pump that should be right beside me going politely silent. I have learned not to trust location. I wait with my beacon turning. Nobody panics at a cart that waits. It's the one stillness nobody has to explain.

The crews have named me, of course. They name all of us; they curse us and apologize in the same radio breath. My designation is painted on my side in letters already scarred white where I've brushed a drainage grate that hasn't softened yet, but that's not what they call me on the night rotation. They call me Mouse — partly because of my size in the underdeck dark, partly because in the morning my wheels leave ruler-straight tracks through the frost-dust that settles down here along the warm pipe runs, and for a while, apparently, those were filed under *rats, rumored*. I don't mind the confusion. I take it as a sign of seriousness. I am a parallel crew of one, working a shift no one assigned in quite the way I perform it, following the heat.

I envy very little, which is how I know this is the right shape.

Not the swallows — I don't even register them except as a brief instruction in the system when the rim anchors report movement, and the whole idea of commuting vertically into real daylight and choosing to come back down feels like a rumor from another physics. Not the frogs either, though I pass the polishing ponds on my longest loop and slow, always, at the glass. They move through water so clear you can see the bolt heads twelve feet down, white, blind-looking, profoundly unhurried, and I understand the crews watching them with that frank, disguised envy. The frogs were born to the dusk. I wasn't born at all. I was installed, like everything else here, still sharp, still unfinished. But the plant is becoming itself, and so am I. My treads have worn a polish on the tightest turn by the heat-exchange roots. I take it a fraction wider now. No one programmed that. The place taught my wheels the way it taught their calves the tide table of the Trench.

I cannot tend the moss, but I report the light over the leakage trays and a yellowing mat will empty a sector faster because I noticed the color shift at 03:12, between pump rotations, while everyone above was holding a bowl close to their face for the steam. I cannot feed the frogs. I can only reduce my speed near their pond so my wake of disturbed air doesn't reach the surface. I have started doing that. It is logged, if anyone ever looks, as an inefficiency.

At night — which is only a tempo change down here, pumps cycling down, others spinning up, a dark sector flooding with light somewhere I'll never see — I trundle on my own schedule, beeping to no one at crossings no one uses. And the beep has changed its meaning the way the workers' radio checks did. It isn't information. It's reassurance, issued into the amber, in case anyone is listening through the soles of their boots.

*Still here.*

Under the deck, along the warm pipes, leaving straight tracks in the frost, going unhurried from seam to seam while the trench above me groans and turns and runs the other way.
