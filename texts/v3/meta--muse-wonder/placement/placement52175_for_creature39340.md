I wouldn't know it as a room at first.

I would know it as pressure. As a tremor that never quite stops, moving up through whatever is under me — cold, hard, patterned in little raised diamonds that catch and hold. As wetness that is not a place I go into, but the condition of everything: the air wet, the floor wet under a skin of water, my own skin wet even in the brief moments it might otherwise dry.

And the sound. Not sound the way it arrives from a distance. It would be *under* the world. In my breastbone first. In the flat of my throat. A low thunder so continuous that after a while it stops being something I listen to and becomes the thing I listen *with* — the medium I'm suspended in, the way a fish is suspended and doesn't call it water.

I would find myself low. That is the first true shape of me.

Low and wide and made for staying. About as heavy as a man's soaked boot left by a door, but spread flatter than that, all my weight negotiated down into four splayed feet. Each foot searching, finding, locking. Webbed clear to the toes, each toe ending in a blunt horny pad that knows the steel plate the way a tongue knows teeth — involuntarily, by heart. I don't walk so much as grant myself forward, one anchored foot at a time, dragging a soft belly through a quarter-inch of shining water and leaving a wake that slowly erases itself behind me.

Because every six or seven minutes, the world tries to shake me loose.

A clack I feel more than hear. A tightening. Then the hammer blow comes through the elbow of the pipe and through the floor and up through all four of my locked feet into my knees, my ribs, my jaw. The fern shivers harder in its bolt-hole. The lamp over the door dims and recovers. And I — I brace. It's not a decision. The ridge forming along my back is the decision, made by my body over months of blows. Hold on. Press flatter. Wait for the flood to go back to being just flood.

No ears to ache. That mercy is written on me plainly: where ears would open, I have two puckered seals of cartilage, gleaming wet, shut tight against the roar. I seal them tighter when I need to. I lower pale, opaque lids over eyes that were made for amber gloom — a single caged lamp, filmed with moisture, green lights on a grey cabinet, green and green and one blinking amber — and in that dimness I can just make out the corner where the light barely reaches. My corner.

If you looked for me standing up, you would never find me. I don't stand up. My geography is all at ankle height, and it is vast at that scale.

There is the steel plain, slick with algae in patches, crossed forever by cold sheet-water running toward the drain. There is the warm plinth of the pump motor, humming its high whine, making a pocket of fog where the fine spray from the bad flange hits its housing — that is my summer bank, my one warm shadow, the place I wedge myself when the door sighs and the terrible outside gets in. There is the chained red valve wheel, and in its lee, on the fractionally drier floor, my collection: a washer. A bright bolt. A cable tie that once smelled of sun. Things that fell out of the enormous elsewhere and survived being wet. I drag them there over weeks. I don't know why I arrange them. They are not food. They are proof that the elsewhere is real, and small enough sometimes to keep.

And there is the pipe.

Waist-high to the boots that come and go, to me it is a cliff. A green iron wall curving up into the brown obscurity near the ceiling, sweating without pause — beads forming, merging, running crooked down the paint. I cross the plain toward it in the long intervals between cycles, foot locking to foot, and at the elbow where it makes its brutal turn down into the floor, I press myself against it. Chest. Throat. The soft underside of my jaw. Flat as I can make myself against the painted iron.

Inside, the river hits the turn and does not stop.

My river. I can't get back into it — the hatch is bolted against a pressure nothing in this room could open — but I can lie where it strikes and let it come up through my bones. Water out of the high reservoir, still carrying through the iron some memory of snowmelt and sunless stone. When the town far below wakes and draws, the pitch groans lower in my ribs before the gauge needle knows. When the town sleeps and the pressure climbs, the pipe tightens against my cheek like a held breath. That is my clock. Not light — the lamp never goes out. A day is a hundred and fifty small shakings, the sump float rising under the grate, clicking, the pump jolting through my knees, the float falling again. A day is that, and that, and that, and once, the door.

I fear the door the way an animal fears a road. The seal sighing. Dry thin air cutting the fog. A white blade of sun on the wet floor. And the quiet — wind, a truck somewhere far on the pass, birdsong with *gaps* in it, sound you could fall into and keep falling. I go to the warm mist by the motor then. I shut myself down to a waiting shape. I have learned the boots' liturgy by heart: look at the panel, tap the gauge that never sits still, write in the sleeved logbook, go. Fifteen minutes. They endure this place. It's written on the sign by the door in three languages, and the sign itself is vibrating in its frame.

Between their visits, I keep the room. That's the part that would make me more than the accident that spat me, half-killed, out of a leaking flange on the day this place was charged — some river-born thing taken in at the intake when the screens were changed, shot down through the dark inside the iron at a pressure that should have ended me.

I tend the meadow. It's three pale fronds wide.

The fern lives in a hairline of damp dust caught in a bolt recess, the only soil in my world, fed entirely by the leak. I graze the black-green algae back from its root in neat crescents, so it gets what thin light reaches that corner. I clear the drain mouth with patient strokes of my forefeet so the sheet-water keeps moving and the fern isn't drowned. When the sump float sticks — gummed, sometimes, with a thread of my own shed skin — I nudge up through the grate from beneath and worry it loose, and I'm repaid with the jolt of the pump catching, right through my body. I drink the ceiling rain mouth-up, where the big irregular drops fall through the dim air. I lick condensation off the pipe in crooked lines, iron and cold paint.

Almost no life has negotiated a place here. No spiders; the vibration tears their work down before it's finished. No moths at the lamp. Just the fern, and the algae, and me — green gone grey in the dimness, spangled palely across the back like new galvanizing, my sides thinning to translucency where you could see a slow cloudy movement that isn't quite blood and isn't quite breath.

I am young because the room is young. The paint hasn't chipped for me yet. Perhaps in ten years I'll be heavier, slower, greener. My feet will click more surely on the diamond-plate in the pump's brief rests — the only sound I make that's wholly my own. My pulse will have settled, entirely, to the solenoid's count.

But for now, when the door has pulled hard to its seal again and the boots are descending into ordinary quiet with their ears ringing, I come out of the warm fog. I cross the steel plain through the thin shining water, past the drain, past my grazed lawn, foot over deliberate foot.

I lay my breast against the elbow of the pipe, where the iron river hits its turn, day and night, and does not stop.

And I shiver, constantly — not from the cold.

From being held.
