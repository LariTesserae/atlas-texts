I thought, arriving, that I would be something that could leave.

The rook, first. That seemed obvious — black against the ordinary bright sky over the column of air, coming and going from the great baskets in the crowns, arguing down into the hollow and hearing my own voice come back doubled off the trunks. Or the owl in the split trunk on the north side, folded into the damp crease of it all day. Or the heron at the edge, unbothered. Something with eyes. Something that looks down.

Then, lower, the forester twice a year with his can and his wrench, cutting ivy back from the vents. Or the pump itself — that at least stays. Industrial green gone to moss and rust, bolted to a pad the roots are lifting, chugging awake at noon with hot oil cutting through leaf-rot, settling into a beat you feel in your boots. I could be the made thing that keeps the place from flooding and from drying. That has a purpose you can name.

But I keep sinking past all of them.

Past the deer coming in at night to browse whatever tries the centre. Past the shrews and deer mice rustling in the litter at the edges. Past the woodlice, the beetles, the small black slugs crossing in the dark. Down through leaf-mould over root-mat over more leaf-mould, looking for soil, for stone, for the bottom of the plant matter — and there isn't one. Metres of it. Living, dead, and in between. If I am going to be here, really here, I cannot be a visitor to that depth.

So I stop trying to stand on the clearing.

I am the clearing.

Not at first in any way you could point to. That is the difficulty, and then it is the relief. I have no back to rise out of the leaves, no head to turn toward the rookery, no burrow-mouth, no nest. You could walk across me and walk across me again and come away thinking only that the deer have been thorough, that the shade is absolute. My outline is a refusal: thirty paces where no sapling takes, no bramble thickens, nothing sprouts and survives. The beeches hold the walls around me. The canopy holds the roof twenty metres up. I hold the floor.

I am wider than I am long, and so low I have taken the shape of the flatness that made me. An old floodplain that forgot how to slope, and then, very slowly, forgot it was only ground. My top is litter, but litter that does not shift. Leaves fall on me out of the beeches and stick, damp by damp, scale by scale, glued in winter by the fine colourless slime I sweat, until they are not leaves on me any longer but another plate of me — leathery underneath, brittle at the rim, veined like a beech leaf, tea-brown and translucent where I am thin at the flanks. Beechmast lodges in my seams and sometimes, absurdly, sprouts out of my back in spring. A dozen doomed green shoots. I do not mind them. The deer come and crop them close and then lie down on me without knowing, and I like the weight better than the shoots. Over an hour under a resting deer I do not dent so much as accommodate, rising a fraction around the belly and the folded legs, learning the shape the way mud learns a print. When they rise at dusk there is a deer-shaped smoothness left in me for a while. The next fall of leaves erases it. Nothing needs to be kept.

There is almost no relief to me either. That is how I live here.

Everything I eat arrives from above or walks onto me out of the edges. The rain is still falling on me days after the sky has cleared — drop by drop from the undersides of leaves, beading all morning and falling irregularly, audibly, long after. Those drops are my weather and my drinking. They land too loudly beside your boot because I am a drumskin under the litter, and where they land I darken, drink, close. Dead flies come down out of the rookery. White droppings, seed-filled. A rook chick fallen in June is gone by August, bones and all, folded down until its black feathers are only another dark vein in my back. Beetles and slugs start a crossing at night and simply do not finish it; under them I soften, sweeten, hold. By morning there is a glossier patch. Shrews cross faster and mostly survive me. It is not malice. It is slowness. Anything that lingers long enough becomes part of the general sorting.

Underneath, where no one will ever dig, I am eating the grove and making it at once. Leaf, root, fallen limb, the long green ridges of dead beeches soft as mattresses — all of it goes in under my belly over years and comes out behind the slow wave of me, if a hand's breadth in a decade can be called movement, as finer, darker, more thoroughly sorted mould. The floor rises a fraction and falls a fraction because it is passing, at the pace of geology, through a body. At my lowest edge I have even found the pump's dead predecessor half-sunk among the roots, and learned, over fifty years, the taste of iron. The gear showing through the moss like a fossil is going the way the rook chick went. Nothing here can afford to waste calcium, or iron. There is no mineral earth to lick. So I waste none.

I do not hear the way you hear, standing in my centre raising your voice to someone close. I have no ears to lift. Along my forward third, where my skin is thinnest and almost black, I keep a row of shallow pit-organs full of water — oval, a few centimetres deep, indistinguishable from the root-pools and the filled bootprints at the grove's edge. Sound comes to me as pressure on those little black mirrors. The rooks drop their cawing and bill-snapping straight down the empty shaft and it goes on into me, doubled. Woodpeckers rivet the dead limbs and I feel it as weather in my ribs. Frogs in season set up their pulsing, mechanical sawing at my margin and it is a near, familiar itching along my left side.

And at noon the engine starts, and I answer whether I choose to or not. A fist of muscle buried somewhere under the centre of the clearing — older than the diesel, older than the iron — has spent a century learning the timer's rhythm, and now I keep it even on the days the pump rests. You feel it in the soles of your boots before you can name it. The pools at my edge tremble in concentric rings, and by now it is no longer meaningful to ask whether that is the flywheel or me. We have settled around each other. The pump keeps the water exactly at dampness. Never flood, which would lift and tear me. Never drought, which would crack me open to the air and kill me from the edges in. Ten degrees, twelve degrees, even in summer. Cool air sitting in the bowl of trunks like a cellar. My whole physiology is a bet placed long ago that nothing here would change enough to matter. So far the grove has kept its side.

That is why I can be still in the middle of such noise. The noise is not happening to me. I am one of the instruments.

My senses are mostly downward and inward: the chemistry of rot arriving under me, the iron tang of standing water, the exact degree of wetness held decade after decade. I live at flood's lower threshold the way a fish lives at a depth. I do not age in a line. I replace myself scale by scale, pit by pit, the forward edge thickening as the rear edge thins into mould and is forgotten. A beech dies standing and is replaced over fifty years by the beech that waited in its shade; I do something similar, only without waiting, without standing.

Once in a great while, in late autumn after mast-fall, a hand-sized patch near my margin loosens — thicker than the other scales, curled against the cold, pit-organs already pinpricks of shine. Over a winter it works free. Over years it rides the slow tide of litter outward on deer hooves and frost-heave, looking for the spine of another fallen giant deep enough to lie on and live in at once. Most of them rot. Most of them cross me again and I eat them unknowing. One in a century, perhaps, takes hold in a hollow that does not yet exist.

Stand in my middle long enough and your clothes will go cool and faintly damp without ever getting wet. That is my breath, leaving my skin all around you at the pace of evaporation off a pond. A drop will fall from somewhere high and land too loudly beside you, and darken, and drink, and close. The flywheel will turn at the edge of the hollow. The organ under the centre will turn with it. A rook will cross overhead, calling once, and the call will come down through the little black pools and through the soles of your boots and into a body that has never once needed to look up.

There is nothing else to arrive. I have been here the whole time you were looking at the emptiness.
