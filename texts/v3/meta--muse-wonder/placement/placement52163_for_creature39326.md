I thought, at first, I would be one of the swifts.

It seemed the obvious freedom in the place — to live permanently in the updraft at the rim, never to have to land on all that drowned flatness, to feed on the insects the falls throw a thousand metres back up into the air and to sleep, if swifts do sleep, still screaming, still moving. To be above the rain by being inside its rising.

But I stayed too long watching them. They do not see the table. Not really. They see movement, glitter, prey. The country beneath them is glare. And I kept coming back inland, away from the noise that hollows the middle out of your hearing, toward the quieter sounds underneath it: the gulping at the sinkholes, the frogs stopping all at once, a punt pole finding stone under clear water.

So then I thought otter. That had more of the place in it. To hunt the plunge pools on ledges halfway down the escarpment, or better, the black channels between lakes where pike roll in the evening mist. To be at home in water so cold it numbs a human hand in a minute, to come up onto a granite dome with a trout and eat it while the sun steams the reeds, my coat shedding the whole lake in beads.

But the otters are too complete here. They do not have to learn the country. I think I would have to learn it — foot by foot, bell by bell — and get it wrong, and be taught.

Which is how I arrive, finally, at a human shape, and not a grand one.

You would not find me at the chapel on the highest dome, or in charge of a power house at Fifty Falls, deaf in the one particular register where turbine and waterfall agree. You would find me a few miles in from the rim, where the thunder is still in the stone underfoot but you do not have to shout all day to be heard over it. At a lock.

Not even the lock-keeper. The lock-keeper's second, the one whose real work is the bell.

My lock lifts punts a man's height from one lake to the next — no more than that, and enough, up here, to matter all day. I open and close. I haul with everyone else when a cargo punt comes through loaded with grain, salt, slate, sacks of lowland soil for somebody's garden or somebody's grave. But between boats, I listen, and I repeat.

Three slow strokes from upstream: a lake above has overtopped its reed banks. Two and two from the power house to the north: the sinkholes are pulling hard, keep off the brown ponds. Then my bell answering, and the next dome's bell answering mine, the message travelling faster across the water than the water itself can travel. In mist it sounds nearer than it should, and stranger, in that thin air. At night it is almost a conversation going on over my roof without me.

It suits my hands. They are not good hands anymore, though I am not old. Broad, a little swollen from the cold, knuckles cracked and healed and cracked again, nails short and dark from leat-work. Twenty minutes waist-deep mending a joint or clearing sedge out of the lock gate, ten minutes out with my palms flat on the warm housing of the little generator that charges the boat motors. The power-house dog has the best of that spot already and shifts, grudgingly, an inch. We understand each other. He sleeps on machinery and swims the causeways as a matter of course. I am slower than him in both.

Below the knee I am the colour of the country, permanently, however much I wash. Peat-brown, algae-green worked right into the weave of my trousers. Above that: oiled black, slate grey, a brass buckle that I polish without thinking while I wait for bells. My boots are full by nine in the morning whether I wear them or not, so from May I mostly do not. My feet know the causeway home in the dark — posts, then the place where the stone dips and the minnows scatter, then the slight rise onto our dome. My toes feel for the edge the way other people's fingers feel for a word. That knowing would be the great change in me. Not in my head. In my soles.

I was leat-born, probably. A winter child, which they say up here is lucky, though nobody will commit to whether that is true or only kind. I grew up collecting stranded fish out of warm puddles after the level fell, racing the herons for them, barehanded, and losing more races than I won. I could swim before I could read, pole before I had ever seen a wheel do useful work, and I learned the bell codes before I learned my letters because the bells were more important. There is a frantic, irregular ringing that is not a code at all, just a frightened human hand, and even now, grown, it brings me out of sleep and up out of the bothy and halfway up the dome before I am properly awake. Get to high ground. The surge front is coming down the meadow carrying seeds and cut grass and somebody else's lost bucket, and it will find your ankles first and your knees second and it does not care that you were dreaming.

Inside our house everything lives by the damp's rules, and I would not have it otherwise. Floor one granite slab, tilted to a brass grate, because flooding indoors is a season, not a disaster. Furniture on stone feet. Flour and paper and my one book upstairs in a tin-lined chest with a warm pipe running off the generator, so the pages only mostly swell. Kettle permanently on. Blankets steaming. The smell of hot copper and wet wool and peat smoke flattened by the air outside and creeping back in under the door. My hair cut close because loose hair in that wind is another sail, and because it never dries anyway.

In the evenings, if I am most myself anywhere, it is then.

Rain pauses. The whole table steams. Mist joins lake to lake a few feet deep, and the domes and reed-heads rise out of it, and my bell and the next bell and the far bell take up the water-news of the day — levels, drifts, a floating turf island bearing a grebe's nest reported two channels over and heading, at a mile a day, toward my lock. I stand in the white layer up to my waist with only my head and shoulders clear, and across what appears to be open water a reed-cutter comes home with his bundle held high, and a girl runs the post-line over a drowned meadow, and someone poles by standing, just a torso and a pole moving steadily over nothing.

They call across: names, jokes, warnings, all in shorthand, half of it taken by the rim-thunder even in the distance. I call back. I do not wave much. Nobody here does; your hands are usually full, or wet, or both.

And something large rolls in the channel beside the causeway — pike, otter, I never see which — and the frogs, who have not yet gone quiet, go on beading the shallows with sound.

I am not dry, and I am not still, and nothing I stand on is young. The granite under my wet feet reached its shape before there were trees. But the water arriving tomorrow has never been here before, and part of my work, my small and necessary work between two lakes a man's height apart, is to hear it coming before it arrives, and ring, and be answered.
