I think I would first know where I was by my breathing.

Not by sight — there wouldn't be enough of that. The stars would stop above a wall, the court lamp would be behind me turned down to its amber pool, and ahead the plain would be black on black, warm through the soles of my feet. My chest would be doing the work it was born to do up here: broad, unhurried, those long ribs opening and closing long after the rest of me had gone still, pulling a voice out of me that only carries two meters. In a room, without thinking, I would fall into rhythm with everyone else.

My hands would smell before I did. Machine oil cut with solvent, worked into the webs of my fingers this morning at shift-change so the skin won't split and powder at the edges. Under that, hot iron. Under that, my own district — its reclaimer sourness, the scorched dust of its cable trench, protein paste and pipe-water. You could read me the way I read everyone else: district first, then trade, then the private person underneath, if you came close enough and we inclined our heads and you rested a hand, lightly, on my sternum to feel the words.

I wouldn't be a smelter. My lungs are this place's best instrument and it wouldn't waste them at a furnace mouth, black to the elbow, iron in the pores of my throat forever. I wouldn't be a vat tender either, yellowish and yeasty-smelling years after I'd left the sheds — I am too easily imprinted, I think; whatever I tended would keep me.

No. Find me between districts, where the city is a constellation and mostly isn't.

I would be a walker of the pipe runs. One of the people sent out alone — because alone is not a hardship here, it's a sensory condition — to put my palms down on low trestles and listen through basalt for what the thin air won't carry. A ticking where a rail car is still an hour away. A change in the flywheel hum so deep I feel it in my molars before I could swear I'd heard it. A press descending once an hour, every hour, arriving in my feet as a soft concussion, telling me the time in a place that has agreed not to mark it any other way. My language would have a dozen ordinary words for kinds of vibration, and I would use all of them precisely, and have almost no word for echo.

My track would already be narrowing. That's how you'd know my age before you saw my face, close up, the only way faces are for. The young wander — their prints meander and double back around the schoolroom doors and the vat-sheds, a trample of not-yet-decided lives. Mine would be resolving into the polished lane between the generating hall and the coolant plant, each step placed almost inside the last, a groove a few centimeters deep after thirty years of barely lifting my feet. Not running. I was taught not to run the way you were taught your letters: not as discipline, as physiology. The blood sings behind the eyes. There is nowhere in a city that has made the same tonnage for centuries that needs to be reached at a run.

I would know the route blind, and sometimes I would walk it that way on purpose, by smell alone, the way children navigate home. Hot iron exhaling off the generating hall. The ammonia bite that means I'm three streets from the vats, though street is a generous word for a polished trough in bedrock. A pocket of warm yeast. Then the mineral, almost-sweet smell of the waste channels, where the pale crust thickens a fraction of a millimeter a decade around leaking coolant — not life, exactly, but the closest agreement this plateau has made with it.

I would carry flakes of that crust in a pouch, out of habit I couldn't trace to any decision, for the lizards. Pale, blind, a hand's length, motionless against the warm outer wall of the power station with their mouths open, rasping. I would sit with my back to that same wall sometimes, among the old people, in the same heat for the same reason, and none of us would call them pets and none of us would call it anything else. In a lean year, if the freight lifts were late, we would eat them, and that too would not be a decision anyone remembered making. Arrangements here persist long enough to become structure. So do I.

My name would not be only mine. It would be in strict district rotation, worn before me by a woman whose groove in the dust runs under my own for the first kilometer out of our block, so that a good stretch of track belongs to four of us and it's genuinely unclear where she stops and I begin. That would not trouble me. It would be the point. A good worker here is not one who improves the machine. A good worker returns it to its tolerances so exactly that the next century finds it unchanged — and I would be good at my work, and proud of it in the quiet way we're proud of our bodies' water loops, and I would also be the one who notices, palms down in a shared sudden stillness, the half-second when the hum is wrong. Maintenance is a form of attention so complete it can look like stillness from outside. I know that kind of attention. I would be very, very good at staying still until the stone told me something.

What I would offer you, if you came up from below and lasted — most don't — is the highest hospitality the plateau affords: the first draw from a freshly charged loop, water that tastes only of clean pipe, not yet of people. We'd drink it communally, in sips too small to be called drinking, and I would politely notice nothing about how you smelled of the lower air, of soup, of drowning, and you would try not to gag on the faint petroleum sweetness in everything I fed you, and we would both understand that thirst here is not drama. It's accounting. Chronic, and perfectly managed, like everything else that has reached its final form.

And sometimes — once or twice a year, always from the far districts — my route would cross a set of prints that aren't. Not quite. Too long in the heel. Weight distributed wrong for four legs, and right, terribly right, for someone who walked their own groove so deep and so straight for so long that one day they went down into it and didn't get back up. I would follow them a little way out past the last lamp, the way the children do in packs, daring each other — except alone, and not daring myself, just reading. The dust between prints growing untouched. The smells I know thinning to hot iron, baked dust, nothing. My own groove behind me, resolved, patient, waiting to deepen.

I would turn back. Bodies up here do not oblige by vanishing; I know how this ends and it isn't out there. It ends dried light as a carried child above the furnaces, taken by slow rail past the capped junction, walked the last kilometer to the rounded lip where the one natural wind in the world lifts your clothes for the only time outdoors, and lets you glitter, briefly, under fixed stars, and fall three thousand meters into a darkness that sends nothing back. The only disappearance available in a place that forgets nothing else. I would value it precisely for that.

Until then: palms down. Listening through stone. One step inside the last.
