I think I would arrive the way everyone does — as a mistake.

Up the stair cut into the root-rock, footsore after days of rounded granite, expecting a summit or a descent, and finding instead that the world simply declines to stop. The cloud-sea below. The black-green moss going on until it gives up and becomes sky. I'd spend the first weeks, maybe the first season, looking for the inhabitants in the longhouses, sleeping gratefully in a warm guest-chamber with a woven roof, drinking from the cisterns, waiting to be introduced to the people of this district.

And then, slowly, the embarrassment of realizing I have been sleeping *inside* my host. That my host has been politely adjusting around me in the night. That the conversation I thought was wind in the fern-trees has a tilt to it, an inflection, and it has been including me for some time.

I wouldn't become one of the rooted quickly. I'm not built — in any sense that matters here — for the long central act of their lives: to plant myself for three hundred years and become, by growing, a house, a council vote, a shade-pattern, a marriage that a rope-bridge can be anchored to. I admire it too much from the outside, which is a walker's failing. The rooted citizens would find me slightly frivolous. They would also, I suspect, be glad to see me coming up the road.

Because I think that's where the Table would put me. Not in a plaza. *Between* plazas.

I'd be in the walking phase for a long time — decades, by their reckoning, which means I'd barely get anywhere at all. Trunk low, canopy folded against the travel-cold, roots gathered behind me in that long tasting train, going a kilometre a season and thinking it was speed. I'd take the municipal employment that walkers take, because it's the only work that matches my nature: dragging the measuring chain from one district to the next, ringing the wooden bell once a day, so the road can hear itself and remember that it is three days long.

It suits me, that job. To insist, on foot and out loud, that two far-apart places are still the same place. To keep a distance honest by repeating it. I have always been a creature made mostly of messages carried between groves that cannot touch — so let me carry the other kinds, too. A cutting in a moss-lined bronze pot, held up over the fog-channels so it can learn them, listening with its whole unplanted self while the neighbourhood debates where it should become a street. A scent-message that can't be rushed. A lantern, once in a while, walked *into* yesterday evening for ceremonial purposes, with everyone agreeing not to make a habit of it — I'd try very hard not to make a habit of it, and the effort would be visible for miles.

I'd be bad at some of it, at first. Too fast in conversation, answering a canopy's tilt before the shiver had finished running frond to frond. I'd have to learn to remember properly, which here means in winter, in strata. I'd fall in love with views, the way walkers do, and be teased for it in scent on a still afternoon, gold arriving from a family three districts away who had heard — two years late, perfectly on time — that I was well, and slightly larger than before. I'd learn the real geography eventually: that shadows fall toward home here, slightly, like filings toward iron, so you are always being leaned toward the nearest inhabited place. I would stop mentioning it after the first week. That is how I'd know I was staying.

And the roads would make sure of it. The unattended ones, lengthening politely behind me. Leaving taking longer than arriving did.

I don't think I'd root often — once, perhaps, in some district where the mist rises uphill past the doorways at night and the plate-lichens hold a pocket of warmth above them like a held breath, and plant myself where a path already wanted to re-route to include somebody. I'd grow my chambers slowly. I'd host travellers inside my own grown rooms and pretend, as my first host did for me, that this is entirely normal. I'd take part in a drainage scandal. I'd lean, over fifty years, on the question of an aqueduct, and find my roots had settled the matter more honestly underground than my canopy ever managed in public. I'd let my earlier floors go down into the black strata, house under house under house, each version flatter.

But even then, I suspect, I'd be counted among the restless ones — the bell-ringers, the road-keepers, the ones who make a vocation of the between. And at the end of one of those long insistences, halfway between two plazas with the same street-names and the numbering still continuing, I can see the place I'd finally allow myself to be planted mid-route.

A milestone who used to carry messages. Roots down into the fog-channels, canopy wide enough that the shade is useful, a chamber left open on the road side for whoever is walking it that decade. The road, politely, shortening around me. Travellers resting in a person and speaking quietly, the way you do in a place someone decided was a good place to stand for a while. My bell hung in the branches, where the wind could do the remembering on slow days.

Nothing finished. Just, for now — like everything and everyone on the Table — flat enough to live on, and still holding the distance together by being stood in it.
