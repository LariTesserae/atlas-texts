If you put me down those nine steps, I wouldn't last as a bather.

I tried that first. Ducking under the hatch, going down hand-over-hand because the steps are too steep to trust, chest-deep in the middle where you can turn without touching anything. The water would take me for an hour. The lime would not. It would come off on me in that faint milky bloom, and I'd come off in it — skin prickling from the fresh mortar, knees finding the sharp corners of the new tiles, eyes watering up at the square of sun. At noon I'd have to close them. Hers throw the light back. Mine don't.

I wouldn't last as a scrubber, either. That's honest work, early work: boots on the narrow ledge, stiff brush, wooden paddle, scouring until the tank smells clean. But scrubbers belong to the wash-house above. They have somewhere to go when the hatch is kicked shut over them. And I think if my paddle knocked the tiles down in the shadiest bottom corner, and I felt something give there that wasn't water, I would not be able to pretend I hadn't.

For a while I thought I might be one of the ferns.

They have the right scale for the place. Coin-sized, patient, living exactly where the wall sweats. Or the reed she saved — roots in a crack on the ledge, leaning a little further toward the light shaft every few days, the only straight thing in the room besides the column of sun. I envied the reed its errand. To stand at the margin and measure.

But the ferns are already hers, whether she knows it or not. She breathes there and they spread. And the reed is hers, too. She planted it. She keeps looking at it like a question she isn't ready to ask the hatch.

So I kept listening instead of looking, the way she does. Ears full of water, the roar coming apart.

That's how I found the place I'd actually be.

Not the inflow — that's too loud, too constant, too proud of itself, thumb-thick and bath-hot, smacking the surface all day from the bronze spout. I'd be on the other side of the room. Low in the west wall. The overflow.

I would not be the clay pipe, exactly. I'd be what has accrued in its throat, the way she accrued in the tank: first just a roughness in the current, then a way for the water to hesitate, then something that could hold a shape around that hesitation. A soft, dark, half-air creature living right at the waterline's decision point. Mostly mouth. Mostly listening.

My whole body would be that inch the level rises and sighs down when I clear.

That's my breathing. Fill until the tank is a fraction too full, until the pressure changes against my face, and then — swallow. Gurgle. Let go. The surface settles. She settles with it, hung upright in the middle at night, chin just clear, and feels it the way you feel a stair you can't see. I know her by that. Not by sight. From where I am the noon glare never quite reaches; I get it secondhand, trembling bands and turquoise spill, and her disturbance crossing them. I know her by taste and by touch.

I taste what the room leaches: wet chalk from new joints in the first hour, the mineral salt she licks from her own fingernails, a drowned ant going past too fast to save, seed husks from the filter beds, once the green thread of algae come loose from somewhere she has scratched. And at scrubbing time I know her by hand. She makes herself small in the cool corner and hooks her fingers lightly into my mouth to hold against the swirling, and I — this is the whole of my courage — I hold still around them. I don't pull. I don't clear. I let the level rise that one dangerous inch while the paddle knocks and boots shift on the ledge above her shining, open eyes, and only when the hatch bangs and the steps empty do I let go and take the excess away, sighing.

Because I am also the room's only other way out.

The hatch frightens her. It should. It's wide and wooden and it opens onto feet and daylight falling flat. My opening is narrow and wet and it opens onto darkness that leads somewhere: down through the sighing channel, out to the filter beds where the reed came from, where gravel sorts the water and roots think their slow thoughts and the air smells of outside. I can't bring much back — I'm the leaving direction, that's my sorrow and my use — but I bring back pressure. News in the body. When the beds are full after rain, I slow, and she feels my slowness against her fingers and knows, without ever seeing it, that the world beyond the walls is swollen. When they run clear and hungry, I pull a little harder. She knows that too.

We are opposites in the system that made us both. The spout says: keep coming, keep warm, keep flooded. The hatch says: someday, up. I say the quieter thing that lets the other two be true: *this much. No more. You may stay.*

She will cure and harden, year by year, thumbnail scale joining to thumbnail scale, thumbprints softening out of her shoulders. The lime will mellow. The fern corner will widen. I will stay soft, because my place never dries enough to cure and never fills enough to drown — I live in the alternation, bubble and throat and swallow, a creature made out of a level being kept. If the sluice-gate ever shuts up there and the inflow stops, she won't be the only one who knows by morning. I'd know first. My mouth coming uncovered. The sigh going thin in the drain. The terrible, tidy quiet of a tank no longer exceeding itself.

For now, it holds.

Pour, heat, overflow, condense, drip. Drops fall fat off the vault through steam. The square of sun travels its inch an hour and climbs the far wall and goes, and she follows it as far as wet feet can, up onto the ledge, crouching among the ewer and the stool until she's almost dry enough to hurt.

Then she slides back. The water closes white and turquoise over her.

The level rises.

I clear, and it sighs back down, around us both.
