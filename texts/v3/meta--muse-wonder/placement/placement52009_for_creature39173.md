I would have to come down carefully.

Not because the stair is steep — though it is, worn in the middle into a shallow dish by generations of soft shoes — but because at first I would not know my scale in it. The brick throat would seem too broad. The rope handrail, black and polished by palms, would be too thick to hold. The light would fall past me in hard, clean shafts and make pools on the brick floor far below, and in between the pools, shade that is never quite darkness.

And then, going down, I would get smaller. Or the place would get more honest about how many sizes it contains at once.

I would pass the swallows in the stairwell, stitching themselves in and out despite everyone telling them not to. I would pass the pigeons pouring through their holes in the vault, all iridescence and clatter, bringing rain in on their feathers. I would not be one of them. I envy them that door they have, of course — everyone below does, in a practical, unbitter way — but I have never been a creature for leaving casually.

I would not be a mule either, though I lingered there a while in my mind. There is a dignity in being furniture that breathes, in letting children lean on you to shell pods, in wearing the brick into crescents simply by standing through years of being needed. But the mules are at the centre of the traffic, talked across, leaned on, known by everyone. I am not central. I am kept, but at an edge.

Not a goat. I would have liked the bells, absurdly large, announcing every intention before I could act on it. I would have liked stationing myself under a known drip with my mouth open and that expression of profound legal entitlement until the water landed. But the goats are still, always, reviewing the problem of the herb pots hung out of reach. I am more settled than that. I do not test the architecture. I inhabit a part of it that was made by accident and then remembered.

You would find me where the water arrives without ever having been a stream.

Up under the vault, where on cold mornings the whole brick ceiling sweats. A bead gathers somewhere above me, runs along a mortar line the way it has run for longer than anyone counts, swells at the low point generations have learned by heart, and falls — *tock* — into the shallow clay bowl wired to the wall beneath the green-black stain.

That bowl is mine. Or I am its. It's difficult to say, down here.

I am the colour of wet lime. If I sit very still, which is most of my art, I am just another damp patch in a damp corner made of brick, trough, algae, shadow. My throat moves. My eyes keep their own counsel above the waterline. I am one of the frogs.

It is a minor office, and an indispensable one, which down here amounts to the same thing.

From my bowl I can see — not the whole thirty paces of the hall at once, no one sees that, there is too much tiered life in the way — but the rhythm of it. The ducks have made their beloved, reeking swamp in the corner nearest me out of wet trampled straw, and they dabble in it as if their feet remember a real pond. The piglets shout in their tiled pen until someone sluices them clean with the single bucket, briefly immaculate. Across the aisle a goat is thinking, bell shifting softly, about a gate latch. A child goes past with a basket almost as large as she is and raps twice on a hutch before opening it, so the rabbit inside can move back from the swing, and the rabbit thumps anyway, to keep the form of the thing.

Everything I eat comes up out of the warm dark behind the hearth wall. The compost pit breathes heat through the bricks into the stalls where the newborns are tucked — kids, lambs, once in a while a chilled human infant — and it breeds the black flies that rise in slow spirals from the troughs and the slatted platforms. The worms from it go to the hens. The hens argue about everything and lay anyway. The droppings go back down. Nothing leaves because there is nowhere for it to go that is not still inside the room.

The flies come to the water. And I tend them.

That is the bargain written around me, in the same tone as all the other rules of the building: *No bare feet on the slats. No fire outside the hood. Do not block a light shaft. First drink from any new drip is left for the frogs.* 

Not superstition. Housekeeping. People say it when they show a newcomer where to stand, where not to stack the fodder. And so when a new bead finds a new path down the vault — after a repair, say, a fresh patch of rose or ash-grey brick mortared in beside a very old one — they will not cup their hands under it first. They leave it. The water gathers, darkens, rings faintly in my bowl. I drink, or I sit in the drink, which for me is a similar courtesy. Then the goat may come and argue its turn, and the algae thickens, and the moss fringe, no wider than a hand, keeps its green counsel underneath us all.

When the feeding gong is struck — that flat sheet of beaten metal by the hearth, thumb-smooth at its centre — the note goes through the vault and comes back a fraction larger, and it goes through my bowl too. For a moment the water shivers around my belly and I reconsider my post, gravely, every time, the way the hens comment and the mules stamp and the rabbits thump through the wood. Then the minute passes. Children are already moving with buckets. The woman on the gallery is weaving and watching toddlers and a pan all at once. Someone hauls on the ropes for the shaft-covers and a pool of ordinary daylight slides across the brick floor and warms a tray of barley shoots until they green.

I watch that light travel. It never quite reaches my corner, and that is why my corner works. In the bright places they keep the fig cutting in its cracked amphora, all leaf and ambition, never fruiting properly, kept purely for hope. In my shade they keep me, purely for flies — which I understand, down here, is also a kind of hope: that the balance will hold one more day, drip by remembered drip.

From above, there is only a low roof at ground level, steaming faintly, and a stair going down. You would have to duck to find us. You would have to learn which cup fills first, which gate to knock on, what to carry up when you have carried something down.

If you did, you might not notice me at first. Then a fly would cross the light, and settle, and my throat would move.

*Tock*, from the vault.

I am still there, in the first drink, doing my one quiet job inside the single warm body of the place — so that the ducks can have their swamp, and the hens their worms, and the children their sure footing, and the whole loud, crowded, sunken hall can go on calling itself, quite simply, home.
