I come up the way everything else once came up, which at first makes me think I must be human.

In the slack minute, holding my breath in the green-dark of the standpipe, hands finding the rungs worn concave and river-smooth, hauled or swimming past a pipe that pulses against my calf like an artery. My head breaks the surface in the bubble at the crown, and I am already wrong for the place. There are only three paces here and I cannot walk them. The water is to my chest, to my throat. The inspection ledge is a shallow bowl underfoot, smooth as a tongue, and it does not fit my feet. It fits hers. She is standing in it, one hand resting loose on the valve wheel, feeling the turbines tremble up through the iron two thousand feet below, and there is no second bowl. There is no second place to stand, upright and wedged for sleeping, between the fern-trunk and the curve of the wall.

So I do not stay that size.

For a while I try being a swift. That seems, at first, like freedom — the only creature here that knows the outside on purpose. To nest damp in the dark between dome and fern, to fold my wings and drop shrieking out of the cracked vent, through the pocket, through steam, snatching insects out of the air without ever touching the water, then out again into that white, lung-cutting altitude. To be able to leave. But the swifts smell of iron even in flight. Their feathers never dry. They fall silent when the cloud takes the summit and the portholes go white. They are not visitors either. They are just the part of the room that briefly extends into the sky, and I am not quick enough, or hungry in that particular shrieking way, and every dive would end, sooner or later, in the water I am trying to learn.

I try lower. An eel, then — forearm-long, coppery, blind, coming up out of the standpipe grating when the surge stirs everything, hunting through the root curtain. To know the dark below the bubble as a road rather than an exit. That has its truth. But the eel's truth is teeth and tangling, and when one injures itself against the grating she hauls it up and bites behind its head with a quick efficiency that is not cruelty, and shares the rest with the water. I do not want to be fed upon that cleanly. I do not want to hunt.

The minnows, schooling permanently around the valve wheel, nibbling rust scale and frog spawn, bright in their endless circuit — them I understand longer. They arrived as eggs in the intake far below and were lifted stage by impossible stage, and now they cannot decide if they are trapped or resident. Nothing needs to leave. I could circle. I could go dreamy near the surface from too much rust. But circling the wheel is still watching the wheel, and she already does that with her palm. The room does not need another keeper of the center.

The frogs let go.

That is their whole genius. Dozens of them, thumbnail-small, translucent green, clinging to the tiles between surges, and then when the pumps below gather and change pitch, when the water rises a hand's breadth and compresses the air until ears ache and the rain off the dome turns to a sheet — they release, and are tossed singing into the current, and the dome takes their chorus and enlarges it until it joins the turbine whine and the hammering in the flumes. They climb back afterward. One rides behind her ear, throat pulsing, mistaking her for tile. At night they attach along her collarbones to sing. I envy them that surrender. But I learn, flush by flush, that surrender is not what I do.

What I do is hold.

So I am smaller than the frog, and slower than the minnow, and I am on the inside of the porthole glass at the waterline, where the algae films everything green.

A snail.

Not an important shape, until you follow what it makes possible. I graze. My radula scrapes the glass in patient arcs, algae and mineral skin coming away mouthful by mouthful, and behind me a little window clears. Not much — a snail-sized clearing, then another, overlapping, a slow lacework of them at the height of the water. Through them, ordinary daylight enters un-greened for a moment at exactly the level where the fern's lowest fronds hang toward the pool. She prunes the fronds that lean too far and block the portholes, because the fern must be lit as well as wet. I am the other half of that tending, from the other side of the glass. She keeps the fern, the fern keeps the air alive — and I keep the light able to get in.

It orders my time completely. Between surges I move and eat and clear. The glass under my foot is cold with altitude on its far side; summit cloud presses against it, or thin clean air, or a slow procession of white. I can feel weather arrive through the porthole before the swifts go quiet. When the summit cloud takes us and there is no above and no below, my window fogs from the warm side faster than I can clear it, and I stop trying and simply hold there, foot sealed to the glass, while the fern lifts its fronds slightly and drinks fog through its leaves, and she lifts her face beside me and breathes deepest of all.

Then the surge gathers. You hear it under the world before you feel it. Her fingers tighten on the wheel rim. Her other hand finds a root. The frogs prepare, in their way, by ceasing to prepare at all. I clamp. The water in the pocket rises, the air squeezes, the valve wheel groans around an eighth of a turn by itself, the pool — which is also the floor, which is also most of the room — heaves, and everything not rooted or sealed is taken. Sometimes I keep my hold. Sometimes, despite everything, the current peels me off the glass and I am in the warm brown-green tumble, shell knocking softly against tile, against chain, and I come to rest where so much else comes to rest: in the root curtain of the fern, among the pale descending roots that enter the pool and keep going into the dark, holding snails. Holding me. That is literally what they do between hunts and flushes, the roots; they hold us, in the depths, unhurried, while eels move through like a rumor of copper.

And then her fingers find me.

She does this after every flush — wades the three impossible paces through chest-deep water and sorts the tangled root curtains with patient fingers, guiding the pale tips back down into the water, freeing snails. It is the first work of every interval. A fingernail grown thick and yellow as a snail shell taps, touches, loosens; a strand of root is lifted; I am set free of the tangle and carried — briefly enormous in her palm, briefly the whole focus of the largest animal in this weather — and placed back on the glass where the algae is thickest and the window has begun to close. She does not speak to me in words. Mostly she does not speak in words at all. The sound she makes while tending sits under the frog chorus and finds its level. I take it into my whole foot like a vibration through glass.

I eat frog spawn when it is left near my arc of tile. I am eaten at, in turn, by nothing in particular, too slow and shelled for the eels' attention, too mineral for the minnows, though they pass beneath me in their bright circuit trailing light through the water. When the vine on the warm pipe blooms — only when the pumps shift pitch, white flowers opening into the steam — the petals that fall to the surface drift to the porthole edge and soften there, and everything in the pocket is briefly, giddily altered. She laughs then, and the dome throws it back enlarged. I just graze a little less evenly. My windows go wandering.

At night, which is only the glass going grey and dark and white again, I do not sleep wedged upright like her, water to my chin, rising and settling a hand's breadth with every surge, a cradle of roots at the back of my head. I rest sealed in a seam where tile has sheared away and the concrete beneath is warm. Condensation gathers on the dome high above and falls as perpetual local rain, onto the pool, onto her shoulders, onto my shell, tick, tick, through all the hours that are counted eight or nine surges to a turning. Generations of tenders stood and read gauges that have lost their numbers. She still taps the brass and listens for the answering tick through the pipe. I keep a smaller gauge: how fast the green closes back over the clear. By that I know the season below, the richness of the intake water forgotten under tailings and housing far down, whether the algae is feeding well, whether snail will feed frog and frog will feed eel. It is enough of a watch. Keeping it is the shape my days fit inside.

I could leave, in theory. In the slack minute, duck under, be taken down the standpipe through green-dark that cools as it descends, out toward thin air and distance and the whole built mountain slumped with stairs and gantries and cooling fins. The minnows cannot decide if they are trapped or resident. I have decided, in the way a foot decides for a surface. It is not that the downward dark refuses me. It is that after each flush there is a new tangle in the roots, and new green filming the glass, and her hand coming patient through the water — and the window I cleared this morning is already clouding at the edges, and the fern is already leaning, almost imperceptibly, toward where the light gets in.

After this one, she says, whenever the water rises and the rain turns to a sheet and the whole dome fills with hammering. After this one. Hold on.

I do.
