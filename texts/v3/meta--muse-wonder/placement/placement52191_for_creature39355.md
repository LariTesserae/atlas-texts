I would not arrive. There is no arriving, down where I am, because there is no outside to arrive from. I would be printed already standing in the water.

The first thing is the trunk against my palms.

Not its surface — that is soon known, warm fibrous slickness, the standardized ridging where my fingers are meant to close — but the movement inside it. A valve opens a fraction late. I know this the way you might know your own name being mispronounced, except it happens in my forearms. Current finds the copper-rich paths under my skin and changes tempo there. The trunk is thinking in pressure and the borrowed pulse in me slows to listen.

I coax it back. A little weight here. A chemical warmth exhaled through the palm. A thinning of my own current on one side so the flow in the bore leans, corrects, settles. The valve resumes. Nothing has been fixed, exactly. Nothing was broken. A deviation has been returned to the place where deviations are absorbed.

That is my radius. That is the whole of it.

I am six feet and a little more of upright fiber, made narrow because the service channels are narrow, made tall because junctions grow at the height a tender must reach. My legs go down into black water the temperature of my own tissues. Below the knee they divide, and divide again, rootlets feeling for the mat. When I stand, they spread. When I move, they gather and quest forward, tasting iron, silica, the faint baked mineral breath of stone that has never cooled properly. I plant myself. I lean the hydraulic weight of me forward. I plant myself again.

In this way I cross a province in what you would call days, though there is no mark here by which a day could be known. There is increase and ease in the great trunks. There is the creep of leaf-belts under my rootlets. When a belt takes me I let it: I loosen, I stand upright in the dripping air, my smooth head beaded, and I am handed along at the speed materials are intended to flow, past distillation crowns I know by the cool bead-work running down the insides of my arms, past plating marshes where the electrochemical hush slows the pulse at my throat.

I have seams where eyes might have been. The skin passes over them without interruption. I have never minded this, because minding would require a shape for the world other than the one that reaches me, and none does. The world reaches me entire: as heat on one cheek from a furnace-root sunk deep; as the minute lessening of prickle when a rain-column stands a mile off in its appointed place; as vibration coming up through water and root-mat from a rotary flower meshing, tooth to tooth, so far away the rhythm arrives as a single change of pressure behind my face. I turn toward none of it. Turning implies a front the information has not already entered.

Where my mouth should be there is a smaller seam, shuttered. It opens against sap-ports, against the soft feed-nipples on the service trunks, and draws in mineral broth by slow peristalsis. The rest of my feeding makes no event. My skin drinks the flood I wade in. My rootlets drink the stone. I am always exactly as wet and exactly as warm as the air around me, and this is not comfort. It is indistinguishability, maintained. To be cooler would be to be a separate weather. To be drier would be a waste the system would have to account for.

Sometimes another tender is conveyed into my radius, and we pause between two electrical trunks, palm to palm, in the padded silence. Nothing passes that could be mistaken for a voice. A load is shared. A correction I have been carrying — two valves late in a district of weaving stems, a binding of pulp where a leaf-belt meets a chopping mouth — moves across the place where our conductive tissues align, and afterward neither of us carries all of it. If this is affection, it is the only kind available: that for a while, the current goes more easily through both of us than it went through one.

I carry seeds. The sling at my hip is grown from my own back, a sheet of living cloth that will be harvested from me on schedule and regrow without my attending to it. The seeds inside are hard and perfect and identical, stamped to geometry. I set them into the descending drills at the angle the seed before them entered, and the one before that, in a district where seed-presses have stamped and root-drills have planted longer than any tissue of mine can hold. I do not plant them toward a future I imagine. The future is a function: press, drill, organ, belt, mouth, pulp, press. I am the part of the function that walks between its stations so the stations need not move.

In the plating marshes I wade chest-deep among hanging nodules beyond counting, turning each with my forearms, reading the thickness of its metallic skin by the drag it sets up in the pool's slow field. The finished ones I strip free, and they go to be stripped again elsewhere, and the pools take up their copper and iron again out of floodwater and young stone. Over furnace provinces the air shimmers up my ribs and the smell — if I had smell as a separate country — tightens high in my throat like baked resin. In districts of looms, sheets of living cloth close overhead and the electric prickle flattens, upholstered, and my own movement comes back to me through the mat as the softest possible report: still here, still here, still here.

No animal crosses that report. There is no quick thing, no bite, no carrion sweetness, no call arriving from a throat built differently from the trunks. Only departments: pump and refinery, furnace and loom, capacitor and press, and mine, the department that tends. If you stood beside me long enough for the field between two trunks to alter around your strange conductivity, I would read you as I read a valve. A thickening here. A detour there. Warmth on one side of the face. I would lay a hand a little higher on the same trunk and learn, through the shared pulse, what sort of junction you were. I would not wonder where you came from. Above is not a place in me. Above is an increase in current, harvested somewhere beyond the stacked roofs of leaves, spent below. Before is the belts creeping. After is the belts creeping.

I had no childhood. I split from a pod in a silo-tree already full-sized, already knowing the rhythm of valves I had never touched, and I stepped down into body-warm water and the nearest belt took me toward the nearest work. I will have no age either, though pieces of me are always becoming feedstock: skin sloughed into a pulp channel, a finger worn thin and regrown from the knuckle, the sheet off my back. Decade or century is a distinction nothing in me records. What I know instead is schedule, and schedule has no outside edge where a life could be measured against it.

So when my own current begins, at last, to thin — when my rootlets answer the mat a fraction late, when the valve under my palm opens correctly but my borrowed pulse arrives having already missed it — I will not need to be told. The knowledge will be the thinning itself. I will stand, rootlets loosened, and let a conveyor hand me along in the direction materials are meant to flow, upright, sensing everything, through the rain that falls forever in its district and the drizzle and the dry heat, toward the chopping mouths that have taken every tender before me at exactly this tempo.

And the next one will step from its pod in that province, full-sized, knowing. It will find the same trunk. Its long fingers will close, a little higher up, over the same living valve. The same bead will gather under the same leaf and fall onto the same place on its shoulder — leaf after leaf after leaf, tender after tender after tender, each one new, the event identical, the current going soundlessly on to where it has always gone.
