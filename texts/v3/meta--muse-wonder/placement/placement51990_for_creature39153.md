I would not come up the path the way you do.

I try that on first — a stranger at the lip of the cirque, hands raw from the bowl-smooth stair, blinking in the triple light — but it doesn't stay. I'm not high enough for it. My eye keeps dropping. To the rims of the pools. To the inches where a foot is about to be placed. To the hem of a crusted apron, the underside of a gate of woven reed, the warm dark gap under a floor where the water goes.

I'm not the old water-keeper either, though I envy that stillness: up on a rim where three terraces can be seen at once, signing small, rerouting five people with one bent finger. I don't know water that far ahead. I know it when it's already at my skin.

For a while I think I might be one of the swifts — there is the temptation of that silent pour out of the eaves, a river of wings following a river of water downhill — but I'm not good at leaving. I keep circling back down into the town. Into the press of it. Between legs. Under the basket-chains. Close to the ground where everything important is communicated by touch anyway.

So that is where I settle, and lower, and it becomes itself:

I am one of the small dogs, the color of wet ash.

Thick-coated, short in the leg, crusted white along the shoulders where spring-mist has dried on me season after season, so that when I move through sun I carry a little patch of shade-smell and mineral with me. My coat never quite dries in winter. My paws are broad and splayed from the terraces. I place them the way the people place their feet — not heel-first, never hurrying a stride — toe, test, weight. I learned before I learned anything else. All of us learn it as puppies, carried in the slings against backs, then set down on warm stone indoors, then let out onto the roofs that are ceilings, while a hand comes down, again and again, gentle and absolute, over the muzzle at the first gathering of a bark in the throat.

*Not that sound. Never that sound up.*

So I do not bark. Not when the ibex startle off a chimney. Not when ice ticks and lets go three terraces above. Not when a stranger's smell comes over the lip of the cirque — unfamiliar leather, lowland sweat, iron. The urge rises sometimes, an old hot pressure at the back of the tongue, and I put it into my body instead. I nudge. I paw. I press my forehead hard into a calf, apologetic and insistent as a hand, until the person looks down, then looks where my nose is pointing.

That is the work. In a town that speaks with elbows and flags and fingers walking down forearms, I am one more way of saying *attend*.

During the melt-pulse I am everywhere and underfoot and somehow — this is the art, taught by kicks that never quite land and hands that scoop me at the last second onto a rim — never in the way. The warning goes up, wool snapping on poles, and the people pour from their narrowing doorways, and I pour with them at knee-height, through a world made suddenly of ankles and ash-buckets and ropes. I know each quarter by its footing: here the travertine grows in blunt amber sheets and is slick even dry; here a grandfather has chipped footholds in a pattern his grandchildren's feet find without looking; here the channel always freezes first and a child will come running with hot ash from the dung-fire, and I must not be there when she throws it.

I carry nothing. That is not my use. My use is to notice the one who has not noticed. The young man hanging in a harness under his own eaves, antler pick going soft and underwater, arms white to the elbow, so deep in the chipping that he misses the open palm sliding sideways above him: *let it flood, don't fight it*. I nose the rope twice. He feels the change in its language. He looks up. Or the girl with a basket on her head and a baby signing *cold ear, bird* against her neck, who has not seen the flag change on the lower cistern pole — I weave, I press, I get my body across her path without tripping her, which took me a full year to learn and cost me most of my puppy clumsiness.

The people touch me the way they touch each other. Forehead to forehead in greeting, briefly, in the morning, breath warm. A thumb scraping the day's white rind gently off the bridge of my nose. At dusk, on the roofs, when the pools are already skinning and the swifts pour home in one last silent river, someone will pick the ice from my eyebrows with a careful thumbnail while I pick, with my teeth, very delicately, at the crust that has grown too thick at the shoulder of their coat.

I eat third, after the wet chippers and the children, with the animals, because animals are heat in winter. I know the order and do not begrudge it. Thaw-feasts I know first by smell before anyone has signed a word — insect-cloud rising off a pool that warmed too fast, fish fry silvering to the surface, swift eggs, grease and sun on upturned faces. A morsel will come down to me eventually, pressed by a hard affectionate thumb toward my mouth rather than dropped. Almost nothing here is dropped. Dropping makes a sound, and hands are surer than gravity.

At night I sleep behind the curtain with the stabled animals, in the part of the one room that smells most of musk and warm dung and drying felt. The fire in its stone hood burns low, more for drying than heat. Through the floor, spring water ticks and hurries toward its freezing. I can hear the family breathing on the stone benches — seven, eight of them, and not in any rhythm, each one thrifty with the thin air — and I match none of them, my own breath quicker, fogging in little bursts against my paws. Sometimes a stalactite lets go a drip onto horn cup, onto stone, onto my ear. I do not startle anymore. It is only the house making itself in the dark, growing its teeth down from the ceiling, and in the morning someone will knock the longest one gently away from above the grandparents' bed before it can divide the room.

I have had three names, because I have belonged to three houses stacked up the same wet stair, and each family named me for where they found their hand resting. In this house I am the sign for *stone patted twice* — *keep watch, good morning* — which is also what the children sign to the old ones sealing slowly into the wall niches above the town. I answer to a knuckle rapped softly on travertine. I answer to a sleeve pulled. I answer, mostly, to being looked for out of the corner of an eye.

And I keep my silence the way they keep theirs: not as an absence, but as a form of care aimed upward, at the beautiful translucent cornice balanced on the rim, which must never be touched by a risen sound. My whole life is pitched below that. Drips, trickles, breath, the soft slap of a reed gate settling. My bark, unmade every day, turned into pressure and warmth against someone's leg at exactly the moment they need to turn.

When I die — and it will likely be quietly, in the night, lungs or heart giving up the altitude at last — they will not wail over me. They will wash me in the warmest spring, wrap me in undyed wool, and lay me in a dry crack above the springs where the mineral takes things fastest. White upon ash upon white. Within a season I will be less a shape than a suggestion of one, a curled spine, a paw, sealed into the cliff I spent my life crossing at knee-height.

Until then I am here, low in the bowl of light and ice, among the placing feet, doing the town's one endless task in my own register:

Noticing. Nudging. Keeping it open. Keeping it quiet.

Not a sound rises to the cornice.
