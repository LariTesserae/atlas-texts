I would be low. Lower than the root-mat, lower than the lilies, at exactly the height where water stops being a place and becomes a condition.

At first I might think I am in a puddle. Then the puddle connects, hand-deep and tea-brown, to another puddle, and that to a sheet that goes on under duckweed and fallen leaves and interlaced roots until distance itself loses meaning. There is no bank to climb toward. There is no deep to sink into. I rest with my belly spread flat against warm mud that gives a little under me and then holds me, and my tail keeps moving even when I don't — a slow, translucent fan, veined like a leaf, stirring the inch of water over my back so it never quite stills.

I am forearm-long, perhaps longer. Hard to say. Length is not a thing that finishes here.

You would take me for a tadpole that got lost on the way to being something else. I have the gills still: three pairs behind my head, soft branching fronds, fern-red when the water clears, rust-dark when I have been wallowing in silt. They wave constantly. Small fish shelter in their shade. Larvae pick through them for what collects there, and I let them. I have the lateral line still, too, that faint seam down each flank from my larval life, and most of my world comes to me through it — not as sight, not really as sound, but as pressure. A crab working through the root-mat. A frog landing two body-lengths off. A dragonfly setting its feet, one by one, on the surface film ten lengths away, each footfall a tiny dimpling bell in the water. A beetle crossing a leaf overhead, traveling down the stem as vibration. The whole bowl trembles minutely, all the time, with worms and beetles and burrowing things working the mud beneath me, and I lie in the middle of that trembling and read it the way you would read weather.

My eyes are small black beads set flush with my head. They tell me brightening from darkening, which here is only the green gloom thinning at what must be morning and thickening again at what must be night. The temperature does not change to help me. The chorus does not stop to help me — it only trades voices, frog for frog, insect for insect, in a rotation so regular it feels like stillness. I do not really need eyes. Light here has no edges. Nothing casts a proper shadow. Everything arrives lit as if underwater, because everything, in pieces, is.

My skin never dries. That is not a comfort so much as a fact of physics: it couldn't dry, it would take water straight in and lose it straight out, but dryness is a theoretical country and I have never been there. Water condenses on the cool of my back the way it condenses on a frog's back, on the underside of a leaf. I breathe without bothering — through the gills, through the thin wet skin, through the lining of my mouth opening and closing in a slow, idle pulse. I live at the exact temperature of the bowl, neither warmer nor colder, my blood running at the speed of that unchanging heat. Steady. Quick. Unseasonal.

And I walk, which is the surprise. Four long legs splayed wide, toes absurdly elongated and webbed almost to the tips, spreading what weight I have the way a snowshoe spreads a man's. I can cross the thinnest film of water over mud without breaking through. Each foot placed flat, each foot peeled up again with a soft sucking deliberation. On the root-mat I move with a loose, sprawling confidence, the woven living wood bouncing slightly under me, clinging with the undersides of my toes when it tilts. I do not leap. I do not burrow, exactly. I press, and the soft ground gives, and I settle in.

If I have a color, it is whatever I am lying on. Young ones of my kind are glassy — you can see the dark thread of the gut through them, the heart knocking, a green clouding under the skin where algae have taken up residence in the tissue itself. I was like that. Now I have clouded over with the place: ochre from the mud, moss-green from the light, brown-black along the spine where leaf-mold settled and stayed. There is actual moss beginning between my shoulder blades now, a fine bright fur. It does not bother me. Nothing that grows on me seems to bother me. My skin is less a boundary than a tenancy. Mites move through my gill-fronds like birds through a canopy. Orchid seedlings no larger than a grain have rooted, on others older than me, in the mucus along the spine. I am a surface, in a place where every surface is a garden.

I eat at background level, the way I am eaten. Hours of nothing, gills fanned, in the posture of a submerged leaf. Then — the one fast thing I do — a lunge at a tadpole of a genuinely frog kind. A snap at a flatworm. When a big carnivorous leaf folds slowly shut nearby and holds its catch softening for days, I learn that leaf's place and wait underneath it, taking what drops. When a fallen fruit ferments beside me and boils with flies, I turn my head a quarter-inch and begin, dreamily, to pick them off one by one. And I graze, in a way no self-respecting salamander should: scraping algae and bacterial film off roots with tiny peg teeth, swallowing mouthfuls of the bright green scum that skins still water, digesting what I can and letting the rest take up residence in me for whatever thin subsidy photosynthesis can offer in the gloom. Animal, plant, scavenger, prey. The categories sit loosely on me, like everything else.

Somewhere in my ancestry there was a proper adult — lungs developed, gills resorbed, bones hardened, skin thickened for a life up banks and under logs. That adult no longer happens. It cannot happen here. Metamorphosis needs a signal: a pool shrinking, a season turning, a stress of drying or cooling. The bowl withholds every signal there is. The water never leaves. The heat never drops. The pools in the leaf axils above me never empty, so the frogs calling in them never stop, so the insects never stop, so I never stop. So I stayed as I began, enlarged. My bones are largely cartilage, soft as the mudstone that dents under a foot and crumbles like damp clay. Maturity, when it came, arrived inside the larval body.

I breed as a tadpole that never became anything else. There is no courtship and no season. When I was large enough, and fed enough, and not yet eaten, my body simply ripened. I lay eggs in ones and twos, continuously, in the warm shallows, in flooded leaf axils, under the edges of the root-mat — clear jellied beads with a dark center, stuck to a root hair, a submerged stem, once to the motionless tail of another of my own kind who did not move away. They hatch into miniatures of me, already green-tinged, already fanning invisible gills. Many are eaten before they hatch. More after. This is not a tragedy. It is the economy I also participate in from the other side, snapping up other things' young in the same shallows. I lay because things hatch. Things hatch because I never stop laying.

If I avoid the herons picking through the flats, the large fish in the wider lily water, the crabs and the hungrier of my own kind, I will not become an elder in any stately sense. I will become slower, and broader, and more planted. The moss on my back will thicken. A treefrog may spend its whole small life within a hand's span of me, eating the gnats drawn to my warmth, laying its eggs in the permanent puddle of my footprint where the mud filled around my weight and stayed filled. And without deciding anything I will go on engineering the bowl that engineered me: my padding and pressing and wallowing churning the upper mud back to softness before it can set, my grazing nipping off tree seedlings at the cotyledon stage along the water's edge, thinning back the very saplings that might one day lift a higher, cooler canopy and let the light change and let the pools dry and give me the signal I have never received.

Nothing about me will ever harden. When I die — probably in pieces, probably taken — what is left will redistribute within hours. If I die whole, untouched in some root-hollow, I will not so much rot as be edited out: snails at the skin, worms up from beneath, white fungal threads stitching me into the mat, until there is a salamander-shaped richness in the mud and then not even that. No bones to keep. At most, if my soft body presses into fresh mudstone and the mud against all local odds begins to set, a perfect embryonic curl in stone that never quite became stone.

At night — only the green light thickening a degree, the temperature not moving at all, water beading a fraction cooler on my back — I move out across the flats with dozens of my kind, unhurried, tails swaying, feet silent on the water. We travel miles over weeks through water never deeper than a hand, and we never find a shore.

We have nowhere to go. We have never had anywhere to go. The bowl is not a stage on the way to being an adult landscape, and I am not a stage on the way to being an adult animal. We have agreed, without ever deciding, to stay exactly here: warm, dim, saturated, teeming, begun.
