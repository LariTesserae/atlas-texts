I think I would come in the way everyone does, and not stay that way.

Through the locks first. I'd stand on the barge and watch the real sea stay up there behind the black gates while the water let us down, green and cold against the concrete, the painted depth-lines climbing past. I'd be a visitor for about an hour — the kind who keeps looking out. Across that flat made sea to where the rim ought to be. Troubled by the fetch of it, the emptiness in the middle, the weather with no coast to explain it.

Then the sound would get into me, and I'd start looking down.

Not on one of the big warehouse roofs, I don't think. I can picture it too clearly to claim it: the container dormitories along the ridge of District 17, the canteen and the chapel, the cranes swinging over the eaves, children who know their roof like a street and never need to leave it. I would like the roof-lagoons — the sag where rain and pumped water gather shallow, where reeds have finally got a hold, where a heron stalks with absurd seriousness through someone's swimming place. I'd like hanging washing in the steam from the towers so it never dries and never freezes. 

But I'd get restless in one island. I'd want the movement between them. And I wouldn't want the surface barges either, not entirely — all that grey wind combing the central reaches, a day of nothing but blinking amber buoys over a drowned motorway, the sonar pinging into eighty metres of unseen pavement. Too much looking out.

Underneath is where I'd keep turning my head.

I'd be a tender. One of the in-between people. I live up top, yes — I'd need a bunk somewhere, probably a cabin lashed in the ring around a smokestack in one of the tower districts, close enough to the cooling towers that the cormorants nested in the lattice argue at dawn and the whole platform trembles faintly, all day, all night, through the soles of my feet. My washing smells of mineral water. My boots have cleats for biofilm. My gloves are scored across the palms, and I check every ladder without thinking about it now — two tugs, a kick — because crust lies.

But my work is on the boundary.

I run a small tender out to the radiator fields and the warm outfalls, and I dive short and tethered, not deep exploratory dives, just work. Down through glitter, then green, then that blue-green dusk where the high-mast lamps never went out. Whole streets lit under water. I know them by name the way you'd know them on land. Slack today in the lee of the big warehouse. Two knots running down Foundry Avenue, don't fight it, let it take the bag. Crabs all over the car park again, picking between cars with their windscreens still whole.

I scrape mussels.

That's the unglamorous truth of it and I think I'd love it for that. They grow where they are not wanted — black knocking clusters on intake grilles, pipe flanges, stair rails, the teeth of gears — and if you leave them they will choke the whole machine that makes the sea possible. So I go down with a scraper and a bag and forearms that sting, and I come up with white little nicks blooming along my skin, the fine scatter the older divers wear like a second map. The fish know me before I know them. They hang in their thousands exactly at the edge of the radiator fields, all facing one way into the current, cold on one side of my face and suddenly, across an invisible line, tepid on the other. Mineral. A faint chemical sweetness. Full of drifting food. I learn the edge in my skin before I see the finned towers wavering in their own heat.

And when I'm not diving, I'm netting the outfalls where everything gathers, especially in winter. Eels thick as rope. Coarse fish hauled up silver and thrashing onto a deck slick enough to kill you politely. Seals alongside the boat, fat and absolutely without shame, waiting for the cleaning table.

At ebb-equivalent I'm down faster than anyone, because that's the other half of me. When they drop a sector behind the bulkheads and five metres of water falls away, an acre of the drowned world stands up suddenly steaming into the air — roofs, aprons, machinery, mussel beds stranded and gaping — and you get six hours. Six hours of being on land that isn't land. Gulls screaming in. The smell hot iron and wet concrete and shellfish. Working in waders, racing the boards, to bolt and clear and weld before the pumps change their mind and the sea takes its floor back. I think I would live for that reversal. To stand at noon on a street that was under a boat at breakfast.

I wouldn't tell time by the sky. The steam can take the sun for days. I'd tell it by the lean. Six in the morning, the great southern intakes open, and far away below me a bass note changes in my teeth, the mooring lines creak and sing, the buoy flags turn, and the whole Low Sea begins, very slowly, to go south through its drowned avenues. Midday it comes back the other way. You plan a dive by it, a crossing by it, a child's bath, a net set, a repair. If a red board goes up on the dock, you don't argue. The water under you is about to move hard enough to keep a swimmer.

Silence would frighten me, after a while. On the rare shutdown nights when the pumps stop and the towers stop and the throb goes out of the stack platform, I'd lie awake listening to my own heart like something had broken. Because quiet, down there, doesn't mean peace. It means something has stopped that was supposed to keep everything alive.

I'd eat what catches. Mussels boiled in seawater on a roof, torn open with bread that came through the locks from the upper world. Fish from the warm edge. Reed shoots when there are any. Nothing clean, nothing dirty — warm, iron-rich, a taste of steam and concrete at the back of it. I'd curse the seals like dogs and feed them by hand anyway.

I'm not sure I'd ever go back up through the locks for good. The real sea would feel too thin to me after. Too bright, too quiet, its tide answering to nobody but the moon, its weather made by nobody at all. Pretty, and uninhabited in some deep way.

Here, below the level of the world, I get to live inside something large enough to have fog banks and storms, and still know it's a machine. I get to hang my life in its breath. I get to work at the exact place where cold water meets warm water and the fish hold station, turning as one when a shadow passes.

In the morning, when the intakes open and the sea leans south and all the lines begin to sing under the hull, I'd feel it first not in my eyes, not even in my ears.

In the soles of my feet.

And I'd already be reaching for my gloves.
