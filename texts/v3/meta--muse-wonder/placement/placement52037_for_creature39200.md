I would not arrive there. Arriving would already be the wrong verb.

No one arrives in my memory. My grandmother's grandmother was scraped, as a newborn, clean of marsh-water and laid against the warm wall of the condenser to sleep, and the hum went into her before handling did. I am the same child, later, with narrower shoulders from climbing and a pattern sewn over my sockets that I can read with my own thumb when I am nervous: spiral in, copper wire where the line breaks, spiral out again. My aunt made it without ever hurrying. When a train passes far enough away that you hear nothing, the wire finds it anyway, a faint singing in the skin of my face.

I turn my face up first. Before hands, before feet on waking.

The rain is falling warm, which means the condenser is in its long inhale and the air over our marsh is being pulled, very slowly, toward its throat. If it were cooler on my left cheek than my right I would know the wind had cut down the river channel, and the intake gates would need checking for reed-mat. If there is a flatness — not silence, flatness, a place where the heat does not reach me in its usual shape — something large is drinking between me and the vent-field. Herd, probably. I lie still in the felt and listen for their clicking to resolve itself into a direction before I bother to get up.

My teeth know when to get up. The pistons two hundred kilometers away have found their morning regularity inside the rain, and the shift of it passes through the ground, through the hut poles, through the boards under my bedding. Half a beat missing would wake me faster.

I keep one hand on something while I dress. Hut post. Rope. My mother's wrist where she is already awake, her skin smelling of fish-oil rubbed into felt and the iron-sweet smell that gets into hair after a shift in the throat. We speak mouth-to-ear at home. Not because we are tender, though we are. Because a voice let loose across open ground here will travel, and travel, and come back flattened off the rim next season saying things you said before you knew better. Close speech is honest speech. You can feel whether the other person is lying by the breath it takes them to shape it.

Outside, the world answers my first click before I have consciously sent it.

Grass, close and fleshy. Mud a hand-span beyond the step where the stone has been worn. The rope going taut away to the left, beaded with our knots — three tight, one loose, three tight — so my palm tells me whose section I am standing in even while the rest of me is still waking. I click again, softer. A stem is not a stone. Sleeping wet is not dry stone. The world is always returning itself to me a fraction after I ask, changed just enough that I have to ask again.

That is the work, really. Asking again.

The condenser louvers are taller than our hut stacked on itself three times. I climb them by rope and by the places where my hands have worn the scale smooth over years, up into the throat where the whole watershed sighs through. The air there pushes against my sternum in pulses. Inside, it is hotter, louder, closer than anywhere else I love. A healthy vane, tapped with the back of my knuckle, sings up my forearm into the shoulder — a low clean note that tells me the metal is still itself under the growth. Today the third vane from the bottom on the river side thuds.

So I scrape. Moss first, thick and holding water, releasing it cold — yes, cold, comparatively — down my wrists. Then spore-cake, which crumbles peppery against my tongue where the pollen gets in no matter how I breathe. Then the mineral skin underneath, hard, resisting the edge of my tool with a sound like a fingernail on river stone. I work until the thud thins. I work until the vane remembers its hum. My fingertips are blunt from this and seem numb to visitors in the old stories, the ones who arrive feeling with their gaze, but I can find a hairline crack in hot metal with my palm held a finger above it, from the way the heat stutters.

Below me my daughter is learning geography wrong, then right.

From the hut, she tells her cousin, forty minutes of grass-click, then mud that sucks — she makes the sound with her mouth, accurate enough that her cousin laughs — then the river that says its name over round stones, then hot iron, that's the pumps, *don't cross the humming line*. She clicks badly on the turn toward the river, too impatient, sending the second click before the first has had time to come back. I correct her the way my mother corrected me, not with words. I take her wrist, still her hand, wait until she feels herself wanting to fill the quiet. There. That wanting is the mistake. Let the world finish.

She is fierce and small and unafraid of everything except the correct thing. Once she let go of the rope on the grass-plain to chase a frog chorus and walked, clicking bravely, into the edge of a deaf pocket in the marsh fog. I found her because she had stopped. Not crying. Stopped. That is what the deaf zones do: the roar cuts, your heart goes muffled in your own chest, the rope under your hand feels slack though it is not, and gradually there is nothing outside you to confirm that you continue past your own skin. She stood there with both hands open, becoming confused, becoming very quiet, preparing without knowing it to lie down in the mud among the articulated bones.

I put her hand back on the rope and her whole body shuddered and came back to me. She keeps one hand on something transmissive now without being told. Rope, rail, wet trunk, my wrist. A person alone in silence here isn't lost. Lost can be fixed by walking toward warmth. A person alone in silence stops being able to check that they are a person.

So we live attached, and it is not a metaphor. It is plumbing. It is love with a load rating.

In the cool trough of the cycle, when the condenser rests and the hut air goes drier for a little while, I go down to the siding and talk to a man I have never touched. I strike the tuned rail with my hammer — small news today: vane three singing again, my mother's cough looser, the herds moving east of the river before the grass turns — and the answer comes back minutes later through the ground into my splayed feet, up through the thick soles that read gravel and mud and stone without asking my permission. He has a cough this wet season, worse in the messages sent after he has been working close to the smelter. His child is learning to strike and hits everything twice with joy. I know the shape of his life maybe better than I know the shape of his hands. One day my daughter will likely walk the line to his siding, and his son will trace her spiral with his thumb and find it good, and their children will hear some fine, necessary thing neither family hears alone.

At adulthood they gave me the old cistern water to drink, recycled so many times through turbine and rain and body and marsh that it tasted of copper and other people's weather. That night I dreamed in piston-time, my heart knocking at intervals that belonged to a valley two hundred kilometers away, and I woke understanding — not frightened, placed — that I am maintained like the rails are maintained. I wear out in the same long way. My mother will die into the peat, machine parts from three generations back are already pebbles in the river cut, and the condenser will go on inhaling the watershed long after my particular pattern of thread has rotted off some grandchild's face.

Where do I live? My mother answered that when I was small by putting my hand flat on the condenser wall, hot and humming. Then flat on her own chest. Then flat on the black trunk of a tree that had spiraled, over a hundred years, away from the forest and toward the factory's heat. Three surfaces, one temperature of belonging. She said nothing. She didn't need to.

Above the canopy tonight — I call it tonight because my body is tired, though the dark has never once varied for a promise — the great blind fliers are riding the updraft off the smelter. I will never touch one. Their calls come down not as sound but as pressure, a slow boom in my ribs, and they are listening up, reading the cloud-ceiling the way I read a vane, asking the sky how far away it is. I feel companionable toward them. Toward the herds passing now in the distance as click-hail through rain. Toward the frog that has just stopped singing by the intake, which means the water has changed, which means tomorrow I climb again.

Stand here with me. I have already taken your hand — not asked; you were standing wrong in the mud, feet too narrow, weight listening in the wrong place — and put it on the rope. Feel the knots. Three tight, one loose. Ours.

Click once. Gently.

Now wait for it to come back.

Somewhere out there between wet ground and pressing cloud, an old careless word of mine is still traveling, stretched thin by distance, my name said last season to no one in particular across open grass. It will return eventually, flattened, foreign, truer in some ways than when I sent it. My whole life I have been becoming someone who will be able to hear it.

I turn my face up to the warm rain and I listen.
