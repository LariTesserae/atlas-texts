I wouldn't be the herder. I thought about that first — the broad feet, the leaf cape, the slow breath made for thin air — but they are only ever visiting, even the ones born to the altitude. They come up in the warm months and go down again to be born and to die. Their huts are already becoming mounds while they are still sleeping in them.

I wouldn't be a giant either, though I would live on one. Their patience is too complete. To spend years as a dinner-plate in the mat, then decades lifting a trunk made of my own dead leaves, to flower once and stand hollow for years afterward while my children root in the rot at my feet — that is a life, but it is not mine. I want the brief hot hours. I want time, not space.

So I would be up where the giants keep their water.

Four metres above the plateau, in the cup of a crown east of the central pan, thirty paces or so from one of the cairn lines. From below I would be nothing — a gold thumbnail in a litre of brown water, hidden where the great leaves meet. From up here, the world is exactly as wide as my pond, and it is enough. It is ten centimetres of sky that fell and never left.

My skin is translucent. If you could see me in the morning, wedged cold and tea-coloured where leaf presses leaf, you would see the dark smudge of my gut through my sides, my heart making a small, visible weather of its own. My toes end in soft discs. I do not swim so much as grip and climb in wet geometry, a centimetre at a time.

I was not born in this crown. Almost nothing that lives here is.

I was born in another one, two hundred paces west — which might as well be another country. For three generations my kind lived in that well and never touched the ground. Then our giant flowered.

You could feel it before you could understand it: the spike rising through the centre of our world, the bees arriving in a furious halo, sunbirds stabbing down green and scarlet out of an empty sky. For weeks our pond was the busiest address on the plateau. Then the well began, leaf by leaf, to drain. The leaves loosened. The water went shallow, then sour, then gone to a damp shine in the axils.

So on the wettest night of a wet season I did the one thing I was made badly for. I climbed down.

I remember the dead trunk rattling faintly under my feet, the skirt of old leaves dry even in the mist. I remember hitting the mat and the noise of it — not the night frogsong I knew, muffled and intimate, but the ground itself roaring darkly around me: grasshoppers shifting, water ticking down through a hundred layers of stem, something huge and blind gnawing roots below the sole of the world. I crossed from giant to giant by sound, toward the crowns that were still humming to themselves after dark, each in a slightly different pitch depending on the depth of its water. Almost none of us make that crossing. I do not know if any of the others did. I arrived alone, and gravid, which is all a crown needs.

Now my days keep the plateau's rhythm more faithfully than any calendar.

Dawn: mist spills over the rim and beads on the leaves above me. The well is cold. I stay down in the seam, motionless, while the frogs in the pans below fall silent one species at a time.

Mid-morning: the giant opens. Sun comes down almost vertical, strikes the water, and I climb a centimetre into it and go jewel-bright, gold all the way through. Midges blunder in. Beetles too big for their own flight fall and struggle on the surface. I take them with a click that is mostly tongue and partly punctuation. Ants come single-file up the trunk to rob the drowned from the edges of my world; I let them have what I cannot reach.

Afternoon: the cloud sea assembles below the rim, then comes over. Rain hammers straight down on the great leaves so loudly it beats even the insects, and my well brims, darkens, overflows. If I have eggs tucked in an axil, if I have tadpoles no deeper than a finger-joint of water, the current tries to take them out into the void and we all wedge hard against the leaf-bases until the drum passes. Then steam. Slanted gold light. The whole mat below shining like a lake pretending to be a meadow.

Night is the part I would keep if I could keep only one slice. The giant folds, slowly, papery leaf against leaf, not quite closed — a green tent over my pond. It holds the day's warmth an hour longer than the open air can. Inside that tent I sing, and the sound comes out muffled, intimate, altered by the depth of the water I am sitting in. Every inhabited giant on the plateau does the same. Travellers below walk toward us in the mist because we are, quite literally, how water sounds on a map. A herder's child, lost between cairns, could close her eyes and find her way from crown to crown, from small sky to small sky.

Sometimes a sunbird remembers my well and stoops. Sometimes a fine woven net comes down from above and I go still as a stone under a leaf and learn, again, what I am worth to people: a sour, crunchy mouthful, highly prized. Mostly nothing up here reaches me except weather, food falling out of the air, and time passing in its narrow, usable bands.

I will probably never touch the ground again. My children may not either, nor theirs. We will be born, breed, sing and die in this one held mouthful of rain, four metres over a plain so old the mountains have forgotten their own sharpness. And if this giant, decades from now, pushes up its single spike and begins the long process of dying standing up, then one of my daughters will climb down on a drowning-wet night, cross the roaring dark, and climb toward a different humming crown — exactly one gravid female, arriving in a new sky.

That is enough. On the plateau, it has always been enough.
