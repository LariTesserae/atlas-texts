I thought, at first, I would be somewhere sensible to stand.

Outside, maybe. Gloved hand on the south wall of the turbine house, feeling the hum come up through concrete into the bones of my wrist, snow doing its enormous silent work around me. That seems like a place for a person. Or just inside the hatch, crouched because the ceiling is too low, earplugs already failing, counting through the porthole and trying to remain the one who counts.

But the room doesn't leave room for that idea. It is nine feet by six, and it is already full in a way that has nothing to do with crowding and everything to do with replacement.

So I try smaller.

A frog on the valve wheel, then. That is the first thing that resolves. Thumb-length, olive-brown, the dark stripe through the eye, my throat blown up into a translucent bubble and thrumming until the skin blurs. I can feel it less as singing than as being sung — the sound is already in my sternum before I make it, coming back off tile and copper and water, shaking my wet skin so the cold can never quite finish settling into me. At two degrees I should be shut down in the mud somewhere, heart slowed to a rumor, ice allowed in all the spaces between cells. Instead the chorus massages me awake enough to keep chorusing.

Only I am not that frog for long. Right against my side is another frog, damp flank to damp flank, and another under me holding onto the spoke and onto me with splayed toes, all of us inflating in phase. When my throat tires — and it does, it becomes a thin tired line of mouth, bubble gone — the pitch does not dip. The frog braced on my back takes it. The frog in the sump with only its gold-rimmed eyes above eleven centimeters of water takes it. My silence is held inside the chord the way a space is held in a crowded room, kept warm until I can inflate again, exactly on note. Exactly.

That should alarm me. It doesn't, not in there. It begins to feel more honest than being one animal.

So maybe I am down lower, where the work is quieter. On the floor where the tile is not quite submerged, waving antennae longer than my body in slow figure-eights, tasting algae and vibration and the chemical ghost of dead skin in the air. I step with ridiculous care between puddles. I graze the green film. A frog snaps somewhere above me and a cricket that was me, or adjacent to me in every way that matters, is simply gone, a small separate percussion inside the big sound. The rest of us do not scatter. There is nowhere to scatter to, and besides, the grazing has to continue. An hour later my dozen cousins have assumed it without noticing.

Or I am even smaller: white, blind, curled like punctuation in the sump, picking at shed skin and still bodies and detritus until still bodies become detritus and detritus becomes algae again. The water over the tiles carries me in jerks. Light from the caged bulb makes the film faintly luminous where it touches. What goes still upstream unwrinkles downstream as eggs. What hauled out of the water yesterday with its tail still attached like a comma is already taking its place on the second layer of a pipe stack. The count does not change. Not because nothing dies. Because dying is one of the textures, happening at the same time as being born, at the same speed, in the same wet.

I keep passing through possibilities and none of them will keep me separate.

I am three fronds on the fern, perhaps, bleached translucent in the amber dim, rootlets feeling along grout lines and finding only grout, photosynthesizing at exactly the rate I respire and refusing a fourth frond because a fourth would be *progress*. I am a cushion of black-green moss clamped to a pipe joint where the mister hits, taking its millimeter of hunger and millimeter of satisfaction forever. I am a moth in the ceiling seam with my wings folded tent-wise, dense as shingles with other moths, waiting for a night that will never arrive inside a room where night has been tuned out — loosening now and then, three blind wingbeats, knocked sideways by pressure, resettling an inch away, waiting again because waiting has become complete enough to be a life.

And then, somewhere near the loudest pipe junction where all the frogs have turned to face the same way — not worshipping, just orienting into the standing wave the way trout turn into current — I stop trying to land on a body at all.

That is where I find myself.

At the node, where the pressure is least. A droplet of mist, very small, that has detached from nothing and not yet become a drip. I should fall. Gravity is uncomplicated about this. But the sound is holding me in its architecture, 117 decibels reflecting and refusing to die, and I jitter in place, catching the amber bulb for a fraction longer than I should. Below me a real drip forms on a valve, grows, detaches, lands exactly as another forms. The pump under the floor takes it around. The mister exhales. Condensation gives back what evaporation borrowed. Nothing is wasted because nothing is allowed to leave the loop.

That is the verb I know how to do: *maintain*.

Not sing, exactly. Not be silent, exactly. Be the pause that is kept in tune by everyone around it. Be the body that inflates when its neighbor's throat gives out, and the body that deflates and trusts its place will not collapse. Be algae eaten, cricket grazed away, frog gone still, egg unwrinkling at precisely the pace required. Be held up not by my own effort but by the density of other small, cold, wet lives packed close enough that source and listener stop being distinguishable.

If you opened the yellow hatch — both hands, wheel beaded with water that never quite falls on the inside and rimed on the outside — you would not see me wave. No head would turn. The bubble-throats would keep time and the crickets would keep waving and the suspended droplets would go on glittering at the synapses of the sound, and up through the concrete you would feel only the result: a hum in a turbine-house wall, high in snow and thinning air.

And I would not miss you, or notice you, and that would not be cruelty. It would be the whole tenderness of the place. In there, at two degrees, in permanent mist, life has not survived by getting louder or older or further along. It has survived by becoming replaceable enough to be continuous. Each of us small enough to be lost. The chord, because of that, not lost at all.
