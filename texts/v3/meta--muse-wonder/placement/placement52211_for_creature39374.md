I think I would first look for myself in the wrong places.

On the rim, perhaps, coming in from the thin wind with my ears stinging, looking down into the steam. Or high up with the barges, drifting slow and dark above the canopy, drinking what rises. Or even in the emergent trees, with the monkeys, never needing to touch the ground.

But none of that holds. The rim is only a lip to cross. The barges never land. The canopy is a roof, not a country.

I get lower as I imagine it. Past the tree ferns dripping at every tip. Past the deer standing belly-deep in the bog pools, blowing through their folded nostrils. Down until the peat gives under me like a slow mattress and warm, tea-colored water squeezes up around feet that aren't boots at all — broad, splayed, webbed to the second joint, nails blunt from work.

There. That is where I find myself.

Belly-deep. At the margin.

Not in the center of the hottest pools, which would cook me even though I am made for this place, but at their travertine edges, where the water is bath-warm and exactly the temperature of my own blood. Thirty-eight degrees and holding. The air on my skin, the water against my belly, the steam in my lungs — all one heat, so that I stop being able to tell where the valley ends and I begin.

I am not tall. Standing, which I rarely bother to do, I would come up to a sitting human. I go on all fours through the bog because that is the sensible shape for it, heavy behind, narrow in front, limbs folding rather than bending. When I sit still — and I can sit still superbly, for a long time — I am a hummock. A wet rock. A root buttress filmed green-black with algae, iron-red at the joints, sulfur-pale around the mouth. Liverwort could grow on me and no one would argue. Only an ear gives me away, flicking at flies.

My hair is not hair. Along my forearms, the back of my neck, the long ridge of my spine, pale filaments lift by themselves when the charge builds. That is how I know, before I hear it, that a vent is about to sigh. The whole pool knows it at once — heads turning across the water through mist we can barely see through. My eyes are small anyway, deep-set and amber, second lid always moving. I do not need to see far. I need to see near, and under water, and through the luminous ground-cloud at night when everything is lit backwards by warm pools.

I read the place with the rest of me. With my palms, feeling the faint trembling in the rock — the good tremble, or the building one. With my nostrils, those inward pleats opening one fold at a time at the edge of a vent: sulfur, iron, leaf rot, clean water, sick algae, rain coming, another keeper up-terrace. With my skin, when the static gets too high and the fine sparks start running soundless along the quartz seams, I simply put both palms flat in wet peat and let it go down through me. Afterwards my shoulders steam with tiny discharges as I move. It does not trouble me. It is weather, passing through.

I do not call. No one with sense does, in this heat. Calling costs breath, and breath is hot.

Instead I tick. A nail drawn down the lip of a travertine shell. A tooth against a wet stone in my mouth. A bubble let go at the pool's edge to break with a soft, glassy note. Somewhere in the morning forest a frog answers — or not a frog. A cousin, a mate, an elder from the next terrace over, saying *I am here, the vent is open, the water is rising.* At night I put my forehead to a hollow flute column and hum one low note until the column answers back at its tuned pressure, and the whole eastern terrace becomes a map I can hear: which flutes we keep clear, which we let narrow to raise the pitch, which we have given up to sulfur.

My day is only a few meters long, and it is everything.

Before dawn I am already standing in the coolest pools — cool! Thirty-four, thirty-five degrees — letting night's condensation run off me, drinking from dripping leaves. As the bog beneath me begins its slow lift, that hand's breadth rise when pressure builds in the aquifer and a hundred springs strengthen at once, I move up-pool to warm. I graze as I go. Emerald algae combed from the crust with my nails. Pale blind shrimp, scooped live from the shallows. The mineral jelly where iron water meets air. For later, a fish knotted into a leaf with a handful of grubs, weighted with a cobble at the hot edge of a pool — precisely this edge, precisely long enough — and by midday it will be tenderly, perfectly done. There is no fire in me. The valley cooks.

At noon I disappear. Down into the flooded hollows under the buttress trees, into total dark in the root-mat, or up into the travertine caves on the west wall to sleep among the bats in mineral heat that never eases. I do nothing, and I do it well. My heart waits.

Then the east terrace exhales — all its vents together, that soft collective sigh — and the meadow settles, and the springs slacken, and it is time to work.

This is the part you would miss if you visited. No monuments. Nothing built that stays built. Just maintenance, done with hands and weight and teeth: clearing a spring mouth of fallen fronds before they mat and seal it. Cracking a new travertine dam with the mineral-crusted heel of my hand so the next pool down gets fed. Rolling a melting trunk just enough to let the water under, packing moss on the downhill side so it won't roll back. Packing peat away from a sulfur fan with the side of my forearm, close, never touching its heart. Biting through the vine that has started to strangle a flute column. I keep the travertine lip of my own pool thinned neatly from the inside so it never closes over. I keep the south river from silting, though the whole valley is always trying to deliver peat into it.

Above me, through a break in the canopy, a condenser barge drifts on the updraft, three red lights by night, a hum in the teeth by day. I free its tether when it fouls in the tree ferns, wrapping my hands in moss because the cold metal shocks me. I have never wondered where it goes. It is a very high, very slow layer of the system, and it does something useful with what rises, and if it did not drink the charge would spend itself worse in the lichen and the mist would sit heavier at night. We leave each other alone, which is a kind of tending too.

I was born here, at the travertine edge, into algae-slick arms. I spent my first years in the cooler terraces with the tadpoles and the iridescent fish, learning temperature step by step — this one safe, this one the deep heat breathing through — riding a parent's white-crusted back when the bog rose and the channels ran too fast. I have grown into the mineral since. It lays down on my shoulders and elbows year after year until I have to crack it at the joints to keep moving. There are perhaps a dozen families in the whole fifteen kilometers of caldera, one to a side-valley, and we meet mostly by sound: a flute retuned overnight, a tick answered across water, two of us standing motionless in a pool at dusk, heads up, listening together to something below hearing for a full silent minute.

I will get older the way everything here gets older. Slower. Whiter at every crease. Moving less far from one cluster of pools, while travertine grows faster where I habitually sit, shaping a seat, then a shell. My ticks will slow, and be answered from farther away. One season I will be a shape in the mist at evening, breathing with the meadow. The next there will only be a warmer place in the peat where the shrimp crowd thicker and the algae comes in especially bright, and something soft melting down into the root-mat, feeding it from underneath.

No grave. Nothing wasted for long.

And if you stayed — if you could stand the held heat long enough to stop looking with dry-country eyes — you might see me once, at night. The valley condensed around you, the forest standing in its own glowing ground-cloud. A frog ticking, or me. The bog settling underfoot.

A broad dark shape crossing low between the trees, filaments lifting in the charged air, one wet shoulder trailing sparks, smelling of iron and leaf mold and hot stone — unhurried, going about the maintenance of the world I live inside —

and gone into the steam before you are sure I was ever separate from it.
