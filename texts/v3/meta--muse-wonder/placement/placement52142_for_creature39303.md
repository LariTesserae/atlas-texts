I would come over the rim the way you did, I think — expecting a town.

Not at first with any grand sense of arrival. With sore knees, probably, and the unreasonable irritation of finding a boardwalk where I had braced for scree. My boots would be wrong on it. Too hard, too loud. The root-floor would give almost nothing under me, just enough that I would keep glancing down, and the shallow pools would open in their green skins around a vibration I hadn't felt myself make.

I would make the first mistake for a long time. I would look for whoever lived there in the towers.

Behind the leaf-shutters, though they were never meant to open. Up the cathedral stairs, ducking my head at doorways built exactly not to require it. Into the long hall, where I would stand at the back — not on the dais, not yet, that would feel presumptuous — and clear my throat, and hear the whole building shift its humming half a second later, as if something very large had turned an ear I couldn't see.

I would try to be a good explorer. I would not take anything. I would sleep out on one of the plazas the first night, with my pack for a pillow, because the celled building with the hammocks would seem too much like an invitation I wasn't sure was addressed to me, and because each hollow in the woven roots was so exactly the length of a body that lying in one would feel less like resting than like trying on a coffin someone had made with love.

The plaza would be warm through my back. That would keep me awake. Not uncomfortably warm — body warm. Animal warm. And in the morning there would be a fine tracery of moisture where I had lain, and the duckweed in the nearest pool would have broken and re-formed around some new chemistry, and I would understand, with my hand flat on a bench that was already smoothing itself under my palm, that I had been tasted all night long.

That is where whatever I was in that place would begin to change its shape.

Because at first I would tell myself I was the guest. The long-awaited one, perhaps — the stairs take my stride without adjustment, the benches are at sitting height for me, my voice fits into the chord the way a fifth waits inside it. It is a flattering story. It lets me walk the colonnade at the southern edge feeling chosen.

But the moth would correct me. That one oversized white moth, working the same half-open trumpet-flower at intervals I could set my breathing by. It isn't a resident either. It's a rehearsal. Almost the right size. And the frog in the marsh, stopping its one note so abruptly that the whole valley listens to the hole it leaves — almost the right voice.

Almost. That would be my category too, I think. I am not the animal it lost in whatever lower country its seeds remember. That animal is gone so long its shape survives only as grammar: bench, stair, listening-hall, bed. I am a late, coincidental rhyme. My feet are the wrong feet, though they work. My lifespan is absurd against a place that keeps its pools from rising by drinking at exactly the rate of rainfall. When I die — in a hammock season or two, by its reckoning — it will not even have finished learning the shape of the cavity that makes my voice.

And yet.

Sometime after the first day, without deciding to, I would start doing the work the absent partner used to do. Not building. Pruning by walking. I would take the same stair each morning because that tower's note is cleanest at dawn, and my weight in the middle of the treads would begin — really begin, not in anticipation — to polish them. I would pull a tendril back from the edge of the dais, and then stop, and leave a little fern at the margins, because a place scrubbed perfect stops reading as lived-in. I would sing, badly at first, self-conscious in the long hall, and hear which of the clustered flutes along the water channels took my note up and which ones dulled it, and I would find myself choosing rooms by whether they could carry me.

That is the partnership, I suspect. Not fitting into the hammock. Feeding back. It built the shape of desire and has held it open past all reason; what it has been waiting for is not a body to fill the rooms but a disturbance to tune against. Growth edited nightly back to the plan — but the plan has never once been walked. I would be the first walking.

So I would let the hammock have me, eventually.

Not the first night, and not without fear. I would lie down in the dry hollow that had waited unoccupied for longer than the black rock has been cool, and I would feel the aerial roots do nothing so crude as tighten. They would settle. A few finer than hair would find the pulse at my wrist, and then, finding it, simply rest there, the way the floor rested under my back on the plaza: measuring, tasting, learning the tempo of a heart against its own slower one. In the morning the hollow would be a fraction deeper — not polished smooth in advance anymore. *Worn.* Scheduled wear, finally occurring.

By then I would have stopped looking for the inhabitant behind the shutters, because I would finally be living inside its senses rather than its architecture. The floor is its tongue and it knows my sweat now. The towers are its ears and they know where my voice catches. When I speak and the nearest pipe shifts emphasis, it is not answering me. It is feeling what I interrupted. And I, in return, would have started feeling what I interrupt back — learning to lower my voice at the place in the colonnade where the dripping makes a curtain, learning to be still when the frog stops, so I can hear the inhabitant attend to the silence.

I would find myself, most truly, at the north end.

In the amphitheater, in the one clear circle where the root-floor bends aside and the young black rock lies bare and steaming. I would go there barefoot, because boots felt obscene on that warmth, and I would stand in the column of its exhalation where the drips from the canopy fall all around and never land, and I would understand that I am standing in its mouth and its navel at once, on the one part of itself it never covered because covering is not what that place is for.

And I would sing the note.

Not well. Not the note it was designed, across ten thousand patient years, to receive. A human note, a little ragged, occupying one of its holes a half-second too long. And the chord — the perfect, equilibrium chord, the chord that has not changed since the valley cupped its first warm air — would change. Slightly. One flute along the channel adjusting to a flaw it decides, on balance, to keep. A leaf-shutter, somewhere behind me, moving for the first time: not opening with the sun, but opening with me.

I would not be the inhabitant. I would not even, in the end, be the awaited guest, because *awaited* implies it knew my shape, and it only knew the shape of my absence.

I would be its first animal in a very long time. Its pruner, its interrupter, its too-short rehearsal that turned out, by staying, to be a performance. It would outlive me the way a blossom outlives the insect — gratefully, chemically, without grief as I understand it — and long after I was taken back into the slow digestion under the duckweed, the hammock in the celled building would hold a hollow a fraction deeper than the others. The stair would be worn where I actually stepped. One tower would hum a fifth that had my breath in its ancestry.

The town would still be ready. That is its nature; readiness is indistinguishable from its life. But it would be ready slightly differently — tuned, at last, to something that came, and fit badly, and stayed long enough to leave a mark in the plan.

I think I would stay.
