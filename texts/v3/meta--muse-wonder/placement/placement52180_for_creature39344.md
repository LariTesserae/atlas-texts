You would not find me up there.

Not on the bounce of the mat, where the light comes down in coins and the visitor's feet make the whole world shudder clumsily. Not on the rim, sieving dust. Not in the canopy, where everything has to shout before its branch falls.

You would find me underneath the shouting.

If you put your ear to a root — no. Ears are the wrong instrument. Put your belly down. Put your jaw down. Put the flat of your spade-forefoot down and wait, and after a while the darkness under the mat stops being silence and becomes crowded.

*Boom. Boom-boom. Boom.*

I am in that.

I am low and wide and built mostly in front. Shoulders first, the rest of me following like something I drag. My forefeet are not for walking, really. They are broad horn spades, callused to the edge, for tearing a root-bundle apart and then packing it tight behind me again so the ceiling remembers how to be a ceiling. I move by shoving the forest aside. It closes over me. It is always closing over me.

Above me is a trampoline of living roots, meters thick in places, hollow underneath. Below me is the ferment. Fallen trunks gone soft without ever being wet. Black mulch that breathes heat against my belly. White larvae turning in it, slow and blind. The air down here is thick enough to taste at the back of the skull — resin, rot-sweetness, hot pepper from a gel-trunk opened too deep, the ammoniac sting when an old chamber has been sealed too long. I breathe in sips. My nostrils narrow to slits. Whatever moisture tries to leave me on the breath gets caught on the way out, in the folded passages inside my head, and taken back. Even that is too expensive to lose.

I do not drink. There is nothing to drink, and the idea would be obscene up top only because they still look for water with their eyes. Water is here. It is just locked.

It is in the pith of a fallen trunk, if I chisel it right — two curved teeth in front, just enough of an opening to worry the gel loose, not enough to kill the tree before it can skin itself over again. It is in grubs fat on rotten wood. It is in roots swollen with syrup, in ferment-mulch chewed twice, in fruit dragged down from above before the birds can make a sermon of it. My back teeth never stop growing because the fiber never stops trying to wear them away. What leaves me leaves dry: urine as a paste that crusts yellow, droppings already half soil. My skin gives up almost nothing. It hangs in oily pleats at my neck and shoulders and smells of the trunks I rub against, and most biting things decide I am part of the furniture.

My eyes are amber pits under bone. If a canopy tree falls far above — and one is always falling, you feel the sentence of it arrive through everything, crash translated to pressure — I may know the difference between old shade and a sudden new brightness. That is all they are for. Green means nothing to me. Loud means nothing to me. I have no outside ears to be deafened with.

I hear with my feet. With my belly pressed to a root braid. With my jaw.

And down here, everything is saying something.

The tick and pop of cellulose stretching as seedlings race for a light-hole. The creak of a root under too much tension, pitched — felt — a fraction too tight before it snaps. The patter of ants in a seam. The careful, too-careful stillness where a snake is being a vine in the wrong layer. My family, spread out where no eye could find them, keeping touch: a quick triple tap where the mat is thin and rich, a slow rolling thump rolled shoulder to root when something above is walking wrong.

We never say it the same way twice. Predators learned the words years before I was born. They lie still in the dark and counterfeit: *here, here, the mat is thin, the mat is rich,* and wait for the young or the trusting to answer their way into teeth. So every colony has an accent, and every adult signs it. I drag my left spade a fraction before the second strike. A hitch. A flaw in the rhythm that my mother taught me to make mine by never correcting it. Anyone who knows me knows that hitch the way you might know a face. Anyone who imitates it perfectly is lying.

I learned it late. We are born pink, sealed, silent even by our standards. For the first days I did nothing but knead against my mother's belly — small, meaningless trembling, my skull too soft to carry a true sound. Then kneading became rhythm. Rhythm became pattern. Sometime in my second week, when the plates in my head hardened enough, I put my shoulder to packed root for the first time and boomed, and the whole local mat took my weight and answered. Not welcome. Registration. One more body under the floor.

Because that is the work. We are the floor, and we keep it from killing itself.

Left alone, this valley would choke on how fast it grows. The mat would thicken until nothing could bear on it. Fallen fruit and carcasses would rot up top in the heat and lock their water away from anything but flies. Gases would gather in the ferment-pits until a whole chamber turned killing. So we drift. No dens, no homes — a home is a trap in a place that reinvents its ground every day. We open, we thin, we collapse what is about to burst, we drag the dead down into the dark where larvae and fungus can unmake them properly. A female packs a birthing chamber tight under a thick braid, lines it with chewed husk, leaves it as soon as the young can grip her back with their little spades. We sleep in drifts too, heads turned each to a different vibration, bodies pressed close in the cooling night while our great shoulder muscles tick and contract. What little vapor escapes our skins in sleep, we lick from each other's pleats before it can be lost.

The huge bald birds up top wait all day for us to make a mistake. I do not know them as birds. I know them as a patience in the vibrations above a place I have opened too shallow, and as what happens to an elder rolled too near the surface. I know them the way I know the rest of my dead: under my feet. One of us gone in the open dark is roots in the ribs within days, white thousands of them, seedlings splitting the cage of him before the smell has finished changing. One of us gone deep is eaten by family, not from hunger. Thrift. Water that expensive is not buried.

I am walking, quite literally, through my grandparents. So is everyone I have ever answered.

I have never been to the rim. I do not know there is a salt plain, a rope ladder, a sky that would arrive on my sealed skull only as heat and a terrible thinness where the mat's voice falls away. The desert might as well be on the far side of death. My world has pressure and tempo and a horizon exactly as far as a thump can travel through root before it frays.

Things fall. The mat thickens. Something above stamps a clumsy warning and gets its direction wrong because the whole valley is ventriloquism. A tree goes over — *move, move, now* in shock through every braid — and in the dark we are already turning toward the place where the light-hole will feed a hundred seedlings whose roots will come questing down to us like rain that never falls.

*Here,* I say, and put my shoulder in, left spade dragging its small, private flaw before the second strike.

*Here. Here. Thin here. Rich here. Still underneath.*

And above me, unheard, unseen, the green roaring pit goes on growing itself out of dust and locked gold — because underneath it, we are making room for it to grow.
