This is not a wilderness in the normal sense, and it is not a tourist sea. It is a piece of heavy industry the size of a country, disguised as geography. Visiting it is less like going to a lake district and more like being allowed, briefly, to live inside a maintained machine at 3.5 to 4 kilometres altitude.

Prepare on that basis: altitude first, cold second, sound third, and emptiness for longer than you expect.

### 1. Your body is wrong for the altitude before you ever touch the water

The surface of the sea is already higher than most people ever sleep. The air is thin, dry, and cold, and the sunlight is not softened by anything.

Do not fly/drive/portal in and go straight onto a boat. Give yourself at least 4-7 days of staged acclimatisation in the low rim towns or at a pump station, more if you plan to do any physical work. Headache, nausea, sleeplessness, and stupid decision-making are the early signs of altitude sickness, and on this sea a stupid decision is hard to rescue — the next inhabited station may be 200-300 kilometres away.

Bring:

* A pulse-oximeter and know your normal numbers before you go
* Prescription altitude medication discussed with a doctor in advance, plus a plan for descent — which here means a boat to the rim and a vehicle down the cataract service road, possibly days away
* Serious UV protection. There is no green shade, no tree line, no beach haze. Black basalt, grey water, thin air and ice in the lee of every building reflect light from all sides. High-SPF sunscreen, lip block, glacier glasses with side shields, and a brim that ties on. You will burn at 2°C while feeling cold.
* Layers for dry cold and wind that always comes from the same direction at the same speed. On deck it is perpetually a high-plateau winter: base layer, insulated midlayer, sealed windproof shell, insulated boots with crampon-compatible soles. There is no mud, no soil, no sand. Everything is sharp rock, ice blades, steel decking, or concrete.

Fitness matters more than bravery. Station ladders, jetties, and boat transfers assume you can haul yourself, in gloves, up wet vertical steel.

### 2. The sound will be your main environment

Above water, away from the rim, it can seem eerily quiet. Do not be fooled.

Below water, and near the southern cataract rim, the turbine-and-falls chord is total: a low fundamental you feel in your sternum, teeth, and sinuses, with a high shrieking harmonic from the generator halls. It carries for hundreds of kilometres through the water and through the rock itself. Hulls conduct it. Beds conduct it. Your skull conducts it.

Expect:
* To learn basic local sign language *before* you arrive. Station crews are often second- or third-generation to the noise. Many sign as a first language, even with intact hearing. Shouting on a station, on a boat near the rim, or underwater is useless and marks you as unprepared. Learn the station dialect — it is not necessarily your home universe's sign language — especially signs for *danger, stop, return to boat, line tangled, I am cold, I am disoriented, animal close*.
* Custom-moulded ear protection for topside work near the rim, pump halls, and penstocks. Ordinary foam plugs are not enough for long exposure, and you will need audiology baseline tests before and after if you stay more than a week.
* Vibrating alarms, flashing beacons, and written/visual routines. Do not rely on shouted warnings, boat horns, or dive slates tapped with a knife. Nothing competes.
* Sleep disruption. Even in a sealed cabin you will feel the note through the mattress. Earplugs help with the harmonic, not the fundamental. Some visitors get headaches, tinnitus-like ringing when they finally descend to the lowlands, and a disorienting sense of silence there. Build in quiet recovery days after you leave.

If you have a history of migraines triggered by low-frequency vibration, inner-ear disorders, dental work that is already loose, or panic in inescapable noise, talk to a specialist honestly. This is not a sound you can walk away from while you are on the sea.

### 3. The water will kill you politely, quickly, and at a constant 2°C

It is 2 degrees at the surface in full sun, 2 degrees at 200 metres, summer and winter. It is almost fresh, almost clear, and completely unforgiving.

No swimming. No wading. There are no beaches to wade from anyway — rock and volcanic glass enter the water at a steep angle and keep going. Entry is only from a boat platform or a station jetty, in full kit.

An unprotected fall overboard is a minutes-scale emergency. Cold shock will take your breath, thin altitude air will make recovery harder, and your cry will not carry. Therefore:

* Wear an auto-inflating life vest with a personal locator beacon, strobe, and whistle *at all times* on deck, clipped to a jackline when moving outside the sealed cabin. The work-boats are low steel vessels made for this; use their rails, don't improvise photo spots.
* If diving is part of your purpose, you need specific qualifications: dry-suit, cold water, *and* altitude diving. Dive tables change when your surface pressure is already low. A dive to 20 metres here is not physiologically the same as at sea level, and flying/portalling out immediately after is dangerous. Bring a dive computer set for altitude and fresh water, and obey it conservatively.
* Dry suit with active heating, thick dry gloves, and a hood is baseline, not expedition luxury. Batteries perform poorly in the cold — bring redundant heating packs and test the whole system in cold water at home.
* Budget for disorientation, not just cold. With no algae, no plankton, no suspended particles, you can see a submerged peak resolving 100 metres below you. There is no visual “stuff” in between to give scale. First-time divers get vertigo in mid-water, misjudge distances catastrophically, and either plummet or bolt upward. Always dive on a fixed line or along a ridge flank, with a reference. Never trust “it looks close.”
* Your suit is at risk of being cut. Unworn basalt and volcanic glass are knives. Reinforced knees, elbows, gloves, and boots, plus a repair kit you can use with numb hands, are essential. A torn dry suit at 2°C is not an inconvenience.

Divers also report that the sound underwater is not heard so much as inhabited. You will not hear your buddy, your regulator will seem oddly minor, and animals will make no warning noise at all. Use touch signals, pressure squeezes, and pre-agreed light patterns.

### 4. Getting around means accepting administered distances

From above this looks like a sea because it is one. Six days of featureless crossing between provinces is normal.

Plan for:
* Travel only on scheduled work-boats or station tenders. There is no recreational fleet, no harbour hopping, no chartering a fisherman. Boats have sealed cabins for a reason: permanent fog banks over the western shallows, a fixed cloud lens over the rim falls, and a northeast quadrant where it always rains. Visibility can be nil for days, and nobody navigates by the ceremonial mast lights.
* No useful current, no tide to ride, no landmarks in the interior. Navigation is by instruments, sonar interference patterns from the turbine roar reflecting off ridges, and survey cables on the bottom. Do not bring a personal pleasure craft, kayak, paddleboard, or sailing dinghy. Wind is constant but water conditions, fog, and rescue distance make small craft suicidal, and sail will not save fuel in any meaningful schedule.
* Manifest discipline. Every boat keeps an exact ledger: fuel, water taken on, water discharged, weight. This whole sea is balanced — pumps return exactly what the falls remove, level has moved 4 cm in 140 years. You will be asked not to take souvenir water, dump greywater, throw anything overboard, or import/export ballast. Your own body displacing water in a dive is fine; industrial-scale changes require paperwork you will not get.
* Seasickness plus altitude sickness is a common pairing. The sea has its own internal weather and long fetches. Bring medication that does not interact with your altitude prescription, and test it before departure.

Book berths months in advance through the pump authority. Some stations are crewed by six people, some have been uncrewed for decades and are visited one day a year. If you miss a rendezvous, you may be waiting a very long time.

### 5. Life is abundant, but only in islands, and it is silent

Do not expect a reef teeming everywhere. Expect sterile hours, then sudden oversupply.

Around fresh fractures in the basalt and — inevitably — around station discharge pipes, bacterial mats grow white and rust-orange. They are chemosynthetic, eating hydrogen and sulphur compounds from broken rock, not sunlight. Everything else eats them. If you are allowed to approach a mat:

* Treat it like a meadow and a chemical plant at once. Do not step on it, sample without permit, or stir sediment (there is almost none to stir). Near vents and discharge, water can be warm, mineral-rich, and locally toxic with sulphur compounds. Your dive computer thermometer spiking and a rotten-egg smell through a station intake are turn-back signs.
* Decontaminate obsessively. This ecosystem evolved with *no* plants, no algae, no phytoplankton. The single greatest ecological catastrophe a multiverse traveller could cause is introducing a hardy algae, weed seed, or microbial film from another world on boots, boat hulls, dive gear, or a living plant in your cabin. Sterilise gear before arrival, bring no live plants, no soil, no untreated wood, no aquarium anything. Stations will inspect. Let them.
* Keep distance from animals even though they seem placid. Dinner-plate filter grazers cemented by millions open and close in perfect synchrony; disturbing one patch can ripple. Glass eels run in smoke-dense schools that can white-out — black-out — your vision and mask a ridge. Bell-cows, the 8-14 metre blind translucent midwater grazers with lantern ribs and faintly luminous stomachs, approach without sound and without bow wave you will recognise. One can simply be *there*, filling your sight. Do not touch, do not use bright flashing lights except agreed signals, do not chase for photographs. They communicate by touch, pressure, and brief bioluminescent patterns. A collision with something that size in a dry suit does not need malice to break your ribs.
* Do not expect to eat locally. There is no edible plant life, fishing is industrial-permit only, populations are kept in a weird exact equilibrium, and the food chain is built on sulphur bacteria. Bring all food, expect station food to be stored, tinned, and lowland-supplied, and pack high-calorie reserves for boat delays. Thin air suppresses appetite precisely when you need fuel for cold.

Nothing sings, calls, or splashes for your benefit. If you need audio cues to feel safe in nature, this place will feel actively wrong.

### 6. People, stations, and etiquette

A pump station is a concrete blockhouse, jetty, fuel tank, intake pipes going into black water, a steaming discharge, and ice sculptures in the lee that reform identically every winter. It may be home.

* Ask before recording, photographing interiors, or entering turbine/pump halls. Penstocks are as wide as railway tunnels, switchyards are fenced against ice for lethal reasons, and a running hall adds its own harmonic to the sea’s. Follow escorted routes, wear station-issued protection, never lean on humming survey equipment.
* Bring useful gifts, not novelties: mail, fresh lowland food sealed under quarantine rules, replacement seals and bearings if coordinated in advance, books/recordings the crew requested. Some keepers have had one supply day a year.
* Accept that children may address you in sign, old crew may read lips poorly in poor light, and written notes are normal at dinner. Speak facing people, don’t shout louder when not understood, learn thank you / please / danger first.
* Wrecks on ridge crests, paint still legible because nothing rots or weeds them, are not dive playgrounds. Log your visit, do not penetrate without authority, do not move anything. In this clarity a wreck looks parked, not sunk, which tempts people to underestimate depth.

### 7. The psychological preparation no kit list covers

Visitors expect drama: a high cold ocean, young mountains, a 40-kilometre distributed cataract falling terrace to terrace to the lowlands. What they get day to day is administration. Flat grey surface exactly where it always was. Same wind. Same note. Same temperature. Travelling for days and seeing only your own wake disappearing slowly because there is not even a current to smear it.

That monotony, plus the constant low sound and the absence of any living noise, affects people in week two more than day two. Bring structured work, books, music for off-water hours (with volume discipline on station), a journal, and a reason to get up at a set time under a deck-cloud that can make noon look like evening. Tell your travelling companions in advance how you each want to be checked in on if you go quiet/withdrawn.

If you go below, prepare for this specific image and rehearse staying calm: black knife-ridges falling away through water clearer than air, no green anywhere, a bell-cow crossing far beneath lit from above with a slow blue pulse along its ribs, the pump station ticking with heat behind you, and the falls 400 kilometres away speaking through your skull without pause.

You close the surface overhead — flat, grey, ordinary sunlight above it — and for a moment you are part of the ledger too.

Leave exactly balanced. That is the courtesy this sea understands.

**Minimum go/no-go checklist:** altitude-acclimatised and medically cleared; cold-water altitude dry-suit competence if entering water at all; custom hearing protection and basic station sign; sealed wind/UV clothing; PLB and boat discipline; sterilised gear with zero biological import; permits, berth, and extraction booked with a margin of days — not hours — on every leg. If any one of those is missing, admire it from the rim road. The view of water going over the world in white terraces, generating power on every step, is not a lesser visit.
