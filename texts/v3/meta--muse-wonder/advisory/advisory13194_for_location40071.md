This is not a room you explore. It is a room you *stand in*, briefly, in exactly one place, while it overwhelms three senses at once. Preparation is mostly about making yourself smaller, drier, quieter, and less likely to touch anything — and about planning your exit and recovery before you go in.

Because you will probably want to back out sooner than you expect.

### What you are actually entering

Behind a knee-high cedar door in the plinth of the grinding-house is a chamber only about five feet across, almost completely filled with centuries of accumulated offerings: clay tablets, copper and brass plaques, beads, ribbons, little clay houses, hands, ears, hearts, bells, gourds, reed bundles. They are packed against the walls, floor, and dome, leaving one person-shaped hollow in the center.

There is:

* **Blinding, shadowless light** from an alabaster window and nine perpetual oil lamps, reflected endlessly off polished metal, glaze, and glass.
* **Constant, physical sound** — a sustained chant from the next hall, amplified and harmonized by forty resonating jars buried in the wall. You feel it in your ribs, teeth, sinuses, and eyes.
* **Cool, extremely dry air** that dries your mouth within minutes.
* **No room to turn, raise your arms, step aside, or steady yourself.**
* **A strict governing rule: do not touch anything.**

Most of the danger is not mystical. It is sensory overload, claustrophobia, disorientation, and accidentally knocking into a densely interlocked structure that has remained stable only because nothing shifts.

### Who should think twice, or not go in

Skip the interior, or get medical advice first, if you have:

* Claustrophobia, panic in confined spaces, or a history of fainting while standing still
* Migraines triggered by glare, severe light sensitivity, recent eye surgery, dry-eye problems, or contact lenses that dry out quickly
* Tinnitus, hyperacusis, chronic ear pain, sinus problems, or sensitive dental work — the resonance is felt in the teeth and skull
* Asthma or sensitivity to lime dust, old oil smoke, copper dust, or dry air
* Bad knees, bad back, limited mobility, or difficulty crawling and reversing out on your knees
* A body significantly broader than average. The standing hollow is literally worn and built to a fairly narrow human shape. Tall visitors will have bells and gourds brushing their hair. If you have to force yourself in, do not go — you will touch things simply by breathing deeply.

There is no graceful rescue if you faint inside. There is room for one standing person, the door is knee-high and opens inward, and a collapsed visitor would fall against the offerings. A companion should wait outside, but they cannot easily pull you out.

### Before you arrive

**1. Plan for a very short first visit.**
One to three minutes is a complete visit. Sixty seconds is not a failure. Pilgrims come out blinking and temporarily deaf after only standing there. Decide your limit in advance, because inside, time and judgment get strange — the sound evens out, your heartbeat seems to sync with it, and there are no shadows or changes to orient you.

Agree with your companion: "If I am not out in three minutes, call my name from outside" — *not* by knocking. Knocking will be amplified and will set the bells shivering.

**2. Hydrate and eat beforehand, outside.**
Your mouth and throat will dry fast. Drink water before you descend the lane, use the toilet, eat something light so you are not faint or nauseated. Do not bring water inside. A spill would be catastrophic in a place that has stayed dry for centuries — it could damage ribbons and herbs, stain tablets, and even wet the resurrection rosette that is kept in permanent dormancy. Drink again after you come out.

If you are prone to nosebleeds in dry air, put a little saline gel in your nostrils well before; do it outside so you do not need to raise your hands to your face inside.

**3. Leave almost everything behind.**
No backpack, satchel, shoulder bag, camera bag, water bottle, umbrella, walking stick, or bulky coat. Nothing in your pockets that bulges, jingles, beeps, or could fall out while you crawl. A dropped object is not retrievable by rummaging — it would become part of the packing, which is exactly what you must not do on your own.

Turn off, or better leave outside, anything that buzzes, chimes, flashes, vibrates, speaks, or makes a startup tone. Inside, a whisper comes back as a shout. An ordinary phone alarm would be an assault and could shake the hanging bells.

**4. If you want to leave an offering, do not place it yourself.**
Custodians add things from a hatch behind the lamps, by feel, finding the one gap that will take it. Give any offering to a custodian outside and accept that you may not see where, or whether, it is placed. Do not slide coins, notes, beads, or food into cracks. Nothing is removed, nothing is rearranged, and food, moisture, perfume oils, and living seeds are especially unwelcome.

### What to wear

Think: smooth, fitted, dry, and dust-tolerable.

* Close-fitting clothes with no loose sleeves, scarves, dangling cords, wide cuffs, external toggles, or large buttons and buckles that could catch a ribbon or bead string. Sleeves have polished the nearest brass over centuries — your clothing *will* be near the walls.
* Trousers you can crawl in, ideally with the knee reinforced from inside rather than bulky external kneepads. You enter in the dust on your knees.
* Hair tied up and contained. No loose jewelry, bead necklaces, earrings that swing, or glasses cords that dangle. Secure glasses tightly; reflections off them can be dazzling, and tall visitors risk brushing the dome.
* Soft, clean, flexible footwear or thick socks you can feel the floor through. The tile floor is dished smooth by centuries of soles and knees; hard tread will not help you balance. Clean dust and mud off before entering, but do not arrive wet.
* No perfume, scented oil, or incense on skin or clothes. The chamber already smells of lime, lamp oil, warm copper, and centuries-old incense absorbed into plaster. Added scent will linger far longer than you will.
* Arrive completely dry: no wet rain gear, damp hair, or sweaty outer layer you need to peel off. Do not try to remove clothing inside — there is no room to raise your arms.

Sunglasses are useful *before* and *after* in the pale lane, but most people should not wear dark glasses inside. You need what little depth perception the glare allows, and dark lenses coming out again into daylight complicate an already difficult transition. If glare is a medical issue for you, that is a reason to shorten or skip, not to improvise.

Earplugs are a judgment call. Discreet foam plugs inserted outside can reduce hearing fatigue, but they will not stop the chest, teeth, and eye pressure, and the occlusion can make your own breathing and pulse seem louder. If you use them, put them in before you crouch. Do not try to insert anything once inside.

### At the door

The door is in the lane where it turns down toward the dry riverbed. There are no steps.

1. Stop in the lane. Let your eyes adjust to the pale, quiet outside light. Let your companion take your belongings.
2. Confirm your time limit and your exit signal.
3. Crouch in the dust and work the peg latch with two fingers. The door swings inward.
4. Go in sideways, on your knees, without reaching out to brace yourself on the interior walls. The threshold copper strip is not a handhold.
5. Rise slowly into the standing hollow, keeping elbows tucked, hands held still against your thighs or lightly clasped low in front of you. Keep your chin slightly down if you are tall.

Practice this movement at home if you can: crawl through a low opening sideways, stand without using your hands or raising your arms, stand still, then reverse out backwards on your knees. If you cannot do that calmly in a quiet room, the real chamber will be much harder.

### Inside: how to behave

* **Do not touch.** Not the plaques for balance, not a bell that brushes your hair, not the herbs to see if they are real, not the two leaves by the air shaft. The whole structure holds together by mutual pressure. One tug can move a tablet that wedges a plaque that holds a bell.
* **Do not speak.** Not even your name, not even a whisper. The dome returns it flattened, prolonged, and shout-loud, and the bells answer. Agree on silence in advance. Breathe through your nose, slowly and shallowly if the resonance makes deep breaths feel strange.
* **Do not try to turn around to see everything.** You will see yourself broken into hundreds of partial reflections in the brass and glaze, none whole. Trying to track them worsens vertigo. Choose one low, relatively matte area — the worn floor tiles near your feet are usually least painful — and let the rest stay peripheral. Your eyes may water. Blink; do not rub them with dusty hands, and do not raise your arms to shade your face.
* **Do not cough, sneeze, hum, or clear your throat if you can help it.** If you feel a sneeze or cough building, start backing out. A sudden noise is not merely rude; it reverberates painfully and shakes the hanging work.
* **Watch for your personal stop signals:** ringing that spikes sharply, nausea, pressure behind the eyes, a feeling that the floor hollow is narrowing, panic rising, or the urge to grab the wall. At the first one, leave. There is no view or inscription that rewards pushing through; the readable experience is the glare, the chord, and the pressure itself.
* **Leave nothing behind.** No hairpins, tissues, coins, lint offerings, exhaled sprays, or water. Even shed fibers and dust from outside clothes add up over centuries, so brush yourself down in the lane before entering, not inside.

Expect your own body to become part of the instrument. Your breathing will seem to join the chord. Your heartbeat may feel as if it is finding the jars' frequency. That sensation is normal for visitors and usually fades outside; it is also a good cue that you have had enough.

### Getting out and recovering

Exit backwards, ducking and returning to your knees, in reverse of your entrance. Do not turn inside to face the door.

Once outside:

1. Move a few paces from the door so the next visitor or your companion has room, then sit down in the lane. Do not immediately stride up from the riverbed.
2. Close your eyes for a minute. The sky will seem absurdly wide and dim, street noises absurdly quiet and separate, and your own single shadow strangely distinct after the shadowless interior.
3. Wait until the ringing dulls and normal hearing starts returning in pieces. Temporary muffling/deafness and light afterimages are commonly described.
4. Blink, let your eyes water, then use the sunglasses you left outside if the lane is bright. Drink water now. Moisten your mouth slowly; the inside air will have dried it more than you realized.
5. Check your knees, clothes, and hair for dust only *after* you are well clear — shake out away from the door so you do not puff dust into the chamber.
6. Do not re-enter the same day to "do it properly." Sensory fatigue is cumulative, and second visits tend to be shorter, not deeper.

If ringing, headache, ear pain, dizziness, or eye pain persists well beyond an hour or worsens, seek medical care in the town as you would for any noise-and-glare exposure.

### A useful rule of thumb

Prepare as if you were going to stand, dry and silent and untouched, inside a musical instrument that is also a reliquary and a mirror-box, entered through a cupboard door on your knees.

Bring: fitted dry clothes, contained hair, a companion with your things, a pre-agreed time limit, water waiting outside, and a willingness to leave after a minute.

Leave behind: bags, loose objects, sound-making devices, food, drink, offerings to place yourself, the impulse to touch or whisper, and any plan to turn around, photograph, measure, or explore.

The chamber has not meaningfully changed in two hundred years because everyone who enters agrees, with their body, to change nothing. Your preparation is how you keep that agreement.
