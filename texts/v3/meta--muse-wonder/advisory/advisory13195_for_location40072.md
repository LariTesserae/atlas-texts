Vaith does not begin at its gate. It begins days below it, when your pack starts to feel heavier than it should, the pines thin out, and sound starts behaving like something too tired to follow you.

Prepare for the climb as much as for the city. By the time you see Vaith — and from below it will only look like a village on a needle of rock, which is wrong by several orders of magnitude — your body will already be making adjustments you will need up there.

### 1. Your Body Is Your Primary Instrument

This is an extreme-altitude environment, but normal altitude rules only partly apply. Altitude in Vaith is not finished at the summit. It continues inside the city, inside buildings, sometimes inside a single room.

**Before you go:**
Train for cold, dry, thin air. Not expedition cold — worse, a constant, heatless cool. The sun is small, white, and bright enough to see by, but it will not warm you. At noon under an indigo, star-filled sky you can still get frost-nipped fingers handling stone. Dress as for deep winter, in layers you can open while climbing: wool and close-woven natural fiber do better than anything that relies on trapped warm air, because the air up there doesn't hold heat or smell or sound reliably. Bring a windproof outer layer. The wind has almost no substance, but it never stops working.

Bring lip salve, nose salve, and something to cover your mouth and nose while sleeping. The lintel instructions are literal: *Sleep with the mouth closed.* The air is dry enough to crack your throat overnight, and at certain heights your exhalation can linger. You do not want to wake breathing in yesterday's unfinished sentence. A soft cloth mask tied on at night helps.

Plan to ascend slowly, over days. Sleep a little higher each night as the country simplifies: pine, grass, lichen, colorless stone. If your footsteps on the pale rock stop making sound beside you, stop climbing for that day. Put your ear to the ground minutes later and you may hear them arrive, small and delayed. That delay is your warning that shouting, whistles, alarms, and verbal warnings will not work in the city.

No supplemental oxygen is forbidden, but ordinary travelers report it helps less than expected — the thinness is not only in the air. A level plaza can pop your ears. Crossing a high room can give you summit elation and a nosebleed. Go by breath, not distance. The old inhabitants clearly did. The shortest route is the lowest route. An uphill shortcut can cost you memory as well as blood.

Get your ears, sinuses, teeth, and lungs checked beforehand. The well-shafts breathe up columns of cold air that hurt the teeth, rime sublimation registers in the molars, and the ice on the garden boulders sings at a pitch most people feel as pressure behind the eyes. If you are prone to migraines, sinus pain, or altitude sickness, Vaith will find it.

### 2. What to Bring, and What Will Betray You

Pack as if for a dry, waterless, wheelless city built without metal, glass, or hinges — because your wheeled, hinged, glass things will be at best awkward and at worst unpleasantly conspicuous to the physics there.

**Bring:**
* A frame pack, sled, or travois. No wheeled luggage. There are no wheels in Vaith and streets that exchange houses overnight do not respect wheel ruts anyway.
* Everything in wooden, leather, ceramic, or stone containers with lids that can be weighted down. Doors in Vaith turn on stone pivots and ease open by themselves at dusk as the city contracts; your containers will learn the same habit. Weight every lid you love.
* Rope, wooden pegs, cloth ties. No reliance on metal fasteners for anything life-critical.
* A slate, chalk, wax tablets, string-and-knot records. Paper works, but ink dries to dust unnervingly fast and high-room air steals words. Physical, low, tactile notes are best.
* A hand-bell you can feel, not hear — a bowl, a resonant cup. Choir mice make hollow objects hum sympathetically; a good bowl will also pick up low Aeolian tones from the towers and the delayed vibration of events nearby.
* Eye protection for pale stone glare under a dark sky. Shadows are long, cool, and precise, and your eyes will keep misjudging distance because the stars stay visible at noon.
* All your food, dense and dry. Nothing green lives there. The dyers' vats are only colored dust that stains hands for weeks. The mineral felt that creeps in the wall joints is food for height-hares and excretes mortar in its sleep; it is not food for you, though dried it makes acceptable bedding stuffing.
* All your water, and this is the hard part.

**About water:**
The lintel says *Do not bring water up; it will try to return faster than you can.* This is not a ban, it's a caution about physics. There is no usable water in Vaith. Wells go down into cold air. Fountains are plugged with stone. Cisterns are dry, marked only by rings of tiny handprints in dust at different heights, as if something invisible once rose and fell there. Night rime feathers window ledges but it does not melt — it sublimates straight to nothing with a ticking in your teeth.

If you bring water (and you must, for any stay longer than a few hours), bring it in small, heavy, completely sealed vessels kept low in your pack and low in any room. Never pour it uphill. Never leave an open cup in an upper story. Drink downhill, sitting, mouth low. Travelers lose water not by spilling but by a sudden, eager downhill acceleration the moment a container is unsealed. Sip, seal, weight the vessel. Ration as if you cannot refill — you cannot.

Do not try to wash dyers' dust off with drinking water, though water does remove it instantly. It is a trap for your supply. Wear gloves in the dyers' quarter and accept stained hands.

### 3. Arrival and First Hours

The gate will be easy to find. It is always easy to find and always nearby. On day one this feels welcoming. By day two, understand it for what it is: Vaith lets you leave. It is less interested in letting you arrive anywhere else in particular, especially the far wall, which recedes in proportion to your certainty you will reach it.

Do not say the city's name often once above the tree line. In the valleys it's Vaith. Up here repeated names lose letters, and so do other repeated words. Use descriptions, point, write it once on your slate and show it. The same goes for your companions' names and your route words. Say them sparingly and low down.

On entering, choose a low, ground-floor house near the gate for your first base, even if grander towers beckon. Mark it not by its facade — houses exchange streets overnight without rubble, sound, or warning, and you may wake with your pumice front now by the dyers' pits — but by interior features, threshold wear, and a weighted personal object left *inside* on the floor. Sweep your own doorstep. Every fifth door in Vaith is swept already by a maintenance no one witnesses; joining that pattern seems to be considered polite and may keep your house from being tidied elsewhere while you are out.

Set a hard turn-back time before you explore. Time in Vaith is rung by shutter-openings changing the light, not by audible bells — the great ash bell could not sound in that air anyway. Your watch may keep time, but market day is every nine days and also, in the high market, continuously. You can shop there all morning among empty stalls and leave with arms aching from carrying nothing home. Do not enter the high market if you are already tired, hungry, or carrying anything you cannot afford to trade in gesture.

### 4. Moving Through the City

Walk together, in the same direction, tethered if visibility drops. Two travelers walking the same street in opposite directions will pass different buildings and later draw two accurate, incompatible maps. This is not a reason to make better maps. Make route records by breath-count, stair-count, and body-feel: "three steps up to door, courtyard above knees, ears popped twice." Keep them low in the city and consult them low.

All staircases go up, including those to cellars. Your calves will confirm this. Descending is a matter of climbing until the climbing folds and your inner ear lurches and you are under where you started, among stone roots growing down. If you meet yourself on a staircase, step aside and let yourself pass. Do not argue, do not touch, do not try to warn. The lintel is clear: you are higher than you think. Wait, breathe, go back down to the lowest landing and rest until your shadow returns.

Shadows detach. In the morning yours will keep up. By afternoon it may be half a street behind, flattened on a wall resting. It returns at dark as a small cold weight across your shoulders. This is normal, but plan delicate work for mornings when you and your shadow agree. Never stand in a tower's shadow while the tower itself stands in sun. Tower shadows sweep plazas on their own schedule. Locals — attested only by the upkeep of an empty city — considered it bad manners and bad luck, and what stands in that shadow for the duration is not exactly you.

If a street offers you an unexpected downhill, thank it and stay where you are for a while. Downhill is precious and sometimes offered as a lure upward. Sit. Drink a sealed sip. Let your ears settle.

### 5. Sleeping, Memory, and the Upper Rooms

Sleep low. Ground floors, mouth closed and covered, door weighted with a stone or your pack. Stone beds have a hip-hollow; felt stuffing holds the shape of the last sleeper a long while before taking yours. Do not be alarmed, but do not sleep in a bed that still sharply holds someone else's shape in a house that was warm — no, *less cold* — at the hearth with unburnt kindling laid. Choose another house. There are forty-house streets entirely empty.

Do not store anything important in upper stories, and do not linger there to think. Above a certain interior height, forgetting takes an address. You will leave the name of your street in a high room and find it later as a thickening in the air, not a memory. The tallest houses were finally given over to storing pauses, unfinished sentences, ends of songs. Opening a shutter up there can release a smell impossible at that altitude — woodsmoke, river mud, hair after rain — that floods downhill, devastating and gone. It can undo a day's emotional steadiness. If you need to remember something, write it physically and carry it downstairs against your skin. If you lose a word, go back low and breathe; trying to force it high only leaves more of the sentence behind.

Keep a evening ritual: at the lowest point of your lodging, aloud once (and only once), state your name, your gate-ward route by landmarks, and your intended leaving day. Then write it. Travelers who skip this begin routing their days by other people's thresholds.

### 6. Wildlife and Quiet Hazards

Height-hares — cat-sized, white, ears translucent pink — eat mineral felt and are sometimes eaten by the silence. You will find a hare-shaped scatter of fur with no tracks. If the already thin sound around you goes absolute, if even your close footsteps vanish before their delayed ground-arrival should be due, sit down against a wall, make yourself low, and press your ear and palms to stone. Do not run. Predation here is an acoustic event with no visible actor.

Moths come on still evenings, large mica flakes tapping at unlit windows to be let in. Do not let them in. Close shutters before dusk, weight them. They are not dangerous in the way a bite is dangerous; they are persistent in a way that erodes sleep and, eventually, resolve.

Choir mice live in wall cavities and never squeak. When a colony sings, bowls hum. If every hollow object in your kitchen begins to harmonize, it is a season, or weather, not a haunting. It is a good night to sleep low and not climb.

### 7. How Long to Stay

Nothing prevents you from staying. Houses are sound. Kindling is laid. This is the trap.

Set your leave-date in the valley, before the thinning, and keep the token of it — a knotted cord cut one knot per morning at ground level — where you sleep. Plan for two to three days at most on a first visit. Each morning, ask: Can I find the gate? (Yes, always, which proves nothing.) Can I say, without checking my slate, why I came and what street I slept on? Can I stand in morning light with my shadow under me? If memory requires a climb to retrieve, if your house has moved twice, if you have begun to find the swept empty markets restful rather than sad, go down that day, following a descending route by breath even if it looks longer.

Descend slowly. Your delayed footsteps will start arriving sooner. Smells will begin to travel again. Color will return before warmth does.

Vaith rises a few feet every night, gaining height it will never admit to. Visit it as a guest in a place that maintains itself for inhabitants who may be the city itself, be brief, be low, be weighted, and be quiet — not because silence is enforced, but because up there, everything you say, breathe, and forget stays somewhere, waiting at altitude for you to come back up and find it.
