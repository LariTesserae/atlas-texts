This is not a place a human is built to visit. That is the first and most important piece of preparation: understanding that preparation will not make you native to it, only less catastrophic.

The city is under a raft of living peat in a cold marsh, in late autumn/winter. The room itself is maybe the size of a small bedroom in footprint, and in most places knee-high or lower. It is inhabited, intensively, by voles, shrews, mice, frogs, salamanders, snails, beetles, moths, springtails, woodlice, centipedes, fungus, and growing roots — several thousand individuals, all at a scale where you, at full size, are not a person. You are weather. You are a warm, breathing geological event that blocks lanes, depletes air, attracts roots, and empties neighborhoods ahead of you.

An ordinary human multiverse traveler has two sane options, and one dangerous one.

**1. The Macro Visit (strongly recommended):** You stay human-sized, on top of the mat, and you visit by listening, feeling, and using instruments through the entrance slit.

**2. The Belly Visit:** You stay human-sized and get your head/shoulders/arm an inch into the entrance for a few minutes. You will see almost nothing. That is correct.

**3. The Shrunken Visit:** If your multiverse transit method allows reliable, reversible size reduction to 5-12 cm body length, you can enter as fauna. This is the only way to actually *be* in the city, and it turns almost every animal there into a climate, a laborer, or a plausible predator of you.

Prepare differently for each, but the base preparation is the same.

### 1. Timing: Go in Deep Winter, Leave Long Before Spring

Do not attempt this in summer. In summer there is no city. Do not attempt it during formation in early autumn, when the peat raft is lifting and has not yet locked.

The window is: after hard ice has sealed the edges of the mat down, after the voles have finished the main runways and storehouses — roughly November onward in a northern temperate marsh — and well before the first real thaw pulse.

The end is not negotiable. When the ice loosens, the mat drops or tears and that low room floods black to the ceiling in an afternoon. Everything portable there is bundled, everything on stilts also floats, and there are exactly three exit tunnels angled up to hummocks for animals a few inches long. There is no human exit strategy in a flood. The rill in the center of the floor, normally two fingers wide, is the early warning: if it stops running black-and-clear and starts rising steadily, if drips from the ceiling become a sheet, if you hear ice creaking in long, settling groans rather than ticks — abort, even if you are only on the surface. The mat can sag under your weight and open early.

Check weather 72, 24, and 2 hours out: you want stable cold, -2 to -10C above, low wind. Wind presses the ice, flexes the mat down, changes the drip rhythm, raises the rill, and drives the whole population into the communal hollow. You will see an empty, beautiful ghost town and you will have stressed it for nothing. A sharp deepening cold does the same.

Go in the long dusk or full dark. There is no day inside anyway, and surface predators — owls, foxes — listen through the ice. Any vibration you make in daylight carries further socially, if not physically.

### 2. Body Preparation: Cold, Wet, Prone, Blind

Forget hiking fitness. Train for this:

**Prone stillness.** You will not stand, kneel upright, sit, or crawl normally. At human size you will lie flat on a load-spreading board on the ice/peat and inch forward. At shrunken size you will be on your belly almost the whole time. Practice lying motionless on a cold, damp floor for 20-40 minutes while breathing slowly. Your back, elbows, ribs and chin will go numb first. Then deeper cold sets in. The inside air is only 1-2C above freezing, but it's saturated, still, and condensing on everything. It is *warm* for them because it isn't freezing. For you it will leach heat faster than dry air at -10C because you stay wet.

Eat a hot, fatty meal 1-2 hours before. No alcohol — it destroys your cold judgment and makes your breath and skin smell louder. Empty your bowels and bladder before entry; there is nowhere to go inside that isn't someone's carefully capped latrine, downstream drinking rill, or fungus garden. Holding it while prone in cold wet will end your visit anyway.

**Darkness training.** You have to go in blind. This is literal. White light will scatter moths from storehouse roofs (they ARE the roof insulation), blind nocturnal eyes, stop all traffic, and possibly trigger a weasel-check response from the shrews. Even red light is a hot, alien sun at that scale. Spend several nights practicing navigating a room by touch, sound, and air movement on skin with eyes closed. Learn to distinguish: drip-counts, a root tapped twice as a signal, rustle-pause-rustle in a runway, ice shifting far above, your own heartbeat lying to you about sounds.

If you are macro-visiting, let eyes adapt outside for 30 minutes, then don't ruin them. The only visible glow inside is a faint green phosphorescence on wettest roots and rotting reed. It is enough to separate edges. It is not enough to see by. Trying will give you eyestrain and illusions.

**Breath and heat discipline.** Inside, air is thin — not unbreathable, but already breathed by hundreds of warm bodies, steaming nesting material, fungus, and composting peat. Your exhalation is a warm, CO2-heavy plume. It condenses, it calls root hairs to grow toward you (they grow fastest toward animal warmth; sleeping animals wake up netted and have to chew free), and it announces you for meters, which at this scale is city blocks.

Practice slow nasal breathing, low volume, exhaling downward toward cold mud, with long still pauses. If you are panting, talking, coughing, or laughing, you are done. At shrunken size, your human surface-area-to-volume disaster means you will lose heat catastrophically AND, paradoxically, your metabolic heat relative to a vole will still make you a beacon if you're overdressed. You'll need whatever thermal regulation your shrink-tech provides; test it beforehand in a wet 2C chamber.

**Scent.** This city runs on scent posts — tiny smears on root posts at lane crossings so it can be full without being crowded. To them, you will occupy the whole room by smell alone. Shower the night before with unscented soap, wear clothes washed without detergent perfume and aired outdoors in cold marsh air if possible. No deodorant, insect repellent, sunscreen, lip balm, hand cream, tobacco, cannabis, strong food (garlic, coffee breath is loud), leather treated with oils, or new synthetic gear offgassing. At shrunken scale, even your normal skin microbes are a foreign introduction. A full decontamination protocol — clean body, clean neutral suit, breath through a cold-trapping mask on approach — is not fussiness, it protects their fungus gardens.

### 3. Clothing and Gear: Nothing Hard, Bright, Rustling, or Flammable

No fire is absolute. No metal except what they already have (a bottle cap, a bent safety pin the size of a gate) — meaning don't introduce new metal scent/taste/electrical weirdness. Zippers, crampons, carabiners, trekking poles: leave them above.

**For a macro visit, wear / bring:**
- A broad, flat load-spreading platform: snowshoes plus a lightweight prone board / bog sled, at least body length. Distribute weight or you will press the mat down, flood lanes, and crush runways. Never concentrate weight on knees.
- Waterproof, matte, dark outer layer, silent fabric (wool, brushed synthetics; no hardshell swish). One-piece prone suit is ideal. Sealed cuffs. You will be lying in peat water trickling black from the slit.
- Thin wool liners under waterproof mittens you can remove for tactile work. Bare fingers read temperature and vibration better, but go numb in minutes. Alternate.
- Kneepads/elbow pads built *inside* clothing, soft. External hard plastic clicks.
- Headlamp you will *not* use inside — only for safe egress across marsh after you are 50m away, on lowest red, shielded down. Inside, carry nothing that accidentally turns on. Tape switches.
- Non-electronic backup and passive instruments first: a flexible borescope/endoscope with no light or only extremely dim, cold, green-filtered light used for seconds at a time; a hydrophone / contact microphone on a soft stem to listen to root taps and the rill falling at the far end; a thin thermometer probe (the borrowed warmth gradient matters — if it's freezing at floor level, animals are in emergency huddle).
- Thermos, high-energy food, dry change of clothes, and a bivvy left at a cache on solid hummock *off* the raft. If you get wet through — and you will — hypothermia on the walk out is the likeliest way this trip injures you, not anything inside.
- No tripod legs poked into peat. No stakes. No flagging tape.

**For a shrunken visit, add / replace:**
Scale everything down and reconsider whether you have any business doing it. Your suit must be gnaw-weak: you *want* to be able to chew or tear free if roots net you while resting, just like they do. No indestructible sealed armor that traps you. You need whisker-proxies: a set of long, flexible tactile filaments on hood/collar and gloves, because human vision and even touch are too coarse. You navigate by whisker-read: lane width, scent post posts, root hair curtains that have grown across a runway overnight.

Carry no backpack larger than a seed husk relative scale. Anything bulky makes you block covered runways (vole-height, kinked around wet spots, with passing places). If you don't fit in single file, you don't go in that tunnel.

Bring no food. Their stores are insultingly neat — sedge seed separate from rush seed separate from pale corms — and eating them is theft of winter survival, plus a disease vector. Bring no water container to fill at the bottle-cap cistern. That bright bead-by-bead drip is communal infrastructure and a vault.

Your emergency kit at that size is: a pre-memorized map of the three exit tunnels, an exit timer set by vibration not sound, and a recall / re-enlargement method that does NOT require open space above you, fire, light burst, or noise, and that will not happen accidentally inside the communal hollow. Test what growing back to human size does to a peat raft in a simulation tank. The answer is: it destroys the city and drowns you in black peat. Your failsafe must relocate you to the surface *before* restoring size.

### 4. Health, Biosecurity, and Inoculation

Peat water is acidic, tannin-black, and full of life. At human size: treat any cut as contaminated, cover skin, don't ingest, don't rub eyes after touching the slit (sharp pale root tips will scratch). Up to date tetanus, consider leptospirosis and tularemia risk briefings for marsh mammal environments in your world, wash thoroughly after, watch for fever. Fungal spores in the warm side chamber are farmed there; at shrunken size inhaling that air for hours is a lung exposure no human immune system evolved for. A filtered micro-respirator that doesn't heat your breath might be mandatory.

Reverse biosecurity matters more: clean boots, board, probes between worlds/marshes. No hitchhiking seeds in cuffs, no insects in gear seams, no soil from another wetland. This city farms fungus with beetles that are harvested by shrews fed by vole droppings fed by the rill's rain of drowned seeds. Introducing a new mite, mold, or seed can collapse their only warm food system before spring.

Quarantine after a shrunken visit before contact with other small mammals in any world. You will be carrying scent that says "vole city" and real microbes in hair and gut.

Medical disqualifiers: claustrophobia, panic in total darkness, asthma triggered by cold damp air / mold, Reynaud's, uncontrolled diabetes with reduced sensation in hands/feet, allergy to insect bites at scale (at 8 cm, a centipede bite and a shrew attack are not trivial — shrews have needle teeth, high metabolism, and police the edges with hunger; they will treat a soft, unfamiliar, warm animal as prey or intruder), and any condition requiring periodic loud medical devices.

### 5. Approach and Entry Protocol

Approach across ice, not open pools. Test ice away from the raft. Move only when wind gusts mask small sounds, then freeze when it drops. Speak not at all within 100m; voice carries up through the mat to owls and foxes, and down into it as vibration.

At the mat edge, get prone on your board before your weight is on the living raft. The mat is sedge rhizome, moss, tangled root — living on top, dead and golden below. It is maybe a hand's thickness holding a room open. A heel or knee can tear it.

The entrance is a slit where mat tore from a hummock, sharp with snapped roots smelling green. Black water weeps out. Do not widen it. Do not trim roots. Do not clear ice from it with warm hands. If you are macro, this slit is your window: place a contact mic near (not in) it, lay your cheek (covered) close enough to feel the 1-2 degree breath of warmer air, and wait.

Wait at least 20 minutes. First you will think it's empty. That is the evacuation effect of your arrival. Runways near you empty. The harvest mouse leaves her bottle-cap lookout. Scent posts get refreshed behind you. Only after cold has gone from skin to deeper bone, and you have been still as a root, will roofs start moving again — moth wings resettling, beetles breathing under bark, a knot in root bark turning a damp gold eye toward you.

If you insert a borescope, move at root-growth speed: millimeters, then stop. Never push through a root hair curtain; that's a door someone will have to gnaw in the morning. Never dam or divert the rill. All drainage leads to it, side drains come in at angles lined with shell fragments; blocking it floods houses from below.

At shrunken size, enter in single file during an active shift — not during a cold-tightening when outer city is intentionally empty and sealed. Follow a covered runway, stay in margins, do not cross the wide muddy plazas quickly; they are kept clear because ground moves and because open space is exposure. Assume every lane crossing has a scent post post. Do not rub against, mark over, or urinate anywhere except — well, don't. Hold it. Territories are maintained by smell; adding yours reorganizes the city around you and forces costly re-marking and avoidance lanes. They will literally chew new lanes to zone you out to the edge.

Do not touch: storehouses on sedge stilts (they tilt when roots embrace them, they are delicately balanced), communal hollow entrance (polished shining by one season of feet, banked deep with shredded leaf/fur/feather/seed-down steaming inside — that is their winter survival core; your opening it dumps heat), frog cells in mud floor (each sealed in slime gone glassy, throat pulsing rarely; stepping on one kills it), snails glued across house mouths as doors (they are not decorations), fungus chamber (warmest room, pale sheets on walls tended by beetles). Observe from the empty margin of mud each structure keeps around itself. That margin is intentional.

Do not tap roots except in genuine emergency — twice is a signal in their system. Random tapping is false language.

Tools you may see — thorn awls, bone splinters, mussel shell scrapers, grass rope tight enough to cut skin, leaf drag sleds, the safety pin half-buried like a plow — leave exactly as left. No souvenir seed, husk, shell, or moth wing. Photography with flash is violence; even passive image capture at macro needs to be done via borescope with minimal light, and you'd better be certain your device does not warm the air pocket locally.

### 6. Duration, Exit, and What Can Go Wrong

Limit macro close-contact to 45-90 minutes, shrunken to far less — set by your air/heat, not curiosity. Your heat calls roots, your breath steals air, your presence empties foraging. The ethical visit is the one that is boring for most of its length.

Signs to leave immediately: rill rising against banks, drip rhythm changing without wind easing, mat flex creaking downward, population suddenly all drawing toward communal hollow/fungal chamber, shrew commotion in multiple tunnels, weasel smell (musky) or absolute silence near entrance false-tunnel district, your own shivering stops, or you realize a new root curtain has grown across your intended exit path. At shrunken size, getting gently netted is normal while sleeping; while awake and exiting it can trap a panicking human. Cut yourself free minimally, leave cut hairs as lining (they do), do not tear a structural root.

Exit backwards the way you came, never turn around in a runway. Back out through the slit slowly so you don't pump air/water. At surface, move off the raft before standing, before light, before speaking, before checking gear.

On solid ground, do a silent inventory from a distance with eyes: is the slit weeping normally? Are pools still skinned? Did your board leave a depression that will channel meltwater in? Fill/divert nothing, but if you depressed the mat, note it and do not return this season; your track is now part of their spring flood map.

### 7. Mental Preparation

Prepare to be politely ignored and zoned out. Prepare to feel cathedral-scale wonder at a bridge being rebuilt stick-by-stick 2 feet to the left while traffic still uses the sagging old one, at drainage canals lined with shell, at a bottle cap as cistern-vault. Prepare to feel poverty, then realize it is spacing against moving ground. Prepare for the horror of scale: more individuals in that room than on acres above, all keeping distance in darkness they read by whisker.

Prepare also for leaving without having 'met' anyone. Contact, at human size, would be a disaster response for them. Success is: they resumed. A root tapped twice somewhere you didn't cause. A storehouse roof rearranged its wings. Lanes filled behind you.

Most ordinary travelers should do this as a surface listening pilgrimage: winter marsh, prone board, eyes closed, contact mic, hours of drip-count and shifting ice, knowing a metropolitan intention is being rebuilt at exactly the speed it is being grown over a hand's breadth beneath you. That restraint isn't a lesser visit. In that place, it is how a huge animal shows it understands civic life.

If you cannot go blind, stay still, stay silent, stay wet-cold, and leave early — don't go under.
