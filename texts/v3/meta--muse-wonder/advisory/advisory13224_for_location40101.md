The Pan is not dangerous the way a cliff or a river is dangerous. It will not chase you, bite you in any serious way, or collapse under you. It is dangerous because it is a complete, closed, finely balanced system with no empty place in it for a human being — and if you step into it unprepared, you will become weather, food, water, and an obstacle in the current, all at once.

Prepare on that assumption: you are not going to conquer, cross, camp in, or study it for hours. You are going to descend a few steps, stand at the coolest edge for a short time, be thoroughly investigated by things that have never seen daylight, and leave before the place has to rearrange itself around you too much.

### 1. Understand what you are entering

It is a roofed bowl, 30-40 paces across, high in thin, cold desert, but hot inside from geothermal vents in the center. The floor is not floor. It is millions of blind crickets, millipedes, beetles, soft subterranean worm-things, and their predators, packed shell-to-shell several bodies deep over a springy mat of compacted molt. Under them are bacterial mats growing on the fumaroles — that is the meadow everything else ultimately eats.

There is almost no water. One condensation stain, an inch wide, high on the back wall. Everything else survives on recycling each other and on moisture. Your breath, your sweat, and the salt on your skin will not be a curiosity to them. It will be rain.

The whole carpet slowly spirals: cool at the edges, dangerously hot at the center, constantly rotating so individuals at the bottom are lifted and carried out to cool. Do not break that rotation. It is how they keep from cooking in their own pile.

And periodically, everything freezes at once, for 5-20 seconds, for no reason you will perceive. Then it resumes mid-step. This will happen while you are there. Expect it.

### 2. Who should not go

Do not go if you have asthma, chronic bronchitis, sulfur / acid-gas sensitivity, a severe dust or shellfish/chitin allergy, altitude sickness history that hits you hard, claustrophobia in low dark spaces, or a panic response to insects crawling on you. They *will* be in your cuffs, on your boots, tasting your ankles, gentle and relentless. You cannot keep them off completely. If you need to be able to keep them off, stay at the rim.

Do not go alone, and do not take children or a large group. One person in the Pan at a time is best, two at an absolute maximum. One companion stays above by the last cairn, in the sun, with water and a watch. Agree on a hard exit time before you descend — 10 minutes is generous, 15 is pushing it — and have your companion call you out. You will lose track of time inside. The darkness does not change, sound is swallowed, and your own pulse gets absurdly loud.

Mobility matters on the approach and the cut steps, which are chipped, uneven, and polished. If you cannot climb back up quickly under your own power while hot, headachy, and being crawled on, do not go down.

### 3. Days before: acclimatize and decontaminate

This is multiverse travel housekeeping made literal: the Pan lives on bacterial films and ten thousand generations of its own molt. A seed in your tread, a microbe from another world, a trace of insecticide on your boots, could be a plague or a feast it has no defense against.

* Spend at least 1-2 nights at altitude nearby if you can. The trail is high desert — thin air, white sun, cold wind outside the rim. You will already be breathing hard when the sulfurous air gives you a headache behind the eyes. Altitude sick + sulfur sick + heat is how people sit down in the wrong place.
* Use dedicated, cleaned gear. Boots scrubbed, soles picked clean, clothes washed without scented detergent, perfume, or insect repellent, and dried completely. Absolutely no DEET, permethrin, flea powder, antibacterial sprays, or hand sanitizer on anything that goes below the rim. You would be poisoning the grazing layer.
* Do the same in reverse for when you leave. Plan a quarantine set: a bag for the boots and gaiters you wore inside, a change of clothes waiting at the cairn. Nothing from the Pan gets carried to another valley, let alone another universe.

Eat and hydrate fully the night before and the morning of, but plan to arrive neither sloshing nor starving. You will not eat, drink, urinate, or spit inside. Human waste there is a salt-and-water bomb.

If you wear contacts, leave them out. Sulfur and chitin dust under a lens in a place where you cannot rinse with clean water is miserable. Wear plain glasses, secured with a strap.

### 4. What to wear and carry

Inside is dark, hot at leg level, slick with life, and abrasive with sulfur crystal. Outside is freezing wind and brutal glare. Dress for a seal, not for comfort.

**Wear:**
- Smooth, tightly woven synthetic trousers and long sleeves — nothing fleecy, woolly, fringed, or with open mesh that small legs can grip and hide in.
- High boots with smooth uppers, laces tucked in.
- Gaiters or, better, trouser cuffs taped/sealed to the boots with a smooth band. They will still find a way in if there is any gap. A seal buys you minutes, not immunity.
- A close-fitting cap or buff covering all hair. Cover ears.
- Light gloves with sealed wrists if you think you might put a hand down. Ideally you never put a hand down.
- A respirator or well-fitted mask rated for acid gases / fine particulates if you are sensitive at all. It does two jobs: it cuts the sulfur and molt dust you inhale in a low bowl where heavy gases can pool, and it reduces the plume of moist breath that draws the carpet up your body. Exhale slowly, downward. Do not pant.
- No sunscreen, lotion, lip balm, or scented anything applied fresh at the trailhead. If you need sun protection for the trail, apply it well before, let it absorb fully, and cover it with sleeves.

**Carry (minimal):**
- A low, red-filtered headlamp, already on the dimmest setting, pointed down. Do not bring a white spotlight or flash photography. Your eyes need 3-5 minutes to adjust; a blast of light just blinds you and startles nothing usefully. Most of what there is to see is felt and heard.
- A simple timer/watch you can feel vibrate.
- If you have access to one, a pocket CO2 / H2S detector. A sunken, roofed, venting bowl is exactly the geometry where heavier-than-air gases collect. If it alarms, if you feel suddenly dizzy, euphoric, nauseated, or your headache spikes — leave immediately, moving steadily, not running. Tell your companion above to not come down after you unless roped and briefed; two collapsed people in the bowl helps no one.
- Nothing else. No backpack to set down (there is nowhere to set it that is not living), no food, no water bottle, no collecting vials, no notebook to drop. Empty pockets. Secure everything.

Leave water, food, first aid, and your change of clothes with your companion at the last cairn and that shallow polished stone bowl. Do not fill that bowl. Do not leave offerings of water or food in it or anywhere else. A gift of water is an ecological disaster here.

### 5. The approach

You will smell it long before you see it: sulfur, hot stone, and a dense, faintly sweet animal smell like a closed terrarium magnified a million times.

The trail is red pumice and basalt plates that ring underfoot, no grass, no scrub, cairns and three low unmortared windbreaks. Pace yourself. Thin air lies. Arrive at the rim un-exhausted, hat on against the sun, and rest *outside*, in the wind, until your breathing is normal. Hydrate here, use the toilet far from and well below the bowl if you must, then seal up: cuffs, gloves, mask, hair, lamp.

Look from the rim first. Note where the steps go down, where the failed concentric stone rings are at the coolest edge near the steps — that is your standing place, and your limit — and where the three low vent cones darken the center. You never go toward the center. At the center the floor temperature would cook meat, and the rotation will be trying to carry anything standing still toward warmer currents.

### 6. Inside: how to behave

Descend slowly, one step at a time, hand on the wall. You go from white glare to a darkness that does not lift at noon. Stop on the last step. Wait. Let eyes adjust, then ears. You will hear your clothes scratch, and an answering scratch below.

Step only into the rings at the edge, with a slow, shuffling, flat-footed placement. Do not stomp, stride, or try to scatter them clear. There is nowhere to scatter to. The surface parts in a reluctant ripple, closes around your ankle, and begins to climb. This is normal. A calm, slow step crushes far fewer than a panicked kick.

Once standing:
- Stay at the edge. Plant your feet wide, knees soft. The floor moves under you in an almost imperceptible spiral. Do not fight it with constant correction; let it nudge you, replant deliberately, and always work your way *outward* to the wall, never inward.
- Keep hands up and in, elbows tucked. Do not touch the condensation stain on the back wall, the sulfur mats (sulfur-yellow, bone-white, iron-orange slick crusts on the stones), the ceiling clusters that hang leg-to-leg like grapes, or the centipedes. The hand-long, wet-ash-colored centipedes ride on top and take crickets without breaking the rotation. They are not interested in you unless you grab, trap, or corner one against your skin.
- Breathe slow and shallow through your mask. Speak little, in a low voice if you must — not because they fear sound, but because every exhalation is moisture. Do not sing, laugh hard, or sigh over the floor.
- Do not shine light on the mats for long, pick at molt, lift a stone, or try to see the pale worm-things underneath by digging. They surface on their own sometimes. That is enough.
- Watch the edges of your vision for heat shimmer over the vents and for pulses — the stone breathes heat in waves. If a pulse makes the air at your calves suddenly hotter, step back to the wall or up a step until it passes.

**When the freeze comes:** Freeze with them. Do not use it to hurry, cheat position, shake things off, or step forward. Antennae will stop mid-wave, centipedes flatten, silence will become total except for rock ticking with heat. It feels, irrationally, like being caught looking. Stand still, count it out, and when motion resumes mid-step, resume with it. Breaking the pause is the surest way to lose your footing when a million legs restart at once under you.

Signs to leave *now*, no debate: headache sharpening behind the eyes, dizziness, metallic taste, eyes watering despite glasses, nausea, feeling the heat climb past your knees toward your waist, carpet reaching mid-shin in a dense tower up your legs, your timer going off, or your companion calling. There is no final sight worth one more minute.

### 7. Getting out and not taking it with you

Climb the steps steadily, without brushing against the walls more than you must. They will follow you a surprising distance into the light, slowing as they cool. Do not swat them off in the bowl, and do not carry them purposefully to the trail.

At the top, on the red pumice well away from the steps, stop in full sun and wind. Let them drop off on their own, one by one. Help only by slow, gentle shaking of cuffs over bare stone where a fallen cricket can, if it is still warm enough, make its own way back toward shade. Check: boot tops, gaiter folds, glove wrists, collar, hair cover, mask strap. Check your companion visually and have them check you — you cannot feel the smallest ones once you are numb to crawling.

Then change. Bag boots and inner layers without shaking them out into the wind. Wash hands and face with water *your companion brought*, well away from the rim, and do not pour wash water anywhere uphill or near the bowl. Pack out everything.

Back at camp/lodging: shower, wash hair, clean under nails. Air and sunlight will kill most stowaways; thorough scrubbing and a hot wash for clothes, and a dedicated cleaning/freezing (per your traveler's protocol) for boots before they ever visit another sensitive site. Monitor yourself for 24 hours: persistent headache, cough, wheeze, eye irritation, or a rash where cuffs sealed is worth medical attention, and mention sulfur/chitin-dust exposure at altitude.

### 8. The attitude that keeps you safe

Treat the Pan not as an animal exhibit but as a finished place in equilibrium — mountain, heat, bacteria, hunger. The rings someone built to stand in failed by subsiding and filling with crickets. The vents have not moved. Your job is to be a brief, boring, dry shadow at the very edge of the current: arrive clean, stand still, do not feed it your water, do not steal its water, do not carry it away, and leave while you still can climb out cleanly.

If you do that, what you will remember is not a swarm. It is a surface that thinks with its whole body — towers forming and dissolving a pace away, a spiral turning under your soles, silence swallowing a falling stone — and then, behind you as you regain the brutal white trail, the floor closing over the exact place where you stood, and keeping turning as if you had never been granted permission at all.
