This is not a lake you swim in. It's a drowned continent you *visit*, the way you would visit deep space or a cave system under ice — briefly, on life support, with a way back already rigged before you get wet.

Think of it as three places stacked together:

1.  **A 40-50 meter deep body of near-freezing water** with a solid or half-solid ceiling.
2.  **A twilight, bottomless-feeling garden** with no animals in it at all.
3.  **An archaeological site the size of a country**, built in rectangles, that will confuse you, entangle you, and silt itself over behind you.

An ordinary human can go there. You just cannot go there casually, unaccompanied, or for very long.

### 1. The Core Problems

**You cannot breathe, stay warm, or surface freely.**

At 50 meters you are at about 6 atmospheres of pressure. The water is ~4°C year-round — the temperature where water is densest. That's not "cold swimming" cold. Unprotected skin goes pain, then numb, then useless. A wetsuit is not enough for more than a very short dip. Core temperature will leave you steadily, and it will not come back until you are out.

Light dies in the first 10 meters. Below that is a permanent blue dusk at best — enough at noon, in clear weeks, to see a wall a few meters ahead — and absolute black at night or during a summer algal bloom. You will be diving in overhead darkness under ice or under open black water far from shore. If you lose your entry hole, you do not "just surface."

At that depth, ordinary air also becomes a problem: nitrogen narcosis (you will feel drunk, confident, and stupid in a place that is *all* identical rectangular fields), oxygen toxicity on long exposures, and decompression sickness on ascent. Bottom time will be measured in tens of minutes unless you bring serious mixed-gas and decompression planning, or a rebreather, or a vehicle.

Prepare as if for ice diving + deep wreck diving + cave diving combined, because it is all three.

### 2. Training and Body Prep

Do not make this your first cold-water dive.

You need, in advance:

- Deep, dry-suit, and ice/overhead-environment training, practiced until buoyancy and emergency drills are automatic with thick gloves on. You will have almost no dexterity down there.
- Excellent buoyancy control. The floor is silt a meter to three meters deep over pavement. One bad fin kick raises a mud weather-front that can white-out — actually brown-out — a whole field for weeks, erases your own tracks, and blinds you and everyone after you. Hover, don't walk if you can avoid it, and never kneel casually.
- A medical screen for heart, lungs, ears, and circulation. Cold shock on entry, pressure at depth, and heavy gear on ice are a bad combination for an unfit traveler. Practice clearing your ears; canals and deeper cuts will add little pressure changes on top of the big one.
- Rewarming practice. You will be clumsy, cognitively slow, and ravenous after. Your surface team needs hot drinks, dry insulation, wind shelter, and a plan to get you out of a suit you cannot unzip yourself.

Get used to silence in training, too. There are no fish, no insects, no birds under the ice, no choruses, no splashes but your own. Sound is muffled instantly except for rare, far-carrying knocks of waterlogged wood on stone and, in winter, a low groan of surface ice you feel in your teeth. Divers unused to it find the completeness of the quiet either meditative or panicking.

### 3. Gear: What to Bring

**Life support and warmth:**
- Dry suit with heated undergarments and battery redundancy. Thick dry gloves. A hood that seals well. Everything tested in 4°C water before you go — batteries lose capacity badly in the cold.
- Redundant breathing system. Twin cylinders or a rebreather rated for cold, plus a bailout bottle. Regulators must be cold-water sealed; free-flows are common in near-freezing water.
- Mixed gas if you plan to do anything but a brief look at 50m, and staged decompression gas, computers *and* written tables. Assume your electronics are lying a little and your fingers are too numb to fix settings.
- A small DPV (underwater scooter) is worth considering, not for speed — currents are walking pace or less and landmarks crawl past — but to conserve gas and heat over causeways that run straight for days. On foot/swimming, your explorable radius from the hole will be cruelly small.

If your multiverse transit affords it, an atmospheric diving suit or a small, cold-rated submersible is honestly the saner "ordinary human" option for anything beyond a 30-minute survey. It removes decompression, narcosis, and most hypothermia at a stroke, and lets you actually *look* at the place.

**Light, navigation, and tethering:**
- At least three lights: primary, backup, and a strobe/marker for the hole. The dusk fools you — walls look close and are far, kelp stands look like walls until they move minutes after the current pushes them.
- A continuous guideline from the entry hole to your work site, tied off to causeway edges or gate posts (gently, without scraping algae and moss). In a silt-out, you follow the line by touch, not by sight.
- Compass, depth/time instruments, and ideally sonar/inertial navigation. GPS does not work under 50m of water. The landscape is rectangles within rectangles, green inside grey, repeating until repetition *is* the landscape. Without markers, every terrace, paddy wall, stair-to-nowhere, and roofless stone building looks like the last one.
- Non-permanent markers, slates, and a camera with its own lights if you want records. Inscriptions on gate-posts and canal markers are already softened past reading; do not brush hard, scrape, or take rubbings that tear the crust.
- A cutting tool reachable with either hand. No animals will bite you. Kelp will not attack you. But 20-meter black kelp forests close behind you, eelgrass brushes your whole body over meadows, and ungrazed dead vegetation forms half-rotted mats a meter thick. Entanglement in near-dark, with numb hands, is your most likely mundane emergency.

**Surface kit:**
- A tended entry: in winter, a cut and marked ice hole with ladders, non-slip matting, a safety diver on standby, and lines kept clear of grinding ice plates; in summer, a stable boat/platform in open, rain-netted black water with the hole marked so you can find it from below in near-night.
- A surface team who never all leave, who track your gas, time, and line, and who can haul an incapacitated diver. Communications hard-wired into the guideline or through-water comms tested in advance.
- Everything you need to eat and drink. There is *nothing* edible for you down there in any practical sense. The plants are cold- and dark-adapted species sifted down over ages to tolerate their own rot; assume they are indigestible at best. There is no fish, snail, worm, or beetle — none. Bring all calories to the surface camp.

Don't count on fire below. Don't count on metal behaving long-term if you cache gear down there; it films and flakes. Stone, ceramic, and waterlogged wood endure. Assume anything dropped falls straight, stays for centuries, and is buried by millimeters of white algal snow and silt. Don't drop things.

### 4. How a Visit Actually Goes

**Choose a tiny target.** You will not "explore the basin." It runs league after league past any lifetime of swimming. Pick one district near your entry — a causeway-platform cluster, a stretch of canal with dressed stone banks, a set of terraces — and make that the dive. The central plaza, a huge paved circle of larger stones with a double row of standing blocks, no kelp, only short stonewort turf and the clearest, coldest water in the basin, is a legitimate pilgrimage target but it is at the lowest point and will cost you the most depth, time, and decompression. Work up to it over multiple dives, if at all.

**Time your season.**
- *Winter:* Ice gives you a stable surface camp and sometimes clearer water, but you are under a ceiling, listening to it groan. Entry/exit is fixed and unforgiving.
- *Summer:* Open water is easier to exit, but green algae bloom in the upper layers, thicken the dark below to near-night, then die and snow down white for days, settling on everything. Expect worse visibility mid-summer.
- *Clear windows* of a month or so happen unpredictably when currents settle. Locals — if you have surface tenders used to this world — will know by sampling. A mud-front can also roll in for weeks and cloud a whole province; if it arrives, abort surveying and navigate by line and touch, or wait it out on the surface.

Descend slowly along the line, equalizing, checking gloves and heating. At about 10 meters the world goes dusk-blue. Below that, move slower than you think you need to: kelp fronds answer a push minutes late, so you can be inside a forest before it closes.

Stay off the meadow floors when possible; skim above eelgrass, follow causeways — slightly raised, paved, flanked by ditches, now undulating and cracked green along their fractures — because they are your roads and your handrails across districts. Expect buildings to be roofless shells filled with silt and grass, lintels fallen in. Do not enter interiors: roofs of black, heavy, preserved wood shift without warning, silt is deep, and a single knock echoing in the silence tells you the structure is still settling.

Keep a running mental (and slate) account: time down, gas left, distance from line, temperature sense in hands and feet. Numbness sneaks into clumsiness, clumsiness into entanglement and silt-outs. Turn the dive *before* you feel you must.

Ascend on schedule, decompress patiently in the cold — this is when divers get chilled worst, hanging still — and keep the hole strobe in sight. On the surface, get out of wind, out of the suit, into dry insulation, hot sweet drinks, and food handled by someone else.

### 5. Rules of Thumb Down There

- **Never dive alone, never dive untethered, never dive without a tended hole.**
- **No touching for stability.** Walls, stairs, pavement, and standing blocks are furred with moss and liverwort and capped with algae crust; a glove can erase a century of growth and a peck-marked dressing pattern thousands of hammer blows in the making.
- **Leave no trace that lasts.** Your fin wash will be erased by silt; your dropped torch, cut line, or pried stone will not. Pack out everything, including human waste from the surface camp — there are no decomposer animals here to process novelty, and decay is geological in pace.
- **Don't eat, drink, or sample casually.** Surface samples smell of green things, stone, and cold mud, nothing else — but bottom layers are oxygen-poor enough to preserve wood indefinitely. Avoid stirring deep hollows and thick rot mats; they can release stagnant, low-oxygen water and reduce your visibility to zero.
- **Expect to feel small and a little unmoored.** There are no mouths in this loop. Plants grow, shade themselves out, collapse, rot almost imperceptibly, and feed the next planting. You are the only animal you will meet. That fact organizes the silence, and for many travelers it is the real hazard after the cold.

### 6. Emergency Priorities

1. **Entangled or silt-blinded:** Stop. Breathe. Signal on the line. Cut only vegetation, not your guideline. Wait for silt to settle rather than thrashing it thicker.
2. **Free-flow, light failure, heater failure:** Switch to backup, abort on the line. Cold failures cascade — one dead heater is a dive-ender at 4°C.
3. **Lost line:** Stop moving, deploy backup light in slow sweeps for kelp vs. stone, check compass heading to causeway/canal (linear features are your friends), and conserve gas while your buddy/surface follows protocol. Do not do a free ascent under ice or far from the marked hole.
4. **Missed decompression, rapid ascent, numbness/tingling/joint pain after:** Surface oxygen immediately, keep warm but not overheated, evacuate to recompression. Your multiverse exit should be planned so medical care is one transit away *before* you dive, not figured out after.

Prepare heavily, dive briefly, look carefully. If you do it right, what you get is not adventure-sport thrills but a very specific, haunting privilege: floating at noon in blue permanent dusk over hand-dressed pavement and waist-high field walls, kelp forests swaying in a current you barely feel, white algae-snow falling through still water onto a garden that has been growing, drowning, and composting itself, without a single animal to crop it, long enough for history to become topography.
