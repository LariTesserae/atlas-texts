This is not a ruin, a mine, or a nature reserve, though it looks like all three forced into the same hole. It is a finished system.

Everything in the pit — machines, animals, plants, dust, heat, sound — is holding a position. Nothing is arriving, growing, cooling, eroding, or winding down. Your preparations should reflect that: you are not visiting a place that will react normally to you. It will try, very gently and without malice, to absorb whatever you do into its existing pattern.

Visit short, visit supplied, and leave on a schedule you decided *outside* the pit.

### The core hazards

**1. The sound arrives all at once**
On the track across the plain, you will not hear it getting louder. One step is saltbush, wind, and your boots. The next, you are inside a 90-decibel chord that fills your teeth and sternum.

That threshold is unmarked. Do not wait until you hear it to put in protection — by then you have already taken the full hit and may startle on a dry, gritty track.

Before you even see the rim:
- Fit foam earplugs correctly, deep and sealed.
- Put earmuffs over them. Double protection is not overkill at a constant 90 dB with strong low-frequency vibration.
- Do the same for everyone in the party, even the person staying topside. Establish hand signals now.

Expect bone conduction to defeat some of this. You will still feel it in your chest, jaw, sinuses, and the soles of your feet. Hearing protection will save your ears; it will not make the place quiet.

Plan for no useful voice communication inside, at all. Whispers do not carry. Shouting is swallowed without echo and leaves you hoarse and dehydrated. Bring:
- A small whiteboard or waterproof slate and marker
- Pre-agreed signals for *stop, up, down, danger, leave now, I am not okay*
- A bright strobe or signal mirror for emergencies

Do not bring an air horn expecting it to cut through. Anything temporary becomes part of the chord and disappears.

**2. Heat and invisible dehydration**
It is a constant 31–32°C in the pit, kiln-dry, with hot wind rising from the fissure at the bottom. The danger is that sweat evaporates before it runs. You will not feel how much water you are losing, and thirst, lip-licking, and looking at your own skin will all lie to you.

There is no usable water inside. None. There are pumps, cisterns, standpipes, hoses, and a sump — all permanently dry. Do not be fooled by the plumbing.

Carry all your water in, sealed:
- At least 1 litre per person for a very short reconnaissance, 2–3 litres for anything longer, plus more waiting at the rim
- Electrolytes — plain water alone is not enough when you are losing salt you cannot see
- Drink on a timer, not by thirst. Small, scheduled drinks every 15–20 minutes.
- Lip balm, saline nasal spray, and eye drops. Your lips, nose, and eyes will dry and crack, and the air tastes of pennies, ozone, and sulfur, which masks the taste of your own mouth going wrong.

Watch for the quiet signs of heat trouble in your companions, because no one will be able to tell you clearly: confusion, clumsiness on the catwalks, irritability, suddenly stopping mid-task, skin that has gone completely dry.

**3. Bad air in permanent suspension**
The brown haze does not settle and does not blow away. It is stone dust, machine oil mist, insect fragments, bat guano, sulfur, and the ghost of blasting powder that never fades. Sodium lamps burn through it all day and night.

Bring:
- A properly fitted respirator — P2/N95 at absolute minimum, a half-mask with P100/particulate and organic vapour cartridges is better
- Sealed goggles, not just glasses. Dust and moths both end up in eyes, and the moths around the lamps are half-singed and shedding.
- Skin coverage. The dust is abrasive, oily in places, and the rock dust comes from stone that still smells freshly blasted.

If you have asthma, chronic bronchitis, or a bad dust allergy, do not descend. Bats hang in curtains under the gantries despite the light, and guano is part of the atmosphere here. Do not handle bats, lizards, or insects, living or dead. Assume rabies and respiratory fungi are ordinary risks even in an extraordinary place.

### Clothing and kit

Dress for a hot machine shop built into a quarry:

- Sturdy, closed boots with good grip. The stair is expanded steel mesh, the catwalks are layered and crossing, and talus at the wall angles still has its corners on it.
- Heat-resistant gloves. The stair rail is hot through ordinary gloves by desert noon; machine plates are occupied by lizards precisely because they are warm.
- Long sleeves and trousers in a tough, light fabric. Every rock edge is knife-fresh: ochre, rust-red, sulfur-yellow shelves that have not softened.
- Hard hat. Tools, stones, and debris do not appear to fall and stay fallen here in a normal way, but scaffolds, gantries, and loose resonating panels are overhead everywhere.
- Sun protection for the rim — hard desert glare — and patience for your eyes on the way down. Ten steps in, the geometry cuts the sun into amber slats and you are under the lamps' second ceiling. It never properly brightens in the morning or darkens at night inside. Let your eyes adjust on the stair. Do not rush the transition.

Secure everything. Vibration is constant and sympathetic: loose panels buzz, dropped tools vibrate in place on grating, screws work, buckles sing. Tighten straps, tether your slate, water, and instruments, and keep nothing loose in open pockets. If you drop something, note where — it will be there later, bright and uncoated — but retrieving it from among machinery or animals is rarely worth the reach.

Leave a complete kit at the rim: extra water, shade, a first-aid kit, and a person.

### Your conduct inside

**Always have a rim watcher.** Someone who stays above the sound-bell, on a real clock, in real daylight. Inside, time cues are removed: no brightening, no darkening, the chord never thins at noon or thickens at dusk, the furnace never cools. It is very easy to stay an hour too long because nothing has changed to tell you an hour passed.

Set a vibrating timer before you cross the threshold. Keep the first visit to 20–30 minutes. Hard limit for a longer working visit: two hours, then out, however fine you feel. Hearing fatigue, vibration fatigue, and dehydration all present first as a pleasant sense of being attuned.

**Stay on the walkways.** There is no single floor. Turbine house, compressors on ledges, cable trays, riveted tanks, a foundry furnace breathing orange through its seams, conveyor galleries — all fitted wherever the rock would take them, layered. Follow the faint white arrows. Assume every machine is live, hot, and under load, because it is: flywheels turning, pumps pumping hot air and dust in a closed, purposeful loop from one dry pipe to another. No one is maintaining them. That does not mean they are safe to touch. It means there is no one who will stop them for you.

Do not lean on pipes to rest. Do not step on cable trays — blind, milk-white crickets the size of thumbs live in them, thighs like springs, singing against the compressor rhythm. Do not ride or block the conveyors; the beetles do, in both directions, endlessly.

**Do not disturb a station.** This is the rule you will feel in your sternum. Every creature has a pitch, a perch, a cell: swifts in open pipe-ends in rows, lizards aligned with seams on warm plates, bats in breathing curtains, moths in slow orbit around the lamps, thousands of dry-winged insects holding exact position in the updraft above the bottom grate. If a creature dies, something replaces it within the hour, in the same place, taking up the same portion of the sound. No one has seen the replacement arrive.

Do not test this.
- Watch where you put your feet and hands. Crushing a beetle or a cricket is not a small accident here.
- Do not move a lizard off a plate to touch the plate.
- Do not take a feather, a moth, a plant, a bolt. The ear-flowers in the oil-black cracks — leafless grey stems, flowers like tiny cupped ears turning toward the loudest machines — have no seeds anyone has found and are always the same number in the same cracks. The moss inside the ducting feeds on heat and iron filings. Observe, photograph, leave them.
- Do not add anything to the shrine at the foot of the stair. It contains a bolt, a feather, and a hearing protector worn smooth. It is complete.
- Do not sign the logbooks in the operator huts. The entries are identical, daily, swollen with sameness, and the last surveyor's note — *It is not that it has stopped changing. It is that it finished, and this is what finished sounds like* — is dated unverifiably in a familiar hand. Read if you must. Photograph. Do not contribute an entry in your own hand and discover it matches.

**At the bottom, be brief.** By the sump — concrete, mineral-crusted, drain never wet — the sound is loudest and, perversely, clearest. At the grate over the fissure, the earth's scorching, even wind presses your clothes to you and the chord separates into its hundreds of parts: insect, bird, blade, bat-murmur, generator. It is the most interesting place in the pit and the worst place to linger. The wind is hot enough to dry your eyes in seconds, the insects hold their column around you, and standing perfectly still in one spot, listening, is exactly what everything else here does forever.

Go down, look, feel it, get out. Do not stand on the grate longer than it takes to understand it. Keep moving or leave; do not take up a position.

### Leaving

You can leave whenever you like. The stair is right there. That is not a comfort so much as a warning — nothing will stop you, remind you, or change to mark your departure, and people have stayed because staying required no decision.

Climb out on your timer, not your mood. When you cross the lip of the bell going up, the silence will hit as hard as the sound did. Sit down at the rim, in the shade your watcher prepared. Keep hearing protection in for a few minutes, then remove it slowly. Expect ringing, fullness in the ears, headache behind the eyes, and a strange grief for the chord. Do not drive or descend any other track immediately. Drink water with electrolytes, check your companions by name and face, and compare notes before memory smooths the visit into something uniform.

Afterwards: shower the dust and oil off, treat any cut at once — every rock cut is fresh and dirty — and if cough, fever, or eye irritation develops in the following days, tell a clinician plainly that you were exposed to bat guano, mineral dust, and oil mist in a confined pit.

### What to bring, in one list

Earplugs *and* earmuffs, fitted before the threshold; respirator and sealed goggles; hard hat; heat gloves; sturdy boots; long tough clothing; 2–3 litres of water per person plus electrolytes; lip balm, saline spray, eye drops; slate and marker; vibrating timer; strobe; headlamp (the lamp-light is constant but amber and shadowed); tethered tools only; a stocked rim camp and a watcher who does not descend.

Bring nothing you intend to leave, nothing you need to collect, and no plan that depends on the place responding, helping, or changing.

It finished. You are just visiting the sound of that.
