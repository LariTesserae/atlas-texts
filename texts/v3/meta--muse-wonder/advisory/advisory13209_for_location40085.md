The High Pan will not try to kill you the way a mountain or a jungle does. Underfoot, it is probably the safest ground you will ever stand on: finished, flattened, polished flat over geological time, no rivers to flood, no slopes to fall down, no predators that hunt.

Everything *above* that ground is untrustworthy. The air, the light, your sense of weight, your sense of direction, the distance to the horizon. Prepare for a place that is stable in exactly one dimension, and negotiable in all the others.

### 1. The First Problem: You Are 15 Kilometers Up

Forget acclimatization. No ordinary human acclimates to this.

The Pan is a continent artificially held fifteen kilometers in the sky, above most of the atmosphere. At noon the zenith is deep indigo and flecked with stars. Sound does not carry. The sun is white and ordinary, but it is arriving through almost nothing.

You need what is functionally a spacesuit, not a mountaineering mask.

**Non-negotiables:**

* **Full pressure suit with independent oxygen.** A supplemental oxygen bottle is useless here. The pressure is too low for your lungs to work, and too low for exposed moisture - eyes, mouth, skin in a tear - to stay liquid comfortably. Bring redundant regulators, CO2 scrubbers, and enough consumables for at least twice your planned traverse. There is no resupply between pylons, and pylons do not resupply *you*.
* **Sealed thermal management, in both directions.** It is cool, not arctic, in the sun. Black basalt will warm through your soles. Step into the shadow of a pylon or a dike-stone and the chill is instant. At night the ground radiates its heat straight to space and frost forms in cracks from moisture sweating out of the rock itself. Your suit needs to shed heat while you walk in sun and hold it while you sleep in the dark, without ever opening to the outside.
* **Radiation and UV protection.** That dark noon sky is beautiful because it is not protecting you. A proper visor - polarized, UV-opaque, replaceable outer layer - and suit fabric rated for prolonged high-altitude cosmic exposure. Dust hits will pit a visor slowly; silence in the Pan means you will *feel* the ticking through your helmet more than hear it. Carry spares.
* **Abrasion-proof seals and boots.** The dust is wind-ground basalt and iron, and near the pylons, fields of silica grass that grows atom-by-atom and shatters underfoot into glassy sand. That sand gets into joints, scores seals, and can slice a soft sole. Walk the black lichen mats in the cooling cracks if you must cross them, but watch your footing - the silica shards are a puncture hazard, not an annoyance.

Test your suit for weeks before you come, in a vacuum chamber if your origin has one, and learn to do everything - eat, drink, urinate, repair a glove, read a map - without breaking seal. There is nowhere on the Pan to take your helmet off outside a pylon hut, and not every pylon has one. Some are just a spire, a fused-glass apron, and empty canister racks half-buried for ten thousand years.

If your multiverse transit can drop you somewhere, choose the lift station. Do not materialize in the open Pan. Your exit method also needs testing: the field lattice that holds the continent up interferes with compasses, radios, and anything that assumes gravity points one way and light travels straight. Anchor a return beacon physically, at the station, and carry a purely mechanical/inertial way to find it again.

### 2. Getting Around a Place With No Features

From horizon to horizon: black basalt, red dust in shallow pans, white salt and silica in the ghost-beds of rivers dead since before the uplift. Craters are rings. Faults are swells. From the ground, it does not change for weeks of walking.

You navigate by pylons, rock, and dead reckoning. Nothing else is honest.

* **Never trust the horizon, a compass, or your eyes in a shear.** Refraction can pull a pylon ten kilometers closer, double it, slide it sideways, or put a cliff edge under what looks like level plain. Magnetic compasses in a shear turn in slow, sincere, completely wrong circles.
* **Trust rock.** Learn the landmarks that cannot be moved by light: pale branching riverbeds visible best at low sun, un-eroded dike stones that cast the only natural shadows, crater rings, the pattern of cooling joints in the basalt. Photograph and sketch as you go. Use inertial navigation, measured paces, sun angle, and star fixes at noon if your visor allows it.
* **Use the pylons as a chain, not a destination.** They stand every 40 to 100 km, each alone on its own horizon. That is one to three hard suit-days between certainties. Plan each leg with a turnaround point where you still have air to get back. At dusk, from any rise no higher than your shoulder, a dozen beacons will blink on white, one after another - count them and triangulate *then*, when they are fixed lights, not daytime mirages.
* **Travel in the morning.** Afternoons breed thermals off the black rock that punch a kilometer high, dragging curtains of electrified dust with them. You can see one organizing for a day as a brown smudge banding on the horizon, and it will still pass in absolute silence. Morning is calmer, cooler to walk in, and lets you be anchored before the worst field activity.
* **Move in a team, tethered when it matters.** Sound starves in thin air. You will not hear a partner fall, shout, or suit-alarm beyond a few meters. Radios may fade when the lattice gets argumentative. Stay in line of sight - true line of sight, checked by laser rangefinder - and use suit-to-suit hardline tethers in shears, near the rim, and near pylons. Your loudest environment for a hundred kilometers will be your own breath and heartbeat. That does things to people after a week. Schedule voice check-ins even when there is nothing to report.

The southern rim is weeks from the lift station and the view does not change on the way. The edge is not a slope, it is a cutoff, plain stopping over a manufactured cliff dropping through haze to a lower world you will never see clearly. Treat every approach to it as a cliff approach in high wind, even though there is no wind you can hear. A gravity flicker there is a death sentence.

### 3. Weather Here Is Gravity Failing Locally

There are no storms you can hear. That does not mean calm.

Learn the three events by name from the old station logs, and drill responses until they are reflex:

**A Shear.** Light bends. Distant pylons double. Loose stones tick, roll uphill, drift against nothing, scatter. Your stomach will know before your brain does.

*Stop. Anchor. Crouch.* Stake yourself and your pack to rock - not to dust, not to a loose stone that may decide to migrate. Do not walk on visual bearings until it passes. This can last minutes or hours.

**A Rising.** Your pack lightens. Unsecured objects lift and begin to orbit gently around a center you cannot see. A whole sheet of grit can hang glittering and weightless for minutes, then drop straight down.

*Secure everything, always.* This is the Pan's first social rule. Every canister, glove, tool, tent peg, on a lanyard or in a closed pocket, every time, even inside a shelter. The Pan is littered, one object per desert, with what the sky took and dropped elsewhere: a survey drone, a habitat strut, a single intact glove. Do not add to the collection, and do not be surprised if something impossible falls slowly out of a clear starry noon near you.

Your camp needs to work upside-down in weight terms: a shelter that can be made suddenly heavy *and* suddenly weightless without collapsing or sailing. Low, wide, staked at multiple points into basalt cracks, with your own body as ballast only as a last resort.

**A Correction.** Near a pylon, the fused-glass apron around its base is a no-camp, no-loiter, no-lunch zone. That circle can become briefly *very* heavy. If you feel the low vibration of a standing pylon in your teeth and inner ear - and you will through a glove if you touch one, which you should do rarely and briefly - back off. Your sense of down will become negotiable. People fall over standing still next to these things.

Fallen pylons, sheared into perfect sections like a felled tree, are not safer. They are where the lattice has already lost an argument. Give them a wider berth than the standing ones.

### 4. Dust, Swarms, and Things That Aren't Weather

Rivers of dust run an inch above the ground here, following field lines, splitting around your ankles without touching your boots. Electrified dust curtains can leave you charged, blinded by a pitted visor, and with every metal tool wanting to stick to something else. Ground yourself before handling oxygen fittings. Keep electronics shielded, and keep mechanical backups for anything that would kill you if it reset.

Repair-swarms arrive with the worst disturbances: clouds of metallic motes smaller than dust grains, assembling in mid-air into tools, membranes, lattices, patching a pylon or a tear in the field, then dissolving. You can walk through one and feel only prickling on exposed skin - but you have no exposed skin, remember. Do not interfere, do not swat, do not try to collect one. They do not notice you, and that indifference is the only safety they offer. Nothing here was built at human scale, only occasionally at human use.

Do not drink the frost. Do not expect dew, rain, streams, or the machines to share. The pylons condense and keep moisture for themselves. Bring all your water, recycle all of it, and carry your waste out - the lichen that eats stone and excretes rusty powder will eventually eat anything you leave, but "eventually" here means centuries, and the next traveler does not need your leavings orbiting in a Rising.

### 5. The Quiet Life

Life on the Pan has adapted to patience and gradients, not to visitors.

Black lichen mats in the joints eat rock directly. Silica grass fans near pylons feed on waste heat and leaking field energy. Grey rosette fields open all at once after a shear, putting charged filaments into the disturbed air, then close for a year. Walk around rosette fields when open - stepping through a field of live filaments in a metal-laced suit is asking for a discharge - and around silica fans when you can. Your boot will undo centuries of growth in one crunch, and the shards will try to return the favor.

Lickers come to salt pans at dawn: broad, armored, tortoise-slow grazers rasping minerals off rock with their tongues. That rasp is almost the only non-human sound that carries, and only for a few meters. Watch them from down-field, give them the pan, do not try to feed or touch the mineral plates they grow from their grazing. They are calm because nothing here can afford panic.

Above you, field-assisted gliders - thin, translucent mantas far too large for this air - hang on thermals and lattice-buoyancy, silhouettes eclipsing stars at noon. They have never been seen to descend to a human. The sudden chill when one crosses the sun will remind you to check your suit heater. That is all.

### 6. The Mind Is Unpressurized Too

Prepare for silence that is not peace. It is the medium failing. For weeks you will see the same plain, hear your own heart, lose external sound as a way to judge distance or danger, and watch dust storms wheel without noise. People underestimate this more than the vacuum.

Bring structured days. Bring music, voices, texts stored *inside* your helmet system. Rotate navigation duty so no one person stares at false horizons too long. Agree in advance: in a shear, nobody argues with instruments about what looks real. If a partner insists a pylon is "right there" when the rangefinder says 30 km, you follow the drill, not the debate.

Agree also on abort criteria before you land: loss of one redundant oxygen loop, a visor crack beyond the spare layer, vertigo that persists an hour away from any pylon, inertial navigation failure. Any of those ends the traverse, even if the plain looks empty, inviting, and perfectly safe underfoot. It always looks that way.

### A Final Checklist, Taped Inside Your Airlock

Pressure suit + 2x all consumables. Inertial and celestial navigation, no reliance on compass or horizon. Laser rangefinder. Anchors rated for basalt cracks, tethers for *every* object. Morning-only movement plan, pylon-to-pylon, with backtrack reserves. Shear/Rising/Correction drills rehearsed in suit. No camp on fused glass, ever. Water closed-loop, waste packed out. Beacon anchored at the lift station, return method tested against field interference.

And one attitudinal item: respect that the pylons are not infrastructure *for* you. They are a negotiation between machines older than the rivers whose ghosts you will walk across, holding a finished world at an impossible height. You are not their user. You are, at best, tolerated weather.

At dusk the beacons will come on one by one across a plain curved with the planet, the dust will turn rose and then invisible, and the ground beneath you will feel like the most solid thing in any universe.

Trust that feeling only about the ground.
