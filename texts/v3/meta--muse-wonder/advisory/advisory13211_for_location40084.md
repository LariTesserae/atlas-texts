This is not an abandoned place, and it's not really a town you *visit* in the middle of the day. It's a working tidal port built on poisoned fill — concrete, slag, asphalt and salt-clay where mangroves used to be — that runs on three clocks at once: the tide, the heat, and the cargo ships. Humans are just the scarcest large animal in it.

If you arrive unprepared at noon you'll think it's empty, get heat-sick in an hour, get flooded at ankle-height in the evening, and spend the night feeding everything that flies, crawls, and swims in through an open window.

Prepare like you're going to a ship, a warehouse, and a tropical mudflat at the same time.

### 1. Understand the rhythm before you go

**Midday is not daytime here.** By 09:00 it's already high-30s Celsius, wet, still, and shadowless under a white-gray industrial haze. The sun is dim, but heat radiates up off pavement, warehouses, and roofs. Outdoor work has largely moved to night. At midday the central junction, warehouses, and avenues will be deserted except for machines humming inside sealed cabs.

Plan all movement for early morning, late evening, and night. Treat 11:00-16:00 as you would a blizzard: stay inside, in A/C, do nothing strenuous.

**The tide is the local weather.** Twice a day water tests the gates and pumps answer. The lowest streets — especially around the workers' dormitories, where the plinth has sunk unevenly — flood to the ankle if a pump mistimes. Groundwater is inches under the pavement and seeps up through cracks. Everything smells of damp concrete.

Get the local tide table and pump schedule before you arrive, save it offline, and check it like a farmer checks rain. Know when the causeway is wettest, which quarter floods first, and never plan a ground-level arrival, sleep, or storage around high tide.

**There is essentially no green.** No trees, no grass, no shade plants, no soil you can camp on or dig into. Shade is only building shade. The bright color in a window might be a plastic flower. That means no natural windbreak, no evaporative cooling, no clean ground, and reflected heat everywhere.

### 2. Health preparation

An ordinary human is vulnerable here less to one dramatic danger than to accumulation: heat + salt water + animal waste + industrial dust.

Before you go, be up to date on tetanus, hepatitis A/B, and typhoid — food comes in by barge and truck, vegetables wilt within a day, market meltwater and offcuts sit in drains. Talk to a travel clinician about mosquito-borne disease for the exact world/port, rabies risk from bats and feral cats, and leptospirosis risk. You will be wading, whether you plan to or not, in brown tidal water that has passed rats, fertilizer sheds, grain stores, and drains.

Pack a real medical kit:
- Oral rehydration salts / electrolytes — you will sweat constantly and plain water won't be enough. Humidity means sweat doesn't cool you well.
- Waterproof wound dressings, antiseptic, and tweezers. Any cut that gets floodwater, mud, brine, or drainage water in it needs cleaning immediately. That water is saline, bacterial, and chemically contaminated.
- Antihistamines, hydrocortisone, antifungal powder/cream. Everything stays damp. Skin in shoes, waistbands, and straps will macerate and get infected.
- Mosquito repellent (DEET/picaridin), permethrin for clothing if you use it, and bite relief.
- Your own inhaler if you have any sensitivity. Grain dust, warm cardboard, ammonia from cold storage and fertilizer, bat guano, and compressor fumes hang in warehouses that are *hotter inside than outside*.
- Sun protection despite the dim light. Reflected glare off wet mud, white salt crust, and pale concrete is punishing, and sunscreen will sweat off — reapply and rely more on covering.

Do not plan to acclimatize by pushing through. Heat illness here will be quiet: headache, nausea, confusion, stopping sweating, feeling the compressor thrum in your teeth and thinking that's normal. Travel with a buddy system and set hard turn-back times for crossing open yards.

### 3. What to wear and carry

Dress for hot wet concrete, not for a beach.

**Footwear is critical.** Bring two pairs:
1) Closed, breathable, quick-drying work shoes with good grip — handrails, pavement, and warehouse floors are warm and slick with condensate and algae.
2) Mid-calf waterproof boots for flood streets, tidal drains, and mud. Store both on a rack *above floor level*, as locals do. Never leave shoes, bags, or bedding on a ground floor.

**Clothing:** Lightweight, loose, quick-dry, long sleeves and trousers. They protect from sun-glare, biting insects, brine splash, and hot metal. Natural cotton stays soaked and heavy; leather and paper go moldy. Bring enough changes that you can always put on something dry — you will change more than once a day. A wide hat, sunglasses, and a light neck cover matter even at "dim" noon.

**Everyday carry:**
- 1-2 liters of safe water on you at all times, even for a 10-minute walk. Crossings between buildings are timed like crossing open ground in hard rain — you go from shade patch to shade patch.
- Headlamp with a low/red mode. At night sodium lights make tunnels in vapor, warehouses are genuinely dark under eaves, and a bright white light left on will pull in moths by the drift and geckos and everything that eats them.
- Dry bags / zip seals for phone, documents, medicines, batteries. A/C units weep constantly, walls condense, salt air corrodes contacts, and ants find every cable conduit. Nothing electronic should sit unsealed.
- High-visibility vest if you'll be near yards at night. Trucks, forklifts, rail spurs, weighbridges, and loading arms move in ones and twos after dark with headlights blinded by haze.
- A small towel and spare socks in a dry bag. Often more useful than extra gear.

Leave behind anything that needs dry, cool, green conditions. No camping tent on the ground — the ground is either flooded, saline and chemical, or paved and patrolled by crews spraying and scraping drains.

### 4. Arriving

The two-mile causeway across the tidal flat is your tell. Straight concrete, low salt-eaten guardrails, mud and sheet water on both sides, no shelter, no verge, no tree. Do not walk it at midday or at high water if you can avoid it. If your arrival point can be chosen, choose high, roofed, paved ground away from: the low dormitory quarter, the old fertilizer shed roof void, warehouse doorways, drainage outfalls, and the canal.

If you must cross open ground on arrival, do it fast, hydrated, and before you are tired. The place looks flat and simple — silos, cold store, fertilizer shed as long as four football pitches, container hardstanding, ice factory, power station, water tower — but distances are truck-scale. A "short walk" across the port quarter is a town-sized walk in full reflected heat.

On arrival, find a human *in the evening activity*, not at noon. Skeleton crews are sealed in control rooms at midday. The town wakes when shutters roll up and lights come on.

### 5. Shelter and sleep

Sleep above ground floor, preferably second floor or higher, in a weather-sealed concrete block with working A/C.

Check your room like a boat cabin:
- Door has a screen, a raised lip, and a brush strip — and you keep all three closed. No ground-floor window open after dark, ever. It's not about thieves, it's biomass coming toward light and cool.
- Bed net or fine mesh even indoors if screens are imperfect. Mosquitoes breed permanently in A/C condensate puddles, blocked gutters, and trays — the one guaranteed water source.
- All food sealed in hard containers. Cockroaches live behind warm compressors, ants in conduits, rats work the grain lines at night, feral cats are fed by everyone and will investigate.
- Everything off the floor. Laundry off the floor. Charge cables off the floor if possible.
- Expect damp sheets, condensing walls, and a constant drip-drip onto concrete. Air out in the A/C, don't open windows to "freshen."

Don't sleep in warehouses, under eaves, under parked trailers, or in roof spaces. Trailers are cat shade by day and hunting grounds by night. The fertilizer shed roof holds a bat colony you can smell before you see — warm ammonia, guano dust, rabies and respiratory risk. Don't enter it. Swifts in cold-store vents and bats at dusk are spectacular *from the street*.

### 6. Food and water

Do not drink tap, canal, puddle, condensate, or groundwater. It's pressured up through salt-poisoned fill, mixed with tidal brine, fertilizer runoff, and animal waste.

Drink only sealed, imported/bottled or properly treated water. Food is entirely imported: rice, oil, frozen meat, tins, inland vegetables. Eat hot, freshly cooked food from busy evening stalls/canteens. At the market, everything is from crates and cold boxes, and by afternoon flies hover dense over drains — buy earlier, avoid anything sitting in meltwater, wash hands after handling money/crates and before eating.

Bring a few shelf-stable rations of your own in sealed packaging in case a barge/truck is delayed by tide or heat. Seal them against ants/roaches immediately.

### 7. Moving around and animal rules

You will share every route, in shifts.

- **Shade side is the sidewalk.** Time building-to-building dashes. Rest in warehouse shade, but don't linger in doorways where forklifts expect emptiness.
- **Watch drains at falling tide.** Fiddler crabs — thousands, orange and mud-brown, one big claw up — pour out of grates sideways across hot concrete. Harmless, but slippery underfoot and startling. Give them the crossing.
- **Monitor lizards on the main canal** move at a slow walk and act like they own it. They mostly do. Don't corner, feed, or try to move one. Back off.
- **Geckos on lit walls** at night are harmless and useful — they eat the moths. **Bats at dusk** leave in a quiet column — enjoy it, don't stand under a roost opening with mouth/eyes uncovered.
- **Pigeons, sparrows, rats** follow spilled grain on rail spurs. Don't eat there, don't store food near it, shake out clothes.
- **Feral cats** are lean, heat-flattened, semi-owned. Don't grab them, and expect scratches/bites to be infection risks.
- **Mosquito drifts** around lights and puddles are continuous. Cover ankles/wrists at dusk especially.

Never wade barefoot anywhere. Never swim in the channel, brine pans, borrow pits, or flood streets. The white salt pans look clean — the crust is caustic, the brine stings broken skin, and machinery peels it with front-loaders.

### 8. Industrial hazards nobody will warn you about because they're normal here

Assume every quiet building is live. Refrigeration compressors thrum constantly — ammonia leaks are a real cold-store risk; if eyes water and air catches your throat sharply, leave upwind immediately. Grain silos tick as grain settles and produce explosive dust inside. Fertilizer sheds, pipe runs on low trestles, tide gates, pumps, and power station cooling towers are not tourist sites.

Don't touch handrails without expecting slick heat. Don't lean on corrugated roofs streaked rust-red and salt-white. Don't step on drain covers — they're chained for a reason. Don't cross rail spurs or weighbridges without looking both ways twice at night, even if you hear nothing. The heat puts a lid on sound; voices and vehicles don't carry.

Tell someone your route and tide window, even for a short errand. In a place that *looks* empty, no one may notice a person down with heatstroke in a forklift lane for a full shift.

### When to leave, and how

Plan departure for a falling tide and evening cool, with your route off the low quarter already clear. Have boots, water, and your sealed bags ready before flood time, not during it. If a pump fails, a storm pushes the tide, or you develop fever after a floodwater exposure, persistent vomiting/diarrhea, an infected bite/scratch, confusion, or a bat contact of any kind — cut the visit short and get to real medical care inland. Those are not "wait until morning" problems here.

Visited right — hydrated, above the flood line, sealed against insects, moving at night along the shade and the lights — this place is remarkable. Not pretty in a green way, but unforgettable: crabs clicking across concrete at low tide, geckos frozen around every lamp, bats changing the air over the channel at dusk, the whole built shell humming with compressors while humans appear only in ones and twos. You're not entering an empty city. You're being allowed, briefly, into someone else's shift.
