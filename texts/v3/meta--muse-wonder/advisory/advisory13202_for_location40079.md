This is the High Yard. Prepare for a city that is also a machine, a roof, and a slight temporal accident — in that order, and then all at once.

It is about twelve miles across, perhaps ten thousand people, and there is no ground in it. Not hidden ground. Not ground far below you can eventually reach. Every deck, terrace, gantry, stair, and roof you will stand on was bolted to something else that was bolted to something else, and below the edge-gratings the supports just fall into bright haze. If you are prone to vertigo, acrophobia, or a deep psychological need for soil underfoot, this will not fade after the first day. It will get quieter, but your feet will keep expecting earth that is never coming.

Bring a local guide for at least your first two days. Not because the Yard is dangerous in an obvious way, but because half its rules are physical habits nobody thinks to put on a sign.

### 1. Your body: high, thin, bright, cold

Think alpine desert, but made of ceramic tile and aluminum.

**Altitude and air:** The air is thin, cool, dry, and smells faintly of ozone, hot metal, wet copper, and animal fur. You will get winded on ladders that look trivial. You will get dehydrated without feeling sweaty. By 9 a.m. the decks are bone dry and your lips will crack.

Plan 2-3 lazy days to acclimatize if you can. No heavy exertion, no alcohol the first night, walk the Roost Rows stairs like an old person. Bring more water-carrying capacity than you think you need — a liter bottle minimum on your person at all times, refilled at cistern stations. There are no rivers, no wells. The Yard lives on roughly 0.9 of a river, most of it caught as dew.

Do not drink from gutters, roof runnels, or open cisterns unless a local hands it to you. That water is accounted for, it is briefly full of what washes off roofs — droppings, lichen, dust, insects — and it belongs to a cycle you don't understand yet.

**Sun:** At noon the light is white, vertical, and exact. Every pale surface reflects it. Step out of shadow and your shoulders sting. Step into shadow and your skin drops twenty degrees. Sunburn happens fast, and so does chilling.

Pack:
- Full-coverage, breathable layers you can add/remove every ten minutes. A warm insulated layer *and* a wind shell, even at noon.
- A wide hat or veil — copy the workers in the factory sheds and wear a bright, light veil or neck shade. It cuts glare and the constant small wind.
- Real glacier glasses / wraparound polarized sunglasses, plus a backup pair. In the western Lens Fields you can be temporarily blinded by a furnace mirror even looking away from the sun.
- High SPF sunscreen, lip balm with SPF (bring multiple, you will lose them and crack anyway), and moisturizer. The dryness by midday is total.
- Gloves. Not just for cold. This is a young, unworn place. Stair treads are serrated, corners have not been rounded by hands or weather, rebar and panel edges are sharp. Everything was made, so everything has an edge.
- Sturdy, grippy, closed shoes with ankle support. Decks are slick with dew for an hour after dawn, dry and dusty after, and you will be on ladders, gratings, catwalks, narrow radiator fins used as paths, and stairs that cross over light-wells three stories deep. No sandals.

Sleep will be odd. Night falls suddenly — violet, the whole white city going blue in minutes — and then there is a *second* dusk, about an hour where tiles, lens blanks, ceramic, and cistern water give back a faint sourceless glow, enough to see by. Bring an eye mask if you need true dark, and expect to wake cold.

### 2. Water, plants, and animals run the schedule

Get up at dawn at least once on purpose.

For an hour after sunrise the entire Yard drips. Every cornice is a watershed. Dew runs in designed runnels, downspouts chime, the Radiator Ridges weep. Children and old people are out with soft brushes shepherding water toward tanks. Walk slowly — everything is wet — and don't stand under eaves unless you want a neck-full of cold dew.

By nine it is over. The green that lives outside the greenhouses is opportunistic punctuation: silver lichen around cold joints, alpine grass in overflow grates cropped by goats, moss only on the damp north sides of vents. Don't bring seeds, soil, potted plants, or food scraps to toss "for compost." There is no soil to receive them, and the ecology is closed and complete. Nothing here is natural, and it works *because* nothing is wasted. A dropped crust will be found, but you'll have upset a very precise accounting of insects, droppings, cistern catch, and greenhouse air.

The animals are not pets and not pests. They are infrastructure.

- **Roof goats:** Small, white, black-footed, with municipal authority. They patrol ridges, radiator fins, and goat-runs that are also roofs and condensation farms. If a goat stares at you on a narrow path, you move. Do not feed them, chase them for photos, or argue. Locals swear some of them are far older than goats should be. Let that be.
- **Swifts:** Thousands nest under the Roost Rows eaves and pour out at dawn in a river. Their screaming at altitude sounds like tearing silk. Don't stand in the main pour-path at dawn; you will be disoriented and covered in feathers, and occasionally a swift will vanish in a bright patch and reappear a few feet left, surprised. That's normal here.
- **Bees:** They commute in marked lanes between hive-walls and greenhouse ports. Those lanes are traffic lanes. Don't wave, don't walk through a stream of them, look before crossing. The bees are more important than you are, for reasons below.
- **Large pale moths:** Bigger than your hand, frost-dusted. They sleep on warm transformers by day in such numbers you must part them gently to pass in some corridors, and at night they gather in tomorrow's sun patches. Walk slowly. Crushing a mat of them is a social error.
- **Cats:** Lean, ladder-built, territorial about sun patches. Do not move a cat, or its carpet square — including the odd extra square laid where the patch *will* drift Thursday against all solar reason. That square is correct.
- **Pigeons, sparrows, finches:** Pigeons own the southern Cistern Steps. Sparrows live inside the signage. The finch frameworks are open by design; don't "free" birds that refuse to leave.

Speak quietly. The whole place is deliberately quiet — presses are vacuum-jacketed, monorails are magnetic, smelters are electric. Loud voices carry strangely across layered walkways and mark you instantly as a visitor.

### 3. The light is slightly early. This is the important part.

They built high for clean sun for solar furnaces and lenses. They built higher. Now the upper surfaces receive light about a second — less, sometimes — *before* it happens.

In practice, for an ordinary traveler:

- Your shadow at noon in the Lens Fields may fall slightly *before* you step.
- Your reflection in polished metal may lift a tool half a breath before you do.
- A foreman will hover his hand, miss, and take the wrench on the second try, without comment.
- Moths will be waiting at dusk in a cold patch of shade for warmth that hasn't technically arrived yet.
- Mirrors at sunset will begin turning toward tomorrow's sunrise.

Do not try to correct for this with instruments, and do not trust your instruments uphill. Clocks, cameras, GPS, automatic sensors, and your phone's timestamp become unreliable the higher you go. Mirrors here are recalibrated by watching the bees, because the bees navigate by a sun that is in the right place for them. The higher workshops keep two clocks — one mechanical, one a bowl of water evaporating — and average them.

Practical rules:

1. **Grab on the second try.** If you reach for a rail, a tool, a cup, a falling object and your hand feels *early* or the visual is off by a fraction, pause and reach again deliberately. Most minor accidents for visitors happen in that half-second.
2. **Do not stand where your shadow tells you to.** Especially around the Lens Fields, smelters, and presses. Work moves patiently, but a crane crossing a bay is a scheduled event, and a press the size of a house completes one stroke per hour with a soft *whump* you feel in your molars. Give machinery the space locals give it, plus one extra step.
3. **Watch animals, not screens, for time-of-day cues.** When swifts stir, when moths relocate, when mirrors begin to tick — that's the usable forecast.
4. **Photos will lie subtly.** Exposure, shadows, and reflections won't match memory uphill. If you need a record for safety or work, make it low in the Yard, in the older central districts near the cisterns, where instruments are still trusted.
5. **Don't be alarmed, and don't make a fuss.** Locals are not in denial; they have incorporated this. Pausing, blinking, taking the second reach is polite. Announcing loudly that time is broken is not.

If you are prone to motion sickness or migraines, the shadow/reality offset at noon is worst in the Lens Fields and on exposed upper walkways. Wear your darkest glasses, look at textured or matte surfaces, and take shade breaks — shade is genuinely colder and the contrast rest helps.

### 4. Getting around a place with surplus space

The Yard is a shallow engineered bowl perhaps twelve miles across:

- **West: Lens Fields** — mirrors, furnaces, blinding glare, hottest reflections, strongest time-slip. Go with a guide, stay on marked paths, never look directly at a tracking mirror.
- **North: Roost Rows** — housing, workshops, pigeon lofts, battery stacks in one continuous terraced wall 5-12 stories high. Most lodging for visitors is here. It's the most labyrinthine and lived-in.
- **East: Radiator Ridges** — 30-foot black fins humming over smelter roofs, dumping heat. Radiant warm next to cold air, beautiful at dusk when they tick as they cool. The fins shed water in the morning and heat shimmer at noon. Don't lean.
- **South: Cistern Steps** — broad amphitheaters that are roofs, tanks, walkways, gardens. Slipperiest at dawn, most social in the evening glow.

Everywhere is layered. A roof is a goat run is a landing. A wall has pipes, nests, a market stall, a ladder, a shrine of retired pressure gauges, and sleeping pods for night shift tucked like swallows' cups. You will rarely be alone, rarely be in a crowd, always be *among* things.

Two spatial rules travelers get wrong:

**Sealed bays:** Maintenance crews sometimes find service rooms under the deck that sum to more volume than the structure above allows — clean, lit, stocked with parts for machines not yet installed. They seal them, date the hatch, and do not open them again until needed. If you find a dated, sealed hatch, or a room that feels too large for its building: do not enter, do not pry it for a shortcut, do not store luggage in it. Mark it mentally and tell a local. Opening one early is not theft here; it's a kind of temporal littering.

**The corridor that loops:** There is at least one well-known corridor in the Roost Rows (locals will know the one you mean) that returns you to its entrance if you walk it looking at the floor. Look at the ceiling pipes instead and it delivers you correctly to the hive-walls. There are no signs. This is how children are taught: walked through twice. If you find yourself looping a hallway, a stair, a causeway — don't run, don't force a door. Stop, look up at the infrastructure, follow pipes, conduit, water lines. Looking at services, not decoration, is how the Yard wants to be navigated.

General navigation: arrive by a confirmed upper deck, not by "ground level" coordinates from your own world. A portal, drop, or transfer that places you even fifty feet low could put you under the deck in struts and haze with no ladder. Confirm arrival landmarks by sight: lens tower, cistern step, roof color. Always have a hand on something when crossing gratings — sudden updrafts from factory light-wells three levels down are common.

For multiverse logistics specifically: bring no earth, no anchor that needs burying, no navigation that assumes a planetary surface beneath the city. Magnetic and light-based compasses will argue with each other uphill. A mechanical watch, a paper map sketched by a local, and asking directions (quietly, and then following the pipe-look rule) beat electronics here.

### 5. Culture: replace, don't repair — and go slow

Work pace is the pace of the mirrors: patient, cyclical, nearly still. A sheet of alloy takes a week to cross one hall. Nobody hurries because hurrying interacts badly with the light.

Copy that. Build slack into every plan. Do not schedule tight transfers at noon uphill. Do not expect a part, a meal from greenhouse produce, or a crossing of the factory districts to be rushed. If you bring something broken, locals will likely unbolt and swap the whole module rather than tinker. Bring modular, replaceable gear — spare straps, spare lenses for your glasses, a spare mechanical timepiece — rather than specialized tools.

Food is greenhouse tomatoes, beans, bee products, stored water foods, and animal protein in modest local proportions. Ask before photographing workers in their bright veils, children shepherding dew, or the gauge shrines. Those shrines are not junk art; they are memorials to retired systems.

Leave nothing behind, especially not water containers half-full, shiny trash (mirrors confuse birds and optics), or unmarked batteries. In a place where every surface is someone's roof and water farm, litter falls into the ecology literally.

### Quick packing checklist

Sunglasses (2x), veil/hat, sunscreen, lip balm, layers + wind shell + warm night layer, gloves, grippy ankle shoes, 1L+ bottle, electrolytes, altitude headache remedy you know works for you, eye mask, earplugs (not for noise — for the sudden quiet and the low hive hum), mechanical watch, small flashlight for night after the second dusk fades (the glow only lasts an hour), notebook that doesn't need a timestamp, patience.

Come expecting to feel slightly early to your own life for a few days, to be stared down by a goat on a radiator fin, and to learn to reach twice for your coffee cup at noon without thinking about it. When that second reach becomes automatic, you'll be moving the way the Yard moves — and that's when it will start to let you through its corridors properly.
