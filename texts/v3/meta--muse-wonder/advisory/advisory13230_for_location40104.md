This is a new, damp, very loud company town that was built big and never filled up — in a shallow basin with no river, where all the water comes out of the fog, and most of it is shipped away in trucks.

It is survivable for an ordinary human with no special preparation, but it will chew up your hearing, your shoes, your paper, and your sense of quiet if you arrive unprepared. Prepare for three things above everything else: constant noise, constant moisture, and rationed drinking water.

### 1. Protect Your Hearing First

The Condenser is not background noise. It is a deep, continuous roar you feel in your teeth, in the soles of your feet through floors, and in window glass that buzzes at the wrong note. By the plant's eastern fan wall you'll have to shout and still won't really be heard. Across town, everyone just talks in a projected stage-voice all the time, because a lot of them are a little deaf from working there.

Bring:

* Good foam earplugs *and* over-ear protectors. You will want both together near the fans, one or the other everywhere else. Get them in a bright industrial color if you can — orange, yellow, lime. Locals wear theirs around their necks like necklaces off-shift. If yours are out and visible, people will correctly read you as sensible and plant-safe.
* Earplugs you can sleep in. The plant does not stop at night. In summer the fans run lower, but then the frogs take over.
* A notebook and pencil for the fan wall. Don't try to out-shout machinery. Workers use a lot of hand signals and lip-reading, learn to do the same.

Do not plan important, complicated, or private conversations for normal time. Plan them for the Quiet.

Every 90 minutes the compressors cycle down for 7 minutes. That is the town's real clock. In that sudden ringing silence you can hear dripping eaves, sparrows bursting out of vents, radios, kids, a loose chain clanking three streets away. Everyone unconsciously pauses and restarts around it. Long sentences are saved for the gap.

When you arrive, sync your watch to the cycle — ask anyone, they'll know to the minute — and save anything nuanced for those 7 minutes. Conversely, don't shout during the Quiet just because you got used to shouting. Your voice will carry absurdly far, and the whole town will hear you. And don't waste people's Quiet on trivia if you can help it. It's social time.

Even a few days unprotected will leave your ears ringing. A few years is what the locals have. Respect the protectors.

### 2. The Two Rules of Water

Learn them on day one, exactly as locals state them:

**1. There is no river.**
**2. Most of the water leaves.**

Water here arrives sideways: evening fog pooling in the basin until streetlights are halos, short rain squalls that fill the gutters for an hour, condensation ticking off corrugated roofs. Then the ground drinks it and everything is just damp again.

The Condenser wrings drinking water out of that fog, cleans and mineral-balances it, and seals most of it into tanker trucks that grind out through the rock cut twice a day for drier towns downcountry. That's the town's reason to exist.

What stays is rationed, gently but absolutely:

* **Drinking water is by blue token.** Find out before you arrive, or immediately from the company store or clinic, how a visitor gets an allocation. Do not assume you can just buy unlimited bottled water, and do not be the outsider who drains a house barrel. Carry a personal bottle and refill it only from token water.
* **Roof-catch water is unmetered, for washing.** Every house has a barrel with a lid. Every lid is against frogs. Every lid eventually fails to some frog. So: look before you dip, filter or settle wash water, and do not drink roof-catch without boiling/filtering it properly, no matter how clean it looks.
* **Never drink the drip from the main ducts.** This is the one strict, universally obeyed prohibition, and it will tempt you — the condensate is cold, clear, and delicious-looking on a warm noon. It has run over copper. It will poison you slowly. The same goes for casual licking/sipping off outflow pipes, which the deer do constantly and you should not imitate.

Do not expect to swim, fish, or fill up at a stream. The artificial holding pond is shallow, heron-claimed, and industrial. The settling tanks, culvert pools, cistern overflows, and the year-round puddle under Tank No. 2 belong to the frogs.

If you arrive by road, note the tanker departure times. Those trucks are also your most reliable ride out, and the cut is narrow. Don't wander it on foot when they're due.

### 3. Pack for a Place Where Nothing Ever Quite Dries

Think vacant-lot lush, not rainforest. Thin new soil over sharp, unweathered rock, waist-high ferns, alders all the same young height, moss making a bright spongy skin on anything still for a season. By noon the fog usually burns off clean and blue and lovely. By evening breath shows, cool air slides off the ridges, and every surface sweats again.

Bring:

* **Two pairs of footwear, rotating.** Boots will never fully dry overnight. Waterproof boots for streets/gravel, and something you can change into indoors. Dry socks in a sealed bag are luxury currency. Antifungal powder and attention to feet — trench-foot conditions are daily here.
* **Quick-drying synthetics/wool, no reliance on cotton or leather.** Laundry hangs wet, doors swell and stick, salt clumps, paper curls in drawers. That good paper journal, untreated leather bag, acoustic instrument, or stack of books will mildew and warp. Keep documents, medicines, and electronics in dry bags with silica gel. Use pencils and waterproof notebooks.
* **A warm layer even in summer.** Fog evening is cold in the basin.
* **Allergy medicine.** On the outflow (east) side of the plant, where the spent air sighs out perpetually moist, there's an accidental jungle: tree ferns, gunnera with child-sized leaves, six inches of moss, escaped orchids blooming in vent humidity. It's beautiful and people take cuttings from it like a library, but it is pollen, mold spores, and slick surfaces concentrated. Intake side is sparser and a bit stunned — less lush, less sneezy.
* **A headlamp with a low, wide beam and reflective clothing.** In evening fog a bright beam just makes a white wall. Streetlights halo out, the far end of Main Street erases, and deer come to lie on warm pavement. Drivers expect pedestrians but cannot see far.

Expect mildew at the base of north-facing walls, slippery moss on concrete and duct supports, and sidewalks still uncracked except where new roots lift them. Off the pavement, be careful: rock cuts are pale, banded grey and black, still sharp-cornered, with angular scree at the base that hasn't learned to settle. Slag heaps behind the maintenance yard are the same. Thin soil slips easily. This landscape hasn't been worn down yet, including its hazards.

### 4. Lodging and Getting Around a Half-Empty Grid

The town is urban on paper: paved numbered streets, painted curbs, storm drains, silver hydrants, a concrete school, dormitory blocks with communal balconies, a clinic, a company store with neon. In fact you can stand on Main Street at noon and count fourteen people, a truck, and three deer.

That sparseness is useful:

* **Choose your room by sound and air, not by view.** Furthest practical distance from the eastern fan wall for sleep and hearing. Hard-west is drier and quieter. Hard-east is warmer, wetter, junglier, and loudest. A room that vibrates at plant frequency will buzz all night.
* **Empty lots are not reliable shortcuts.** Between buildings are blackberry brambles, young pine, scrap-pallet gardens, and at least one poured cinema foundation that became a skate park and then a frog pond. In fog, a lot can hide water, roots, or sharp slag. Stick to streets after dark.
* **Move slowly in fog.** Familiar distances vanish. The plant remains your landmark — a huge galvanized hall, three riveted intake towers, red aircraft lights blinking on top, fans visibly pulling white air in slow spirals. Orient by it, and by sound: louder and more in-your-molars means closer/east.
* Watch for animals in the street. Nothing here is properly shy; there haven't been enough people or years to teach them.

### 5. Wildlife: Close, Unaffected, and In Your Water Barrel

Fauna has moved into every damp edge the plant creates. Swallows nest by the hundreds in warm duct joints and hunt in the fan wash. Crows sit on intake towers at dawn and briefly out-scream the machines. Black-and-gold salamanders commute solemnly along condensation channels — step around them, don't handle them with salty/sunscreened hands. Herons own the holding pond. Frogs colonize every accidental pocket of water and will be deafening on low-fan summer nights.

Rules of thumb:

* Secure hair, scarves, loose clothing near intakes and fans. Air literally leans eastward there. Stay behind rails; don't explore ductwork — it's hot, oily-smelling, loud enough to disorient, and full of nesting birds.
* Don't feed deer or try to pet them. They're tame by inexperience, not domestication, and they gather around outflow pipes and warm roads.
* Always lid water, food, and boots at night. Frogs, insects, and moss are opportunistic, not malicious.

### 6. Social Preparation

This is a small workforce keeping huge machines repainted against rust that hasn't quite won yet, producing water drop by drop at a surprisingly slow rate. Life runs on slow clocks: reservoir inches over weeks, alders a foot a year, the Quiet every hour and a half.

Bring patience, bring your own entertainment in waterproof form, and be visibly respectful of rationing, ear protection, and the Quiet. Ask where to stand to talk, where visitor tokens come from, and what shift pattern is running. Offer to wait for the next 7-minute gap for anything complicated — locals will think well of you for it.

At night, when the fans sigh back up after the Quiet and fog erases the street, you might hear — very loud and very close — an owl from the uncut trees beyond the last street. The whole town pauses for that, too.

Arrive dry-bagged, double-eared, double-shoed, with a bottle for token water and a timer set for 90 minutes. You'll fit in faster than you'd expect.
