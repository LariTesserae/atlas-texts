The Table is not dangerous the way a desert or a storm is dangerous. Nothing will attack you quickly enough for you to notice. Nothing will run. Nothing will decay, heal, burn, bleed, or hurry — including, eventually, you.

Prepare less for an emergency and more for erosion of your ability to tell that one is happening. Go in as if you are visiting a place where the normal evidence for "I am fine, I have time, I can turn back" will quietly stop being reliable.

### 1. Understand the main hazards before you climb

**Altitude is real even though the place is flat.** The approach is two days of scree and old, worn switchbacks. No peak, just a country lifted into the sky and polished down. The air is thin, cold in shade, surprisingly warm in sun. Expect broken, gasping sleep, stupid fingers, headaches, crisp shadows, and stars visible at noon if you look away from the sun. Acclimatize as you would for any high plateau. Do not let the gentleness of the terrain talk you into rushing the climb.

**There is no usable water on top.** Not "very little." None. It does not rain. The salt hollows are mineral crusts, not dry lakes. Every well-pit among the huts goes down to dust.

The local trick — pressing your mouth, just before dawn, to the coin of damp under a living grazer's chin where its slow breath has condensed — yields a taste of salt, iron, and wool, and will keep a shepherd alive in the sense that the shepherds are still technically commemorated by their huts and loom-frames. It will not hydrate a visitor. Bring every drop you will need, plus reserve, and carry the reserve as if the trip were twice as long as planned. Because subjectively, it may become so.

**Time and metabolism slow by participation.** This is the central fact. Birds overhead are descending, but at the rate of weather. A grazer's blink may take a day. A breath may take an hour to finish. A fire laid in the morning will still be at the same red eye in the evening. Your hunger will arrive and forget why it came. Your nails will stop bothering you. A cut will neither bleed properly nor heal.

It is not stopped time. It is time stretched until cause and effect stop holding hands.

**Sound dies.** The felt of the *ums* — the waist-high, six-legged grazers that cover the plain horizon to horizon — drinks noise. Your footsteps dull. Your clothes stop rustling. A shout falls apart within meters. You cannot rely on calling to a companion, hearing a fall, or judging distance by voice.

**Distance and counting become polite liars.** The middle of the Table feels like walking downhill while your ears and altimeter insist you have climbed another thousand meters. You will recount the same animal — same torn ear, same scar — in two places, not because it moved but because somehow it is shared between them. Shadows graze, stoop, and lie down while the animals above them stand on. Do not navigate by "I recognize that one."

### 2. Stage everything at the rim

Do not make your base in the middle of the herd.

Set camp below or just at the edge of the Table where the ground still behaves — where dropped stones still bounce promptly, dust settles at a normal rate, and a lit match burns in a normal time. Leave someone there if you possibly can. That person is not just rescue. They are your control sample.

At base, establish:

* **A hard turnaround time measured off the Table.** Not "when we feel tired." Not sunset. A clock kept at the rim, tended by someone who has not gone in, with an agreed absolute limit. If you can maintain a portal, tether, radio relay, or beacon at the rim, anchor it there, not in the herd.
* **A physical line in.** Run a bright, inorganic rope or tape line from the rim along your intended lane, anchored to rock cairns at intervals. The herd lanes shift only geologically, but *you* will lose track. Do not trust footprints in dust that hangs too long and settles too late.
* **Visual signals.** Headlamps, flags, mirror flashes, hand signs. Assume radios may work electronically but voices will sound wrong and near — test them among the animals before you depend on them. The felt swallows ordinary sound in open air, not necessarily a speaker pressed to bone or a tapped code on a shared rope. Bring whistles anyway; abandon them the moment they prove theatrical.

Agree on hourly companion checks that are deliberately stupid and mechanical: speak a full sentence at normal speed, count backwards from twenty, blink on command, walk ten paces and return. You are not checking morale. You are checking rate.

### 3. What to carry

Pack like a high-altitude desert traveler who cannot cook, cannot be rescued quickly, and cannot trust his own body clock.

* **Water in several small, sealed containers,** not one large bladder. If one is lost among the lanes you should not lose all. Include electrolytes — the damp under a grazer, the salt crusts, and the lichen diet of the place will pull your sense of thirst out of true.
* **Food that needs no fire and no waiting.** Ready-to-eat, high calorie, familiar. A stove is not useless, but combustion here settles into a patient ember; heating a meal may take an afternoon and teach you nothing except that you stayed too long. Do not plan to live off lichen, felt-parings, dung fuel, or the herd under any circumstances. Their whole ecology recycles scant moisture and centuries-old organic matter through bodies that spend almost nothing. Your gut has not learned that agreement, and the parasites in the felt — ticks like polished seeds, mites, tiny white spiders, moths — have had a very long time to wait for a warm mistake.
* **Sun and still-cold protection together.** At noon you can open your jacket; in shade your hands go clumsy. Bring real UV protection, lip cover, and sunglasses. The light is normal. That is part of the trap. It looks like an ordinary afternoon while the hawks take a season to fall.
* **Sealed clothing.** Tape cuffs. Tuck trousers. The undergrowth in each animal's coat is alive, patient, and mid-stride. You may not feel a tick attach because sensation itself can be delayed. Do full-body checks with a mirror and a companion at the rim, and then again the next day — a bite may become noticeable only after you leave.
* **A serious wound kit, and a plan to not need it.** Cuts do not bleed well and do not heal on the Table. That means infection risk without the warning of inflammation, and a minor injury becoming a permanent companion. Bring antiseptic, sterile dressings, closure strips, antibiotics if you know how to use them, and gloves. Avoid knives where you can. The tally hut has a knife set down under an unfinished notch. Leave the archaeology of good intentions alone.
* **Navigation that does not depend on the herd.** Compass, map of the rim, salt hollows, grooves, and hut clusters sketched from outside; altimeter treated as advisory only. Mark your turns with paired stones — one white, one black, like the old shepherd markers — and photograph each junction facing back toward the rim. The return view is the one you will need when every lane looks like every other lane of breathing felt.

### 4. Rules for moving among the ums

Walk slowly, which will happen anyway, but walk deliberately.

You will mostly not walk on rock. You will thread lanes between bodies, brush felt on both sides, duck under chins, step over legs where lichen has grown in one unbroken crust from hoof to stone. Do not break those crusts if you can avoid it. Do not try to push an animal aside. A dead one may be propping up a living neighbor; a living one may be a hundred years into not noticing you and then, halfway through tomorrow, begin to respond.

**Learn the warm ones and leave them alone.** Most of the herd is ambient temperature, cool as rock. Occasionally you will brush a flank and find deep mammalian heat, a smell of living skin, and — horribly — all the motionless flies around that animal suddenly make sense as having just arrived where they are. That animal is, on herd time, awake. Step back out of its lane. Do not touch its face, do not look for its eyes in the mat, do not wait to see if the head finishes turning toward you. You do not want to be present, hours later and slowed yourself, when that attention lands.

**Do not count.** Not legs — five planted and one folded into the belly felt will unravel you if you stare — not animals in a transect, not notches, not birds. Counting invites the Table to show you that arithmetic is a local custom. If you must survey, photograph and count later, at the rim.

**Do not assume dead is dead, or still is safe.** Mummified predators lie among the herd with teeth bared around throats they have not finished biting since before the huts were built. They have joined the general agreement, which does not mean they have renounced the bite; it means the bite is proceeding. Keep hands, face, and sleeping body out of any open mouth, any shadow that is behaving independently, and any lane where a long low shape crosses yours.

### 5. Eating, sleeping, and staying human

If you must overnight, use the lee of a *confirmed* dead, cold animal or a roofless hut, noted from a distance over several hours of rim-time. Line your sleep hollow as the shepherds did, but with your own sealed groundsheet — never directly with woven herd hair. Sleep with your rope line in hand. Sleep in shifts if you are paired. One person's job is to stay boringly awake, tap the line at intervals, speak aloud at full speed, and wake the other even if the other insists, reasonably and calmly, that they have only just closed their eyes.

Eat and drink by schedule, not appetite. Sip by the clock you brought from the rim. Urinate before you feel desperate; bladder signals, like hunger and pain, may arrive late and under-committed. Keep a written log with timestamps for water, food, companion checks, and landmarks. Your memory will smooth gaps because nothing here provides the punctuation of events.

Limit your first visit to hours. Not days. The second day is when visitors report the rule entering their joints. By then dust kicked up around your own boots hangs a little too long. Your shadow starts to make independent suggestions.

### 6. Absolute signs to leave immediately

Turn back on the line, without debate, if any of these occur:

* Your shadow gets ahead of you — moves toward a posture you have not taken, stoops or lies down first.
* A companion's blink, greeting, or answer is still completing after you have finished a ten-count.
* You cannot remember making the last log entry, though it is in your handwriting.
* A cut has neither bled nor hurt.
* The same marked animal appears on both sides of your lane.
* You feel no urgency about any of the above.

That last one is the most important. The Table does not produce panic. It stretches tenderness, hunger, violence, birth and death so thin they no longer interrupt the silence. Feeling peacefully at home among the ums, deciding there is no hurry, deciding to finish the notch in the wall neatly for whoever left the knife — these are not insights. They are symptoms.

Leave while leaving is still a decision your body can perform at human speed. Walk the rope. At the rim, stay under observation for at least a day; delayed blinks, delayed pain, and parasites may finish arriving after you do. Eat only when your off-Table companions say the interval has passed, rest when they say you have been awake too long, and do not return until your dust, on ordinary ground, falls the way dust should.

Nothing will chase you here. That is exactly why you must be the one to go.
