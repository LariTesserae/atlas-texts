Sallowmere Works is not dangerous in the way a desert or a high mountain is dangerous. Nothing there is trying to eat you, fall on you from a great height, or freeze you in a night. The danger is slower and more domestic: you will get wet, you will get lost three streets from where you started, you will put your weight in the wrong place, you will cut or dig or light or build the wrong thing, and the whole town — which is also a single enormous plant — will quietly make problems for everyone downstream of you.

Prepare for it as an industrial village built inside a living organism that has to be mown, pruned, drained and negotiated with every single day to remain habitable. The clearing you will stand in is the product. Everything else — the board, the fuel pellets, the paper stock that leaves by the rim railway — is by-product.

### 1. When to arrive, and where from

Always come in over the rim, by the Top End road. That is the dry end, near the school, the clerks' offices and the Cooperative store, and it is the only sensible point of entry. Do not try to cross into the bowl cross-country. From the ridge it looks like an unbroken green sea eight hundred metres wide with a few roofs in it. Under that canopy there is no soil, no path, and no firm ground — only rhizome, leaf-mould, moss, black sour wetness, and stems growing fast enough to push up through a boardwalk if they aren't lifted in time.

Time your descent for late morning or early afternoon.

From about 6 to 9 a.m. the bowl makes its own rain. Cool air trapped under the leaves all night condenses and drips steadily from every underside, roof, and leaf-cup. It is not heavy rain, but it is everywhere and inescapable, and it will find every gap in your clothing. By midday it has usually stopped and you get the town's normal weather: cool northern daylight, grey-blue above the rim, broken underneath into a clear, even shade by leaves broad enough to shelter a table for four.

Season matters more than weather:

**High summer** is the full experience — maximum green, maximum growth at roughly 10 cm a day on a good stem, maximum work. Boardwalks bow and are re-laid, cut gangs are out all day, trolleys run constantly to the Press House, washing takes two days to dry, and children go to school with cutting hooks on their backs. In August expect one week of uncountable moths at the lamps, then none.

**Winter** is the revealing season, and what the postcards show. The leaves are down, the town stands startled and exposed in a field of pale cut stems, the lamps make small pools in early dark, and you can actually see the layout: the Terraces following an old root artery, the boardwalk network, the Press House on its central knob of real rock. You will also see next year's curled leaf-fists already pushing. It is colder, darker, and much easier to navigate — and much less representative of living there.

Spring is the worst time to be a stranger. Everything is unfurling, hardening, and outgrowing last winter's root-map at once.

### 2. What to wear and carry

Think wet dockyard, not jungle expedition.

* **Waterproofs that work from above and below.** A broad hat or hood for the morning drip, a fully waterproof outer layer, and above all boots that will come up dark every time. The ground-mat is permanently spongy. If you step off the boardwalk - and you should not without a local with you - you will sink into root and mould, not mud in the ordinary sense. Bring at least three pairs of socks in sealed bags and rotate them ruthlessly. Trench-foot, fungal irritation, and clothes that never quite dry are the town's ordinary medical complaints.
* **Quick-drying, synthetic layers.** Cotton will stay damp for the duration of your stay and absorb the smell: green, cold, medicinal, like crushed stems and wet iron. You will smell of Sallowmere for days afterwards no matter what you do, but synthetics make it manageable.
* **Gloves.** Motherleaf sap is bitter and pervasive, used locally (boiled down) as a preservative so foul and effective no insect will touch treated timber. Do not get it in cuts, eyes, or mouth. Do not eat anything grown directly in the bowl. Local vegetables grow only in raised boxed planters of imported soil for a reason.
* **A latchable, waterproof bag for everything.** Houses, boardwalks, and roofs drip. Paper maps swell. Electronics need sealed cases. Nothing can be stored on a floor or in a cellar — there are no cellars, because what is under the mat will take a spade and then slowly close over the hole, sour and black and never dry.
* **A small light.** Not because it is magically dark — the light is ordinary daylight — but because the canopy breaks it into coins and takes it away early. Amber lamps line the boardwalk infrastructure for a reason. At night the town is quiet and muffled in a way that makes distance hard to judge.
* **No fire kit.** The Press House at the centre is the *only* building allowed a fire. It is corrugated iron for that reason. No camp stoves on a raft house, no candles as a charming local touch, no smoking on the boardwalk where a dropped end lands in leaf-mould and pressed board. Paraffin is sold at the shop under local rules — ask before you use any heat source at all.

Leave heavy, point-loading gear behind. Houses here stand on wide flat rafts of pressed leaf-board and iron-shod timber to spread weight across the rhizome mat. A traveler with a massive pack, hard narrow heels, spikes, or wheeled luggage that concentrates weight will damage both boardwalk and mat. Wide soles, soft luggage, distributed loads.

### 3. Moving around without doing damage

There are no streets. There is a raised boardwalk on screw-piles, and layered around it is everything else: insulated electric cable from the Works, sap-pipe, condensate pipe (hot — do not grab it as a handrail), the maintained drain-gap in the rhizomes, handrails, lamps, and a narrow-gauge trolley line that sits lightly and adjustably on the mat itself.

That means:

1. **Stay on the boardwalk unless invited off it.** A glade that looks empty is not waste ground. Sparseness is the first rule — everything needs room to be cut back. An empty-looking space may be a drip catchment, a future cutting lane, or over an arterial rhizome.
2. **Never, ever nick a whitewashed rhizome.** The Arterials are thick as a man's thigh, pale, jointed, numbered and whitewashed like city water mains. A cut in an arterial at the Top End drops sap pressure in the Terraces and makes a whole row of cistern taps sigh air. What looks like a root to trim may be plumbing. Children learn this before they learn much else. As a visitor, touch no rhizome with any blade.
3. **Expect the route to have changed.** The town map in the town hall is redrawn every winter in ink and pencil because the town underneath the town has moved. Stems come up under boardwalk sections, houses travel centimetres sideways over decades as the mat thickens under one corner and is cut away under another, front steps appear and disappear. Use lamps, the Press House chimney with its thin pale steam (which the leaves lean very slightly toward), and people — not fixed landmarks or an old map.
4. **Give way to work.** Duck cableway wires, step over pipes where marked, and get off the walk at crossings when a trolley bell rings once. The trolleys carry netted bundles of cuttings all day in season to the hydraulic presses. The Press House thumps through the boards under your feet more than through the air. That vibration is normal.
5. **Latch every door behind you.** Not against thieves or animals — there is almost no animal life — but because houses shift a millimetre in the night. An unlatched door will swing in the morning and let in the drip. You will also hear the place at night: a creak through a raft, a board settling, a pipe ticking as something thickens beneath it. Locals sleep through it like a river. You probably will not, the first night.

### 4. Living there for a few days

Arrange lodging at the Top End if you can. It is driest, easiest for luggage, and closest to the shop — which stocks tea, paraffin, bootlaces, replacement trolley couplings and postcards, which tells you honestly what level of self-sufficiency to bring. Bring any specialty food, medicine, batteries, and spare parts with you. Bring cash/trade acceptable at a Cooperative; do not assume supply runs are quick.

Water is abundant and needs respect. People collect leaf-water from favoured drip-lines and funnel it along split stems into barrels and cisterns. It sits in leaf-cups trembling and clear, and it is the town's supply. Ask which cistern you are drawing from, whether it is for drinking or washing, and do not contaminate a drip-line by washing boots, hands with soap, or clothes upstream of a barrel. Frogs in cisterns are a known occurrence, not a sign of disaster.

Expect to be offered — or gently rostered into — cutting hours. Every household owes them, every street has a list. Refusing all work while staying more than a day will mark you out badly. Accepting does not mean being handed a long curved knife and left alone: it usually means hauling netted cuttings, lifting and re-laying a boardwalk plank, holding, carrying, or clearing yellowed leaves before they collapse and smother what's under them. That is safe, useful, and how you will actually learn the place. Do not accept a cutting hook until someone has shown you, on that specific spot, what may be trimmed.

Do not dig, plant, hammer piles, or "helpfully" clear ferns from gutters. The ferns slow the drip. The small moss on a new roof within a month is expected. Gardens are boxed for a reason. Building permission here is really permission about the root-map under a proposed house.

### 5. Health, senses, and strangeness

Medically, Sallowmere is mundane: damp cold, mould spores, bitter sap irritating skin and stomach, sharp industrial tools, hydraulic presses, electric cables in wet conditions, slippery moss-filmed board, and falling weight when a huge leaf yellows, collapses and is taken down. Wear gloves, wash sap off promptly, keep cuts covered, do not drink sap or leaf-cup water from an unapproved source out in the bowl, and do not wander into the Press House yard — it is the only tall building, iron-clad, vent-bristling, and run as a factory floor.

Psychologically, be prepared for the two things every visitor remarks on. Second is the absence of birdsong. The monoculture offers little to eat, the sap is bitter, the cutting never leaves cover long, and so there are almost no animals: a heron occasionally standing out of proportion in a glade, a moorhen some years by the outflow treated as news, nothing else much. First is the smell, which will get into everything.

Voices do not carry. The canopy takes sound and does not give it back. You can feel oddly alone ten metres from a work gang, and oddly overlooked while standing in an apparently empty green cathedral. Do not shout to navigate. Walk back to a lamp or a junction and ask.

Do not bring seeds, soil, live plants, or uncleaned boots from another world into the bowl, and clean them thoroughly before you leave for anywhere else. Sallowmere has reached a hundred-and-fifty-year balance because it is one organism (or a family so intergrown the distinction fails) with almost nothing else in it. You are the most likely vector for breaking that, in either direction.

### 6. Leaving

Leave by the rim road in daylight, after the morning drip, and check your gear for leaf fragments, rhizome mould, and damp. Expect everything you own to need two days spread out somewhere dry before it smells neutral again.

And understand, before you ask a local what Sallowmere makes as the board and pellets go out on the railway, that they will probably look mildly puzzled. They are not being coy. The exports are what is left over after the real work — which is tomorrow's cutting, and the next lifting of the boardwalk, and the map being wrong by spring.

You are not visiting a town that happens to be overgrown. You are visiting the maintainers of a clearing inside something alive, settled and unfinished at the same time, growing ten centimetres today. Pack dry socks, keep off the whitewashed roots, and make yourself useful.
