This is No. 3 — Low Condenser. A small, sealed, underground greenhouse built over industrial machinery, about forty meters across and eighteen high, with engineered plants that live by drinking steam in near-total darkness.

It is not immediately lethal to an ordinary human. It is something more deceptive: hot, wet, silent, slow, and scalding in places that look safe. Most injuries here will come from heat exhaustion, a burn you didn't expect, or a slip in the dark — arrived at gradually, while you were waiting for something dramatic to happen.

Prepare for a sauna built inside a boiler room inside a fresh volcanic crack, that also happens to be a sterile botanical experiment.

### 1. Before You Go Down

**Get medically honest with yourself.**
The air will be hot, saturated, and dense enough to feel like it has weight. When humidity is that high, sweat stops cooling you. Your core temperature just rises, slowly and steadily, no matter how fit you are.

Do not visit with heart or blood-pressure problems, respiratory issues, heat sensitivity, pregnancy, fever, or if you're dehydrated, hungover, or have been dimension-sick in the last day. There is no easy, fast exit — it's a galvanized switchback stair bolted into a pit, and the handrails get too hot to hold continuously.

Plan on a *short* first visit: 20-30 minutes inside, maximum. This is not a place to acclimate by pushing through. Heat illness here will hit while you're far from the surface, in the dark.

Hydrate and take electrolytes *before* you descend, and use the bathroom before you descend. There are no facilities below except a control shed with a kettle and a clipboard, and heed the stencil on the wall: NO OPEN DISCHARGE. This is a closed water system. Do not add anything to it — no draining bottles, no spitting, no urinating in a corner, no opening valves to "see what happens." Everything is recirculated.

**Decontaminate like you're entering a clean room.**
This chamber has no insects, no rodents, no frogs, no larvae in the warm water cupped in the leaves, no eggs under the fronds. That sterility is not an accident. You are the most likely animal vector it will ever meet.

Shower, wear clean clothes that haven't been worn outdoors, clean your boots of all soil and seeds, empty pockets of crumbs, tissues, and plant matter. Do not bring fresh food down. Do not bring leather or untreated wood that could carry spores if you can avoid it. You can destroy years of isolation by tracking in mold, mites, or fern spores on a cuff.

And plan to decontaminate on the way *out*, too. At the north end, young plants are already colonizing a fresh fracture, orienting not toward light, but down toward heat seams. Do not take a souvenir fern, a leaf cutting, or mineral crust. You do not know what engineered thing you're exporting to a world with sun and pollinators.

### 2. What To Wear And Carry

Everything you bring will get wet, hot, and filmed with mineral within an hour. Dress for protection, not comfort.

**Clothing:**
- Light, long sleeves and long trousers in a fabric that won't melt or sag when saturated — thin merino or technical workwear is better than cotton jeans, which become heavy and chafing. You want coverage against hot drips from ceiling pipes, accidental brushes with hot copper, and glass-sharp new rock.
- Waterproof boots with aggressive tread and ankle support. The floor is wet black stone in fresh polygonal plates, with white and ochre mineral crusts. It's slick, uneven, cracked, and in places warm underfoot. No smooth soles, no open toes, ever.
- Two pairs of gloves: thin heat-resistant work gloves for the entrance stair and handrails — you will need to shift your grip constantly — and a clean pair for below if you must handle anything. The white mineral scale on the rails makes them both burning and slippery.
- A hard hat or bump cap. There is a ceiling 18 meters up with condensing pipes, a choked light slit, and new fractures in the walls whose faces are still glassy and able to cut. Things drip, and occasionally, things flake.

**Light:**
Your eyes will adjust, and keep adjusting, and never quite finish. The only natural light is a diluted grey smear that never reaches the floor. The rest is amber pilot lamps, red valve eyes, and one caged bulb.

Bring a headlamp, but bring the *right kind*: low-lumen, diffuse, preferably with a deep red mode. A bright white beam will blind you on wet stone, fog instantly, destroy your dark adaptation for ten minutes at a time, and turn the chamber into flat glare and black pits. These plants live without sun; don't blast them — or the gauges behind fogged glass — with one.

Bring two lights. Electronics should be sealed/waterproof. Lenses, glasses, and camera sensors will fog the second you descend. Anti-fog wipes and a dry microfiber cloth in a sealed bag are not optional.

**Instruments and safety kit:**
- A portable thermometer/hygrometer, ideally with wet-bulb reading. If wet-bulb is climbing toward body temperature, leave. Your feelings are not a reliable gauge in here.
- A personal O2/CO2 monitor if you have one. The description is iron and wet stone on the mouth, machinery felt in teeth and sternum, a floor that "exhales" on cycle — assume the air may be CO2-enriched and oxygen-displaced near the floor and in low corners. The dense feeling is a warning, not an atmosphere to trust.
- A timer/watch. The whole place runs on a ~45 minute cycle. Set it the moment the pilot lamps brighten. That is your clock for everything else.
- Insulated water bottle, filled before entry. Do NOT drink condensation, leaf pools, or pipe water. It's warm mineral water that has run over copper returns beaded green, industrial mains, and engineered leaf surfaces. It will be full of dissolved metals and treatment chemicals, however clean it looks.
- Small first aid for scalds and heat: sterile non-stick dressings, clean water for cooling a burn (in your bottle), oral rehydration salts. Treatment for a steam burn starts with getting *off* the hot floor, not with finding a kit in the shed.

Communications: radio will be unreliable through stone, sprayed concrete ribbed with steel, and pipe bundles. Go with a buddy, leave a surface watcher, agree on a hard return time, and log in on that clipboard in the shed — even if the last entry is under a film of water.

### 3. The Descent

The stair is where the place introduces itself. It's a fresh pit — black, sharp basalt and broken concrete with no rubble slope and no dust to soften edges. Do not lean on walls, do not run a hand along rock to steady yourself. It will cut.

Go slow. Test each landing; the mineral film is slick. Shift grip on the rail every few seconds. If you start feeling dizzy on the stairs, stop on a landing and go back up. Descending further into gathered heat will not help.

At the bottom, pause. Let temperature, humidity, and darkness equalize. Your breathing will feel like a decision. Breathe slowly through your nose if you can; panic breathing in saturated hot air accelerates overheating. Let the individual pilot lamps separate from the black before you move.

### 4. Moving Inside

**Stay on the catwalk.** It crosses on low steel legs for a reason. The floor rules are learned in the body:

1.  When floor vents sigh, be off the floor entirely. The pump cycle sends warm water through overhead lines, condensation thickens across the dome, and floor vents exhale. That exhale can be steam and displaced bad air.

2.  Do not touch, step on, or lean over the bright white and ochre crusts. They bloom around vents and are *thinner than they look over scalding pockets*. What appears to be solid mineral can be a shell over superheated water. A boot through one is a deep scald plus a fall.

3.  Do not touch the pipes to steady yourself. Insulated mains, copper returns, and a red pressure line whose paint is blistering from heat all run above and half-buried below. The roots of the plants grip them and whiten where they're crusted — that's your visual cue for "too hot for skin."

4.  Watch above as well as below. Condensation forms slowly on the cooler ceiling pipes — a bead swells for minutes, then falls. In some corners it becomes a fine local shower. It is not cold rain. It's warm, mineral-laden, and startling enough in the dark to make you flinch into a hazard. Don't stand under copper lines.

Walk a full circle around plants if you must observe them, but do not touch the leaves. They are three meters long, rubbery, near black-green, perfect and unbitten — because nothing is allowed to bite them, and because their surface is part of the condensing system. Oils from skin, a torn edge, a introduced microbe can ruin a specimen that took invisible time to grow. The hand-brushes laid out in size order in the shed are for trained pollination; without animals, these plants likely depend on careful human work. Do not improvise.

Keep voices low, not out of reverence, but practicality: the silence is almost total. Sound arrives slowly — a tick of expanding metal, a single drop absorbed by moss before it can strike stone, a faint electrical hum. A shout will not help you hear a vent sigh or a pump starting.

### 5. The Cycle

Every three quarters of an hour or so, with no alarm: pilot lamps brighten a fraction. A distant pump breathes in. Condensation thickens all at once.

For two to three minutes a soft manufactured rain drifts plant to plant, darkening leaves, beading on the catwalk, raising a smell of hot stone and green sap almost sweet. The great leaves tip one by one and pour their held pools to their roots.

This is the most beautiful moment, and the most dangerous to be casual. Floor is wettest, catwalk is beaded, vents exhale, air is hottest and thickest, visibility drops as your lights hit falling water.

Best practice: when the lamps brighten, move to the catwalk, find a handhold that isn't a pipe, plant your feet, and *wait it out*. Don't try to photograph through it, don't cross the floor to get closer to a tipping leaf. Enjoy it from stillness. When the pump subsides and lamps dim, everything is slightly wetter and slightly hotter than before — including you. Check your timer, your buddy, and your own pulse then. That's your decision point to leave.

Do not expect anything else to happen quickly. A leaf takes a week to open. A fern unfurls over days. If you came to watch growth, you will be disappointed; if you came to understand a weather system that a building makes for plants that have relearned direction — warmth instead of light — you need patience and very little movement.

### 6. Leaving And After

Climb out before you feel exhausted, because the stair will exhaust you. Hot rails, slick landings, and rising *into* trapped heat under the dome as you ascend to the slit is the worst part physiologically.

On the surface: cool down gradually, don't chug ice water and collapse. Continue electrolytes. Check skin for scalds you didn't feel — saturated heat numbs perception — especially ankles, shins, palms. Check boots and clothing for pale shoots, fern fragments, or mineral mud and clean them before traveling onward.

Log your exit time, any crusts you saw cracking, any new drips or young plants in the fracture. In a place this new — its pit walls unworn, its ceiling part raw stone, its plastic chair still slowly warping in the heat — observations matter. It's still in the first sharp youth of becoming something else.

Visit it like a guest in a sealed terrarium that could cook you politely: clean, covered, lit dimly, hydrated, timed, and on the catwalk. Leave nothing but a dry signature, take nothing but the memory of rain that never came from a sky.
