This is not a ruin to explore, and it's not a garden to walk in. It's a working factory whose workforce, building, plumbing, and power supply are mostly the same single living plant.

Think village-green sized, sunk in a bowl, filled edge to edge. From the levee around it you will only see canopy. Inside, under three or four layers of leaf, it is permanently green-shaded, warm, dripping wet without rain, and surprisingly loud. Riveted engine houses, cylindrical presses, copper pipes, concrete sumps and gauges are still there, but trunks grow through the roofs, hollow vines continue broken pipes at exactly the same diameter, valves are turned by stems thickening around their spokes, and roots have made the concrete plinth into islands.

Most of it, followed through the root grafts, is one immense many-trunked organism. It keeps the factory geometry and doesn't crush the main shaft. In return the pump keeps bringing it marsh water, and the presses only take the amber pulp it sheds anyway. The human tenders don't really operate it. They negotiate with it, mostly by pruning.

Prepare accordingly: you are entering an industrial site, a wetland, and one animal's body, all at once.

### Before You Arrive

**Get permission and a tender to go with you.** This is non-negotiable. There are no paths to follow independently, no map that stays true until evening, and no safe way for a stranger to tell a decorative vine from a pressurized water main. The tenders live/work in sheds and a control hut at the edge. Meet them there, not inside.

**Decontaminate like you're entering a hospital ward.** This is the most important multiverse-specific rule.

The whole bowl is essentially one organism and a tiny, specialized ecology that has balanced itself around it for 50-100 years. There is no bare earth, no spare light, no competing understory. A foreign fungal spore, insect egg, seed in your tread, or pollen on your jacket from another world could be a catastrophe - or, just as likely, the plant will treat you as food/infection and seal off the section you're standing in.

Before visiting:
- Clean, new-ish clothes with no soil, seeds, or plant matter on them
- Scrubbed footwear, no mud from anywhere else
- No live plants, cuttings, fruit with seeds, pets, or biological samples to trade
- No insecticide, herbicide, fertilizer, strong perfume, or antibacterial spray on you. Chemical signals are probably language here, and yours will be shouting nonsense.
- Shower off anything strongly scented. You will leave smelling of sap and iron anyway.

Check allergies. Sap, resin, pollen from the pods, algae, and mold are in the air constantly. If you have asthma, plant-resin allergies, or mold sensitivity, do a very short test visit to the levee first. The wet green air is hard to breathe for some people - warm, iron-tasting, close.

Make sure tetanus is up to date. There's old steel, old concrete, stagnant water, and a thousand small chances to cut yourself. Any cut will get wet and dirty immediately.

### What To Wear

Accept that you will be damp within minutes and steamed damp within an hour. Dress for a tropical glasshouse crossed with a machine shop.

- Light, quick-drying long sleeves and trousers. You will be *pushing* between stems all day. Bare arms get scratched, sap-stained, and bitten by the few specialized stem-insects that exist. Tuck trousers into socks.
- Soft, flat, waterproof ankle boots or well-fitting water shoes with good grip. You need to feel the springy root-mat through your soles - you'll feel the deep pump that way before you hear it - and you need to not tear it. No cleats, spikes, heavy lug soles, or rigid hiking boots that crush roots. No sandals: black pools, sumps, and sharp cane conveyor slats are hidden under leaves.
- Wool or synthetic socks, and a full spare set in a waterproof bag outside the bowl. Trench foot is a real risk on a long visit.
- Thin work gloves you can take off easily. Good for pushing stems and gripping algae-slick metal, bad if you need to feel whether a vine is hollow, pressurized, or dead. Tenders will often work bare-handed for that reason.
- A light brimless cap. A wide hat will snag constantly and there is no direct sun below canopy anyway. No sunglasses needed - light arrives already sieved to green shade.
- Hearing protection you can put in and take out quickly. See below.
- Nothing dangling: no loose scarves, lanyards, long hair untied. Gears mesh somewhere under the foliage, conveyor slats of flattened cane and old belt rubber move at plant speed, and stems knock together under pressure. Slow does not mean weak. A press that closes over an hour will still crush a hand that was told to wait.

Leave jewelry, good leather, and anything you mind staining permanently amber-green at home. Sap and pulp do not wash out well, and your exit clothes will smell of crushed green for days.

### What To Bring, And Not Bring

Bring:
- Water and electrolytes in a sealed bottle. It is hot, humid work shouting over noise. Do *not* drink the bowl water, from leaf axils, pipes, sumps, or the black lenses between roots. It's marsh water, condensed machine water, iron, sap, algae, and frog habitat.
- Food eaten *outside* on the levee. Don't eat inside unless a tender offers. Food waste is fertilizer, and fertilizer is an uncontrolled engineering instruction to this plant. Same rule for urinating/defecating - use facilities at the sheds before you go in. Every liter of light and nutrient here is already accounted for.
- A waterproof pouch for phone/electronics. Condensation forms on every cool surface. Assume anything electronic will be misted, dripped on, and filmed. Paper notebooks swell shut; use a wax pencil and waterproof slate if you need notes.
- A small first aid kit with waterproof dressings, antiseptic, and tweezers for thorns/splinters.
- A hand slate or just your hands for communication.

Don't bring:
- Cutting tools. This is crucial. Cutting *is* engineering here. A casual snip to clear your way can be the equivalent of opening a valve, severing a pipe, or amputating a finger. To fix a leak they coax a new vine around it for a season. To increase pressure they thin a canopy panel. Only tenders cut, and only after pointing and waiting for agreement. If you are handed pruners, repeat their gesture back and wait for the nod.
- Marking tape, chalk, GPS pins, string trails. Paths close for physiological reasons, markers get grown over or constrict stems.
- Open flame, smoking materials. It's wet, but the resin pods and amber pulp are the factory's product. Don't test flammability inside a wooden engine.
- Any drone or probe that will push through canopy panels. Light is power here. Tearing a skylight changes transpiration lift and pressure for the whole works.

### Entering And Moving Inside

Enter from the levee where the tenders indicate, in the morning if you can.

Go in single file, behind your guide, hands visible. Learn their signs before you descend: short, broad gestures because everyone is half-deafened inside. At minimum: *Point, Wait, Cut, Stop, Back, Look Up.* Shouting is possible but exhausting - the roar is at conversational height, continuous: water hissing through a thousand leaf nozzles, hollow stems knocking, wood creaking under hydraulic load, leaves rasping as the canopy inches toward light, and the pump's bass thump in your feet.

Earplugs help on a long visit, but don't seal yourself off completely. You need to hear a wet crack as a pod splits overhead, a change in hissing that means a pressure surge, and your guide trying to get your attention. Use low-reduction plugs or one in, one out.

Movement is pushing, not walking. Expect ankle-deep springy root-mat, water a handspan below it, and occasional breakthroughs: still black lenses between roots, and shallow concrete sumps made opaque by algae. Probe with your foot. Never step onto a black or green flat surface assuming it's solid - it could be water, a sump, or a gauge glass filmed over.

Watch for:
- **The floor rising.** By afternoon sap pressure builds and the whole floor lifts perhaps two inches. Pools shrink, roots swell, your morning path may be closed at dusk and a new gap opened elsewhere. This is normal. It means you must plan to leave *before* late afternoon, with your guide, by whatever route is open then. Do not insist on going back the way you came.
- **Swollen pressurized vines.** Many pipes end mid-air and are continued by hollow woody vines. They knock, shiver, and sometimes sweat at the bark-ring joints. Don't lean on them, hang bags from them, or use them as handrails unless shown. A trunk that was trained square up scaffolding is structural. A leaf shaped by a radiator grille is a working heat exchanger.
- **Pods and presses.** Ordered thickets around the presses swell, split with a wet crack, and drop pale amber pulp onto conveyors. It's sticky, slippery, and heavier than it looks. Don't stand under a ripening thicket, don't taste the pulp - resinous, possibly purgative, and it's the harvest, not a snack.
- **Slow machinery.** Valves turn because stems in their spokes swell and thin over hours. Things that look stationary are under load. Keep hands out of handwheels, gear housings, leaf-chutes, and woven screens. Point, don't touch.
- **Quiet wildlife.** There is almost none, and that absence is part of the place. A grey heron sometimes stands on the tallest press in the morning, frogs live in leaf-axil pools and call one note at a time, soft insects live *inside* stems. Don't go looking in axils with your fingers, don't startle the heron, and don't swat - if something is on you, show a tender.

Don't sit or stand still for long. Apart from missing the pressure changes, you are a warm, damp, CO2-emitting object in a place that responds to warmth - hedgerows have grown straight along buried warm cables. A tender will keep you moving or place you deliberately on concrete islands.

### Health, Time, and State of Mind

Heat exhaustion sneaks up because you're never dry, so you forget you're sweating. Drink on schedule, not on thirst. Headache, nausea, or sudden irritation in that noise are signs to get out to the levee.

Noise fatigue is real. You leave with ears ringing even after an hour. Voices get hoarse. Tempers get short when everyone is miming. Keep visits short - two to three hours inside is plenty for a first ordinary human visit. Longer stays are for tenders who have learned to read the creaks.

Disorientation is also real. No horizon, no sky except ragged skylights, no straight line that wasn't grown, landmarks that swell shut. If you panic, stop, put a hand (gently) on your guide's indicated stem, and breathe. Do not thrash through growth to get out. Thrashed stems are damage the plant will spend a season sealing.

Do not plan to sleep inside. Night isn't dormant - the plant has not gone dormant in living memory, even for winter - and your morning landmarks will have literally moved. Sleep in the sheds or on the levee, if anywhere.

### Etiquette With The Organism

Treat your assumptions about speed as wrong. It studies a machine for fifty years and learns its habits. It learns your visit in root-pressure and canopy angle, not in conversation.

That means:
- No sudden changes. Don't open a canopy panel, divert a chute, or block a skylight for a photo. Light is fuel and steering.
- Don't expect a shutdown for your convenience. Turning it off would mean persuading one being to go dormant. Maintenance is done live, slowly, by negotiation.
- If something leaks, bursts, or creaks alarmingly to you, point and step back. A leak may be condensation, irrigation, or a self-sealing sap response that takes days.
- Ask before photographing with flash. Sudden bright light in permanent green shade will make every tender flinch and may not be neutral to light-accounted leaves.

You may be tempted to think of it as sentient in a human way. Don't anthropomorphize, but do behave as if you are inside a body whose owner notices weight, warmth, chemistry, and injury, and will respond thoroughly, if not quickly.

### Leaving

Climb out by the route your guide chooses, even if it's not your entry. Wash footwear and hands at the edge - again, to protect other places from *this* place's specialized algae, spores, and stem-insects when you travel onward. Change into dry clothes on the levee. Drink something, sit in open daylight, and let your ears recover.

From above, it will do what it did when you arrived: disappear. Nothing but a low bowl of green, vibrating almost imperceptibly, letting off a thin plume where warm water falls back through leaves into shadow.

You will be damp, a little deaf, green-smelling, and probably humbled by how loud and competent something can be while moving two inches in an afternoon. That's a successful visit.
