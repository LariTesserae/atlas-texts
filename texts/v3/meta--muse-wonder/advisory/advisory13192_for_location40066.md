This is not a destination you hike to. It's a destination you sidle into, sideways, for a few minutes at most — and most of your preparation is about making yourself smaller, slower, quieter, and less interesting than you normally are.

It's 40 centimeters wide, 6 meters long, cut by accident between a crusher house and a fresh-blasted andesite face at 3,800 meters. Outside is thin, freezing, violently bright high plateau. Inside is 40 degrees, dark in under a meter, loud through the bone, and already completely occupied: lizards on the ledges, swallows at the mouth, beetles, earwigs and wolf spiders in the cracks, and at the far end a wardrobe-sized pocket carpeted shoulder-to-shoulder with hundreds of mouse-eared bats.

An ordinary human is too big, too warm, too loud, and too panicky for this place by default. Prepare to correct all four.

### 1. Before you even go to the plateau

**Acclimatize. This is non-negotiable.**
At 3,800m the air will already make you stupid, headachy, and short of breath. Inside the seam you'll add heat, ammonia, dust, a respirator, and claustrophobia. That combination can turn mild altitude sickness into a faint in a place where you cannot fall down properly and cannot be dragged out forwards.

Spend at least 3-4 days ascending and sleeping high before you attempt it. No alcohol, heavy hydration, know your own altitude symptoms. If you have a headache that won't quit, nausea, a cough, asthma, or you're immunocompromised — do not go in. Guano dust is not worth it, see below.

**Get permission and a schedule.**
This is the back wall of a working processing plant. You need plant permission, site induction, and a human spotter who knows exactly where you are. Learn the plant's rhythm: when the crushers idle, when conveyors run, when blasting or truck movements happen, and especially when shift change is. That brief early-evening lull is real, and it's also when the bats wake and leave one by one. You do *not* want to be blocking their only exit then.

Ask about the bats' season locally. Avoid maternity season if the colony has pups, and be extra conservative in the cold season when they're relying on that stored industrial heat to survive freezing nights. Your visit is trivial. Their roost choice is not.

**Medical prep:**
- Up-to-date tetanus — that rock face is fresh, unweathered, and razor-sharp.
- Talk to a travel doctor about rabies pre-exposure vaccination. You will not touch the bats, ever, but in a space that tight an accidental brush, scratch, or a panicked bat into the face is a medical emergency, not a story.
- Talk about histoplasmosis and other guano-associated fungal risks. Old, dry, powdered guano disturbed underfoot releases spores and ammonia in a wave. If you've been told to avoid caves/bat roosts for lung reasons, obey that here.

Test yourself for claustrophobia somewhere safe first, in a 40cm gap you can walk out of. In the seam you will have rock at your back and a hot industrial wall — or rock on both sides — a handspan from your face. There is nowhere to turn around at the deep end. You exit by reversing, slowly.

Measure yourself sideways. Chest depth, hips, shoulders turned. Many adults simply do not fit past the shoulder-turn point. That's the seam deciding for you. Don't force it.

### 2. What to wear and carry

Think: smooth, slim, disposable, and nothing dangling.

**Outside layers, inside layers:** On the plateau you'll need serious sun and wind protection — UV is brutal at altitude, heat vanishes the instant wind crosses. Wide hat, SPF, warm windproof layer for waiting. Strip all of that before entry. Inside is a stored-heat oven.

For entry itself:
- A single thin, long-sleeved, smooth-fabric shirt and trousers you are willing to ruin. No fleece, no toggles, no cargo pockets, no backpack, no belt buckle bulk. Fabric will catch on blast fractures. Long sleeves are for abrasion, not warmth.
- Thin gloves. The andesite will learn your hands otherwise and cut them.
- Low-profile shoes with good grip. Guano slope gives slightly and is treacherously soft. No bulky boots if they compromise your profile — follow plant rules for the yard, then change if permitted at the mouth.
- A well-fitted respirator, ideally P100 / FFP3, fit-tested. A paper dust mask is theater here: mineral dust, diesel soot, powdered guano, tan acrid fines that get into the seams of your skin. Be aware a respirator at altitude in 40°C heat makes breathing feel much harder. That's your hard time limit.
- Sealed, low-profile eye protection. Dust plus ammonia plus sudden swallow or bat movement near your face.
- Ear protection. The crushers aren't just heard, they're transmitted through rock into sternum and molars. The generator hum sits at the edge of pain. Wear plugs that take the edge off industrial noise — you'll be communicating by pre-agreed hand signals anyway. Speech inside is useless and disturbing.
- No headlamp on. No flash. Bring a tiny red light in a pocket only for a genuine emergency exit, pointed at the floor, shielded by your hand. A white torch beam can panic bats into a space too small for flight and injure dozens.

Leave behind: perfume, scented sunscreen on exposed skin at entry, insect repellent (poisonous to the bats and invertebrates who live there), food, a water bottle strapped to you. Hydrate heavily *before*, with electrolytes. Carry nothing in your hands.

Decontamination matters in both directions. Clean, dry footwear and clothes going in — bat colonies across worlds are devastated by fungi travelers carry on gear, white-nose syndrome being the Earth example. Coming out you will be covered in guano dust. Bag your entry clothes separately, brush off outside away from the mouth, shower and wash hair before you touch your face, eat, or enter another cave/roost.

### 3. Timing and approach

Go with a spotter stationed at the yard side, in sight of the mouth, with a timer. Agree: maximum 10-15 minutes inside, a tug/signal system, and that if you don't emerge/reverse-signal in time they raise plant help. Never go alone. Never go when blasting, heavy rain (there's almost none, but flash runoff changes everything), or high wind is throwing plant dust straight in.

Approach mid-morning on a working day is often the least-bad compromise: plant in full roar — which the animals are already habituated to — bats still settled in daytime roost, swallows commuting, but not yet the worst midday heat in the pocket. A night visit after the bats have left sounds kinder, and the empty seam — hot rock, spiders repositioning — is fascinating, but you miss the colony and floodlight moth-hunting changes animal behavior at the mouth. If your purpose is to meet the place as described, go by day, briefly.

Walk the service track slowly to find it by temperature first. You'll pass a shadow that breathes hot air at midday. Stop. Let your eyes adjust, let the lizards see you coming. Duck under the swallow nests at the mouth — don't touch the mud, don't stand exhaling up into them.

### 4. How to be inside

The seam has one rule: **calm is contagious because flight is impossible.**

Move sideways, inches at a time, exhaling on moves. Keep your back aware of which surface is behind you — hot wall vs sharp rock. No sudden turns of the head. No reaching into cracks. Wolf spiders fold their legs tight, beetles pack into fissures, lizards flatten and pulse their throats. If you're seeing animals flee, you're moving too fast.

As you pass the first meter the light fails. Don't compensate with light. Feel. After the shoulder-turn there's no comfortable turnaround — commit only if you are calm enough to reverse the whole way without rushing. Disturb the guano slope as little as possible: step where it's already compressed, don't scuff. Every disturbance is a fresh ammonia wave in a space with no ventilation except that slow breath at the mouth.

At the bat pocket: stop before you think you should. You will feel their collective body heat before you can make them out, like an oven door cracked. There is no bare rock, just a continuous mass, ears twitching in rotation, adjustment ripples passing slowly through the cluster. Thin social squeaking under the crusher grind. Stay low, stay still, breathe shallowly through your respirator, count to thirty in your head. That's the visit. More time doesn't get you more intimacy, it just adds your heat and CO2 to an already used-air pocket and raises their stress.

Warning signs to reverse immediately, no debate: dizziness, metallic taste, headache spiking, respirator panic, ammonia burning eyes despite protection, bats lifting heads in unison / squeaking rising sharply, a swallow trapped bouncing off walls, or any rock dust trickling from above. Spalls are rare — a slab a decade — but fresh faces shed.

Reverse out exactly as you came. Don't turn to "have a last look." At the mouth, emerge slowly into glare — you'll be blind, sunburned in minutes, and suddenly freezing once wind hits sweat. Your outside layer goes back on immediately.

### 5. Afterward

Sit down in the yard. Water, electrolytes, shade. Altitude + heat + respirator work can hit you five minutes *after* you feel fine. Check hands and forearms for cuts, wash them well. Any bat contact, bite, scratch, or saliva in eyes/mouth: wash 15 minutes with soap and water and seek urgent rabies post-exposure care that day, wherever in the multiverse that means — tell them “bat roost, tight contact.”

Write notes later, not inside. Check in with plant staff that you’re out.

If you prepare right, the paradox is what stays with you: an industrial accident of a crack where nothing grows and everything is imported — moths to floodlights, flies to droppings, bats to waste heat — that has become calmer and older-feeling than the plant that made it. You don’t conquer it or explore it. For a few slow minutes you’re allowed to be one more warm body, wall to wall, breathing carefully, and then you back out and let the lull at shift change empty it on its own terms.
