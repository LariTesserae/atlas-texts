Before you go to the Basin, re-train your instincts. Almost everything an ordinary off-world human relies on — quiet, dryness, empty space, fixed addresses, green things, being left alone when you sleep — does not exist there. Not as a crisis. As the normal, stable, 300-year-old condition.

You are not visiting a very large city. You are entering a single, continent-sized organism that happens to be made of 90 billion people, machines, engineered animals, and buildings that are rebuilt while you stand in them.

Prepare accordingly.

### 1. Protect Your Hearing Before You Can See It

You will hear the Basin 12 kilometers out, from the rim road, while the map still says "empty country." First as pressure in your sternum, then as a roar.

At street level it is 105-110 decibels, continuously. That is not loud background noise. That is physical injury. Unprotected, your hearing will be damaged within an hour, and conversation by shouting is impossible.

Locals have a selective gate grown in the inner ear. It cuts the city to a brown murmur and only passes voices that are addressed to you, bone-conducted and subvocalized. You cannot get this on arrival. It is *grown*, not installed.

So, months before travel:

* Get fitted for high-grade external equivalents: sealed ear gates with active cancellation, bone-conduction pickups, and a subvocal throat mic. They will not be as good as the implant. They will be bulky, hot, and will fail in the wet if you buy cheap. Buy two sets anyway.
* Practice using them until switching filters is reflexive. You will need modes for: crowd-flow warnings, one-person conversation, transit announcements, and near-total mute for sleep.
* Put them on at the rim, *before descent*, and do not take them out outdoors to "get used to it." There is no getting used to it.
* Learn Basin sign. Children there learn by age three to sign with their whole forearms while their mouths make tiny subvocal shapes. You don't need fluency, but you need the 200 or so survival signs: STOP, FLOW THIS WAY, DANGER DEMOLITION, I AM VISITOR, I NEED GUIDE, MEDICAL, WATER, EXIT UP, DO NOT TOUCH SWARM. Your voice will not work as your primary tool.

Bring a waterproof writing slate and stylus. Paper does not exist outside sealed archives — it rots and is eaten almost instantly. Radios and open-air transmission are also unreliable in hot, wet, noisy air. Most communication that matters is physical, carried, or bone-conducted at very short range.

### 2. Prepare Your Body for Hot, Wet, Heavy Air

The Basin floor is 40 meters *below* outside sea level. Air pools there. It is warm, saturated, and slightly pressurized. Add the waste heat of 90 billion bodies and dark roofs, and the Basin makes its own weather.

Every day, between roughly 2 and 4 p.m., it rains — not a shower, a warm sheet-flood that closes the upper bridges, turns plazas to rivers in minutes, and makes every wall a waterfall. Then the pumps take it all down through bus-sized gratings to be reused as cooling water.

There is no "staying dry." Clothes are designed to be worn wet.

Pack:

* Only synthetic, quick-shedding clothing with no cotton, leather, wool, or natural fiber that can host growth. Sealed seams. Everything will be damp within an hour of arrival and will remain damp.
* Anti-fungal skin treatments, barrier creams, and spare seals for ears, eyes, and any medical ports. Mold would eat the Basin if the cleaner-fauna weren't faster. You are slower than the cleaner-fauna, but you are still edible.
* Enclosed footwear with armored soles that can grip wet ceramic, solar glass, and bio-concrete. Do *not* plan to walk barefoot, even though locals might. The mite, ant, and roach swarms — biological, mechanical, and by now indistinguishable — will groom your soles, clean between your toes, inventory your pockets, and clean your ears if you lie down. For a local this is hygiene. For an unadapted visitor, it can mean allergic reactions, minor skin loss, and panic.
* Get medical screening for reactions to: algae pastes, vat-meat and vat-grain, filter-kite gut protein (a major food source), eel meat, bio-concrete sweat, and common swarm species. There are no plants outdoors, no pollen except as a classified biohazard, no weeds, no moss, no trees. Your gut and immune system have never met this ecology. Start on Basin-approved probiotics and anti-allergen protocols weeks in advance, under a travel physician who has actually treated Basin returnees.
* Train for heat and crowd exertion at home. Heat acclimation, swimming in warm water, walking for hours in high humidity while wearing hearing protection. If you cannot do that in a controlled setting, you will collapse in a human river in the dawn surge and be carried — literally — to somewhere you did not intend to go.

Expect to need more water and electrolytes than you think, despite being constantly wet. You will be sweating into saturated air that cannot take it.

### 3. Forget Addresses, Maps, and Empty Rooms

This is the hardest mental adjustment.

About 4% of the Basin is *unmade* and rebuilt every day — not renovated, reduced to slurry overnight and raised as something else by morning. Neighborhoods migrate at walking pace. Partitions flow.

An address is not a place. It is a trajectory: a predicted path with error bars. Mail is physical and follows probability.

And any volume left unoccupied for more than 58 minutes is reclaimed by the municipal substrate and re-offered to someone or something else.

Practical consequences for a traveler:

* **Never leave a room, bunk, stall, or rented locker unattended and unoccupied for "just a bit."** Set a timer for 40 minutes. Keep an occupancy beacon running, keep your belongings on your body or in a sealed, registered container that counts as "occupied," or pay a human sitter. You may come back to find your hotel room is now part of a clinic corridor and your bag has been inventoried, moved, and courteously re-delivered to a probability address two districts away.
* **Never navigate by landmark alone.** Navigate by flows and time. The dawn surge runs from down-level dormitory warrens to the heat-exchange fields. Canals reverse at night. Certain bridges exist only until the rain. A market will condense tomorrow where it condensed last week — not because the stalls stayed, but because the flow predicts it.
* **Hire a local guide for at least your first three full cycles, and pay for a trajectory service.** This is not tourism upsell. A good guide reads smell (ozone = fabrication, yeast = food vats, hot iron = transit, sweet rot = down-levels and mole stables), light (bioluminescent signage crawling with demolition dates), and crowd density the way a farmer reads weather. They will keep you out of buildings advertising their own demolition in six hours.
* **Register your own trajectory constantly.** Tell the system, your guide, your lodging collective, where you predict you will be. In the service tunnels below the city, tunnel-moles — huge, blind, horse-strong animals bred to drag cable sleds — also find, by smell, the dead who were not reported, and are treated with superstitious affection. The way you avoid becoming an unreported problem is to be predictably *alive* in the system.
* Keep all documents, money tokens, medication, and translation slates in a waterproof, body-worn pouch. Backpacks get opened by crowds, wet by rain, groomed by swarms, and left behind by moving walkways.

### 4. Eating, Sleeping, and Privacy Are Negotiated, Not Given

There is no countryside, park, vacant lot, or interval inside the rim. Density is law. A roof wants a market, a cistern, a nest pad. A corridor wants traffic. People sleep in shifts, in hammocks strung over transit lanes, in bunks that will be someone else's in eight hours.

Privacy does not mean a locked empty room. It means other people and swarms politely dampening their attention. Ask for it explicitly, pay for it where it's customary, and learn the sign for "do not address me unless emergency." Without that negotiation, you will be talked to, bumped, guided, groomed, and helped continuously.

For sleep:

* Bring an eye mask that seals against humidity. Night does not get dark; the light just changes from sun-glare to sodium and bioluminescence.
* Bring your own hammock liner treated against swarms, and expect to sleep in your ear gates on low. True silence does not exist, except — locals swear — at the Sump in the center, where the roar is so total the implants cancel it to a strange perceptual quiet. Do not go there alone to test this on your first night. The Sump is 300km of steaming artificial swamp, reactor towers, heat-fauna, floating districts, and water too hot to touch in the outflow channels.

For food:

* Eat only from sealed interior vat-farms and licensed vendors until your gut adjusts. Do not eat a dropped meal. It will be disassembled in about 40 seconds by beetle-porters and smaller swarms. That is not contamination theater; that is the sanitation system working.
* Eat quickly, with your container covered between bites. The air belongs to filter-kites — blind, dinner-plate-sized engineered bat-rays that sieve spores and particulates from head-height air and are harvested for protein — and to the swarms. Open food is an invitation.
* Do not swat wall-runners (gecko-like condensate cleaners), filter-kites, eels in the canals, or any swarm. They are infrastructure, often property, and sometimes protected by guild or ancient swarm-law. Watch, step around, let your guide move you.

### 5. Movement Rules That Will Keep You Safe

1.  **Move by current, not intention.** Step into a human river going your direction, step out at eddies. Fighting a surge at dawn or pre-rain will exhaust you and may cause a crush.
2.  **Plan everything around 2-4 p.m.** Be under solid cover, on a mid-level, not on an upper bridge or stepped plaza, before the rain. Flooding is minutes-fast but pump-managed — if you are where the water is supposed to go, you are in the way of a joyful, bus-swallowing drainage event.
3.  **Check demolition signage every time you re-enter a building.** Buildings advertise their future. If your lodging says it trades a floor tonight, believe it. Your street *will* be somewhere else in the morning.
4.  **Stay out of the down-levels and flooded service tunnels unless you are with a mole-handler's guild party.** It is dark, hot, full of cables, eels, and animals that can drag a sled — or you.
5.  **Do not look for an edge, a view, or green relief.** There is no outside view from inside. The rim itself is stacked with quarantine, arrivals, and desalination throats. The absence of a single blade of grass in a warm, wet, sunlit place is profoundly disorienting after a day or two. Schedule time in interior spaces with archival plant images, or sealed conservatory archives if you have access, if you know you are sensitive to this. Many first-time visitors weep on arrival — from sound, relief, or horror. That is considered normal.

### Pre-Departure Checklist for an Ordinary Human

**Months before:** Ear-gate fitting and training, Basin sign language basics, heat/humidity conditioning, full allergy and gut-microbiome workup, arrange guide and trajectory registration, arrange lodgings that guarantee occupancy-holding while you are out.

**Weeks before:** Start Basin diet in sealed form at home, load offline translation and flow-prediction tools onto waterproof, heat-sealed devices (expect them to be slow and short-range only), practice sleeping in noise and light with gear on.

**Carry on your body, always:** Ear gates + backup plugs, subvocal mic, waterproof ID and medical token, water and electrolytes, anti-fungal barrier, sealed slate, occupancy beacon, tethered pouch for valuables. Nothing important in an unattended bag. Nothing made of paper. Nothing you cannot afford to have get wet, inventoried by swarms, and moved to where you are *predicted* to be tomorrow.

The Basin is not failing. It is not a disaster zone. It has been churning at exactly this pace for three centuries, and old women who have never left their district can predict tomorrow's market to the minute. You are the fragile, temporary variable.

Prepare to be loud-proof, wet-proof, crowd-carried, constantly located, and never, ever alone — and you may come to understand why people go to the roaring center of the Sump just to think.
