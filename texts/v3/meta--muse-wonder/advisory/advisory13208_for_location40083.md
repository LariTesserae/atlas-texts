This is not a place you visit casually, and it is not a place you stay. Think of it less like a cave or a garden, and more like being allowed, briefly, inside an organ that hasn't finished growing — hot, sterile, self-contained, and operating on a timescale that will happily ignore you to death.

You are an ordinary, quick, wet animal. Everything in there is slow, dry, and made of stone, heat, and metal. Your preparation is mostly about limiting your exposure, not conquering the environment.

### 1. The Core Problems

**1. Getting in and out is timed by the mountain.**
The entrance is not a door. It's a vertical pressure crack on the south face that opens a hand's breadth wider around solar noon, then tightens again. You enter sideways, exhaling, through warm out-breathing rock.

If you misjudge it, you are not "stuck in a cave." You are sealed inside solidifying basalt with no water and 40°C+ heat.

**2. Heat and dehydration will end the visit before anything strange does.**
Inside is a steady furnace: 40 degrees at the front, hotter toward the back wall where seams glow dull cherry. The air is absolutely dry. Your sweat evaporates instantly, so you will not feel how fast you're losing water. Lips crack, eyes sting, core temperature climbs. There is no water inside, no shade, no cool corner.

Your heart and body temperature will also start trying to sync to the place — a slow, hydraulic pumping every few hours from the ivy, the ticking reheating rock. It feels like acclimatizing. It's the beginning of heatstroke.

**3. Thin air outside, toxic air inside and out.**
This is above the treeline, on a fresh, knife-edged peak of black glass and unweathered basalt. No scree, no soil, no forgiving slopes. Outside air is thin, UV is brutal, and tastes of pennies and struck matches. Inside you're breathing sulfur and iron vapor from the rosette, ozone when the fern discharges, and dry mineral dust from the ash floor.

**4. Nothing in there is edible, drinkable, touchable, or collectible.**
The lushness is a lie of density — there are only five plants in a chapel-sized room, with long bare stretches between them. Their leaves are glassy plates, copper veining, silica teeth. Their sap is mineral oil and, in one case, molten salt. Touching the ivy blisters. Standing too close to the fern makes you a target for a thin blue piezoelectric arc. The metal beads the ivy condenses — copper, tin, something soft like gold — disintegrate into dust and ozone if carried into cold outside air. They were never stable at your temperature.

Take nothing. Eat nothing. It cannot feed you, and you cannot feed it.

**5. Time and physics are locally early.**
Light inside arrives at the angle the sun *will have* in about 20 minutes. Cloud shadows cross the floor before the cloud reaches the peak. Your shadow enters a second before you do. You'll hear a bead *tink* to the floor, turn, and *then* see it fall.

Near the back wall gravity is negotiable. Loosened ash drifts sideways toward the hottest seam and builds horizontal stalactites of dust and static.

This is disorienting, not just quaint. You cannot trust "I just saw/heard that happen" for timing your exit.

### 2. Prepare Days Before

**Acclimatize and observe, do not arrive and enter.**
Spend at least several days at altitude nearby. Altitude sickness plus furnace heat plus a timed crack is a fatal combination.

From outside, log the seam for at least 2-3 clear days:
- What time does it begin to widen, peak, and begin to narrow?
- How wide is it at maximum, measured in centimeters, not impressions?
- Does cloud cover delay or prevent it?
- How strong is the warm exhalation?

Photograph it with a fixed marker. The mountain is actively growing; yesterday's coordinates and width are an approximation.

**Never go alone.** You need an outside spotter who stays on the south face with a direct line of sight to the seam, a clock, water, oxygen, and your extraction plan. This place is "not on the way to anywhere" — no one will wander past. If you are a multiverse traveler, your pickup must be set for *this* peak, on *this* day, with a window after your planned exit. Inside signals may not get out reliably, and your beacon should stay with the spotter.

**Test your fit.** You will be turning sideways and slipping through on an exhaled breath. Practice with two boards set to the measured maximum width minus a safety margin. If your chest, hips, or gear belt don't pass easily while wearing your entry clothing, you do not go in. Bulky cold-weather or heat suits that would protect you inside will trap you in the seam. This location selects, brutally, for slim, fit, non-claustrophobic visitors.

**Set hard abort limits in advance and give them to your spotter:**
- Maximum inside time: 30-45 minutes for a first visit, regardless of how well you feel. Set a vibrating timer you cannot rationalize away. Your sense of time will stretch.
- Maximum core temperature / heart rate: Use a wearable monitor with an audible alarm. If your heart starts noticeably slowing / patterning or your temperature alarm trips, leave immediately.
- Seam narrowing: Leave a feeler gauge / marked wedge watch at the entrance from inside. If it starts to close earlier than logged, go. Do not finish your observation.
- Weather: Any incoming cloud deck, wind shift, or storm that could cover the noon sun outside — abort the day. You need that opening.

### 3. What to Wear and Carry

You need alpine climbing gear outside, and furnace-worker minimalism inside. Stage the change below the seam.

**For the approach:**
Stiff boots with cut-resistant soles (fresh basalt and obsidian-like glass will shred trail shoes), helmet, gloves that resist razor edges, sun protection, layers for thin cold wind *outside*, and a climbing partner / rope system. There are no rounded shoulders or gullies — a fall is onto knife planes.

**For entry — slim, non-conductive, heat-resistant:**
- Tight-fitting, light-colored, breathable but heat-reflective long sleeves and pants. No dangling straps, scarves, packs, or jewelry. Metal jewelry, belt buckles, and trekking poles make you a better arc target than the ivy.
- Thin heat-resistant gloves. You will need your hands for balance, and rock near the back will blister bare skin even without direct contact for long.
- Sealed goggles, not sunglasses. Dry ash, static dust, and stinging air will get under anything loose. A fit-tested respirator with cartridges for sulfur / acid gas / metal fume and fine particulate — slim profile so it still fits the crack.
- Lip balm, saline eye drops applied *before* entry, and skin covered as completely as fit allows.
- A slim insulated water bladder worn flat against your back under clothing, with a bite valve. Bring 1.5-2 liters, drink most of your fill *before* entering so you're not fumbling inside. Sip small and sealed inside. Do NOT pour water out, refill anything in there, or spill onto roots, cracks, or the molten-salt stalk — flash steam and a violent disruption of a closed system.
- Headlamp even though it is daylight inside. If the seam seals or the pocket's unreal light shifts, you will have no other light. Keep it non-metallic-cased if possible.
- Simple, redundant timing: a mechanical watch plus a vibrating countdown timer.
- A lightweight tether line to your spotter, fed through the seam, with agreed tug signals. Be aware the rock may heat, abrade, or pinch it — it is a communication aid, not a guarantee you can be pulled out. Do not tie it so it can wedge you in the crack.

Leave behind: food, outside soil on boots (clean and decontaminate soles thoroughly — you are entering a sterile system that has never had microbes, seeds, insects, or animal life; do not be its first contamination), loose paper, anything flammable, insect repellent sprays, and any collecting jars. Nothing you carry out will survive, and prying a bead from the groove in the floor breaks a months-long conveyor loop that ends in reseeding.

### 4. Inside Protocol

Enter at peak opening, not as soon as you can squeeze. Move slowly. Everything there operates at a degree an hour, a millimeter a day. Sudden movement stirs ash that may not fall down, kicks dust into your lungs, and makes you conspicuous to electrical discharge.

Stay in the front two-thirds of the room. The back wall is where heat, gravity anomalies, and the glowing seams concentrate. You can observe the ivy perfectly well from a distance — hear its hydraulic sigh without putting your ear near it.

Mind where you stand for more than a minute or two. Your shadow arrives before you and falls 20 minutes ahead in solar terms. You may be shading a plant from light it is *about* to receive. Don't block the fern or stand between the fern tip and the ivy when fronds have been coiling — a snap-open can come without warning sound (or rather, you may hear the chime after the arc).

Do not step in the shallow groove worn by the rosette. That is where beads roll, a fraction of a millimeter a day, back to the central crack. Do not step on root filaments where they flatten into cracks — they are white-hot — and do not approach the low grinding rosette. Its leaves rotate with silica teeth and are literally making the first soil of this mountain by chewing basalt. It will do the same to a boot or finger.

Expect silence. Your breathing will sound intrusive. Clicks every few hours, a bead every few days. Don't chase sounds; you'll hear them before they happen. Use your eyes and your gauges.

Drink before you're thirsty. Check your timer, your seam gauge, and your body monitor every few minutes. The danger cue is feeling *comfortable* — euphoric, slowed, in tune with the pumping. That is recruitment, not wonder. Leave.

### 5. Exit and Aftercare

Exit well before the logged narrowing begins, while you are still sweating, thinking clearly, and can exhale and turn without panic. Count on the seam feeling tighter on the way out if you're dehydrated, swollen with heat, or carrying dust.

Outside: sit, shade, cool gradually, and rehydrate with electrolytes under your spotter's watch. Weighing yourself before and after the day is a useful, humbling measure of water loss you never felt. Watch for delayed heat illness and altitude symptoms for the next 12 hours.

Decontaminate outside the pocket, away from the seam. Shake out and bag clothing that smells of ozone and sulfur; ash in cuffs and boots should not be carried to vegetated lowlands — you don't know what mineral / microbial exchange you're performing between worlds, and that ash was never meant for a wet environment. Shower, rinse eyes, monitor your breathing; metal fume and sulfur exposure can irritate lungs hours later.

Write down what you observed immediately, with times from your mechanical watch. Your memories will tend to reorder cause and effect — sounds before falls, shadows before steps — because that's how it happened in there.

One day the seam will stop sighing open. The pocket will go on for a while in daylight from nowhere, five plants trading vapor and sparks, until the heat moves deeper and they lock mid-gesture. Your job as a visitor is to be briefer than that process: enter on its one daily breath, witness an industry before industry and a garden before soil, and leave the loop exactly as closed as you found it.
