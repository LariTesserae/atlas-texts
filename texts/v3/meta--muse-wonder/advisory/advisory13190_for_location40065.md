First, prepare for the sound. Everything else — the heat, the water, the etiquette — comes second, because the sound will reach you hours before the city does, and it will change how you do everything once you're inside it.

This is not a loud town. At noon, it is not a town that can be *in* at all, in any normal human sense. Plan accordingly.

### 1. Arrive at the wrong time, and you will not arrive

Do not cross the salt pan at midday.

The bowl is sixty paces across and sunk twelve deep into a flat white pan with no river, tree, wall, or smoke to mark it. You will hear it for miles — a low hoot, like wind across a bottle, rising until you feel it in your molars — and then, without warning, the ground will fall away in front of you.

Approach it the way the locals live in it: on the edges of the day.

**Best arrival: late afternoon**, as the wind shifts and the rim-flutes begin to drop out one by one. That is when shutters open, earplugs come out, ovens are lit, and people can hear you introduce yourself. Second best: just after dawn, before the singing starts, though you will have to make your business very fast.

Under no circumstances try to walk the pan under full sun if you can avoid it. The salt reflects the light up into your face as bright as the sky. Local people walk chin-down behind a veil. You should do the same:

* Dark, wrap-around eye protection, plus a light veil or scarf you can see through. Sunglasses alone will not stop the glare coming up from underfoot.
* A wide hat, long loose sleeves and trousers in pale cloth, and thick-soled, closed shoes. The salt plates are sharp at the curled edges, blisteringly hot, and will cut thin sandals and bare feet.
* More water than you think you need, carried on you. Rain may not fall here for years. The locals import drinking water by mule, twice a year, in stitched skins, store it sealed below the lowest houses, guard it by law, and measure it by the cup. No one will be able to rescue a careless, dehydrated visitor for free, and you should not expect them to try.

Travel with a guide or a mule train if you possibly can. The city gives no visual landmark. Its sound is its beacon, but by the time the sound is guiding you reliably, it is also becoming dangerous to listen to.

### 2. Hearing protection is clothing

Children here receive their first earplugs — beeswax molded warm with plant resin — before they receive shoes. Take the hint.

Bring several sets:

1.  **Custom-molded, soft plugs** that seal completely, for sleeping and for the midday hours. Wax/resin style works better in heat and dust than foam, which will get gritty with salt and stop sealing.
2.  **A backup set, and a backup to the backup.** Dropping your only plugs at noon on an exposed terrace is a medical emergency.
3.  **Over-ear protection to layer on top** if you are caught in the open as the wind rises. At the rim and on the upper terraces, sound is painful. Down toward the center at noon, it is a physical pressure: vision shivering, chest aching, small stones dancing on the packed earth. Hearing damage there is not the only problem.

Get a hearing test before you go and after you return, so you know what it cost you. Don't plan to "tough it out" for a first visit. Local ears are trained from infancy, and even locals do not talk, linger in exposed places, or stand at the focal point at noon.

Do not bring loud equipment. No speakers, no drones, no engines, alarms, or musical instruments you haven't cleared with the council. Vibration loosens screws, cracks pottery, rattles shutters, and, most importantly, *interferes*. These people have perfect relative pitch the way you have balance. They can hear, from inside a shuttered house, if a rim-stone has cracked or a terrace wall has shifted enough to change the bowl's chord. A cracked stone is read like news, like bad weather, like illness in a relative. Touching, leaning on, climbing, or "testing" the pierced stones and clay blocks set into the rim is roughly equivalent to tampering with someone else's mill, church bell, and weather station all at once.

If you sing, sing carefully — or better, at night, only when invited, and be prepared to be corrected with great precision and no malice.

### 3. Learn to live on their clock

The city inverts the normal day.

**Dawn:** A brief, busy, spoken interval. Council business is done now, quickly, in low murmurs before the wind rises. Markets prep, work is laid out, arrangements are made. If you need to ask a question, negotiate lodging, hire help, or apologize for something — do it then.

**Mid-morning to afternoon:** The bowl sings. Conversation does not become impolite; it becomes impossible. The entire population switches, without thinking, to a fluent sign language of hands, shoulders, tapped elbows, and stamps on resonant threshold boards.

Learn at least survival signs *before* you arrive, from an interpreter or a trader who works the mule route, and carry a small slate or matte notebook for the rest. Bright white paper at noon will dazzle you and everyone you're trying to show it to. At minimum learn: *water, help, pain/ears, shade, sleep, food, stop, wait until evening, where may I sit, may I cross, thank you.* Do not shout. Shouting hurts you, annoys no one only because no one can hear it, and marks you instantly as unprepared.

This is when locals retreat to double-walled houses with sand poured between the walls, to sleeping alcoves dug deep into the terrace and hung with layered mats. Ask for a deep alcove when you arrange lodging. It will be dark, close, and blissfully muffled, and you should plan to sleep or lie still through the worst hours. Set nothing loose that you don't want rattled apart — the thousand doorway hangings of bone, shell, reed, pottery shards, and seed pods all clatter at once, and your equipment will too. Lash, wedge, or bury in cloth anything fragile, toothed, or hollow.

**Late afternoon:** The chord thins. A single wooden knock can carry the length of a terrace. This is the social hour exploding into being: doors unsealed, plugs pulled, jaws stretched, markets assembled in minutes on ledges that stood empty all day.

**Night:** This is the real city. Ovens, cooking steam, talking, arguing, mending, storytelling at normal volume. Gardens and vines that clenched all day open up. The trumpet flowers turn pale to the sky and release a sweet, green scent that seems impossible in salt and stone. People sit on terraces and listen, deliberately, to the bowl not sounding.

Shift your sleep to match. If you stay up all night being social and try to sightsee at noon, you will be exhausted, sunburned, and half-deaf by day two.

### 4. The center is not a plaza. Do not treat it as one.

The broad white floor at the bottom is empty on purpose: no buildings, no well, no stalls. At its middle is a darker, polished circle about the width of a threshing floor, strewn with cracked husks and fine dust.

That circle is their millstone, hammer, and second set of hands. In the morning they lay out grain in shallow baskets, nuts, dried clay to be powdered, flax to be loosened, fouled jars to be shaken clean, succulent leaves to be split for fiber. In the evening they retrieve the work the sound has done.

**Never build, camp, picnic, or leave gear there, and do not cross it at noon** except in a genuine emergency with a local physically guiding you out. It is legally, practically, and spiritually the place no one may occupy at full voice. Standing there for the experience is like standing under a waterwheel to feel industriousness.

Watch where work has been laid in the morning, even at the quieter edges. You are a guest in a workshop.

### 5. Water, food, plants, and animals: touch almost nothing

Bring a gift that respects scarcity. Good trade goods are what the mule trains already value: salt-cured trade items are redundant — they *trade salt away* — but beeswax and resin for earplugs, good copper or flaked stone tools, dyed cord, palm weaving, seed oil, and above all clean water in a sealed skin, offered to the communal store through proper channels, will be understood. Ask at dawn, through the council ledge, how gifts are received. Do not hand out private cups of water to children in a way that undermines rationing.

Expect food to be salty, dry, and concentrated: salt cakes, dried lizard meat, seed oil, salt meat, bundled fiber plants. Your own water needs will rise because of it. Ration a set amount per day and do not supplement by begging mouthfuls that are accounted like coin.

Despite the lack of rain, the bowl is lush — vines over every terrace, thick wrist-sized stems older than the houses they shade, squash-like leaves, resinous shrubs, waxy climbers, hanging trumpet flowers, gardens in baskets and cracked jars. Almost none of it is watered. The plants store bitter gel, oil, and sour pulp, unfold a leaf over a season and keep it a decade, and run roots sideways into shaded wall-backs to gather night damp.

Therefore:

* **Do not water a plant as a kindness.** You are wasting the community's drinking water on an organism that did not ask for it.
* **Do not pick, prune, or "tidy" a vine**, even one blocking a path. Ask whose it is. It may have been trained along that wire by three generations.
* **Do not eat a succulent leaf** because it looks like stored water. The fillings are bitter, oily, or sour for a reason.
* **Do not introduce seeds, bees, or pollinators.** There are essentially no bees here. Pollination happens at noon, when the sound shakes pollen loose in visible golden puffs from flower to flower. The lushness and the deafening are the same fact. An introduced insect is not helpful; it is an unmeasured change to a closed system.
* **Do not touch the moths.** There may be five or six in the whole bowl, treasured, emerging at night. Same courtesy, if less reverence, for the pale wall lizards and the black salt-beetles in the lower stores: they belong here in a way you do not.

By day, the only larger animal you may see is a single hawk making one slow circle above the rim and leaving. If even the hawk does not stay for noon, neither should you linger to see why.

### 6. Where to stay, and how to leave

Aim for the middle rings if you can: houses with painted doors — ochre, black resin, powdered blue — below the upper workshop ring and above the kitchens and dye-pits. The workshops near the rim get the wind first and the worst of the sound; the lowest stores are cool but close to beetles, stored oil, and salt meat.

Many houses you pass will be sealed with mud, roofs intact, jars stacked, swept by someone out of obligation. Half the town is absent or gone. Do not pry open a sealed door out of curiosity, and do not assume an empty house is lodging. Terraces have boundary stones and are argued over even when they look abandoned.

Secure lodging before your first noon, confirm in sign where you should retreat when the sound builds, and agree on a stamp or knock signal for emergencies. Lay out your plugs, water, veil, and shade *the night before*. The transition can feel gradual from inside a house — cords starting to hum, shutters ticking — and then suddenly be too much to think clearly through.

Leave as you came: at dusk or dawn, plugs in hand, pack cinched tight against salt dust (it will get into everything, corrode metal, foul jar seals, and cake in your ears around your plugs — clean gear at night, gently), following a guide until the bowl's voice drops back to that bottle-mouth hoot behind you.

You will know you have understood the place, a little, on your last night, when you find yourself sitting on a terrace after the market and the singing, doing nothing in particular — just listening with everyone else to the white center below, starlit and empty, holding its silence as completely as it held its noise, both of them on purpose.
