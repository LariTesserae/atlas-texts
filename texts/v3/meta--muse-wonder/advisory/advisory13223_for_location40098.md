You hear it before you find it, and that is your first practical problem: you will not be able to sneak in, orient yourself quietly, or have a private first conversation. Prepare for a town that is one wet, loud, vertical room thirty feet underground, with 41 people in it, and for the fact that everything about visiting — breathing, sleeping, talking, carrying luggage — has to be negotiated against those four facts.

### Before You Even Go Down: Arrange Your Place In The Count

There are 41 people. Always 41. This is not a census observation, it is infrastructure. The chamber can support that much breath, cooking smoke, damp, waste, and noise, and no more.

An ordinary visitor is not exempt. You are another pair of lungs and another body on a shelf.

* Do not arrive unannounced with the intention of "just looking around for a few weeks." Arrange entry in advance through whoever tends the shaft and ladder from above. In practice this will mean waiting for a departure, a temporary exchange, or explicit permission from the mayor for a very short stay.
* Keep your stay finite and say how long it will be, out loud, on arrival. Vagueness reads as an attempt to become a 42nd person.
* Pack as the arrivals do: one bundle you can carry on your back while climbing a wet ladder with both hands. If you cannot carry it down yourself, do not bring it.
* If you are pregnant, seriously ill, likely to need rescue, or traveling with children or animals who have not been counted in, reconsider. One birth requires one departure. One death makes room for one arrival. They do not treat this as cruelty, and they will not make an exception because you find it harsh.

Bring trade that matches what they must import. Nothing is grown, raised, or mined in any useful quantity down there. Useful gifts are not ornaments. Bring, in descending order of welcome:

Dried and salted food (grain, beans, hard fish), lamp oil, fuel, good dry timber cut short enough for the shaft, rope fiber, leather, salt, simple iron or copper stock, knife blades, chisels, needles, oil for tools. Bring things that store, burn slowly, or can be repaired.

Bring almost nothing fresh, green, fragile, bulky, perfumed, or single-use. Fresh food rots in the perpetual damp before it can become festive. Paper swells and mildews. Anything you throw away becomes their problem forever; a broken plank down there gets reused as a shelf, a crutch, and finally firewood. Carry your non-reusable trash back up yourself.

### Dress For Permanent Cellar-Damp, Not For Rain

It is always the same temperature: stone-cool, summer and winter alike. You will not be cold in the expedition sense and you will never, ever be dry. Condensation forms on timber, rope, metal, and faces. Your clothes will be damp within minutes and will stay damp. The indoor rain in the corner is just where this general condition concentrates enough to fill the copper basin.

* Wear wool or other fibers that remain tolerable when wet. Avoid cotton that will sit cold and sodden against you, and avoid anything that needs to dry out overnight to be usable the next day. There is no overnight dry-out.
* Bring one change of clothes sealed in oiled leather or a waxed wrap, not because you will keep them dry indefinitely, but so you can rotate into something merely damp instead of soaked.
* Footwear is safety equipment. The stone flags are dished by generations of feet and slick as river rock, salt-crusted at the edges. You need soft, flexible soles with real grip that let you feel the practiced, less-slippery places on the street planks. Hard smooth soles, heels, and stiff hiking boots are a fall waiting to happen — and a fall from a tier, or into the street, is a fall onto neighbors.
* Expect everything metal you carry to want to rust. Tools are kept bright down there by constant use and constant oiling. Wipe and oil blades, buckles, and instruments daily, or leave them above.
* Leave behind anything that relies on open air, sunlight, or a dry environment: solar chargers, most medicines in paper, matches you can't protect, books you care about. The town runs on oil lamps trimmed to an agreed height, wooden gears, stone weights, rope, and patience.

### Entering: The Ladder, The Bend, The Wall Of Sound

Access is thirty feet down a ladder with wet rungs, through the shaft, behind a cistern wall that looks like every other cistern wall. Go slowly, three points of contact, bundle tight to your back, nothing dangling that can snag on rope or pulley.

Halfway down is the bend in the shaft, and it is disorienting in both directions. Below it, the roar arrives all at once — the copper rain-basin clanging like a clock, the drain, and mostly the buried aqueduct behind the north wall, a forearm's thickness of stone away, reflecting off the vault until sound becomes a physical density. Above it, on your way out, the roar simply stops, and ordinary air will feel thin and exposed for a while.

Do not try to shout up or down the shaft to communicate. Descend, arrive, and announce yourself properly once inside: go to the basin, give your name, where you've come from, who vouched for your place in the count, and how long you intend to stay. Important things — marriages, debts, insults, apologies, prices — are announced there so the copper carries them. Your arrival is an important thing.

### Talking, Privacy, And Not Looking Like A Plotter

Whispering is impossible and therefore suspect. A person trying to be quiet is assumed to be ill or scheming. Conversation happens at a full shout, inches from the other person's face. This will exhaust you the first day. It will also reorganize your sense of manners.

* Practice before you go, if you can: projecting without anger. Market haggling there is cheerful bellowing. Lullabies are sung fortissimo directly into infants' ears. If you bellow compliments and apologies with a relaxed face, you will fit in faster.
* Do not lean in and murmur confidences. State ordinary things loudly.
* Understand their privacy system and honor it absolutely: privacy is not silence, it is practiced deafness. You will hear neighbors' arguments, griefs, endearments, and bodily functions through hanging blankets and reed mats. Pretend the roar took the words. Never repeat something you "couldn't help overhearing" unless it was announced at the basin. This pretense is load-bearing social architecture.
* Conversely, assume anything you say can be heard by a tier above and below. If it is truly not for the town, save it until you are back above the bend.
* Do not wear heavy ear protection while socializing; you will miss bells, announcements, and footing warnings, and you will look withdrawn. For sleep, soft plugs are understandable — everyone understands miller's sleep eventually — but tell your hosts you're using them so a missed bell isn't read as illness or sulking.

Long exposure matters. This noise never stops, sleeps, or thins out at night. If you are prone to migraines, tinnitus, sensory overload, or insomnia, plan a short visit. You will likely sleep badly the first nights, then in exhausted snatches timed to other people's kettles, looms, the smith's hammer felt in his teeth and yours, the bucket-pulley squealing, and someone always sweeping water back toward the drain.

### Living Vertically In Nine Paces By Seven

The town is a main street of planks over the central drain, with three to five tiers climbing the walls: sleeping shelves, lofts, work platforms, whole households on ledges the size of a table, partitioned by blankets, salvaged doors, and mats stiff with damp. Ladders, knotted ropes, and a stair of pegs connect them.

* Learn the route to your assigned shelf before dark — though "dark" only means a different bell. The lantern flames stay the same. Learn it by hand and foot count. Look up constantly: your front step may overhang a stall, and someone's cook-pot, bedding roll, tool chest, and grandmother are directly above your hat.
* Move slowly, duck under blunt stalactite teeth and low beams, and never grab a curtain, rope, or shelf edge without asking; it may be someone's wall, stair, or bed support.
* Keep your belongings ruthlessly contained. A ledge household is everything in arm's reach. Nothing of yours should drip, dangle, or shed onto the household below. Washing and dripping laundry are the subject of a multi-generational property dispute. Do not add your socks to it, and do not try to mediate it. Arguments that have repeated for decades are structural here; "solving" one would be like removing a beam.
* Ask where to put your feet on the planks. Locals step in soft, practiced places. Walk single file, yield in the street without backing blindly off the edge into the drain, and never block the street for a conversation. Nine paces long is the whole public world.

### Air, Smoke, Food, And Water

There is exactly one small cooking brazier near a flue-crack that draws, more or less. The whole town permanently smells of woodsmoke, lamp oil, wet wool, iron water, bodies, and underneath it the clean cold smell of stone wet for a hundred years. If you have asthma, smoke sensitivity, or panic in close air, this is the deciding factor, not the darkness.

* Do not bring your own stove, candles for atmosphere, incense, or any extra flame. Wick height is agreed because air and smoke are rationed just like the headcount. Cooking is communal and scheduled; offer your dried goods to the common pot rather than preparing private fragrant meals on a shelf.
* Food will be hard, salted, dried, and boiled or stewed into submission. Chewing is work, salt is constant, variety is not expected. If you have dietary needs, bring the entire supply yourself within your one bundle and state the need loudly and without embarrassment.
* Do not drink from the drain, and do not casually drink from the rain basin. The drain is finger-deep, black, iron-smelling, carrying everything out through a hand-wide grate. It hosts a rust-orange bacterial scum with fine trailing hairs — admired at a distance, not a seasoning. Drink what is hauled down by bucket and designated for drinking, and ask which vessel is which every time. Do not foul any water with soap, waste, dye, chemicals, or food scraps except where you are explicitly shown.
* Expect sanitation to be close, efficient, and unprivate by your standards. Follow the local bucket and drain routine exactly, wash hands in the designated place, and never improvise. In a one-room town, your gut illness is everyone's.

Scrape nothing. The pale, almost mineral slime under the rain-basin is removed by townspeople with wooden paddles and returned to the water without ceremony. The calcite curtains, flowed-together steps, and waxy sheeting on the lower bricks are not dirt to be cleaned or souvenirs to be chipped. Do not carve initials, collect stalactite, or "tidy" stone. The cave and the building have agreed on their boundary; you have not been consulted.

### The Other Citizens

Fauna is rare enough to be liturgical.

* Cave crickets, blind and colorless, live singly in the far corners. Leave them alone. Do not move one "for a better look."
* A bat enters down the shaft perhaps twice a year and spends a frantic night stitching the lantern light. Children who have only heard it described find it sacred and terrifying. If it happens during your visit, sit down, make yourself small, keep your hands off, and let locals catch it in a sack for release upward. No flash photography — indeed, no sudden bright light at all. Eyes here live in dim amber lamplight doubled on wet surfaces; a camera flash is a blow.
* The eel in the deepest stretch under the planks has never been seen entire: a snout at one grate, a tail weeks later at another. It is fed crumbs, consulted, possibly forty years old, possibly — the old people are certain, on no evidence — the original eel from the first shelf, and the only citizen exempt from the count. Feed it only what you are handed for the purpose, at the place you are shown. Do not lift planks to find it, fish for it, prod the water, or demand proof of its length. Consulting it means asking your question aloud like everything else, accepting whatever interpretation the elders offer, and not treating belief as a debate prompt.

There is nothing green down there. Nothing has ever grown, not even moss. Do not bring a plant as a gift, scatter seeds, or expect a window box. Green would be an imported corpse rotting in the damp, not a kindness.

### Health, Nerves, And Duration

Prepare for three slow accumulations:

**Damp.** Skin stays moist, small cuts stay open longer, bedding never feels fresh, and leather needs care. Bring a small, sealed tin of antiseptic and plasters in waterproof wrapping, tell hosts about any wound, and keep feet as dry as the place allows — which is to say, change socks, elevate them on your shelf, and accept the limits.

**Noise.** Protect your hearing over a long stay by stepping, when permitted, to whatever corner is fractionally less reflective, and keep your visit short if your ears start to ring through sleep.

**Density.** Forty-one people in one chamber means no solitude except the agreed fiction of not-listening. You will be watched climbing, sleeping, eating, and arguing by people pretending not to watch. If you need true aloneness daily, you will not get it below the bend. The cure is a shorter visit, not searching for an empty corner that belongs to someone else.

Claustrophobia, fear of heights on peg stairs, and discomfort with darkness are all rational here. The vault is low in places, the ledges overhang, the light never changes, and time is told by the basin's clang and by a day-bell and a night-bell rung from above. Keep a simple personal routine — meals, work offered, sleep after the night bell — or days will smear together within forty-eight hours.

Offer labor early and cheerfully. Everyone works within arm's reach of everyone else, and a guest who only watches is freight. You can sweep water toward the drain with a twig broom, haul on the pulley under direction, hold fiber for the second-tier looms, oil tools, mend sacking, trim wicks, carry messages by shouting them accurately, and replace a street plank under the eye of someone who knows the pattern — one at a time, so the street is always old and always new in exactly the same places.

### Leaving

Leave exactly when you said you would, announce it at the basin, settle debts aloud, and thank hosts at full volume by name. Take everything you brought that cannot be reused, including broken things you might be tempted to abandon as "useful scrap" without asking.

Climb slowly. At the bend, the town will not fade — it will be cut off, full-volume one rung and gone the next: lamps, basin, hammer, looms, and forty-one raised voices replaced by a quiet that feels weatherless and exposed. Sit at the top a minute. Let your ears ring, your clothes begin their first real drying in days, and your eyes adjust to a world with distance in it.

Then go. Below the bend, unchanged, the rain keeps falling in its one square foot, and room is waiting for the next single arrival with a single bundle.
