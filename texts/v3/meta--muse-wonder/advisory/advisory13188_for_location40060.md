This is not a dangerous place in the obvious way. There are no animals, no insects, no drop to fall down, no river to rise, no one has ever lived there or left anything behind. Visiting it safely is mostly a matter of preparing for three conditions that ordinary humans are bad at:

**Total darkness, total quiet, and total dryness — sustained, warm, and featureless.**

If you arrive unprepared, you will not be attacked. You will become disoriented ten paces from daylight, dry out faster than you expect, lose all sense of time, and leave footprints and disturbances that may last longer than you will. Prepare as for a very small, very pristine sensory-deprivation cave.

### 1. Understand what you're entering

The chamber is only about 30 paces long by 12 across, with a flat, clear floor and a few old fallen blocks near the walls. You could hold the whole floor plan in your mind.

You will not experience it that way once inside.

The entrance bends once. After about ten paces the entrance is only a faint warm smudge behind you. After twenty, turning the wrong way makes even that disappear. There is no blue twilight, no silhouettes, no reflective water to catch light. The walls have to be found by touch.

There is also almost no echo. Stone and still air swallow footsteps, breathing, and cloth. You cannot judge the size of the room by sound, hear a companion clearly across it, or shout and get a useful sense of direction back. Your own voice will sound close and dead.

It is constantly in the high twenties Celsius — roughly 80-85°F — day and night. Warm wall, warm dust, warm air at your ankles and face. Nothing cools, nothing condenses. You won't sweat dramatically, which makes dehydration easy to miss. Your skin and mouth will simply get drier and drier, and begin to taste of limestone.

There is no water inside to find, ration, filter, or fall back on. Not a drip, not a seep, not a damp corner.

Plan to bring everything in, and take everything — including your disturbance — back out.

### 2. Arrive from the outside, in daylight

If your mode of travel lets you choose, materialize outside, not inside.

Outside is pale limestone, gravel, dust, sparse wiry grass, and hard glare. That gives you time to orient, gear up, and let someone — or a timed beacon / return device — know where you are. Arriving directly into the back alcove in absolute dark with your light in a bag is how a 30-pace room becomes a trap.

Do this before you go in:

- **Scout the entrance on foot.** It's a sloping throat of gravel and dust. It sheds rain away and will shed your footing if you hurry. Note exactly where the bend is.
- **Set an outside anchor point.** A pack, marker, or companion in sight of the entrance, not inside the chamber. Note sun position and what the entrance looks like looking *out* as well as in.
- **Set a hard turn-around time and a hard exit time.** Inside, you will have no light change, no temperature change, and no sounds to tell time by. Tinnitus, your heartbeat, and your own thoughts will get loud. Decide on the surface: "45 minutes in, then out," and keep a vibrating watch timer, not an audible alarm you hope to hear.
- **Go with at least one other person if you can.** Not because something will get you, but because light failure, panic, a twisted ankle on a loose limestone flake, or simple direction-loss are all trivial with a second hand to hold and serious alone. If you must go solo, leave a written/ recorded plan and a check-in time outside.
- **Hydrate and eat beforehand, and use the toilet outside, well away from the entrance.** There is no water cycle in there to process waste, and no insects or microbes in evidence to break anything down. Urine, food crumbs, or feces would not be "returned to nature." They would sit, dry, and change a system that currently runs on dust alone. Bring a waste kit if you plan a long stay.

### 3. What to wear and carry on your body — not just in a pack

Assume anything not attached to you may as well be in another universe once the light goes.

**On your person, before you cross the threshold:**

- **Primary light in hand or on head, already on.** Warm-white LED headlamp is ideal. It leaves both hands free for the wall, which is your real navigation system.
- **Two independent backup lights**, in two different pockets, with fresh batteries. Not two lights in the same bag. Phone light does not count as a backup — dust will film it, limestone flour will scratch it, and you'll want the phone sealed away.
- **Water on your body**, at least 500ml-1L per person even for a short visit, more for an hour-plus stay. A soft bottle is better than hard metal; sound doesn't carry, but you will feel clumsy in the dark and drop things. Add electrolytes. Your mouth will taste of mineral dust regardless; small frequent sips are better than arriving back outside suddenly ravenously thirsty.
- **A physical tether/guide line if you are at all nervous, in a group, or staying long.** A light cord anchored *outside* the entrance and unreeled as you go, kept low and loose, gives you a foolproof way out if all lights fail. Do not tie it to the shrub, the grass, or a root. Anchor it to your outside gear or a rock well away from the threshold plants.
- **Watch with vibrating alarm, and if you have it, a small CO2/air-quality monitor.** The entrance bend means the chamber breathes very poorly — no draft ever searches it. One or two calm people for an hour are fine, but a group lingering, exercising, or (don't) burning anything can make still air feel close and headachy. If you feel headache, air hunger, or sudden drowsiness, that's an exit signal, not something to sit through.

**Clothing:**

Dress for a warm, dry room, not a cold damp cave. Light, breathable, full-coverage clothes are best: they slow skin drying, protect knees and palms from sharp limestone and gritty dust, and keep your own skin cells and oils off walls. Thin-soled shoes let you feel how warm and soft the packed earth-and-limestone-flour floor is, but make them closed, sturdy, and clean — there is at least one unshaped limestone flake on the floor sharp enough to cut a hand, and it will cut a foot.

Bring sunglasses and a hat for outside. The transition from that pale glare to complete black is abrupt. Also bring a light layer for *after* you exit, especially at dusk. Inside never cools; the country above does, and you'll come out dust-dry and temperature-confused.

Consider glasses over contact lenses. Fine dust, dry air, and no water to rinse with make contacts miserable. Bring sealed saline and lip balm/moisturizer for the exit; do not apply scented creams inside — smell, like dust, will linger in still air and on stone.

A light dust mask or buff you can pull up is worth it if you are asthmatic or dust-sensitive. The floor gives with "a faint, almost inaudible crunch" only if you walk gently. Scuffing, hurrying, or a group crossing repeatedly will raise limestone flour that hangs in windless air for a long time and coats lights, lungs, and everything else.

### 4. Clean your gear like you're entering a sterile room

This is the multiverse-traveler part people forget.

There are no ants, spiders, bats, droppings, chew marks, or webs. Dead leaves at the mouth simply bleach and become dust because nothing carries them off. That absence is the ecology.

Ordinary shoes and packs carry seeds, insect eggs, fungal spores, food crumbs, and soil microbes from a thousand other worlds. Before you visit:

- Shake out, wash, and dry boots, clothes, and pack away from the site.
- Seal food outside the chamber. Ideally, don't eat inside at all.
- Don't bring firewood, plants, soil souvenirs, untreated wood walking sticks, or anything that sheds organic material.
- Do not introduce a "helpful" light-and-leave, offering, marker stack, or hearth. There is deliberately nothing built there. Leave it unbuilt.

Walk on the clear center floor on a single line in and the same line out. Your footprints in that settled dust — especially in the deep, soft, overlapping drifts of the back alcove, where the ceiling lowers — may be legible for years. The most respectful visit to the far end may be to *not* go all the way into the alcove, or to stop at its edge, touch the curving back wall once, and turn around.

### 5. How to move inside

1. **Stop just inside the bend.** Turn around and look back while you still can. Memorize by touch: which wall is on your entering hand, where the slope changes, where the loose polygons of cracked earth near the mouth give way to smoother floor deeper in.
2. **Pick a wall hand and don't swap casually.** The primitive method is the intended one: palm flat to the wall, steps placed slowly, body learning distances eyes can't check. If you cross the empty center, do it slowly, arms slightly out, counting paces, knowing the nearest block or wall may be farther than your voice suggests.
3. **Don't touch everything.** At about hand-height in one place there's a thin white crust of salt, only a few crystals wide — the relic of moisture from another decade. The pale roots that come through ceiling fractures and feel blindly down the walls are equally fragile. Brush one and you end years of slow growth. Keep your guiding palm low or high consistently, watch with your headlamp for roots ahead, and duck/ step around them. Never use one as a handhold or route marker.
4. **Stay in touch distance in a group.** Because sound dies, a companion five paces away may seem absent until you walk into them. Agree on touch signals and light signals (cover/uncover lamp = stop; two flashes = return to wall) *outside*. Walk single file, each person knowing who is in front and behind.
5. **If you sit — and it's worth sitting — sit in the clear center.** This is a place where the main event is doing nothing in warm darkness until silence stops feeling like a pause and starts feeling like the condition of the world. That's a powerful, sometimes unnerving experience. Brief companions beforehand: you may hear ringing, blood in your ears, stomach noises strangely isolated; time may stretch or collapse; a strong urge to speak, laugh, or move just to produce a return from the world is normal. A vibrating timer and a hand on a companion's arm make it grounding instead of dissociative. Do not stay to the point of panic to "push through." Sensory deprivation is dose-dependent.
6. **Never run, never throw anything to test distance, never shout to test echo.** There is no echo to learn from, a thrown pebble will be lost and will permanently litter a floor where a natural flake is noteworthy, and running raises dust and risks a fall onto blocks whose edges are only softened, not safe.

If all lights fail: Stop. Sit down where you are. Do not grope forward on momentum. Wait for your hands to remember the last wall. Then crawl slowly, one hand sweeping in a low arc before each movement, following either your guide line or a single wall in the direction of the bend until the entrance becomes a paler absence again. This is why the line and the wall-hand discipline matter.

### 6. What not to bring or do

- **No flame.** No candles, lanterns, cooking, smoking. Soot would blacken pale limestone permanently in still air, flame consumes oxygen, and heat/flavor/smell have nowhere to go. Electric light only.
- **No carving, scratching, chalk, tape arrows, cairns, or stacked stones.** If you need a marker, use your removable cord.
- **No digging, collecting, or "tidying."** Don't take the limestone flake, the salt, a root tip, bleached leaves, or dust as a souvenir. Don't move fallen blocks. Erosion has finished its decisive work here and stopped; don't restart it.
- **No music, speakers, or ritual noise-making to fill the silence.** Not because you'll wake anything — you won't — but because you'll miss the point and your own noise will feel oddly flat and intrusive.
- **Don't step on the threshold life.** Three or four straw-yellow grass tussocks and one spindly resin-smelling shrub with thick grey-green leaves live exactly where light fails. They grow so slowly a dead blade from years ago still looks like a blade beside them. Step over the cracked polygons carefully on entry and exit, in full light, watching where each foot lands.

### 7. Leaving

Exit slowly, coiling your line as you come, giving your eyes a long minute at the bend before stepping into glare. Put sunglasses on *inside* the threshold if you can.

Outside: drink, check each other for cuts from limestone, nosebleeds from dry air, headache, or lingering disorientation. Brush dust off clothes and boots away from the entrance so you aren't seeding the mouth with foreign material or carrying its unique dust ecology off stuck to you in bulk. Photograph your soles / gear if you're a meticulous traveler — you'll be amazed how much pale flour you carry.

Check that you took: every battery, wrapper, cord, bottle, tissue. In a place where a fallen leaf takes a year to become dust, a bit of plastic will be an artifact forever.

---

**A minimal pack list to have ready at the entrance:**

Headlamp + 2 pocket backups + spare batteries • 1L water + electrolytes per person • vibrating timer • guide cord + outside anchor • clean light clothes/shoes + sunglasses/hat • dust buff, lip balm for after • sealed waste kit • small first aid (plasters, saline) • air monitor if staying in a group • nothing that burns, marks, digs, or is eaten inside.

Go in expecting not a sight but a set of sensations learned in order: gravel slope underfoot, bend, wall under palm, floor warming through soles, mineral taste, breath with no return echo, ceiling as openness above your head you feel rather than see. Leave it exactly as it was found — warm, dry, dark, nearly empty, and not waiting for you.
