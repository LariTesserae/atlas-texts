This is not a place you enter. That is the first and most important preparation to make, mentally and physically.

You will travel to 5,400 metres, acclimatize for days, climb service stairs in thin air to a compressor hall on a summit ridge, open a 90 cm maintenance hatch behind Compressor No. 2 — and then stand there with your face in the opening for a few minutes. There is no room for a body inside. There is no room for a hand without displacing leaves. Visiting it is an act of looking, listening, and not disturbing a system that has reached perfect equilibrium without you.

Prepare accordingly.

### 1. Prepare for the altitude first — it is the real danger

At 5,400 m the air pressure is roughly half what it is at sea level. The closet itself is a footnote compared to that.

An ordinary human multiverse traveler should **not** arrive directly at the summit. Materializing instantly at that altitude, however you travel between worlds, gives you altitude sickness without the warning of a climb. Stage yourself lower.

* Spend 7-10 days ascending in steps if you possibly can, sleeping lower than your highest point of the day.
* Expect shortness of breath on the service stairs no matter how fit you are, headaches, broken sleep, loss of appetite, and stupid, slow thinking. Plan all complicated decisions — latch procedure, signals, retreat route — *before* you go up.
* Talk to a doctor beforehand about acetazolamide and emergency oxygen. Carry a pulse oximeter and know what numbers mean "descend now." Past about 5,000 m, willpower does not substitute for oxygen.
* Carry supplemental oxygen for the hall itself, even if you don't plan to wear it constantly. You will be standing still, breathing fast, with your face in dry, ammonia-tinged air while your ears hurt. That's exactly when people faint quietly.
* Never go alone. Hypoxia at this altitude makes people insist they are fine while making basic mistakes with machinery.

Outside the plant it can be -30°C in a storm with wind scouring black scree and old snow. Inside the engine hall it is industrial and hot from machinery. Inside the hatch it is always 12°C. Dress in layers you can strip *before* you open the hatch, or you will overheat, sweat, pant harder, and fog everything. Windproof shell, insulated boots with good grip on grating and icy stairs, glacier glasses for the hard thin blue outside, warm gloves you remove indoors, and a hat. The contrast is violent.

Have a hard weather window and a descent plan. The ridge is no place to wait out a whiteout because you wanted one more photograph.

### 2. Prepare to not enter, not touch, and not feed it

Every instinct a traveler has will be wrong here.

Do not bring water for the plants. They have never needed it. Their soil is coal dust, cinder, powdered insulation and dried lubricant compacted into a black cake that has never been wet. Their roots drink oil aerosol. Their fronds condense refrigerant vapour. Crushed sap is not green juice but a clear cold fluid that evaporates off skin instantly and smells of ammonia.

Water would be pollution, not kindness.

Likewise:

* **Do not prune, part, or push through the fronds.** There is no empty pocket larger than a hand. Forcing a space tears stems that are fibrous as cable insulation, breaks a growth/decay balance that has held for decades, and will not create a path. The leaves press back toward their shape in advance.
* **Do not bring a cutting home.** Aside from the ethics — this is a complete, closed terrain 90 cm wide — the sap is a refrigerant/ammonia irritant and the plant is adapted to oil mist, 100 Hz drone, and 12°C stability. It will not live in your kitchen and it will not thank you for trying.
* **Do not introduce life.** No food open near the hatch, no unwashed outdoor clothes shedding seeds and insects, no soil on your boots. There has never been an animal in there: no gnat, spider, beetle, or dead moth. The ammonia trace and the constant sound keep it acoustically and chemically sterile. A single pregnant insect, fungal spore load, or microbe colony from a lower, wetter world could be the first asymmetry in fifty years. Arrive clean, in clean indoor clothes and smooth overshoes, and brush down *outside* the hall.
* **Do not turn the compressor off for a quieter look.** The ecology faces the back wall, not the bulb. It feeds on sound, heat, oil weep, coolant vapour, and metallic dust. Shutting down No. 2, even briefly for "maintenance access," is not pausing the exhibit. It is removing its sun, rain, and seasons all at once.

Your tether/anchor point for any multiverse arrival or departure should be in the engine hall proper, well clear of machinery — never inside the hatch. Arriving inside a volume already completely filled with root mat, pipe, and leaf would be fatal displacement for you and catastrophic for it.

### 3. Prepare for the sound

The latch is seized and, in the old accounts, a man had to take his ear defenders off to work it. That was the first mistake. Do not repeat it as ritual.

The sound does not rise and fall. It is a solid column: a ~100 Hz drone through steel, a pressure hiss that never empties, a relay clicking at the same interval forever. Shouting does not fit into it because it leaves no gaps.

* Bring **both** low-profile in-ear plugs *and* over-ear defenders. Have your companion work the latch while you keep protection on, or pre-arrange for plant staff to free the latch on a prior shift with proper tools and hearing protection staged in sequence: plugs in while muffs are briefly lifted.
* Agree on hand signals before opening. You will not conduct a conversation at the hatch. Decide: close it, oxygen, step back, medical problem, photograph done.
* Limit unprotected exposure to seconds, total face-at-hatch time to a few minutes. Your ears will ache. Tinnitus afterward that does not fade within a day, muffled hearing, dizziness, or nausea deserve medical attention — and at altitude, they also mean descend.
* Expect the psychological effect. A forest should rustle, drip, and chew. This lush green place makes no biological sound at all. The machine fills the space where birdsong should be, uniformly and without variation. Many people find that more unsettling than the volume.

A remote alternative is kinder to everyone: a borescope/endoscope camera on a slim, cleaned stalk, a small sound recorder left by the hatch lip for 60 seconds, and a photographer's flash. You will learn almost as much and be exposed to almost nothing.

### 4. Prepare for dry, poisoned, perfectly stable air

The air that sighs out is bone-dry, cool on the skin, and cracks lips within minutes. It smells of hot copper, ammonia, ozone from motor brushes, and a bitter green smell like heated refrigerant.

* Hydrate and apply lip balm and a barrier moisturizer *before* you go to the hatch. Do not apply greasy products near hot compressor surfaces or open a water bottle over the root mat.
* Bring eye protection. Dry air plus ammonia trace plus ozone makes eyes water and contacts miserable. Wear glasses, not contacts, for this visit.
* Consider a properly fitted half-mask with an ammonia/organic vapour cartridge (K-type, depending on your world's rating system), especially if you are sensitive, asthmatic, or plan repeated looks. Have plant staff check their own exposure monitors first — let their fixed sensors tell you today's compressor weep, don't discover it with your lungs.
* Wear nitrile gloves if you must steady yourself near the rim. Do not reach in bare-handed. Hot pipes sit ten centimetres behind leaves, fronds are dry as paper but warm, and crushed stem fluid flash-cools skin.
* If you get a sudden sharp ammonia hit, burning eyes/throat, coughing that doesn't stop when you step back, or a sweet/chemical taste: close the hatch if it can be done safely, step back, oxygen, tell your companion with signals. Don't "push through" for the visit. At 5,400 m your respiratory reserve is already spent.

Asthmatics and anyone with chronic lung, heart, or blood pressure problems needs explicit medical clearance for both the altitude and brief industrial-gas exposure.

### 5. A practical kit list

For the traveler, not for the closet:

Altitude/summit: layered cold-weather system, windproof outer, spare dry gloves, headlamp (for the hall stairs, not to "help" plants — they ignore light), pulse oximeter, personal oxygen and mask, prescribed altitude medication, high-energy food eaten *away* from the hatch, insulated bottle kept closed in the hall.

In the hall: ear plugs + muffs, hand-signal card, glasses/safety specs, nitrile gloves, lip balm applied in advance, respirator as above, camera with flash to compare against the dated photograph wired inside the hatch, notebook (paper outperforms touchscreens when you're hypoxic and gloved).

Leave behind: insect repellent sprays (unnecessary — nothing lives in there — and contaminating), plant food, misters, extra seeds, pets, soil samples from home, any intention to "rescue" the original fern. The cracked empty pot at the centre is a ruin now. The ecosystem has been polite around it the way ivy is polite around a ruin, which is to say not at all.

Bring a wrench only via the plant fitters, and let them own the hatch. You are a guest in an operating air-liquefaction and ore-hoist station, not in an abandoned site. Lockout procedures, hot surfaces, high-pressure lines, and moving machinery outrank your itinerary. Permission, a staff escort, and their maintenance window are non-negotiable.

### 6. How the visit will actually go

You will climb slowly, heart hammering despite the stairs being trivial at sea level. The hall will be loud enough to feel in your sternum. Behind Compressor No. 2 your companion will signal, the latch will complain, and the drone will drop—or rather, rise toward you—by a small, unimportant fraction.

For a moment it will look like a trick: a linen closet cut into a loaf of green. Fronds the size of dinner plates at face height, lit bottle-green from behind by one caged bulb, veins like wiring. Black-green moss around bolt heads. Vines spiralling at the exact pitch of pipe threads. Dozens of red, amber, and green LEDs watching steadily from the undergrowth. Below, in the dimmest layer, leaves almost black; above, saturated green. The same half-unfurled frond from the faded flash photograph will still be half-unfurled.

Your forehead will almost touch a leaf. Your lips will start to split. There will be no dampness, no animal movement, no smell of earth after rain. Only copper, ozone, ammonia, and hot refrigerant, at twelve degrees, forever.

Then your companion will touch your shoulder — your agreed signal — and you will close it anyway. The latch will take. The leaves, already pressing toward where the door was about to be, will go on facing the compressor in the green dark, drinking oil-mist at exactly the rate it is produced.

It does not need a witness to continue. The best-prepared traveler is the one who knows that in advance, arrives healthy enough not to become an emergency on the summit, looks for three clean minutes without reaching in, and leaves the stalemate exactly as humming, dry, full, and unchanged as they found it.
