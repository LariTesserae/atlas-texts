If you go to the Table, do not plan it as a trip. Plan it as a temporary change of timescale.

Almost everything dangerous about the place works too slowly to look dangerous, and almost everything useful there works too slowly to save you if you arrive unprepared. There are no predators, no insects, no storms that chase you, no hostile population, and no heights left to fall from in the usual sense. It can still strand you, starve you, misplace you, freeze you at night, and quietly rearrange the distance between you and home while you are sleeping.

An ordinary human runs at the wrong speed for the Table. Prepare for that first.

### 1. Decide how long you are *really* going

Do not visit for a weekend. Do not visit with a hard return date.

The climb alone is days up a stair cut into polished, root-old granite, wide and worn concave by feet. There is no cable car, no porter animal — there are no animals at all — and no descent on the far side to make you feel you have crossed something. You just arrive at a level so high the ordinary clouds go by *underneath* the rim.

Once up there:

* Distances between districts are 10 to 60 kilometers of empty mossland, and they do not stay fixed. A road that is not regularly walked gets longer.
* The city is continent-sized and considers itself one municipality. A local address may include a four-day walk *inside* one district.
* Civic processes — an uprooting, a graft-marriage, a conversation with a rooted citizen, one cut through one foundation stone — can take a season to a century.
* Leaving will take longer than arriving did, because the roads behind you will have been unattended while you walked forward.

Tell people at home, in writing, that you may be months late and that this is normal. Bring supplies, money, medicine, and patience for at least three times your intended stay. If your multiverse transport requires a scheduled pickup, schedule it at the Stairhead, not at a district, and arrange for someone — ideally a paid local road-insister, or failing that, your own companion on rotation — to walk the Stairhead road and ring a bell on it every day you are gone. That road needs to hear itself to remember how long it is.

Do not go in winter with the intention of "waiting it out." An unattended district is farther away in spring. Winter is how the Table edits out places nobody insisted on.

### 2. Train for altitude, cold, and flatness

The Table is high, thin-aired, and cold in the way very old stone is cold. Days are ordinary — blue sky, white sun, honest light — but:

* Nights drop hard. The whole plain ticks with contraction. Ice-flowers open in shadow at temperatures that kill other plants.
* Mornings are frost-white even when noon steams faintly off the moss.
* The fern-trees uncurl in cold and fold *tight when warmed*. Learn from them: dress in layers you can open at noon and seal completely an hour before sunset, which lasts a long time here. Watch the light spill across the plain like a tide; when you see it going, you are already late to make camp.
* Physical training should focus less on steep climbing and more on endless level walking in wet cold. Knee-high moss, peat pans, inch-deep dew-lakes ten miles across. You will not get dramatic blisters on day one. You will get trench foot, strained calves, and a peculiar exhaustion from springy ground that swallows every step and gives almost nothing back acoustically or mechanically.

Acclimatize on the stair. Climb slowly. Headache, nausea, stupid decisions at altitude will be compounded up top by silence and featurelessness — you can make a navigational error at dawn and not have a landmark disagree with you until night.

### 3. Pack for a world with no animals in it at all

This is the part travelers underestimate. "No animals" does not mean "no wildlife hazards." It means an entire support ecology is missing.

You must bring, import carefully, or ethically negotiate for:

**Food:** No meat, no fish, no insects, no worms, no birds, no eggs, no milk. There is nothing to hunt and nothing to fish, and a dew-lake with no life in it is just a sheet of water an inch deep. Worse: much of the local plant life is *people*, or houses, or children in moss-lined bronze pots being debated by the neighborhood prior to planting.

Do not assume a fern-tree is firewood. Do not assume a grove is a forest. Do not eat cuttings, do not forage in a family grove, do not harvest plate-lichens — neighborhoods are founded around them because they exhale warm air, and picking one may be vandalism, assault, and destruction of heating infrastructure in one act.

Bring dense, animal-free rations for your entire journey between negotiated pantries, and when the citizens offer food, ask — slowly, through a walking-phase interpreter — what part of whom it is, whether it is shed, grown for eating, fallen, or ceremonial. Some plant cultures will be honored to feed you pruned fruit. Some will regard a salad the way you would regard someone eating roof shingles off a nursery. Err toward imported food and offense-free hunger.

Bring vitamins, especially protein, B12, iron, and anything you normally get unthinkingly from an animal-based food web. There are no pollinating insects, either; local plants use golden spore-weather carried on remembered damp currents. Human agriculture you bring with you will not be pollinated unless you do it by hand.

**Decontamination:** This is a multiverse ethics emergency. You arrive crawling with life: gut bacteria, skin mites, insect eggs in boot treads, fungal spores in clothing, seeds in cuffs, possibly a mosquito in your pack. The Table has evolved for an eternity with *none of that*. A single introduced insect species could become the first pollinator-thief, wood-borer, or root parasite in its history.

Before the stair: clean everything, freeze or heat-treat clothing, wear sterile outer layers for arrival, leave pets behind absolutely, do not bring live plants, soil, or untreated wood. Declare your microbes to whoever meets you. They will understand symbiosis better than you do — they *are* the ecosystem — but give them the choice.

**Medicine:** Bring more allergy treatment than you think reasonable. Pollination here is visible weather: golden fronts rolling across districts on still afternoons, carrying news and pollen from families who have not touched in three hundred years. If you have hay fever, asthma, or mold sensitivity, wear a proper filter mask during spore weather and sleep with it near. Moss spores, peat damp, and ice-flower cold will find every weakness in your lungs.

Also bring altitude medicine, frostbite care, water purification (condensate is clean but peat pans are not drinking fountains), and a paper medical record. Local medicine, if it exists for your kind at all, will operate at sap speed.

### 4. Water works by memory, not by gravity alone

Do not trust a map of water. Fog rises out of the moss at night and flows, visibly, *uphill* into open cisterns because it has done so for ten thousand years. Rain does not fall everywhere; it condenses in travelling pockets a few streets wide, or beads suddenly on every leaf in one spot while the ground a hundred paces away stays frost-dry.

Practical rules:

* Camp near an established cistern or condensation channel, with permission. Do not dig a new ditch and expect water to adopt it. For a while the fog will fill the *ghost* of an old, diverted channel in the air above it and ignore yours.
* If citizens plant scent-leaved shrubs in a line, they are teaching water a new route. Do not move them. Do not plant your own markers where they could be mistaken for instruction to water, spores, or fog.
* Carry at least a day's water even across "wet" country. A dew-lake can occupy ten miles and offer you only a wet inch over bedrock, gone by dusk tomorrow. A lens-pond can appear overnight in a hollow and be gone by the following evening.
* Water clocks, dripping buckets, gravity, wind, and patience power almost everything. Bring mechanical, wind-up timepieces and repair tools, not gear that assumes rivers run downhill and batteries are easily replaced.

### 5. Navigation: the rules that will betray you first

* **Shadows lie helpfully.** On the Table they fall *toward the nearest inhabited district*, slightly, like iron filings leaning, regardless of the sun. This is excellent for finding people and terrible for solar navigation. Learn to notice which instinct you are using. Do not orient a map by your shadow out of habit.
* **The horizon is useless.** No curvature you can trust, no far side visible, black-green ground simply giving up into sky. Mark routes by sound, by scent-shrubs, by walked-in paths, and by inhabited shade. Fern-trees migrate their shade. Recheck bearings daily.
* **Walk roads to keep them real.** Travel with a bell and use it. Local road-keepers drag a measuring chain and ring a wooden bell once a day "so the road can hear itself," and their job is not ceremonial. Join the ritual. If you establish a temporary route to a camp, latrine, or water source, walk it at the same time daily or accept that it will quietly acquire extra plain.
* **Travel in pairs and split the returning.** One person's job, every day, is keeping the way back the same length as the way out.
* **Settling is universal.** Towers bow, walls slump, anything vertical is persuaded flat over years. Do not store critical gear on a tall shelf and expect geometry to respect it, do not build a signal mast and leave it for a season, and inspect any aqueduct, rope-bridge, or lift before trusting it — not for breakage, but for having become politely flatter than it was. Some aqueduct arches are single stones the size of houses, moved demonstrably by levers. Respect the maintenance crews. If a bronze-saw team is making one cut, do not stand in conversation with them expecting them to finish by lunch; the flawless cut may be the work of your whole visit.

Falling, too, is off. Things dropped from a height take too long to land, as if the ground were considering them. Do not let this teach you carelessness. The ground finishes considering. It always does.

### 6. Talking to citizens who are trees

Prepare linguistically and socially for slowness.

Rooted citizens have hollow, chambered trunks, flat insulating canopies, roots that tap fog channels and sometimes incorporate a doorway. A family is a grove. A marriage is a graft maintained for a century. Children are cuttings in pots, debated for a generation before planting.

Thus:

* An introduction may take days. A question may get its answer after you have left and be sent after you by spore-front or by a fast walker. Leave a forwarding address in walking-time, not just district name: street-names repeat from district to district and numbering continues across 60 kilometers of moss.
* Address rooted citizens at the trunk-door, not at the canopy, and announce yourself with the bell rather than knocking on bark that may be a wall, a body, or a grandmother.
* Never lean a pack against a tree, carve initials, hang a hammock, or take a cutting as a souvenir. When a walking-phase citizen finally moves, they trail excavated roots for perhaps 40 kilometers over a decade and then become the next house they will be. Excavation is communal, careful, seasonal. If you are invited to help dig, that is high social trust and heavy ceremonial labor — accept only if you can stay for it.
* If one dies mid-route and is planted there, the milestone is now an ancestor and the road will shorten politely around the grave. Move your camp. Learn the name.

Bring gifts that are time, not objects that demand vertical display: road-walking labor, bell-ringing, carrying water by counterpoised lift, helping re-teach a fog channel, paper records that can be compressed into a cellar archive. The city literally builds itself in layers — new streets on fossilized moss on old plazas become cellars become black stratum. Anything accurate you write about them may become foundation. Write carefully.

### 7. Psychological preparation: the silence has texture

There is no birdsong, no insect hum, no animal rustle. Moss eats echo. What you hear is frost ticking in stone, a fern-tree creaking toward light, distant dripping where a fog-river reaches a cistern, and then continental silence.

Most travelers talk too much the first three days, then too little. Bring:

* A companion, ideally, and scheduled conversation, music made by hand, work songs — with local permission, because sound here carries civic meaning (bells keep distances).
* A paper journal, dated redundantly. Sunset can arrive in the western districts while eastern ones are already in night, and ceremonial messages are occasionally sent "into yesterday evening" by a fast walker with a lantern. Everyone agrees not to make a habit of it. Follow that agreement. Do not try to exploit light-lag, do not send yourself instructions, do not trust a message's date without two independent clocks. Time feels normal locally; that's what makes the exceptions dangerous to your planning.
* Low expectations for stimulation. The Table is furiously active at speeds not including yours: weather spirals born over peat pans where moss exhales warmth in patterns the sky copies, dew-lakes evaporating in geometric shapes, festivals that began before you left and are still in progress years later. If you need visible progress daily, this place will feel like a beautifully vegetated abandonment. It is not empty. It is running on a clock with different gears.

### 8. Campcraft specific to the Table

* Sleep on a platform, not directly on moss. Moss is knee-high in wild districts, damp, insulating, alive, and possibly someone's fallow relative. Ask where fallow ground is.
* Open your shelter at night only as much as the cold allows; ice-flowers bloom in shadow and citizens stand with doors open dreaming at sap-speed — nighttime is a social and biological shift, not just darkness. Observe before you wander with a bright lantern through a grove.
* Keep a fire policy of *probably don't*. No evidence the locals use open flame amid fern-trunk roofs, woven halls, peat pans, and cities made of people. Use a contained stove, ask first, and never burn "dead wood" you identified yourself.
* Watch peat pans and fitted basalt plazas alike after rain-pockets pass. One is bog; the other becomes a mirror-slick compression of a thousand older plazas.

### 9. When to turn back

Turn back if: your back-road has not been walked and belled for two days, your water route begins filling a ghost channel instead of yours, your shadow is your only argument for direction, spore weather puts you on continuous mask, or a festival invites you to stay "until it ends" and no one can tell you within a year when it began.

Leave the way locals insist on distances: slowly, repeatedly, in company, announcing yourself. Walk your exit road more than once before committing your full pack to it. The Stair will be there. Its root-rock mountains have forgotten summits, but the stair remembers feet.

Come prepared, above all, to be unfinished. The Table's one law applies to visitors too: nothing here is finished. It is only, for now, flat enough to live on.
