This is not wilderness, and it is not quite a building yet. Prepare as if you are visiting a newborn, artificial ecosystem mounted on an industrial platform high in thin air — warm and forgiving inside, physically precarious underneath, and strict about contamination because almost nothing in it has learned how to defend itself yet.

The dangers are not dramatic. They are wet feet, slipped footing, altitude, sun, vertigo, boredom that turns into inattention, and you accidentally becoming the first invasive species.

### First, understand the shape of the place

It is small enough to walk end to end in four minutes, about eighty meters across, in five broad stepped terraces under a translucent vault. That smallness is misleading. The planting is dense, the walkways become causeways, and each terrace floods in rotation for roughly an hour, ankle-deep. Your real map is not distance. Your real map is: *which terrace is wet, which one is next, and where is the high side of it?*

There is no true ground anywhere. Under the soil trays is grating. Under the grating is a dark service void with pumps. Under that is structure, anchor cables, and open altitude. Through the lower vents you may see cloud moving *below* your feet and valleys impossibly far down. People prone to vertigo should prepare for that more than for the garden itself. Inside will feel close, green, safe, and jungle-warm, while your body keeps registering, through vents and views and the thud of pumps in the deck plates, that there is nothing solid under this forest.

Arrive, if you can choose your entry point, at the airlock / loading bay end, ideally near the crew, not in the middle of a planted terrace, not under the deck, and absolutely not in the cistern at the top. The cistern is a riveted tank the size of a house, the highest climbable point, perpetually sweating and overflowing. It is infrastructure, not a pond.

### The air problem: warm inside, hostile outside

Inside is steady shirt-sleeve warmth, wet and close. Outside at that altitude should be thin and cold, and the weather comes up from underneath.

Dress for both, in layers you can strip fast:

- A light, quick-drying base layer inside. Cotton will stay damp all day.
- A warm insulated layer and a windproof shell in your pack for any exit, transfer, loading-bay work, or emergency on the open platform. You can go from sweating among ferns to dangerous cold very quickly.
- Sun protection despite the roof. The vault diffuses high-altitude daylight to a milky brightness at noon, but it is still strong mountain light. Hat that won't drip sweat into your eyes, sunscreen on neck/ears/hands, sunglasses for glare off wet leaves, standing water, and translucent panels.
- If you are susceptible to altitude sickness, treat this as a high-altitude visit even though you will be warm indoors. Arrive rested if possible, hydrate, avoid alcohol the first day, know the early signs — headache, nausea, unusual fatigue, poor sleep — and tell the crew. A multiverse jump does not acclimatize your lungs for you.

The humidity will get into everything. Bring electronics, paper, medication, and spare clothes in genuinely waterproof bags, not "water resistant" ones. Expect a brief artificial rain at dawn when the fans start and overnight condensation falls from the skin. It drums on leaves and tanks. It will find the inside of your pack if you leave it open.

### Feet are your main equipment

Bring knee-high rubber boots if you can, the kind the crew wears. This is the single most important item.

Not hiking boots. Not sandals. Not waterproof trail shoes.

You will be stepping in and out of ankle-deep warm marsh, algae-slick rills, wet grating, steaming black made-soil, and concrete channels. If you mistime a flood, assume you will be wet to the knee. Inside your boots, wear synthetic or wool socks, and bring at least two spare pairs sealed dry. A day here is measured in sock changes.

Also bring:

- Quick-drying trousers you can roll or that fit inside boots. No long hems dragging through fertilized water.
- A small towel.
- A dry change for leaving. You will smell of hot plastic, mineral fertilizer, wet concrete, chlorine, and crushed green leaves.
- Light gloves. Fresh concrete is alkaline and still curing in places, raw steel is unpainted, vines and stems are being handled and redirected constantly, and you may be asked to move crates, hoses, and hand tools up a terrace ahead of the water. Wash skin after contact with wet concrete or irrigation water.
- Optional: thin knee pads or a waterproof sit-mat. There is very little dry, clean, finished furniture in a place that was built yesterday. Much of it is waist-deep planter frames, pipe-rack, and trays.

Walk like the place is younger than it looks. Algae is already filming the channels. Moss is starting on concrete. Broad leaves hide the edge of causeways. Do not lean on soil trays or planter beds — roots have not bound anything yet, and a whole tray can shift. Do not step into the beds or on the woven capillary mats unless crew put you there. Stay on walkways and causeways, especially during flooding, when the line between path and rill disappears.

Feel the deck through your soles. Pump thuds, relay clicks, sluice clunks, the tick of sun-warm pipe expanding — that is your information system here. Learn the flood sequence on arrival and repeat it back to someone: which terrace, in what order, what warning sound, where do people move tools and conversations. The electric sluices clunk open in rotation, pumps sigh through the grating as a terrace drains, soil steams faintly afterward. Move *ahead* of the water with the crew, carrying your own gear up a level without being asked. If you are caught, don't run in flooded planting. Step to the nearest causeway, lift your pack clear, and wait or wade slowly out. Hidden edges are the risk, not depth.

### Biosecurity is not optional

This place was planted all at once from sterilized, sealed shipments. It has one famous frog — probably a stowaway egg in some matting — pale moths at the work-lights, and boxed pollinator hives still stacked, humming in quarantine by the airlock, not yet opened. There is no birdsong. No leaf litter. Almost nothing has died or decayed yet. The forest floor hasn't been invented.

That means you are a biological event.

Before visiting:

- Arrive with clean boots, clean pack, clean clothing — no mud from another world, no soil in tread, no seeds in cuffs, no fruit, no untreated plant material, no insects in gear.
- Expect boot-washing at thresholds between terrace-systems, and obey it even when it seems fussy. The crew are keeping young ecologies from mixing with themselves too early.
- Do not open, tap, move, or "helpfully release" the quarantined hives.
- Do not feed, catch, relocate, or make a pet of the frog in the third-terrace rill. It sings at irregular intervals, one startling note in the quiet. Listen, note where you heard it if the crew keep records, and leave it.
- Do not plant anything, bury anything, scatter ash, pour out foreign water, or leave food waste. Do not pee in a rill or terrace because "it's all water anyway." It is not. It is a managed chemical system that has not built up decomposers yet.
- Ask where human sanitation actually is. Do not improvise into the irrigation, cistern, or soil.

On departure, clean again just as carefully. Check boot treads, cuffs, pack seams, and damp clothing for seeds, algae, moss fragments, moths, eggs, and wet soil. You do not want to export this place's first life — or carry its fertilizer and chlorinated water film — into the next world.

### Water, food, and health

Water is everywhere and almost none of it is for drinking.

It is in misting lines, condensation, open rills, the cistern, standing flood. It also carries mineral fertilizer — sharp-smelling — and chlorine, and it runs over fresh concrete and galvanized steel. Bring your own drinking water or accept only what the crew explicitly designate as potable, from a sealed/filled container. Refill where they tell you, not from a convenient tank because it looks clear. Early on, clear here means uncured concrete bottom, not purity.

Do not forage. The lushness is deceptive: broad leaves unfurling faster than their stems, vines already colonizing handrails and irrigation booms, young trees still in fabric-wrapped root balls. This is not an established edible landscape, much is likely inedible, and everything is growing in made soil under a fertilizer regime. Bring sealed food, eat it where the crew eat, and pack out every crumb. A brand-new sterilized planting has no cleanup crew for your sandwich crust except microbes no one has invited yet.

Other health notes:

- The smell is not rot. It is hot sunlit plastic, mineral fertilizer, wet alkaline concrete, clean chlorinated water, and green sap. If you have asthma, migraine triggers, chemical sensitivity, or plant/fertilizer allergies, bring your medication and test your tolerance in short periods at first. Warm wet air can feel oppressive even at a pleasant temperature.
- Wash hands before eating, after handling soil, tools, vines, or concrete. There is little decay, but fertilizer burn, alkaline irritation, and algae-slime on a small cut are real enough.
- Heat sneaks up because the work is slow and the air is saturated; sweat doesn't cool you efficiently. Drink on schedule, sit in fan-moving air between the huge slow vault fans, and say something early if you feel dizzy. Add altitude to humidity and visitors often misread the result as "just being unfit."

### How to behave with the crew and the work

The crew is small, in rubber boots and sweat-darkened shirts, working from laminated checklists, washing boots at thresholds, pollinating early flowers by hand with soft brushes row by row. This place runs on ritual slowness inside an industrial frame. Be useful in that rhythm.

Ask before touching plants. Vines that have found a handrail today may be redirected tomorrow; your helpful training of a stem can undo a plan. If invited to hand-pollinate, wash hands/tools as instructed, learn the brush stroke on one flower under supervision, and go row by row without skipping to the pretty ones. Records matter in a place where mineral scale is only just whitening a pipe joint — people are watching firsts happen.

Keep the quiet. Machinery is mostly tucked away: pumps under deck, fans overhead, a winch whining briefly as a soil-pod comes up the inclined rail from the loading bay. Between that is dripping, leaf rustle, and your own splashing steps. Don't fill the absence of birdsong with constant loud talk. Speak low, especially at dawn and dusk. You'll hear more — sluices, sighing drains, the frog — and the crew will trust you faster.

Do not go under the deck void, into pump shadow, up the cistern, onto the pipe-rack spine, or along the inclined loading rail without explicit escort and a reason. The central spine under the pipe-rack past the cistern is the fastest route, but nobody sensible goes straight and fast here, because planting, flooding, hoses, crates, and work are always crossing it. Announce yourself around blind green corners. Carry tools flat and low. Move gear up before floods, not during arguments about floods.

Photography and instruments: ask. Flash, drones, soil probes, water sampling, leaf collection — all are introductions or removals in a system that is still being inventoried. Wipe tripod feet like boots. Waterproof your camera; dawn condensation will form above the leaves as thousands of bright points and then fall.

### Timing a visit

- **Dawn:** fans kick on, condensation rains briefly, leaves patter, empty tanks drum. Beautiful and wettest inside your clothing. Be already booted and packed sealed if you want to watch it.
- **Morning:** clouds below may be a white underside to the world. Good for orientation, seeing the terraces as they drain and steam, joining checklist work.
- **Noon:** milky, bright, hottest-feeling in humidity. Best for short observation, worst for exertion. Hydrate, shade your neck, expect glare on water.
- **Afternoon:** cloud thins; you may see blue, hazed valleys far below through lower vents. Best views, worst vertigo.
- **Dusk:** level amber light through panels, clouds below catch fire then go grey, inside temperature holds warm while condensation beads overhead for tomorrow's rain. This is when the place most convincingly dreams it is a landscape. Stay for it if you can, but know your flood terrace and your route back in dimming green before the light goes.

If staying overnight, assume there is no conventional lodging unless the crew offer it. Sleep only on a designated non-flooding terrace/platform, off soil, in a waterproof bivvy or hammock rated for constant damp. Secure everything against scheduled water and condensation drip. No open flame: hot plastic, sealed growing loads, electrical systems, and young planting are a bad mix, and there is no established fire ecology here at all. Use a low light — moths will find it — and expect to wake to pump thuds through the structure and the dawn shower on the vault.

### The mental adjustment

Prepare for a specific uncanniness: tropical density without age. No leaf litter underfoot. No rot smell under the green. No layered birdsong. No worn paths. Steel still has mill sheen and stenciled numbers; plastic tags flutter; concrete still smells alkaline; yet vines are already claiming the architecture and moss is improbably bright on new walls. Your instincts will keep telling you this forest is old enough to absorb mistakes. It is not. It is an earnest machine learning to be a place, and it needs visitors to be gentler, cleaner, and more schedule-conscious than the lushness suggests.

Leave it exactly as young as you found it: boots washed, gear sealed, nothing planted, nothing taken, the frog still singing alone from the third terrace, and the next flood arriving on time whether you understood it or got wet learning.
