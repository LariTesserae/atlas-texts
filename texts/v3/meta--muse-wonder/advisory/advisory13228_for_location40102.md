This is not a city with animals in it. It is an animal habitat that humans also live inside, at 2,000 meters, on black stone, in permanent damp.

Prepare for it the way you would prepare to live for a while inside a very large, very old, industrial barn, aviary, stable, and bathhouse combined — because your nose, lungs, ears, skin, and sleep will understand it that way long before your eyes get used to the architecture.

### Before You Go

**1. Test your lungs and your allergies first.**
This is the make-or-break issue for an ordinary human. The air is coal smoke, ozone from trams, hot ammonia from guano, wet feather, mold, sulfur, and the dust of dried droppings scraped off roofs by the ton. Pigeon and swift dander is not occasional, it is weather. Cellars bloom white mold after rain. Bacterial mats — black and rust-orange, slick on every canal — smell of hot iron.

If you have asthma, chronic bronchitis, a bird/feather allergy, or a mold allergy, get medical advice and bring more medication than you think you need. People develop sensitization there: histoplasmosis and similar fungal infections from old guano dust, psittacosis-type pneumonias from pigeons, eye and sinus inflammation that does not clear until they descend. Pack:

- A well-fitted respirator and spare filters for roof terraces, markets, guano-drying districts, and any cleaning work. A cloth mask is theater.
- Antihistamines, saline nasal rinse, eye drops, inhalers if you have ever used one.
- Antifungal powder for feet and skin folds. Everything weeps. You will be damp at altitude, warm by noon, steamed by cisterns and laundries, and never quite dry. Foot rot, groin rash, and infected insect bites are the common souvenir complaints.
- Up-to-date rabies, tetanus, typhoid, hepatitis A/B as your home physician advises for dense animal cities. You will be around feral dogs asleep in doorways, cats on warm pipes, rats moving unhurried through grates, bats pouring out from under canal bridges for twenty minutes at a time, mules, goats, and market slaughter. Do not touch a bat, ever, even a dead one. Do not try to be friendly with a doorway dog until a local tells you which dog it is.

Also plan for the altitude. Two thousand meters is not extreme, but it is enough for headache, poor sleep, breathlessness on the dished stone stairs, and worse hangovers for the first two or three days — especially after an hour hanging in a freight lift on a cliff face. Arrive hydrated, go slow, no heroic walking tour on day one.

**2. Prepare for no green.**
Literally none. No trees, no weeds, no parks to recover in, no grass smell after rain, only black basalt, white guano fans, tile, iron, grey birds, and tea-colored runoff. First-time visitors often don’t realize how much they will *crave* a plant until day four.

The diet follows from this. Everything photosynthetic is imported up the lifts: lowland grain, preserved plant foods when you can get them. Everything else is local animal product: eggs, squab, preserved meat, tallow, milk from roof goats, communal-kitchen boiled grain. It is protein-rich, fatty, salty, fiber-poor, and monotonous. Bring fiber supplement and a multivitamin if you are staying more than a week. Vegetarians can survive on eggs and grain; vegans will have a hard, expensive time. If fresh fruit or leafy greens matter to your sanity, bring dried/preserved supplies — they are also excellent trade goods and gifts, in small, legally declared amounts.

Do NOT bring live plants, seeds, soil, live insects, or a pet. The one imported frog is now ineradicable in every drain in the city. The locals learned that lesson permanently, and any sane port authority will too. Whatever roots here gets eaten, poisoned by ammonia, or starved on basalt anyway. On departure, expect to have your boots, cuffs, and luggage cleaned. Guano dust, spores, midges, cockroach eggs in a warm hem — you do not want to seed another world with this city’s undergrowth.

**3. Re-set your clock.**
Nothing moves fast. Trams inch, barges inch at walking pace, a reservoir tower is begun by masons and finished by their grandchildren. Bureaucracy — especially roof-rights, nest-rights, and water litigation — is measured in seasons, not days. You cannot re-roof in nesting season. You cannot seal a facade without building equivalent cavities elsewhere. A factory must build its rookery ledge before its chimney.

Budget twice the time. Book the lift up and down well ahead; the cables weep, loads are heavy, and weather the city makes for itself can stop operations. If you have a tight connection in the lowlands, you have already made a mistake.

### What To Pack

Think waterproof, washable, disposable outer layers, and nothing precious that breathes.

**Clothing:** A broad hat you wear *always* outdoors — not for sun alone (UV is strong at altitude and there is no tree shade anywhere) but for droppings, drip from a million ledges, swifts screaming through chimney smoke just overhead, and midges rising off canals. A light canvas hood or veil like the guano crews wear is not overkill in rookery districts. A sacrificial outer shirt/jacket you can abandon at the end; the ammonia-and-wet-feather smell gets into seams and does not fully leave.

Waxed canvas or synthetic rain shell, not untreated leather or cotton that will stay wet and mold. Several thin layers — cold steaming mornings, hot tile-and-iron afternoons, cooler damp nights. More dry socks than shirts. Closed, ankle-supporting shoes with aggressive tread, broken in: curbs are rounded, stair treads are dished, mule routes are worn into troughs, guano is slick dry and slicker wet, mats and post-rain snails make canal stone treacherous. Smooth soles will betray you.

**Sleep and skin:** Serious earplugs and an eye mask. The dawn chorus in rookery districts is a physical pressure that starts before light and makes conversation pointless; at dusk starlings settle like gravel poured on tin, bats leave, dogs start. Foundries thump at intervals you feel in your molars all night. If you are a light sleeper, this matters more than bedding. Insect head-net for canal evenings, repellent, bite cream, a fine bed-net even indoors — cockroaches in bathhouses, house spiders lacing alleys with webs every single morning, flies in constant orbit around food. The webs, by the way, are routine: sweepers cut them with long poles. Just duck and don’t walk mouth-open through an alley at dawn.

**Gear protection:** Dry bags inside your bag, sealed pouches for documents and electronics. Condensation forms on cool basalt and cool metal every morning; paper swells, leather molds, unprotected steel rusts visibly. Leave heirloom books and instruments behind. Bring a headlamp — interior rooms (see below) can be cave-dark — and a small clothesline and pegs. You will be washing and failing to fully dry things constantly.

Sunblock, lip balm, sunglasses. The afternoon cloud is white and flat, but before it forms the sun on black water and pale guano is glare from every direction.

### The Ascent and First Hours

The freight lifts climb for almost an hour. Eat lightly beforehand if you get motion sick, face outward if heights bother you, and keep hats/gloves secured — updraft, cables dripping, birds using the same airspace. By the top, your ears, sinuses and clothing will already smell faintly of the city.

Try to arrive morning. By mid-afternoon the plateau makes its own weather: heat off a hundred square kilometers of tile and shallow black cisterns, moisture from laundries, bathhouses, canals, and uncountable animal breath, forming a flat white cloud that belongs to this place alone. Rain often falls inside the rim and nowhere else. It is not a disaster — frogs call from every drain, birds bathe violently in roof puddles, steam comes off foundry yards — but it is a poor moment to haul luggage across guano-wet stone while disoriented.

Spend the first afternoon doing almost nothing. Drink treated water — not cistern water, which stands warm as bathwater and feeds mats and midges, however clear it looks. Assume all canal, cistern, gutter and roof water is for washing, stock, and industry until a local shows you a verified drinking source. Boil or filter your own.

### Where To Stay

Ask specifically for an *interior* room. Buildings are habitat in their skin: nesting holes, ledges, cavities by the thousand, humans inside. That is charming at noon and deafening, hot in patches from roosting birds, and feather-dusty at 4 a.m. The deeper in the building, the quieter and cleaner, though darker. Check before paying:

- Is there a working loft-keeper for the building? A good one means cleared chimneys, harvested guano on schedule, controlled pigeons. A bad one means chaos.
- Are your room’s vents screened against bats/midges but not illegally sealed? Never block a nest hole or vent yourself to stop noise — that can be a legal offense and will simply drive animals into the walls.
- How are food and trash stored? Rats and crows are professionals. Use the lidded metal bins provided, never keep open food overnight, shake out shoes and clothing in the morning (cockroaches, spiders).
- Bathhouse expectations: they are communal, steamy, clean enough by local standards, and alive. Shower sandals are mandatory.

If you cannot sleep in noise, pay more to stay away from rookery towers, markets (crows), grain elevators (sparrows), and canal bridges (bats). There is no quiet green suburb to retreat to. Quiet here means thick basalt and distance from a roost.

### Moving Around and Behaving

Walk like a local: slow, center-of-trough aware, hat on, look up before you stand under a ledge, look down before a canal edge. In the lower quarters canals are the streets — barges with coal, salt, grain, machine parts pass at walking pace, and a passing barge lifts clouds of midges that bring swallows down in low cutting arcs right at face height. Step aside, close your mouth, let them pass.

Do not feed or scatter food for birds, dogs, or goats. Feeding rights, loft rights, and market crow territories are managed, owned, litigated. A tourist tossing crumbs can start a fight — avian and human. Do not reach into nesting cavities for photos, do not flash lights into roosts, do not try to move a sleeping dog with your foot. Ask porters, loft-keepers, and sweepers before photographing work crews; guano scraping is skilled, hazardous work, not local color.

Learn three useful phrases in whatever lingua franca your translator settles on: *Whose nests are these? May I pass/re-roof/repair? Where is drinking water?* Respect for nest-rights will open more doors than money will.

Carry small, non-live trade goods if you want goodwill: dried fruit, coffee, tea, spices, good cloth, salt is already traded but plant luxuries are valued. Cash economies exist around lifts and markets, but this city exports fertilizer, leather, tallow, bone meal, glue, lamp oil, preserved meat and breeding stock — it understands barter in kind very well.

### Eating, Health in the Daily Round

Eat hot, cooked, at busy communal kitchens and stalls — boiled grain, eggs, fresh squab — and watch the fly situation with calm judgment; a stall with a boy fanning and covered pans beats a prettier open display. Peel or avoid imported raw produce unless you know its wash water. Wash hands constantly and after any contact with animals, ledges, canal rails, tram straps furred with droppings. Assume handrails, worn bright by generations, are beautiful and filthy.

Daily antihistamine at night helps many visitors sleep despite dust. Air any sneezing that worsens daily, fever, chest tightness, pink eye that won’t clear, or a bite that swells — get seen locally early. Clinics here are very good at exactly the zoonotic, respiratory, and soft-tissue infections this city produces. They are less impressed by a traveler who waited.

Let your body acclimate to the smell over 2-3 days; do not drown yourself in perfume, it only layers over ammonia and attracts flies. A vinegar rinse for outer clothes, sun-drying on a parapet when the goats allow it, helps.

### Season Matters

Spring: swifts arrive over weeks, air thickening day by day, nesting law at its strictest, maximal noise. Autumn: departure, somewhat thinner air, melancholy locals and easier roofs to repair. Winter: mats go dark, cisterns steam harder, animals crowd every warm vent — buildings literally warm in patches, indoors cozy, respiratory dust lower but mold and crowding infections higher. Summer: strongest city-made cloud and rain cycle, midges at peak, heat off tile intense.

If you have a choice for a first visit, late autumn after the swifts leave and before winter battening is the gentlest introduction.

### Leaving

Plan a slow descent, a thorough wash, and a day in the dry low country just sitting in relative quiet and looking at something green if you can find it down there. Your ears will ring with absence. Your clothes may need two washes or retirement. Check luggage seams for hitchhikers before you transit home.

Come prepared not to conquer this place — nobody does, in less than a generation — but to be temporarily tolerated by a city that was already old, already whitened, already softened round at the edges when your ancestors elsewhere were learning whatever they were learning. Walk slowly, breathe filtered, keep your hat on, don’t seal a nest, and watch, once, from a roof at dusk when the keeper opens the lofts and the pigeons go up all at once like a book the size of a building being riffled, circle twice over steaming water, and settle already at home.
