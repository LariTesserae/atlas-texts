This is not a jungle you hike into. It is a bowl of reflected sunlight, eight or nine kilometers wide and a hundred and fifty meters deep, where the climate, light, and growth have locked into a perfect, unchanging balance.

Nothing down there is dangerous in the usual predatory way. There are almost no animals, no mud, no storms, no streams to flood, no night cold. The danger is cumulative and seductive: heat that never eases, light that comes from everywhere, water that is everywhere but hard to drink, ground that is not ground, and a stillness that will quietly change your pace, your appetite, and your sense of time until leaving feels unnecessary and difficult.

Prepare for a short, disciplined visit. Do not plan to "live off the basin," explore until tired, or camp until conditions improve. Conditions will never improve, worsen, or help you mark time.

### 1. Understand what you are entering

The rim is ordinary, warm, dry plain. The pit below it is a separate world:

* **Constant heat:** About 98-100°F / 37-38°C on the floor, day and apparent day, with saturated air. There is no cool morning, evening relief, shade temperature drop, or breeze. The rim cuts the wind. Exertion that would be moderate on the plain can become heat illness down below.
* **Shadowless light:** Pale clay walls bounce sun from leaf to wall to leaf. Undersides of leaves, undersides of branches, the underside of *you* are lit. Glare is worst toward the north wall, where there are fern meadows and succulent rosettes almost too bright to look across, but there is no truly dim forest floor except deep inside the root layers.
* **No soil floor:** In the bottom you walk on a living root-mat, sometimes two meters thick — interlaced trunks, vines, fallen logs incorporated rather than rotted, leaves filling every gap. Below one layer is another, and another. It gives like a raft. A misstep can punch through into a dim green hollow with no bottom you can trust. The original clay is theoretical.
* **No running water:** It never quite rains. Condensation forms and evaporates at the same rate; true rain arrives only as warm mist absorbed before it can run. Water sits in bromeliad cups, hollow canes, fleshy stems, and inch-deep tea-colored pools in root hollows. There will be no stream to follow out, orient by, wash in, or reliably fill from.
* **Almost no decay, growth, or change:** Leaves soften to mat texture and stop. Fruit hangs ripe while seeds sprout through its skin. Saplings stay knee-high for years. Abandoned huts are neither collapsed nor overgrown. This applies to your traces, too. A broken stem, trampled moss, food scrap, or human waste will not conveniently rot away.
* **Sound dies:** Leaf and moss absorb sound within about ten meters. Under the low insect/plant hum, which you will feel as pressure more than hear, you will not shout to a partner, hear them approach, or locate yourself by distant noise.

### 2. Fitness and heat preparation

Visit only if you can, in hot conditions:

- Climb 150 vertical meters on a slippery, plant-covered slope, both down and — much harder — back up while heat-exhausted.
- Walk for hours on springy, uneven, ankle-twisting root mat, often stooped under broad-leaf tunnels, sometimes in oven-like clearings.
- Carry all your water in and all your waste out.

Acclimatize on the plain for several days first if you are not heat-adapted. Do a test hike there at midday in the clothes and pack you intend to use. If you get headache, nausea, confusion, chills, racing pulse, or stop sweating on the plain, do not descend. There is no cooler recovery place below, no creek to lie in, and rescue would require rescuers to make the same climb.

Set a hard turnaround and exit time before you go down, and obey a watch/alarm, not your feelings. Time flattens in the basin. Your movements will naturally become slower and smaller after a few hours. That is not acclimatization; it is the beginning of trouble and of the place's pull.

Best pattern for an ordinary traveler: **one daylight excursion, rim support waiting above, no overnight stay on a first visit.** If you must sleep below, treat it as a technical expedition, not camping.

### 3. The descent is the crux

There is no trail, only a place on the rim where vegetation is "less impossible." You will descend moss, then wall-ferns, then sideways trees whose roots fan over raw clay like hands.

Bring:

- A fixed rope system anchored securely on the plain, well back from the rim — vehicle, buried deadman, or grouped scrub anchors, never a single bent tree. You need at least 200 meters of rope for rigging, safety lines, and retrieval, even if you intend to free-climb most of it.
- Helmet, gloves with grip, knee protection, and boots with aggressive tread and strong ankle support. You will grip roots belly-to-wall in places. Gloves are also to protect the plants: tearing moss stitching is not just bad ethics, it can start the erosion the roots are preventing. The wall should be crumbling and isn't only because it is held.
- A second, independent line for packs. Lower gear; do not descend overloaded.
- Markers for your exact descent line that you place *on your rope and equipment*, not by cutting blazes. Cut marks will persist, and a torn wall will not heal on a human timescale.

Never descend alone, never descend in the fullest midday glare if you can time the wall work earlier from the plain's day, and pre-place small water caches along the rope if your party is large enough to do it safely. The climb out must be planned as the main event, with energy and water reserved exclusively for it. Many visitors will feel able to wander "one more clearing" at the bottom. That feeling is unreliable.

Leave a rim watcher with water, shade, radio, binoculars, and your written exit deadline. If you miss the deadline, they should assume heat or a fall through the mat, not that you found something interesting.

### 4. Clothing and light protection

Dress for reflected, omnidirectional sun in a sauna:

- **Full coverage, loose, light-colored, quick-drying clothing.** Long sleeves and trousers protect from sun, sap, abrasive stems, and constant wet leaf contact. Shorts and a tank top will sunburn and chafe within hours.
- **Wide-brim hat with a neck flap, plus protection underneath.** Because light bounces upward, burn sites include under the chin, under the nose, ears, eyelids, and the underside of forearms. Use high-SPF sunscreen there too and reapply constantly; sweat and condensation will strip it.
- **Polarized sunglasses with side shields, and a spare.** In the glare-meadow and clearings you can become effectively snow-blind. Keep a lanyard on them; if you drop them into the mat layers, assume they are gone.
- **No cotton if you can avoid it.** Everything will film with sweat and mist and stay that way. Clothes will never dry below. Bring a sealed dry set kept on the rim for after the climb, not in the basin.
- **Rain gear is almost useless.** It does not rain properly, and a shell will cook you. A tiny breathable sun layer beats waterproofing.

Bring a headlamp even for a day visit. Not because the floor is dark — it mostly isn't — but because tunnels under meter-wide leaves, layers below a mat break, and any delay can put you in pockets of green dimness, and because finding rope footholds on the climb needs directed light.

### 5. Water, food, and waste

Do not count on local water. The inch-deep pools are still, tea-colored, shared with plant matter, microorganisms, and tiny frogs living in bromeliad tanks. Squeezing a fleshy stem, as inhabitants apparently could, may give a mouthful of peppery, unfamiliar sap. For an ordinary traveler:

- Carry a minimum of 1 liter per hour of planned basin time, plus a separate sealed reserve for the ascent. That is heavier than you want. Carry it anyway.
- Bring electrolytes. You will sweat continuously even standing still.
- If you must refill, filter and then purify (boil-equivalent chemical or UV treatment) cup/pool water. Do not drink straight from bromeliads, and do not empty a plant's tank — that tank is an animal's whole habitat.
- Bring all food from above. Local fruit is uncanny, often sprouting from within, unknown in toxicity, and low in reliable bearing. Animal protein is essentially absent. Pack calorie-dense, salty food that tolerates heat; it will not spoil normally in your timeframe, but heat will melt, separate, and make most trail food unappetizing.
- **Carry out everything, including human waste.** There is no soil to bury in, no decay to process it, and no stream to carry it. Use a pack-out toilet system. Urinate only where your rim guide/local protocol directs, and never into bromeliad cups or drinking pools.

A small stove is of limited use. Fuel will burn, but surrounding growth is perpetually damp, the air still, and smoke will hang at head height rather than clear. Fire in the clearings appears not to have been a regular feature of the people who built there. Cook on the rim if possible, and eat cold below.

### 6. Navigation and communication

A compass is more useful than the sun, because sun position will not give you useful shadows and the light is diffused by reflection. But even compass work is hard: clearings look alike, groves close within meters, the white wall arcs in every direction once you are below center, and sound cues are absent.

Use boring, redundant methods:

- Continuous line or breadcrumb system from the descent point if you go beyond sight of the wall: bright flagging on *removable* cord, GPS track if your multiverse equipment works there, numbered waypoints, and a paper map with pace counts.
- High-visibility clothing. Green, brown, and white all disappear — white against clay glare, green against foliage. Fluorescent orange or magenta, ugly as it feels, is a safety feature.
- Radios for each person, tested below the rim. Whistles are heavily muffled but still better than voice at close range. Flares are nearly pointless in that brightness; signal with radio and movement in a clearing.
- Stay in voice *and* visual range where possible. Ten meters can be isolation if one person stoops into a leaf tunnel and the other takes a wrong root junction.

Mark nothing permanently. Do not stack stones, cut poles, or clear a campsite. The existing stone circle and platforms have been kept clear impossibly; do not imitate, "tidy," or occupy them.

### 7. Shelter and sleep, if you stay

Do not sleep directly on the root-mat. It is damp, inhabited in layers below, easily damaged, and your body heat/pressure will leave a long-lived depression.

- Use a hammock strung between established trunks, or a lightweight platform system that distributes weight, copied in spirit from local woven sleeping platforms but brought in and removed.
- You need a complete mosquito/no-see-um style fine mesh enclosure even though obvious biting wildlife is scarce; the low hum is insects, and the cups and hollows breed small life you cannot see. More important is a **blackout eye mask and earplugs plus an alarm**: temperature and general brightness cues for waking/sleeping are weak, and sleep in 100-degree saturated air is poor. Without imposed rhythm, people drift, nap in clearings, and lose the exit window.
- Keep one absolutely dry sealed bag for medications, radio batteries, and a change of socks. Socks matter: feet will be wet all day on spongy mat. Change, powder, and inspect them at every long rest.

Treat any cut with unusual seriousness. In ordinary jungle the fear is rapid rot and infection; here the fear is stranger — wounds, like leaves and fruit, may not progress normally, while heat and constant moisture still macerate skin. Clean immediately, antiseptic, cover, and make any spreading redness, fever, or confusion an abort-and-climb decision while climbing is still possible.

### 8. The structures and the people who are not there

Scattered one to a clearing you may find low domes of bent saplings thatched so thick they have rooted at the edges, sleeping platforms, and a circle of eight uncut stones with pressed bare mat inside. There are no tools, hearths, bones, potsherds. That does not mean abandoned forever. It means the owners build only from what grows there and the basin preserves the result.

Behave as a guest in a house whose occupants stepped out:

- Do not enter domes, lie on platforms, rearrange stones, leave offerings, or take plant, stone, thatch, seed, or "abandoned" material.
- Do not assume a structure is a safe emergency shelter except for immediate life-threat, and if you use one, log it and restore it as exactly as you can.
- If you see smoke, a fresh mattress, or hear the glass-note frog calls suddenly stop in a pattern, withdraw the way you came. Inhabitants, if present, have no reason to be findable and every home advantage. Animal life is a rumor here; human life is even quieter.

Photograph if you wish, but do not clear leaves for a better shot. Every tear persists as evidence.

### 9. Mental preparation

Expect monotony that becomes vivid: miles of variation in mood rather than kind — bright northern fern glare, darker central groves smelling of unopened mushrooms, southern tunnels under meter-wide leaves. A single white long-necked bird crossing crowns, a lizard emerald on a trunk, one frog answering another, will assume enormous significance.

This is pleasant, and that is the risk. People slowed down, rested in slightly less brilliant green, drank from stems, and stopped wanting to break things — including, eventually, their own schedule. Counter it artificially:

- Buddy check-ins by timer: talk, drink, eat salt, state location and next waypoint.
- Tasks in sequence: descend, reach named clearing, rest ten minutes, return. No open-ended "see how far it goes."
- A non-negotiable rule: any member can call ascent, for any symptom or unease, without argument at the bottom. Arguments happen on the rim.

Do not bring children, pets, or anyone who cannot self-report heat symptoms. Pets especially will suffer silently in the heat, damage the mat, and be unable to climb out.

### Minimum kit summary

Fixed rope and anchors, harness/climbing backup, helmet, gloves; rim support and radios; compass/paper navigation and removable cord markers; polarized glare glasses plus spare; full-coverage breathable sun clothing, hat with under-protection, sunscreen; boots fitted for wet root-mat; 1 L water per hour plus ascent reserve and electrolytes; purification system; heat-stable salty food; pack-out waste kit; hammock/mesh/blackout mask if overnight; first aid with antiseptic, blister and heat-illness supplies, personal medications in waterproof; headlamp; bright outer layer; sealed dry bag kept largely for the rim.

Go down as if visiting a finished work of art that happens to be alive, hotter than your body, and indifferent to whether you leave. It has finished happening and will keep finishing, leaf for identical leaf. Your job is to be the one temporary thing in it: enter lightly, keep your own time, and climb back into wind, dry dirt, and ordinary shadows while you still clearly want to.
