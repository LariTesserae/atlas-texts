You are not visiting a barn that happens to be underground. You are visiting a single, closed, living room — about thirty paces long, twelve across, roofed in brick, floored in brick, walled in brick — that contains goats, mules, pigs, rabbits, hens, ducks, pigeons, people, children, frogs, geckos, mice, bats, crickets, a loom, a cook-fire, a compost pit, and almost no empty space at all.

It has been balanced like that for a very long time. Preparations are mostly about not unbalancing it, and not being overwhelmed by it in the first ten minutes.

### 1. Decide if your body can do it

It is never dark down there, but it is close. It is never wet, but it is always damp. It is warm — warmer than the surface — from animal bodies, breath, and the compost pit heating the wall behind the hearth. That warmth does not turn off at night.

Do not go down if you have:

* Asthma, serious dust / straw / feather / animal-dander allergies, or a weak stomach for ammonia and dung. The smell is not filthy — there is limewash, herbs, woodsmoke, and a sweet mineral tang underneath — but it is *total*. It will be in your clothes and hair for days.
* Claustrophobia or a panic response to noise. The vault returns every sound a little larger. Goat bells, wings, hens, a mule stamping, fodder being chopped *chuff-chuff-chuff*, children calling, and at feeding time a beaten-metal gong that makes the whole hall answer for a full minute. There is no quiet corner to recover in.
* Bad knees, bad balance, or a large frame you cannot duck and sidestep with. You will duck at the entrance, duck under arches and gallery beams, step sideways around baskets and animals, and never once walk the length of the hall in a straight line.

There is only one human exit: the stairwell you came down. Everything keeps that throat and the central aisle just-clear for a reason.

### 2. What to wear and bring

Pack like you are entering someone else's body: nothing disposable, nothing scented, nothing you cannot carry back out.

**Wear:**
* Closed, soft-soled shoes with good grip. The rule is spoken like architecture: *No bare feet on the slats.* Slats are gapped, splintered, and slick with wear; the brick floor is polished glossy; the stairs are dished in the middle from centuries of soft shoes. Hard cleats and heavy boots scuff the brick and frighten animals on the wooden platforms. Walk a little to the side of the worn hollow on the steps.
* Washable, breathable clothes you do not mind smelling of straw and goat. Natural fibers are best near the hearth. Leave perfume, scented soap, insect spray, and strong deodorant on the surface — goats, rabbits, and pigeons will react to you as a smell before they react to you as a person.
* A soft cap or headscarf. This is for literal ducking — low brick, rope, hanging herb pots — and for the regular drips from the vault, the pigeons in the upper tiers, and the dust and down that hang visible in the light shafts at feeding time.
* Layers you can tie around your waist. Surface-warm will become close and hot within minutes below.

**Bring, if you can:**
Useful weight is welcome. Useless weight is a problem, because everything that goes down has to live somewhere down there.

Good gifts / trade: a waterskin *full* (water is hauled down and measured animal by animal), clean straw or dry fodder, barley or other sprouting seed, rope, undyed weaving fiber, salt for animals (ask first), a sound wooden slat or lashed gate-piece, lime for mortar and whitewash, a dented-but-sound pot or gourd for drip-catching.

Do not bring: disposable packaging, outside animals of any kind, meat scraps, unfamiliar feed in quantity, open flame, lantern oil, or anything perfumed or chemically treated. Cross-world disease is a real risk in both directions here — the mouse runs in the walls, the pigeon traffic through the roof holes, and the bats in the half-bricked shaft cannot be quarantined. If you have recently been around sick livestock, other byres, or bat caves, say so *above ground* and be prepared to be turned away kindly.

Carry a small cloth for your face, earplugs for sleeping if you are invited to stay (use them loosely — you still need to hear the gong and the animals), and a handkerchief you can wet against straw-dust. Leave flash photography and bright, sudden lights up top. The place is lit by hard-edged pools of ordinary daylight from glazed-brick shafts. A sudden beam panics hens, flushes pigeons, and blinds people whose eyes are set for shade-between-pools.

### 3. Arrival: the roof is the ground

From above you will almost miss it. Look for a low, broad roof at ankle height — fired tile and packed clay, steaming faintly — and a thick brick stair throat.

Do not wander across that roof. It is a vault, patched in rose, ochre, ash-grey and greenish brick, and it is also their ceiling, their light system, and their pigeons' front door. Approach by whatever worn path the locals use and announce yourself before you start down. Voices carry oddly; a short, projected call is normal manners, mumbling is not.

Going down:
* Duck early and stay ducked.
* Use the rope. It is not decorative — it is black and polished by palms, tied to iron rings that have grooved the plaster oval. It will give a little. That is expected.
* Go slow. Swallows nest in the throat despite all discouragement and will swoop past your head. Let them.
* Pause halfway. Let your eyes and ears adjust. The light does not vanish; it changes to shafts and shade. The noise will seem to rise to meet you. That is the vault.

### 4. The rules are physical facts

Newcomers are told them as if they were features of the building. Repeat them to yourself until they become reflex:

1. **No bare feet on the slats.**
2. **No fire outside the hood.** The only fire lives in the hooded clay hearth at the far end. No candles, pipes, or cook-stoves elsewhere. Straw is stacked dense as masonry everywhere.
3. **Do not block a light shaft.** Not with your body for long, not with a basket, and *especially* not with fodder, however perfectly a bale wants to fit there. Light is heat control, is fodder sprouting on the gallery trays, is the fig in the cracked amphora trying forever to fruit, is the animals' sense of time. Locals haul woven shaft-covers open and shut on ropes to move those pools. Do not touch the ropes unless shown.
4. **First drink from any new drip is left for the frogs.** Condensation is a water source here. It beads on the vault, runs a mortar line, and drips — *tock* — into wired clay cups, algae-black troughs, or onto an animal that has positioned itself on purpose. The pale frogs stationed in the drip bowls eat the flies from the lower troughs. Let them work. Do not drink from drip bowls at all unless a resident hands you a cup; they know which drips are clean, which are for ducks, and which stain plaster green-black.
5. **Knock a gate twice before you open it.** Every niche, hutch, and pen — pigeons high up like a dovecote, rabbits and hens in the middle tiers, goats and piglets low — has a lashed wood gate and something behind it that needs a second to move back from the swing. Never reach through a feeding hole. Never open a gate just to look.
6. **If you bring something down, you carry something up.** Empty waterskins, old straw, a basket of dung for the surface, a broken slat for repair. This is not politeness, it is plumbing. There is nowhere down there for waste to go that is not still in the room.

### 5. How to move and behave inside

* **Think in tiers.** Top: pigeons, coming and going in a clatter through the roof holes; geckos flat by the shafts. Middle: rabbits, hens, ducks in the damp corner, the gallery with its loom and fodder trays. Floor: goats four-and-five to a slatted platform with bells too large for them, two patient mules whose hooves have worn crescents by their tethering rings, piglets (never grown pigs — they are taken up at a certain size) in a tiled pen that can be sluiced with one bucket.
* **Follow children and baskets.** Human children move between everything with baskets almost as large as they are. They know the current clear path. If you are in the way — and you will be — a short step sideways to a stall front is better than backing into the aisle.
* **Project, don't shout, don't whisper.** Speak in short, practiced phrases, or ask to use one of the clay speaking-tubes set into the pillars if you need the far end of the hall. Cupping your hands and yelling just sets off the hens.
* **Hands visible, movements slow.** Goats will inspect you neatly and precisely. Mules will tolerate you until they don't; stay out of the crescent behind them. Ducks have claimed their corner and will announce you. Offer no food from your pockets — feeding is measured, timed to the gong, and the wrong scrap can spoil a gut adapted to cut barley shoots, husk, and pod.
* **Accept the loop.** Droppings fall through slats into brick channels, are raked toward the compost behind the hearth wall, warm the back stalls (prized for newborns in cool months), breed worms for hens and flies for frogs. Old straw down, new straw down, animals up blinking, animals down. Do not try to "helpfully" clean, reorganize stacks, re-hang pots out of goat reach, or sluice a pen unless you are handed a bucket and shown where the single bucket is allowed to go. Well-meant tidying is how light gets blocked and water gets wasted.
* **Food and sleep, if offered.** Eat only at the hearth end, wash hands before and after handling animals (lime and ash are usually there — use what you are given), and expect water to be offered in measured cups, not refilled carelessly. If you sleep on the gallery, you will sleep to bells, wings, the loom's treadle in memory, and drips landing exactly where generations expect them. Tie your shoes and bundle *up* — mice have a whole second architecture inside the brick cavities and view loose grain as a storage invitation.

### 6. Leaving

Climb up slowly; your eyes will need the reverse adjustment, and your arms will likely be full per Rule Six. Above ground, shake out straw and down away from the roof and shaft-holes, wash thoroughly, and quarantine or hot-wash the clothes you wore below before visiting any other animal place — in this world or any other you travel to next.

Go down expecting a barn. Come up understanding a body: brick ribs, wooden guts, held warmth, and human hands everywhere keeping the passages just clear enough to move. Prepare to be useful, small, and unhurried, and it will make room for you.
