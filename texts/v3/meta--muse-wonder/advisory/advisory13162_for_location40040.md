This is not a sea you visit. It's a workplace you are allowed to float across.

The Low Sea / the Basin is a 400km-long, below-sea-level industrial heat sink that happens to be flooded, inhabited, and navigable. Everything there runs — pumps, factories, fisheries, traffic, weather, tides — but almost none of it runs for natural reasons, and almost none of it is built for visitors. An ordinary human can go, safely and enjoyably, if you prepare like you're embedding on an offshore oil complex crossed with a canal system, not like you're going to a lake district.

### 1. Before You Go: Permission, Purpose, and Paperwork

You don't just show up at the rim.

Entry is only through the locks, and the locks are only for scheduled barges. The outside ocean is literally higher than the land inside — you sail in at normal sea level through 60-metre black steel gates, sit while the green water sinks under you past concrete depth-lines, and exit *below* the surface of the world. If that process makes you uneasy, decide now. There is no road down, no beach access, no walking in along the dike.

Book:
- A berth on a licensed barge in and out. Private pleasure craft are a terrible idea and usually prohibited — the shipping lanes follow drowned motorways, but everything else down there is uncharted-by-you: warehouse roofs 2 metres under the surface, lamp posts, gantries, radiator fins, mooring chains. You need sonar, a pilot who knows District shorthand, and permission to be in a lane.
- Accommodation in advance on a specific platform/roof-town. District 17 is not "near" District 9 in any useful sense. From the middle you can't see the rim in any direction. Crossings take a full day in good weather. Roof-towns are container dormitories on warehouse roofs, stacks, and cooling-tower platforms — they fill with shift workers, and they will not have a spare bunk because you turned up.
- A safety induction. Expect to be treated as a liability until proven otherwise. You'll likely be issued or required to rent: hard hat, hi-vis, steel-toe non-slip boots, a life vest with locator, and ear protection. Bring your own if you have odd sizing — the biofilm makes *every* deck, stair rail, and ladder treacherous, and mussel shell cuts like glass.

Get the pump rota. This is more important than a weather forecast. There is no moon tide. At 06:00 the southern intakes open and the whole sea leans south — 2 knots down Foundry Avenue, 3 knots through the narrows in District 9, slack behind warehouses. At midday it reverses for the cooling cycle. Every boat departure, dive, fish-tender, submersible tram, and maintenance job is planned around it. Learn to read the flags on buoys and the boards on docks: **a red board means the water under you is about to move hard enough to take a swimmer and keep them.** Do not argue with a red board.

Tell someone your exact route. Mobile signal is patchy in fog and useless between steel stacks. File a float plan with your barge captain and your host platform.

Medical prep: Up-to-date tetanus is non-negotiable — everything submerged is sharp, new, and only nine years drowned. Corners haven't softened. Paint is still intact. So are gear teeth in open pits, pipe flanges, broken windscreens in drowned car parks. Bring any prescription meds for double the trip length; storms can batten a roof-town for a day while waves break over the lower platforms. Seasickness medication even if you don't usually need it — it's not open ocean swell, it's a short grey chop combing across cold 12°C water, plus constant vibration through the hull from distant pumps.

### 2. What to Pack

Think North Sea workboat in autumn, plus factory floor.

**Wear / carry daily:**
- Waterproof outer shell, truly waterproof trousers, insulated mid-layers. Rain over warm districts falls warm; at the rim it falls cold. Fog banks form where cold air hits steam columns — you will sail into a white wall and come out soaked and suddenly next to a crane you could only hear.
- Non-slip, ankle-supporting boots with good tread. Algae fur makes handholds lethal. Assume every surface is iced.
- Warm hat and gloves that you can work a rope/phone with. Wind across the central emptiness has a 400km fetch once or twice a year, and a daily bite the rest of the time.
- Earplugs *and* ear defenders. Underway, the Basin is loud in a physical way. Sound travels through water into the hull, into your feet and teeth — circulation pumps throb like a bass note that sets loose tools humming, turbines whine, pile-drivers tok-tok-tok behind the mist, every sub and barge pings sonar. On deck add diesel generators, crane alarms, steam hiss, gulls. Sleep without earplugs will be poor.
- Sunglasses and also a headtorch. Glare off grey water by day; at night, triple-layer light pollution — white/sodium towns above, moving shipping lanes, and a dim green lit grid going down and away below the surface. Decks are lit, edges aren't always.
- Dry bags for everything. Spray, rain squalls born from evaporation inside the Basin itself, steam condensation. Nothing stays dry in a locker.
- Personal life vest if you were not issued a good one, worn *on deck at all times*. Falling in is not a swimming event: 12°C, a possible 2-3 knot current along a drowned road, near-invisible submerged steel, and water that is industrial coolant, not bathing water.

Do not pack: a swimsuit for recreation, a recreational dive kit, a drone without written clearance (cranes, steam, security), nice shoes, anything you can't afford to smell faintly of diesel, hot metal, and mussels for a week.

### 3. Arrival: The Locks Change Your Head

Going down in the lock is slow, cold, and quiet except for water and steel. You'll read stencilled warnings on the way down because there's nothing else to do. Seals have learned the locks and may commute with you — fat, impudent, entirely unimpressed. Don't feed them, don't lean over for them. They steal from mussel barges and know boats mean food.

Once the lower gates open you're in another sea, made one. Near the edges in clear weather you can see straight down the boat ramp: motorways continuing under water, lane markings intact, signs for districts now 30 metres down. Most newcomers spend the first hour staring over the side and feeling vaguely ill. That's normal. It was a factory floor nine years ago; it still looks like one that someone filled too quickly, trucks parked neatly, archives sandbagged.

Get your bearings by sound before sight. Districts announce themselves long before you see them — crane alarms, generators, steam. Ask your captain where you are *by infrastructure*, not compass alone: "lee of the warehouse," "edge of a radiator field," "down-current of the twin towers." Those are the real place names.

### 4. Moving Around

- **Stay on licensed boats and marked decks.** Islands are cooling towers rising straight out of black water, warehouse roofs turned ports, smokestacks with platforms welded at flood-height +10 metres. Between them, the "ground" is 80 metres of dimming green above pavement you can't see. There is no wading, no shore walking except the dike toe and occasional reed beds in sagging roof-lagoons.
- **Never cross under a crane load, never walk a roof without asking where it sags.** Sagging roofs hold shallow lagoons and reeds — pretty, and structurally the weakest bit.
- **Navigation is by buoy line and sonar.** The amber blinking buoys marching out of sight mark lanes that follow surveyed motorways. Off-lane is work-subs, radiator fields stretching for kilometres, intake grilles the size of housing blocks. Those intakes, in their concrete pump cathedrals, can pull in water, debris, and you. Exclusion zones around them are not advisory.
- Ship traffic is three-dimensional: barges above, work-submarines and submersible gantries below, propellers passing in the dark. If you're on a small tender, stay where the captain puts you and keep a handhold.

If you get a ride in a work-sub — the only sensible way to "see" the underwater country — expect light to go glitter, green, blue-green, then a dusk lit by industrial floodlamps never switched off. Whole flooded streets still burn underwater, haloed in plankton. Fish hang in thousands at the boundary layer of radiator fields where cold water meets a few-degrees-warmer outflow. It shimmers like air over a road. It's beautiful and it's a burn / entrapment hazard in a suit. Recreational diving is essentially not a thing here; suit diving is tethered, guided, task-specific, and cancelled the moment the pump rota changes.

**Absolutely do not swim, dive in, or put your hands in recreationally.** Besides currents and sharp steel: residual industrial chemicals despite the pre-flood strip-out, bacterial biofilm, mussel/oyster beds that cut and cause infected wounds, and warm outfalls that attract fish and seals — and disorient you with sudden temperature changes. Falling in = immediate rescue, get out, shower, disinfect cuts, monitor for infection.

### 5. Living on a Roof-Town

Expect container dormitories along a warehouse ridge, canteen, clinic, maybe a chapel, everything supplied by boat. Food is hearty, tinned/fresh-by-boat, plus the local fishery: mussels harvested by divers scraping intakes (a real job, constant), coarse fish and eels netted around warm outfalls in winter. Eat cooked seafood from the canteen freely; don't buy raw shellfish off a barge and eat it on deck.

Water: drink only platform-supplied potable water. Tap sources are clearly marked — industrial water systems are everywhere and not always obviously different.

Sleep: vibration, 24-hour light from below, pumps through the structure. Earplugs, eye mask.

Etiquette: This is not a tourist ruin. Production didn't stop; it climbed — what could drown was redesigned drowned, what couldn't moved up. People live in shipping containers *above* their old commute. Don't photograph workers, dorm washing lines, chapels/clinics, or maintenance pits without asking. Don't wander during an "ebb-equivalent": sectors behind mobile bulkheads are dropped 5 metres for six hours, exposing steaming machinery and aprons that become land again. Workers go down in boots, gulls scream in for stranded mussels, then the boards come up and the sea takes it back. That window is dangerous, scheduled to the minute, and not a sightseeing opportunity unless your host explicitly takes you in PPE with the crew.

Wildlife etiquette: Cormorants nest in cooling tower latticework, herons stalk roof-lagoons, seals beg. Observe, don't approach nests, don't feed. Mussel crust on every pipe will be scraped anyway — don't chip souvenirs; it damages intakes and infuriates the divers who have to redo it.

### 6. Weather and Emergencies

Weather is made locally. Brown-green clouds gather over warm districts and drift to cold ones. Standing fog banks hide whole tower clusters. Rain can stipple the surface hard enough it looks solid. In a real storm — long fetch across the central emptiness — lower platforms are cleared, roof-towns batten like ships attached to the sea floor. Know on arrival: where is your muster point, where is the highest platform, where are life rafts / fast rescue boats, and which pump-house (they sit like fortresses around the dike) is your evacuation point if your district must empty.

In fog: stay put. Sound becomes the only sense — cranes, sonar, alarms — and small-boat movement stops for a reason.

If you hear an unfamiliar alarm, follow the nearest worker, hands empty, no questions until you're where they put you. Industrial alarms here may mean electrical fault in a drowned substation, a bulkhead moving, a pump cycle starting early — things that move water.

### Checklist Before You Step on the Barge

- [ ] Return barge + platform bunk booked for specific districts/dates
- [ ] Induction done, PPE issued/fitted, pump rota understood
- [ ] Tetanus current, meds for 2x trip, seasickness + ear care
- [ ] Full waterproofs, insulated layers, non-slip steel-toes, dry bags
- [ ] Life vest, earplugs/defenders, headtorch, eye mask
- [ ] Float plan left with captain + host, no expectation of signal
- [ ] Mental rule set: red board = stop, no swimming ever, stay on deck/roof, ask before going anywhere

Go at night to the edge of your platform in District 17 if you can, rain pricking the black surface, and look once properly. Towns above, lanes between, the endless green-lit grid of avenues, radiator forests full of silver fish, pump cathedrals still running far below sight. That's when it clicks: you're standing on a roof, over a drowned motorway, inside a sea held in a bowl below the real sea — and it's working.

Prepare like a worker, move like a guest on a boat, and you'll be fine.
