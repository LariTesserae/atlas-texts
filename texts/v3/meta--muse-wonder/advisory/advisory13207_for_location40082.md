This is not a mountain you visit. It is a mountain range in the process of being born, and you would be walking on it while it is still happening.

That changes the preparation completely. The dangers are not just altitude, cold, and exposure — in fact, cold is one of the few things you will *not* get in the normal way. The dangers are heat from below, air too thin to breathe properly, absolutely no water, rock sharp enough to cut you and unstable enough to move under you, and a landscape that can invalidate your map, your campsite, and your return route while you sleep.

Do not go alone. Do not go expecting to stay long. Do not go expecting rescue to be plausible.

### 1. Understand the core conditions

**It is high, thin, and breathless.**
You are above a sea of dry dust, under a black, close sky. The stars are hard and unblinking because there is very little air above you. You will be panting even standing still. Expect altitude headache, broken sleep, nosebleeds, dizziness, impaired judgment, and the slow stupid mistakes that come with hypoxia. The warmth will fool you into thinking your body is fine when it is not.

**It is warm from underneath, not from the sun.**
The rock radiates stored geothermal heat. Wind on your neck will be warm. Stone through your boots will be warm. In cracks it will be hot enough to shimmer the air and occasionally glow dull red at the bottom. This means burns, heat stress, and melting gear are real risks — while the thin air around your head can still steal heat and moisture brutally fast. You are in a vertical contradiction: cooked from below, desiccated from above.

**There is no water. At all.**
No snow — it melts from beneath and evaporates from above. No springs, no damp hollows, no ice in shadow. Everything you drink, you carry. Your mouth will dry, your lips will split and sting, your throat will feel scraped by dust. Dehydration will arrive faster than you expect because you are breathing hard in warm, zero-humidity air.

**The ground is new, sharp, and moving.**
Nothing has been eroded. There are no rounded stones, no soil, no sand softened by water. The rock breaks in blades, cubes, and glassy shards that can slice leather. It ticks, heaves, cracks, and occasionally shrugs off entire ridge-sides in near silence. A trench can open where a foot-wide crack was in the morning. Elevation is a recent report, not a fact.

**It is dark and quiet in a way that disorients.**
A permanent dust canopy blots out normal daylight. Up above it you get a deep starlit twilight. Sound does not carry — the thin air and dust steal it. Distant collapses arrive as pressure on your skin, not noise. You will rely more on balance and vibration through your soles than on hearing. Many travelers find the silence and the subtly tilting horizon more unnerving than any single hazard.

### 2. Before you go

**Scout remotely, and treat the scouting as already out of date.**
If your multiverse transit allows drones, probes, or a brief aerial pass, use it to identify the thickest old flows in the center of the plateau, active-looking crack fields, and the current line of human cairns. Then assume a third of that has changed. Bring the survey, but navigate by what is in front of you.

**Fix your entry and exit off the ground.**
Do not place a portal, beacon, or cache directly on the plateau surface if you can avoid it. Ground that is level at dusk may be tilted, cracked, or buried by morning. Anchor your return point in the air if possible, or on the thickest, oldest-looking flow you can find, and set a timed extraction window. If your return depends on walking back down a specific slope, you may find that slope has become a fresh cliff into the dust-sea.

**Acclimatize — below the dust, if you can.**
Spend several days ascending in stages in comparable thin air before crossing. Learn your personal altitude symptoms while you still have the option to descend easily. Consider talking to a clinician back home about altitude medication. No drug substitutes for turning back when your headache becomes vomiting, confusion, or ataxia.

**Train specifically for this terrain.**
Practice walking on loose, angular talus with a heavy water load. Practice moving calmly — not running — on ground that shifts. Practice navigating by stars, inertial dead reckoning, and line-of-sight cairns, because magnetic compasses may be unreliable among unoxidized iron, and any map is fiction within days.

**Set hard limits in advance, while you are thinking clearly.**
Maximum time on the plateau: for an ordinary human carrying their own water, think in terms of one night, two at the absolute outside. Agree on turnaround triggers: loss of a water container, any burn more serious than a blister, ringing shards that do not stop, a new crack between you and your exit, or any member showing altitude confusion. Hypoxic people do not get to renegotiate the rules on site.

### 3. What to carry

**Water and electrolytes — your heaviest problem.**
Carry more than you calculate, in several small rigid containers rather than one large bladder a single shard can puncture. A hard-working, altitude-breathing adult in warm dry wind can easily need 4-6 liters a day here, and there is zero resupply. Add electrolytes; split lips and cramps are early warnings. Bring lip balm with a sealant, saline nasal spray, and a breath-warming humidity mask — breathing through dampened cloth helps enormously.

**Breathing protection.**
The dust below and on the plateau is dry, abrasive, mineral dust — ground rock, with fresh iron, copper, salts, and sulfur. Wear a well-fitted respirator with fine particulate filters when crossing the dust-sea, near fresh slides, or near vents. Bring sealed goggles. Ordinary sunglasses will not keep dust out of eyes that are already drying and tearing. If you have any ability to carry a compact gas detector, do — fumaroles here do not steam visibly, they only shimmer, and sulfur-rich, oxygen-poor, or carbon-dioxide-heavy air can pool in hollows without any visual warning.

**Oxygen, if you can carry it.**
Even a limited supplemental supply for sleep and for emergencies will dramatically improve judgment. At minimum, carry a pulse oximeter and actually use it: record resting readings, and treat plummeting numbers plus symptoms as a descent order.

**Feet and legs: armor them.**
This is where most injuries will happen. You need:
- High boots with puncture-resistant soles, stiff shanks, and heat-resistant outsoles — thin hiking shoes will be cut and will conduct ground heat.
- Cut-resistant gaiters to the knee. Shards will find your shins.
- Trekking poles with carbide tips for probing. Test every suspiciously bright, fresh-looking patch before committing weight. Listen for hollow ringing.
- A spare pair of dry socks sealed against dust.

**Clothing: protect from cutting, heating, and drying.**
Long pants and sleeves in tough, natural or aramid-type fibers that will not melt if you brush hot rock. Leather or heat-resistant gloves for any handhold — you will instinctively catch yourself with a palm, and here that can mean a deep glassy cut or a burn. A wide hat and high-protection eyewear for the brutal thin-air glare when the canopy thins, but expect to navigate mostly by headlamp. Use a headlamp with a red mode to preserve night vision under starlight, plus a backup light sealed against dust. Batteries suffer and dust infiltrates; keep spares warm and bagged.

**Sleep system: insulate from the ground, not the air.**
Your pad is a heat shield first and a comfort item second. Bring a thick, puncture-resistant insulating pad — never sleep directly on warm stone, however tempting people in the past have found it. A low bivy or simple windbreak is better than a tall tent: stakes will tilt or pull out as the ground moves, and a tent gives false confidence. Sleep in your boots' liners or with boots immediately reachable. Keep your respirator and headlamp on your chest.

**Navigation and marking.**
- Star chart / celestial navigation aid for that sky, if applicable.
- Inertial tracker or meticulous pace-and-bearing log.
- A rangefinder to judge distances across the deceptively clear plateau.
- Expendable marker wands with reflective flags. Build or repair cairns as you go, but expect them to lean. Note the direction of lean — it tells you which side is rising.
- A loud whistle is nearly useless in this air; carry visual signals — strobe, mirror, bright panel — and any radio you have, line-of-sight permitting.

**Medical kit, tailored.**
Blister and burn dressings, wound closure strips and irrigation for shard cuts (they will be dirty with metal-rich dust), tetanus status checked before departure, tweezers for glass splinters, rehydration salts, altitude and pain medication as advised by your clinician, and a tourniquet you hope never to use on a leg opened by a fall onto blade rock. There are almost no pathogens here — life has barely started — but a cut packed with copper- and sulfur-rich mineral dust is its own problem.

Leave behind: anything you intend to cache for a return trip, anything sentimental you would grieve when the plateau buries it, and cotton clothing that will chafe split, dry skin raw.

### 4. Crossing the dust-sea

You ascend through dry dust with no moisture in it — it will not wet your face, it will coat your lungs and abrade your equipment. Seal everything before you enter it. Breathe only through your filter. Visibility may drop to arm's length while your altimeter climbs. Move on instruments and tether to your companions. If you are climbing on foot at the margins, expect new cliffs where the plateau sheds material into the dust; do not hug the edge looking for a way up. Emergence above the dust is abrupt: black sky, hard stars, and the whole plateau opening at once. Stop there. Let your eyes forget the sun for a full twenty minutes before traveling by starlight.

### 5. Rules on the plateau itself

Locals who left the cairns and measuring posts learned these fast. Copy them.

1. **Walk the middle of old flows**, where stone is thickest and darkest and has survived longest.
2. **Never camp in a hollow**, however sheltered. Hollows are where cracks prefer to open and where heavy gases gather.
3. **Trust the slope that is already shedding** over the silent one. The noisy neighbor has spent its energy; the quiet one is storing it.
4. **If shards underfoot begin to ring** — a faint musical pinging as stress passes through them — move uphill, calmly and immediately, and keep moving until it stops.
5. **If the warm wind suddenly goes still** and the dust canopy seems to lower, the plateau is about to breathe out somewhere. Get off any crack line, get to thick ground, face away from the nearest vent field, and protect your airway.
6. **Step over cracks; never step into them** to test depth, and never bridge one on a slab that ticks when you tap it. By morning a step-over crack may be a trench with walls still cooling.
7. **Do not touch the crystals.** Overnight, salt needles and sulfur flowers bloom perfect around vents. They are beautiful, fragile, and contaminated with exactly the minerals you do not want on skin or in a cut. Photograph them and leave them to be ground back into the mountain.
8. **Check the ground twice for sleeping and once again before you actually lie down.** Look for the faintest orange, yellow, and pale-green films in warm cracks — that is life simpler than lichen, and it marks ground warm and chemically active enough that you should not sleep beside it.

Move in short legs, cairn to cairn. At each cairn, stop, drink whether you are thirsty or not, check feet, check your companions' speech for altitude slurring, and re-derive your bearing. The horizon will not hold a straight line here; trust instruments and stars over your inner ear when they disagree, and rest when your balance starts lying to you persistently — that can be tilt, fatigue, or hypoxia, and all three kill the same way.

### 6. When to leave

Leave early. The classic accident here is not a dramatic eruption — the plateau heaves rather than erupts — it is a traveler who stays one extra morning to watch crystal fields bloom, finds their descent route split by a fresh scarp, and runs out of water in warm wind while picking through bright new rubble in the dark.

If you stand at the center long enough, you will feel it: the slow rise under your boots, the lowlands falling away, new stone arriving from below and pushing the old new stone aside. It is an astonishing thing to witness — an embryo mountain range, warm in the black, still pushing itself closer to the sky.

Witness it briefly, respectfully, and from a position you can retreat from. In a thousand years it may have rain, soil, and seeds. For now, it has heat, height, darkness, and motion, and none of the gentler habits that would make it habitable. Prepare as a guest in a place that has not finished arriving, and it may let you leave before it rearranges itself around you.
