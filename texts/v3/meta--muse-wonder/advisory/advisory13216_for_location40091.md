Halu Cirque is not a town you visit. It's an instrument you are allowed to stand inside, very carefully, while it is running.

It was not built for you, it is not scaled for you, and almost everything that makes it survivable for the veilwings will make it actively miserable and dangerous for an ordinary human: polar night, thin high-altitude air, -40 to -50C cold, and somehow, everywhere, running water.

Prepare on the assumption that you will be cold, wet, deafened, blind, and in the way the entire time.

### 1. Before You Even Take the Lift Up

**You need an escort, and you need to be cleared by the caretakers.** The human population is a few dozen people living in half-buried pressure domes on the southern rim. There is no hotel, no public street system, no visitor center in the towers. Those towers are roosts. If you go without being expected, you will have nowhere warm to retreat to.

**Acclimatize to altitude and cold separately, first.** The air up there is too thin to carry weather properly, and too thin to breathe comfortably if you're coming from sea level. You will be panting in air that is also killing exposed skin in minutes. Headache, nausea from altitude, plus the subsonic hum from the towers that you feel in your molars and sinuses, is a combination that puts a lot of first-time visitors on the floor. Arrive rested, hydrated, and already used to an oxygen-assist mask. You will be wearing one outside.

**Learn how to talk without talking.** This is non-negotiable.

During a chorus peak — which happens every few hours and can build for an hour as it ripples across a tower face — human speech is physically useless. The caretakers communicate by pressure-pads in their gloves and by a tap-code on the forearm. High shrieking echolocation, lower chattering like rain on metal, wing membranes, dripping amplified by stone, and that low tower hum underneath it all. It's not loud like a concert. It's dense like weather. Your ear bones will ache before your brain registers it as sound.

Learn the basic taps before you go: stop, back, danger, need air, need warmth, I can't hear, escort-lead. Practice moving and answering with gloves on, in the dark.

**Train your eyes for red and infrared.** Halu runs on light discipline. Inside the domes: dim red, never white, never bright. Outside: essentially nothing. It's been night for eleven weeks when the description is set, and will be for five more. There is no horizon, no skyline. Black above black.

A camera flash, a white headlamp, even opening a bright tablet outside could blind a quarter-million echolocating animals at once and collapse a chorus. You will navigate by infrared smear on your visor, heat-bloom, and faint beacons. Everything living will look like a blur. Everything dead and new — the basalt — will look like nothing at all. If you are prone to vertigo, claustrophobia, or panic in total darkness, rehearse in a dark room with IR goggles. Out on the open cirque floor at noon, your visor will show you almost nothing ahead of you except your own breath freezing in your mask.

### 2. What to Wear: The Wet-Cold Paradox

This is the central survival problem of Halu. It is fifty below, and you will get soaked.

The condenser forest — rows of ceramic fins and black vanes taller than houses, still being grown by printer-gantries at the edge — pulls water out of air that shouldn't hold it. Each fin sweats constantly, ticks as rime forms and sheds. Under the roost towers, millions of veilwings clustered yards thick trap heat and raise districts 10-20 degrees. Their breath condenses and rains back down in warm, ammoniac streams. You can have frost forming on your shoulders while lukewarm, stinking water soaks through to your back.

A normal arctic suit will fail. You need:

**A sealed, ammonia-resistant outer suit with a truly waterproof membrane, not just insulated parka layers.** Guano runoff is chemically aggressive, full of bacteria, mites, and mineral slime. It will eat zippers, degrade untreated seals, and if it gets into insulation, that insulation stays wet and then freezes into boards when you step out of a cluster's heat-bloom.

**Soft soles, absolutely.** Rule Two of Halu is softness: soft soles, soft voices, no metal on stone. No crampons, no metal toe caps clanking, no hard trekking boots. The rock is newborn black glass and knife-edged slag, unworn, unweathered. Metal on it rings — and this valley literally listens for a living. It also chips the rock and your footing. Wear thick, soft polymer soles with non-metal grip. Accept that you will slip. Slipping slowly is better than startling a roost.

**Layered hearing protection that is not total silence.** You need attenuation for the high pulses sleeting down above speech range, and for sustained chorus peaks that will give you a splitting headache and permanent damage. But you also need to feel when a shepherd-thing is behind you, or when a new note enters the roar. Caretakers use filtered plugs plus bone-conduction pads. Bring spares — everything gets wet.

**No exposed skin, ever, outside a dome.** Not to adjust a strap, not to wipe a visor. Inside a veilwing cluster it may feel merely freezing-damp. Two steps out of the lee and it's killing cold again. The transition is invisible in the dark.

**A visor that does not fog and does not auto-brighten.** Disable every auto-flash, focus-assist light, recording light, and bright UI on every device you bring. Tape over LEDs. Set screens to deep red and minimum. Bring manual, red-filtered light only, and only use it when your escort says so.

Bring more dry inner gloves, socks, and face liners than you think humanly possible. In Halu you don't dry things outside. You rotate them into the domes and accept being damp.

### 3. Moving Through a Town Not Built For Walking

Forget streets as you know them.

Between condensers, it's street after street of towers of fused basalt and self-healing nanoceramic, pocked with ten thousand nesting cells each. Bridges arc between towers not for walking, but for hanging upside-down. Eaves, conduits, abandoned printer bays — all coated in veilwings. Each one is small, cup-in-two-hands small, fur like wet ash, wings folded like a damp cloak. Together they are a breathing surface.

**Rule Three is slowness. You do not run here. Nothing runs here.**

A veilwing takes four minutes to unfold a wing. Its heart in deep roost beats once every nine seconds. A flock does not startle like birds; it wakes like a tide, an edge shivering, then a ripple taking an hour to cross a tower. That makes them sound safe to disturb. They are not. Waking a section early dumps heat, breaks the insulation for everything behind it, ruins hours of sonar mapping, and can overheat dense clusters if they panic-move inward.

Walk at printer-gantry speed. Pause often. Let movement pass around you.

The ground makes this harder. There is no soil, no drainage channels, no worn paths. Erosion hasn't started. Water simply stays, weeps, pools, and when a cistern bursts it advances in a perfect black sheet at a crawl, finding no drain. Your soft soles will be on glassy slag under a film of supercooled water and black ice. Knife edges can slice a suit knee if you kneel carelessly. Never put a hand down without looking in IR first — you might be putting it on a cluster of pale beetles grazing bacterial slime, in a condensate film full of chemosynthetic colonies, or inches from a veilwing pup that got nudged out.

Never lean on a tower. You are not leaning on a wall, you are leaning on ten tons of sleeping animals resettling with a sound like slow thunder.

### 4. The Other Residents — You Will Mostly Not See Them

Where there are veilwings, there is everything else, all of it adapted to dark, wet, and sound.

**Shepherd-things:** Gaunt, flightless, tall as a person, blind, on long soft feet. They patrol roost levels eating the dead, grooming clusters that get too dense and overheat, nudging pups back into warmth with their noses. They are caretakers too. If one brushes your suit in a passageway, freeze. Let it catalogue you by smell and touch and move on. Do not push it, shine it, or step into its path when it is damming a flood with its body.

**Cistern herons:** Things like herons made of night stand motionless for hours in freezing black water in the open cisterns and strike once for the blind, translucent, thumb-length shrimp drifting there. Do not reach into a cistern. That water is kept liquid well below freezing. It can supercool-freeze on contact with your glove, and you will be invading a feeding station.

**Small life:** Mites move through veilwing fur like herds. Beetles graze the mineral slime on tower bases feeding on iron and sulfur. All of it will try to explore your warm, damp suit seams. Expect a full decontamination shake-down and heat/chemical wash of outer gear before you enter a dome and before you leave Halu. Do not bring this ecosystem down-valley in your cuffs.

**Veilwings themselves:** Do not touch, feed, imitate calls at, or record with active sonar. Even sleeping they echolocate constantly. Your active ping is shouting into a library where a million whispers are making a map of water pockets, stress fractures, and mineral veins forming underground in real time. Passive listening only.

### 5. Life in the Human Domes

The domes are dim, red, cramped, and smell permanently of guano processing. That sludge — the washed runoff from towers — is potent, exportable, and pays for everything. The processors churn day and night, so there is no true quiet inside either, just a lower industrial hum beneath the chorus leaking through the walls.

Sleep with ear protection and an eye mask you trust. Eat what they eat: nothing green grows here, no moss, no grass, even in shelter. All food is imported or vat-grown under red light. Water tastes of minerals and condenser treatment — safe, but strange, like licking cold stone.

Respect dome discipline instantly and without debate. Doors cycle slowly to avoid pressure thumps and light spills. Voices are kept soft even inside. If a chorus peak rolls in, people just stop mid-sentence, wait, tap each other. Join that rhythm or you'll exhaust everyone.

Bathrooms, airlocks, drying racks — all shared, all limited. Your wet gear will stink of ammonia until you leave the cirque. Get used to it, and do not use scented anything. Smell is information down there.

### 6. Going Out Beyond the Condensers — Only If Invited

Caretakers sometimes take visitors past the last fin row onto the open cirque floor. It is an eight-kilometer bowl of unbroken, newborn basalt to a wall you cannot see but can hear because the chorus echoes off it, faintly delayed.

If you go: stay tethered to your escort by line or by constant tap contact, keep your beacon on, check oxygen and suit heat every few minutes by habit, and turn back before you feel "fine." There are no landmarks, no wind sounds like wind (there is nothing for it to move through), only drip, shriek, and tower hum behind you like a low wet fire. The rock under ice still faintly rings when struck, which is beautiful and also means a fall can crack like a bell through the listening system.

Disorientation happens in minutes. People wander in what they think is a straight line and describe perfect circles.

### 7. Emergencies Specific to Halu

**Falling ice/rime:** Condensers shed skins of ice constantly. Don't stand directly under a fin edge listening to it tick.

**Flash flood, slow version:** A black sheet advancing across a street looks harmless. It is near-freezing, contaminated, and will fill your boots and undermine footing on glass rock. Move perpendicular, slowly, to high slag.

**Suit soak-through at -40:** If warm guano water gets inside and then you exit a cluster, it freezes. Signal "need warmth" immediately. This is a medical emergency, not discomfort.

**Chorus disorientation / sound sickness:** Nausea, ear pain, feeling of leaning against the hum, inability to tell up in darkness. Sit — carefully — back against something not alive if possible, tap your escort, breathe through your regulator until it passes.

**Startling a roost:** If you do — freeze, go low, go silent, kill any light. Do not wave animals off you. Let shepherds and caretakers re-settle them.

### A Final Checklist to Tape Inside Your Dome Locker

- Escort confirmed, tap-code learned, all lights taped and set to red
- Oxygen tested, filtered ear protection in, soft soles on
- Sealed ammonia-resistant suit, triple dry liners, no metal exposed
- IR visor cleaned, auto-brightness OFF
- No flash, no active sonar, no perfume, no running, no shouting
- Move like the printers: centimeters at a time, pause, listen
- Assume every surface is either razor basalt, freezing water, or sleeping animals

Halu is a valley that hasn't finished being born. In a thousand years the water running off those towers will have cut the first real streams. Right now it just weeps, listens to itself through a million small throats, and tolerates a few humans in red-lit bubbles who have learned to be soft, dark, and slow enough not to break it.

Be that soft, dark, and slow, and you'll hear what it's hearing — just a little of it, through aching ears, standing wet in the killing cold, while ten tons of veilwings sigh and shift above you in a night that isn't ending anytime soon.
