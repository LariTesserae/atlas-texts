This is not a cave, a pond, or a dive site in any useful sense, even though it borrows pieces from all three. Treat it like this:

A new, flooded room under a marsh, larger inside than the crack that leads to it, completely full of water, plant matter, silt, roots, and stored things. It breathes — barely. Its gravity changes. Its time pools and eddies. It keeps what enters it for much longer than it should: seeds, leaves, shells, breath, words.

An ordinary human can survive a short visit. An unprepared one is very likely to panic on entry, get tangled, lose the exit, disturb things that should not be disturbed, and become one of the things kept.

### Who should not go

Do not attempt this if you:

- Are broad-shouldered, stiff, pregnant, injured, or cannot turn sideways and pass, head-first and face-down, through a slit a hand's breadth wide without scraping. The limestone at the break is fresh, white, and sharp. There is no wider back door.
- Have asthma, heart or inner-ear problems, sinus trouble, seizures, or a strong startle/panic response in cold water. The first minute inverts every normal reflex.
- Are claustrophobic, nyctophobic, or get vertigo when up and down swap. Inside, they will swap, slowly and completely, while you are touching something on every side.
- Need to be able to talk, shout for help, surface quickly, or be pulled out on a rope. None of those work reliably in there.
- Cannot accept leaving without a sample, photograph, or souvenir.

Go only with a topside companion. Never alone. Your companion stays on genuinely solid marsh ground — not on the duckweed mat, which looks like a green floor and is not. They are your clock, your anchor for returning to the ordinary world, and the person who notices if the entrance overgrows while you're below.

### Train your body first

You are not preparing for swimming. You are preparing for controlled drowning, cold, compression, and disorientation.

Practice, well before you go:

**1. The entry breath.** You put your face into cold water, turn sideways, and inhale water. It burns. It triggers panic. Then, if this place accepts you as it has others, it gives back a thin, mineral breath — cold as well-water, enough to think by, not enough to exert yourself or speak easily.

Train in a safe, supervised setting to slow your heart under cold shock, to exhale slowly instead of thrashing, and to tolerate several very uncomfortable breaths without bolting. If you cannot calm yourself in under a minute in practice, do not trust that you will in the dark.

**2. Moving without standing or swimming.** The chamber is perhaps three paces across but you cannot pace, stand, or do a normal stroke. Everywhere is stems, sponge, roots, fern, shells. Practice moving in a cramped, dark, filled space by small pushes, pulls, and turns, using almost no strength. Exertion will outrun the thin breath instantly.

**3. Orientation loss.** The silt starts on the floor, ends up in a crescent on the ceiling. Footprints go up the wall. A pebble will detach and fall patiently sideways to stick to the left wall, and then that wall is down. Practice being slowly re-oriented — hanging, turning, eyes closed — without grabbing wildly for a fixed up.

**4. Silence.** Learn a small set of touch signals with your companion and, if you go with a second diver, with each other: *stop, hold still, gravity shifting, exit now, I am tangled, I am calm.* Speech inside makes slow silver beads that do not rise. They sink and lodge overhead, kept by the place. Assume anything you say there stays there, literally. Go in planning to say nothing.

### What to wear and bring

The rule the chamber itself seems to follow: **no metal, no plastic, nothing that could not be made by fingers and teeth and time.** The little cairns, twig ladders, shell gates, woven rush dams, and scratched tally marks inside are all made that way. Don't introduce what it cannot digest. It may simply keep it forever.

Bring / wear:

- **Tight, natural-fiber clothing**, wool or linen, close enough that it cannot snag. No loose cords, toggles, carabiners, belts, jewelry, watches, or trailing gear. Everything loose will tangle. Your hair will tangle almost at once — braid it tight, tie it with natural cord, or cut it short. A smooth wool cap helps.
- **Thin protection for skin on the entry.** The fresh limestone catches. You need your face and side to pass the slit. A close leather or heavy wool layer on the leading shoulder/hip reduces cuts, but it must not bulk you up. Any cut you get in there will be packed instantly with silt, plant matter, and old leaves. Clean it aggressively afterwards.
- **No scuba tank, no snorkel, no mask with a strap tangle problem.** They won't fit the slit usefully, the exhaust will become those sinking silver beads, and you'll fight the equipment instead of the room. If you absolutely cannot trust the place's breath, abort — don't engineer around it.
- **No bright light.** The duckweed closes behind you like a lid. The dark has a grain, a resistance. Bright light has no proven benefit here and may be a violation: moss over the stone flashes vivid green only when your hand passes near and disturbs the current, as if it remembers sun at a touch and then forgets. If you need a marker, bring a small piece of pale, undyed bone or shell you can feel by shape — and bring it back out with you.
- **No food, no foreign seeds, no soil from another world on your boots.** The hollows are full of unsprouted seeds, stored perfectly. Do not add yours. Shake out pockets. This is an invasive-species problem and also a courtesy problem.
- **A topside anchor outside the chamber.** Set your return point, portal anchor, or rendezvous on solid ground well back from the duckweed. Do not try to open or maintain a portal inside; the room is larger than its entrance, its centre interferes with direction, and roots all grow toward a point and stop just short of it. Anchoring to that would be a mistake.

Leave behind: cameras, lights, knives (they tempt you to cut tangles — cutting roots and rush-work is exactly what you must not do), containers for specimens, writing materials. The slate inside already has tallies scratched in clusters of seven, more than anyone's stay should account for. Don't add yours.

Set a hard time limit with your topside companion before you go in, and hold yourself to a shorter one inside. Time moves in eddies. Your sunken breath-words will still be descending past the ferns when you have already been there long enough for moss to map your shoulder where it pressed. External clock, internal brevity. A good first rule: **out before the second gravity shift.** The first shift teaches you the place changes its mind. The second is how you get lost.

### Entry

1. **Find the lowest part of the low ground**, where the marsh never quite becomes a pond. The duckweed seals it so completely you could walk over it without knowing. Test every step. Cool through the shoes, slight give, is not ground.
2. **Mark the two stones and the slit between them** from above, visually and by landmarks on real ground. Do not widen it. Do not chip the white break. The place is geologically new, still hurting. Widening is injury, and it will change the flow that made the room.
3. **Enter face-down, sideways, slow.** Expect cold that takes speech. Expect the duckweed to close the light behind you like a lid being set down. Expect the first breath to be wrong.
4. **Stop moving the moment you're in.** Let your eyes fail. Let your heart be loud and then quieter. Let the thin breath establish itself. If it does not — if after the initial burn you are only drowning — back out immediately, while the slit is still where your body remembers it. There is no shame protocol here. Backing out late is not an option.

### Inside: how to behave

**Don't try to stand.** There may be no standing surface, and after a while you won't be sure which surface that would be. Keep three points of gentle contact. Knee to sponge, elbow to snail shells, back to stems — everything is close. Move a hand's length, then reassess.

**Don't fight tangles.** Hair, rootlets, algae veils, fungal thread: the chamber has an interior scaffolding of pale roots braiding down, and gardens hanging between them. If you are caught, go still. Work backwards along the path you came, by touch. Tearing free tears the room, and panicked effort eats your breath.

**Watch the gravity cues, not your inner ear.** Ferns lean sideways in unison. Motes cross the room between one thought and the next. Silt begins to drift. A loose thing falls slowly toward a wall. When that happens, stop, hold a root or stone lightly, and let the new down announce itself. The little structures on the walls — thumb-high cairns, a gate of two snail shells and a bone lintel, a twig ladder leaning on nothing with three pale leaves opened underwater at its top — will suddenly make sense under the new orientation. They are not decorations. They are proof someone built for more than one down. Don't lean on them, add to them, or go through the little gate. It's not sized for you and not meant for you.

**Respect the centre by not finding it.** About a body's length from every wall, roots stop just short, tips curled. Plants lean there, silt gathers there when gravity forgets, and the blind fish's circuit tightens around it every seventh pass. The water there is colder and thicker, and putting a hand in gives the acute, embarrassing feeling of interrupting sleep. Something fist-sized is there, or the shape where something fist-sized was — seed, heart, knot in the plumbing. Do not touch, probe, hold your hand in, or anchor anything there. Cross around it, if you must cross.

**Leave the fauna entirely alone.** Fauna is rare and therefore enormous. A blind fish no longer than your little finger, the color of the water, may pass through a fern without bending it and through your open hand — not around: through, with a brief cold pressure, like being remembered. Do not close your hand. Do not chase it. A white isopod may walk across a temporary ceiling carrying a green seed larger than itself. Let it. The many empty snail shells may be residents, or wash-ins from ponds that have not existed for a long while. Either way, not yours.

**Listen, but don't answer.** It is quiet in a way that makes your heartbeat impertinent. A bubble ticks out of deep silt. A root creaks as it thickens, somewhere impossible to locate. If rain starts on the marsh above, no rain gets in, but hundreds of tiny impacts arrive all over the inside of the chamber at once and the dark gets denser, the cold cooler. That's information about the outside world. Use it as a cue to check your elapsed sense of time, then start leaving.

### Leaving

Do not look for the slit where logic says it should be. It will appear to be above you, behind the shell gate, under the tally slate, as gravity and growth shift.

Look instead — by touch, in the dark — for **the way that is most densely grown.** The entrance faithfully overgrows itself to keep the dark in. The thickest, most recently packed tangle of stems, root-hairs, and suspended leaves is your door.

Go out the way you went in: sideways, face in the cold, patient with the sharp stone. Expect the water to let go of your lungs with reluctance on the other side. Your companion should be ready to steady you on the marsh edge, keep you warm, and not demand a report while you're still coughing up well-water cold.

Do not re-enter on the same day to retrieve something non-essential or "just check." You've left words, breath, skin cells, moss-contact, and disturbance behind already. One visit per crossing is enough.

### Aftercare

- **Warm slowly.** You will be colder than you think; the thick cold at the centre gets into you. Dry natural-fiber layers waiting topside.
- **Clean every scrape** from the limestone as if it were a dirty marsh wound — because it is, plus fungus, sponge, and things that soften leaves to leather without rotting them. Watch cuts for days.
- **Expect after-effects:** ears that can't settle on down for an hour, vivid dreams of roots stopping short of a point, finding silt in places silt shouldn't be, or a faint green pressure-map on skin where moss touched you. If vertigo, chest tightness, or cough persists, treat it as a real drowning-adjacent injury, not an adventure story, and get medical care in whatever world has it.
- **Do not describe the exact entry location casually.** The marsh hides it naturally, with a mat anyone could walk over unknowing. Keep that protection.
- **Account for what you carried out, including unwillingly.** Shake out clothing away from waterways in your home world. Unsprouted seeds are stored in every hollow there; you do not want one stored in a cuff, sprouting years later in a world with no chamber to hold it.

### Emergency truths

If you get lost: go still, conserve breath, wait for a gravity settle, and feel for densest growth.

If you panic: you will thrash against everything at once, spend your thin air, and inhale in earnest. That is the death this place offers, followed by the worse courtesy of preservation. This is why the training and the time limit matter.

If your companion must choose between pulling you through the slit and injuring you, they pull. A torn side from fresh limestone heals in the ordinary world. Remaining in there while the duckweed seals and the room rearranges its floors probably also heals, in a way — just not as you.

Go in curious, silent, empty-handed, and brief. Come out the same way, plus cold. The room will stay behind you under the green mat, full to the stone on every side, not draining, not collapsing to the size of its crack — keeping your sunken words descending toward a floor that will be a wall by the time they land.
