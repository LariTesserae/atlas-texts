This is not a ruin you explore, and it is not quite a greenhouse, a factory, or a mountaintop either. It is all three operating at once, in one room, on a schedule older than anyone now living there. Prepare as if you are visiting a working industrial animal that happens to have an ecosystem living in its skin.

An ordinary human can visit safely — but only at the margins, only on its timetable, and only if you prepare for two completely opposite environments separated by a single door.

### 1. Choose your arrival point very carefully

Do not arrive inside the building if you can avoid it.

The middle of the room is a swept, lethal void. A horizontal piston, a rocking beam, and a flywheel turning once every seven seconds occupy an invisible ellipsoid in the center. Its counterweight passes close enough to the floor to polish the iron. Anything — person, pack, vine — that persists in that space will be removed by the machine. It does not stop for visitors, and at full blast you will not be able to hear anyone shout a warning.

Aim instead for the exterior plinth:

* You will materialize not on rock or soil, but on iron catwalk, concrete, and riveted plate. Expect an immediate sense of vertigo. Below the plinth the mountain has been capped and smoothed away; there is no slope to orient you, just a drop into thin air.
* Arrive in daylight, mid-morning to early afternoon if you can control it. That gives you light for the ladders, the least interior “rain,” and time to meet the keeper before a blast cycle confuses everything.
* Arrive already holding a railing, figuratively speaking. Wind on the plinth is constant and has nowhere to go. A loose pack strap, hat, map, or scarf will be gone instantly.
* If your method of travel is imprecise, err toward outside and high cold rather than inside and warm. Outside you will be miserable in minutes. Inside the sweep zone you could be dead in seven seconds.

The last 300 meters, if you come up by foot or cable, are catwalk, switchback stair, and ladder bolted into the concrete plug. No handholds in nature, no place to step off and rest on grass. Travel light enough to climb a ladder with your pack, and clip yourself in where a cable is provided.

### 2. Prepare for altitude first, jungle second

Outside: cold-bright, starved of oxygen, UV-intense, wind-scoured. This is high alpine exposure on a metal platform with no shelter except the building itself.

Inside: warm as a laundry, wet, green-filtered light, air that never stops moving, and deafening.

You need a layering system you can strip and reassemble in under a minute at the door:

**For outside**
- Insulated windproof outer layer, gloves, hat, sunglasses or glacier goggles. Sunburn happens fast in thin air, and the light off iron plate and glass is brutal.
- Sturdy boots with real grip. Catwalks frost, ladders ice, and the plinth runs with condensed water blown out during blasts.
- Acclimatization. Do not plan to run up the stairs with a pack on your first hour. Altitude sickness here is dangerous because the only “down” is technical and exposed. If you can stage your arrival or ascent over a day or two, do it. Bring whatever altitude medication you personally have cleared with a doctor, water, and carbohydrates. A small supplemental oxygen canister is not overkill for the climb and the first night.
- Sunscreen, lip balm, and far more water than you think the cold warrants.

**For inside**
- Light, breathable clothes underneath that you won’t mind getting damp, oil-flecked, and smelling faintly of hot copper and leaf mold for days.
- Waterproof-but-ventilated overshirt or apron if you’ll be near the walls. Moisture condenses on glass and trusses all night and drips at dawn along established channels. You will be dripped on. It will be warm drips.
- Non-slip soles are even more important inside. Floor plates are oil-dark, wet in bolt heads, green with algae in troughs. The safe paths are at the edges and they are narrow, crowded with pipes, roots, and troughs.
- Nothing trailing. No long scarves, loose cords, wide sleeves near the gallery belts or flywheel. The machine trims vines to its exact reach. It will do the same to fabric and hair. Tie hair back before you open the door.

Bring clothes in a dry bag. Humidity inside will find paper, leather, electronics, and untreated cloth and soften them. The keeper’s logbook has pages like cloth for a reason. Seal cameras and instruments; expect lenses to fog instantly on entry and again on exit.

### 3. Hearing protection is not optional

There is a lidless tin of wax plugs nailed by the coat hooks for a reason. Do not rely only on that tin.

Bring:
- Your own fitted earplugs, plus spare foam/wax pairs
- Over-ear defenders to wear over them during a main blast
- A small notepad and pencil (pens fail in humidity; pencils are what the keeper uses on the walls)

The sound is continuous: diesel throb in the floor, compressor sighing, belts slapping overhead, and a tearing resonance from the horn even at idle. When pressure builds for 40–50 minutes and the blast comes, conversation and even internal thought briefly stop. Needles blur. Water shakes out of the gutters as mist.

That means:
- Agree on hand signals *before* you go in. The walls have them painted between gauges — learn those specific ones: Stop, Back to Wall, Blast Coming, Look Up (drips/falling debris), Get Out.
- Set a hard limit on your time inside. Even with double protection, the vibration is through sternum, teeth, and floor plates. Headache, tinnitus, nausea, and disorientation are cumulative. People with migraine, inner-ear conditions, or sound sensitivity should plan very short visits, or view from the doorway in a lull.
- Never remove protection to “hear the frogs better” near the sump at night. Tree frogs amplified through the horn and compressor can hit you like weather. The resident moths are functionally deaf. You are not.
- Sleep, if you sleep there at all, with protection in. There is no quiet night cycle.

### 4. Learn the perimeter law and obey it absolutely

All life in there crowds to the margins because the center belongs to the machine. You do too.

- Stay with your back near a wall, tank, or known safe margin unless the keeper personally walks you across a safe corner.
- Never cross the bright polished arc on the floor where the counterweight passes. That arc *is* the boundary, maintained by physics.
- Never assume idle means safe. The flywheel turns every seven seconds. The beam rocks. Wind alone makes the horn resonate. “At rest” does not exist.
- Watch the living arch of vines. Plants have been trimmed by the passing beam into a perfect green negative of the machine’s reach. If leaves are vibrating hard, you are too close.
- Do not duck under pipes or climb on tanks to get a better view without asking. Those tops are soil beds — ash, leaf fall, bird lime made into earth over decades. You would be standing on a garden and a root system.
- Mind overhead: belts in a gallery, dripping channels, swift flight paths, choughs walking trusses. Hard hat or at least a stout cap is wise near the boiler gallery; eye protection during a blast keeps out mist, spore, and rust grit.

Heat hazards are quiet: boiler lagging where lizards bask is hot enough to burn through thin trousers if you lean. Copperwork conducts. Valve housings grow maidenhair fern precisely because they are warm and wet — beautiful and scalding by turns.

### 5. Air, water, and small-body biology

The interior air is breathable, but it is not clean mountain air. Oil mist, algae-rich humidity, verdigris and rust dust, bat roosting felt above tanks, swift guano behind baffles, pollen and fern spore — all suspended in moving air.

If you have asthma, mold allergy, or immune compromise, bring a well-fitting respirator/mask suitable for humid use and test whether you can tolerate the heat in it. Many visitors do better with short exposures and outside breaks.

Bring your own drinking water or a reliable purifier, and do not assume the keeper’s condensation tap is ideal for a newcomer. It is what lives there, it is mineral- and metal-touched, and the kettle has been fed from it for rotations. Likewise, there is no soil-grown food, no shop, no waste system for visitors. The fig fruits — ripening almost all at once in the week after the longest summer blasts, splitting with a tick in lulls — belong to the bees, the keeper, and the seeding cycle first. Eat nothing from wall pockets, troughs, or mortar wallflowers unless the keeper offers it and can name it. Some of what grows on copper-rich substrate concentrates what the pipe carries.

Wildlife rules are simple: observe, don’t rearrange.
- Swifts enter through the horn mouth and nest behind the baffle plates where vibration is fiercest. Do not approach the baffle colony, shine lights in, or stand in the horn throat. During spring arrival, for three days, dawn pours birds out of that mouth. Be outside or flat to a wall.
- Bats above the tanks, choughs on trusses, lizards on lagging, bees working fig and wallflowers — all are resident and unafraid. Give bees their flight line to the fig; a room with never-still air makes their navigation miraculous and easily disrupted by a waving hand.
- Lizards and frogs were carried up in crates generations ago and cannot leave. Do not repeat history. Do not release anything, carry anything in soil, or bring live food that could escape. A new species here never gets a second ecosystem to retreat to.
- Clean your boots and gear before leaving — and especially before traveling to another world. During a seeding event you will be dusted with fern spore, fig pip, and winged wallflower seed without noticing. That is biosecurity, not just courtesy. This peak reproduces by blowing its margins down-valley; don’t make it reproduce into an unrelated reality.

### 6. Working with the keeper

There is one keeper, in rotation, relieved by cable car once a season. That tells you the logistics: rescue is slow, company is scarce, and self-sufficiency matters.

- Announce yourself outside, in a lull if possible, and wait to be beckoned to the keeper’s one safe corner — a lean-to of canvas and boiler plate the beam cannot see. That corner, with cot and kettle, is the only truly still human space. Do not set up your own camp elsewhere inside without explicit direction.
- The keeper reads some gauges by touch when vibration blurs needles, logs pressure in a soft-paged book, greases bearings, and clears drip channels *partially* — a note on the wall records that clearing too much in ’61 dried the south tank and killed its ferns back. Do not “help” by pruning, clearing a channel, moving a trough, or oiling anything. In this room, maintenance and gardening are the same act, and it is calibrated in decades.
- Ask before touching any valve, governor, or the cable head wheel outside — which turns once a day whether a car comes or not, so don’t mistake movement for an imminent arrival.
- Gifts that matter are practical and low-impact: dry socks, pencil leads, salt, lamp oil or whatever the current keeper has listed by the door, news, music played *outside* in a lull. Bulk supplies may never get down again; don’t leave trash for a once-a-season car.
- Expect communication to be slow, tactile, and scheduled around pressure, not your itinerary. Everything changes across hours and seasons, not minutes.

### 7. Timing changes everything

- **Every 40–50 minutes:** a main blast. Plan crossings, conversations, measurements, and doorway entries for the gathering lull that follows one.
- **About one blast in twenty**, when wind and governor align and the brass balls lift to the red line: the horn clears fully. This is the seeding. Air-feeling empties, your sternum buzzes in silence afterward, and a green-gold breath of seed hangs over the drop before mountain wind takes it. Witness it from the side wall, mask and goggles on, never from in front of the horn, and never from the catwalk directly leeward. It is the culmination, and it will coat you.
- **Winter dawn:** heaviest interior rain, whole room dripping and steaming, frogs briefly louder than the idle pipe. Most slippery, most atmospheric, worst for electronics.
- **Spring, three days:** swift colony arrival. Noise, guano, frantic flight through the horn. Fascinating and overwhelming.
- **High summer:** longest blasts, fig ripening week. Heat inside peaks. Bees busiest.
- If you are prone to being stranded, note the cable relief is seasonal. Agree in advance on your exit — cable, descent route, or your own multiversal departure from the stable exterior plinth in clear weather. Do not plan to depart from inside during a blast; vibration, air movement, and pure sensory obliteration make concentration and instrument readings unreliable.

### A compact pack list

Altitude shell + insulation, glacier glasses, sunscreen; light breathable inner clothes; waterproof dry bags; headlamp with red mode (for bats/swifts, check with keeper); double hearing protection; mask/respirator and goggles; non-slip boots you can ladder-climb in; gloves for cold iron outside and heat-resistant caution inside; pencil and waterproof notebook; 2–3 liters water capacity plus purification; high-energy food for your whole stay plus emergency day; personal altitude/first-aid kit, blister care, rehydration salts; small tether/harness if you are uneasy on exposed catwalk; sealable bag for spore-dusted outer layer on exit; no live plants, soil, or untreated wood souvenirs.

### The souvenir ethics

Take photographs if condensation allows, drawings, sound recordings made from the wall. Do not take fern, fig pip, lichen-on-rust, eggshell, or a vial of made-soil. What looks like a cutting is a piece of a closed, century-built skin that keeps the iron from simply rusting away — verdigris ferns and iron-red ferns and moss-mapped drip lines are infrastructure.

Leave knowing why they built it: a foghorn to warn airships and blind mine trains away from a peak. What you are visiting now is its second life — the peak using a warning machine, a green breath at a time, to send its improbable warm margins down into the crags below.

Prepare to be small, marginal, quiet with your hands, and attentive to cycles longer than your visit. Stand where the moss says water goes, where the vines say the beam does not quite reach, and listen — protected — until the room stops being noise and becomes a place.
