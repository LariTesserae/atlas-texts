You are not going to arrive at this city. You are going to realize, hours too late, that you have been inside it for a while.

There is no gate, no wall, no outskirts to prepare yourself against. The moor gets a little more managed - a wall to keep a pool from spilling, a paved ford, a house that turns out to be three houses, a terrace following a contour you didn't see. By the time you understand you're in streets, you are already wet, tired, and committed. Plan for that.

This is a mundane place for an ordinary human body - no thin air to kill you, nothing predatory - but it will wear you down through cold, damp, scale, and disorientation if you treat it like a normal city visit.

### Before You Go

**Pack for permanent damp, not for rain.**

It is cool on a high plateau even in summer, and wet from below, above, and sideways. The ground sighs water back into your footprints. Streets have open channels down the middle. Walls weep, condense overnight, and streak black by morning. Canals mist in the late morning and make their own cloud over the center, which then rains, briefly and reliably, on the northern quarters in the afternoon.

Cotton will kill your comfort. Down will collapse and never truly dry. Bring:

* Layered wool, top to bottom, including underlayers and at least three times the socks you think you need. Locals wear wool year-round and dry everything by a hearth every evening - boots, coats, bedding aired over stone warmers. You will do the same or sleep clammy.
* A heavy outer layer that sheds water but isn't a sealed plastic sauna - oiled wool, waxed canvas, leather apron if you plan to work or ride the punts. You will brush walls, turn sideways in alleys in other people's wet coats, lean on algae-slick stone.
* Boots you can walk in for two full days on stone, boardwalk, stair, and bog without stopping. Waterproof is less important than *drainable and re-dryable*. You *will* step in a channel. The paving is limestone worn into shallow bowls at every turn, algae in the slow places, ice at the edges in a winter snap. Smooth leather soles are a fall waiting to happen. The locals' wooden soles click on pavement and go quiet on wood - they are also raised above the seep. If you don't wear those, wear deeply treaded, stiff boots and walk like it's always just rained, because it has.
* Everything in roll-top dry liners inside a backpack. No wheeled luggage. A lane here becomes a stair, goes under a second story, emerges as a boardwalk over a canal, narrows until you turn sideways. Carts, where they exist, have wooden wheels wrapped in rope for quiet. Most freight moves by flat-bottomed punt. You will carry what you bring, up terraces where the roof below you is someone else's yard.
* Sun protection for noon and a warm hat for 12:10. Sunlight at altitude is clean and hard - white limestone walls will hurt your eyes, shadows have cut edges - and within ten minutes of cloud it is October-cold and your breath shows. Sunglasses, then gloves, same hour.

Leave behind: dogs, loud equipment, anything glass and fragile, anything that needs a dry cellar. There are almost no dogs here, cats keep to themselves, pony hooves are muffled in sacking inside the bounds. A barking dog, a generator, a speaker, a shouted conversation will not just be rude - it will be *obscene* in a place built to eat sound, and everyone will turn and look.

### The Approach

Give yourself a full day on the moor before you think you're "there."

High grass like old straw, thin soil over stone, pools in every hollow reflecting a perfectly normal sky, no trees. Your boots won't sink exactly, but the ground gives constantly. You will get wet feet on a sunny day. Follow drier ridges marked by wind-bent shrubs when you can, and paved fords where runnels cross - those are the first sign of the city, long before houses thicken.

Do not try to time your arrival to a gate closing or a market opening. Do try to arrive at a terrace before dark. There are no street lamps. Houses have shutters and oiled cloth, not much glass. At night the city completes its daytime quiet by going genuinely dark. If you are still wayfinding after sunset, you will be feeling your way along wet channels you can hear under the plinths beneath every building.

### Getting Oriented - There Is No Center

Forget maps with a middle. This city has heights and *watersheds*.

Where the channels in the street start running the other way, you have crossed into, socially, another town - people will name themselves differently, pronounce district names differently, though the stone is unbroken. A district can be in hard sun while another is in rain. Locals don't ask if it's raining. They ask *where*.

To cross from the south marsh-gates to the north quarries is two days on foot through inhabited land the whole way, three if the middle canals are high. You could live on one terrace for a year and know its water like a face, and live ten years and never see the far side.

Practical rules:

1. **Hire by watershed, not for the whole city.** A guide, a punt-boy, a porter who knows *his* gradients is gold. That knowledge - which sluice lifts when, which alley floods an inch at noon, which cistern runs peat-brown after a moor storm - is inherited and local. Hand off to a new guide when water starts flowing the other direction.
2. **Navigate by water and carving, not street names.** Public cisterns are landmarks, marked with carved water-lines and softened carvings of hands, fish, rain clouds, founders. Note the cistern, the direction of flow, the height of the sun. Up is generally toward moor and quarry, down is toward reed-ponds and marsh in the south where waste water spreads, clears, and leaves.
3. **Move with the sluice day.** At dawn you will hear a hundred wooden sluice boards clatter up. Night water is released to turn mills, flush dyeyards, carry refuse down. Mid-morning the interior cloud gathers. Noon puts a shimmer off wet slate in the lower town and is the best drying and long-view hour. Afternoon rain raises middle canals about an inch - enough for market boats to pole one street farther uphill, enough to make a morning ford a wade. Evening reverses it all: boards down, cisterns filling from roofs.
   Plan crossings of the middle for morning. Plan to be *somewhere* with a roof and a fire by evening board-down.

Expect punts to be silent, slow, and to have right-of-way in practice. Expect to get on and off at boardwalks that are slick. Expect to help. When a canal silts or a wall fails, the neighborhood turns out with shovels and sorts the stone on the spot, mossy face in, and rebuilds within the season. A visitor who stands filming will be remembered. A visitor who holds a sluice rope, rakes algae, or carries a bucket will be fed.

### Water Etiquette Is Law

Every roof feeds a butt or a public cistern. Every building sits on a stone plinth a knee high because below that, water moves constantly in culverts. There are *no cellars* - nothing stored low survives.

* Never pollute upstream. Don't wash, spit, dump grey water, or step with muddy boots into a clean channel if a lower runnel is provided. Upstream/downstream is not abstract here; your street's drinking water was someone else's roof an hour ago and will be someone else's mill an hour later.
* Ask before drawing from a household butt. Public cisterns with water-lines are for all, but levels are watched. In a dry spell (relative - it is never dry) that line matters.
* Don't drink canal water, however clear. Drink cistern water as locals do, but note changes: clear, then peat-stained after moor storms, then edged with ice in winter. If you have a delicate stomach, boil it where you lodge - every house has a hearth lit most evenings anyway, for drying if not for heat. Eat as they eat: fish from raised ponds at the edge, sheep from high grazing, kale, onions, herbs from walled courtyard gardens, and a *lot* of pickled and stored food. Fresh variety is seasonal and local to a terrace. Bring any essential medicine or dietary need with you; you cannot assume you can buy it two districts over, let alone across a watershed.
* Store your own things high. Lofts, wall niches, raised platforms. Anything left on a floor overnight will be damp at best. Air your bedding daily.

### How to Behave in the Quiet

A city this dense - houses shoulder to shoulder, roofs as yards, lanes under houses - should roar. It doesn't. Water, stone, damp air swallow sound, and people have turned habit into custom.

Keep your voice low, even outdoors. A shout carries obscenely far on a still morning. Market noise is a murmur, a slap of fish, a clink of scales. Workshops are sunken into thick-walled yards to contain themselves. The loudest normal sounds are water in a stepped channel after rain, doves under eaves, swallows screaming over cisterns in the frantic summer months, and that dawn clatter of sluices.

Walk quietly. Speak in shops and markets at conversation-to-one-person volume. Silence your phone completely - not vibrate on stone. If you must make noise to work, ask where the sunken yards are.

This quiet is not emptiness. People are everywhere - washing wool to spread at noon on upper terraces, combing algae with wooden rakes weekly, cutting reed beds hard each autumn, training a single plum tree flat against a warm courtyard wall. It's a working density, not a tourist old town. Nothing looks new and nothing is allowed to become ruinous. Don't climb on roofs that are yards, don't enter courtyards uninvited just because the lane went *under* a house, and don't treat worn thresholds and lintels blackened by buckets as picturesque decay to touch.

### Health, In Small Ordinary Ways

The dangers are cumulative: cold wet feet leading to blisters and trench-like soreness, a slip on wet limestone stairs, sunburn at noon followed by chill, getting lost and benighted in identical terraces, drinking the wrong water, sleeping in damp bedding for a week and developing a cough that won't leave.

Dry your feet at every fire. Change socks at midday. Watch children and older travelers on boardwalks - blind-looking small fish drift under them in cold shadow; the water is not deep in most street channels, but main canals take punts and will take a person. Winter ice ticks softly against stone at night and forms first exactly where you step off a stair.

Animals are few: long-haired grey cats, ducks on outer canals, corvids on high chimneys, ponies that leave the same day. Frogs in the outer reed pools in spring, insects starring cisterns briefly before the swallows arrive. No special vaccinations beyond sensible tetanus and water hygiene suggested by the setting, but bring plasters, foot care, and any inhaler - damp stone houses and woodsmoke evenings will find a weak chest.

### Leaving

You leave the way you came: gradually. Roofs thin, boardwalks end in marsh, channels become runnels, reed beds thicken into pools. Check you are following *outward* flow to the south reed-ponds if you want the marsh route, or climbing against flow toward quarries and moor if you want the north.

Give yourself that extra half day you didn't think you needed. If the middle canals rose in the afternoon, if the interior cloud parked over your district, if a neighborhood dredging closed a lane - two days becomes three. That is not a delay. That *is* travel here.

Come prepared to be wet, quiet, slow, and oriented by water rather than distance, and the city will stop feeling endless in a frightening way and start feeling endless in the way its inhabitants intend: terrace after terrace, each one complete, each one knowing exactly where its rain goes.
