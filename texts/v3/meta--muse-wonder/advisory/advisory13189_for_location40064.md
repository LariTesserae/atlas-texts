The Sere Table is not dangerous in the way a desert or a glacier is dangerous. It will not try to kill you. It will try, quite literally, to listen to you — and to grow toward you while it does it. Most of preparation is learning how to be quiet, dry, and uninteresting in a place that is loud, dry, and very, very attentive.

You are going to a single organism the size of a small country, at 3-4,000 meters, in cold thin air under a permanently hazed, dim sun, where there is no soil, no open water, and no silence between 10am and evening. Plan accordingly.

### 1. Your body first: altitude, cold, dryness

The ascent shaft is not just transport, it's acclimatization. Do not rush it, and do not accept a fast lift if one is offered. The air on the Table is thin enough that newcomers breathe in short sips and get headaches, nosebleeds, and stupid, clumsy thinking for the first 24-48 hours. Walk slow. No alcohol for the first two days — you'll get drunk faster, dehydrate faster, and, worse, spill more.

Cold here is a dry, scouring cold. At noon in full wind your knuckles will ache inside good gloves. At dawn there is frost in every crack in the mat and on bark plates, but it is not water frost. It is frozen carbon dioxide. It looks like white glitter, it sublimates with a ticking hiss around 8am, and it will burn bare skin on contact like dry ice. Never kneel, sit, or put a bare palm down in the early morning to "feel the ground." Children do the ear-to-the-mat trick at *evening*, for a reason.

Your skin, lips, eyes and sinuses will crack before they get cold. The Plant runs on oils and salts, not water, and the air takes moisture from you constantly. Water-based lotions, lip balm, wet wipes, gel hand sanitizer, and most cosmetics are both useless and illegal to open outdoors. Pack like the locals:

- Oil-based barrier balm for lips, nostrils, knuckles, in a screw-sealed tin
- Sealed saline eye drops in single-use, fully enclosed applicators — use them indoors only
- A soft buff / wind mask you can breathe through. It warms air, stops bark dust and rock flour, and muffles your voice a little outdoors, which is good
- Dark, wrap-around wind goggles. The light is dim and pewter-grey, but visibility is enormous between Trunks and the wind carries sharp leaf fragments and grit. You will squint from wind, not brightness
- Layered, windproof clothing with no loose flapping edges. Flapping fabric makes a constant rhythmic noise, and rhythm is language here. Zips and sealed cuffs are better than toggles that clack. Outer layers get coated in resin dust and smell permanently of hot pepper, resin, and iron afterwards. Bring clothes you do not mind ruining

Sweat counts as water. So does spit, urine, a bleeding cut, a steaming cup of tea. You produce more moisture than you think. Wear absorbent underlayers, change them indoors, and seal used ones immediately in the dry-bags your lodging provides. Never wring anything out outside. Never spit on the ground. Every building entrance has a sealed spit cup and a sealed waste chute — use them even if it feels rude. It's the opposite of rude.

### 2. Hearing: You will need protection the natives refuse

Natives shout cheerfully at each other at midday and have the hearing loss to prove it. You, as a visitor, should not imitate them.

From about 10am until the evening drop, Trunk bark-plates — each the size of a door — lift on their hinges and clap in the wind. Canopy leaves, metallic and black, ring like faint bells. The mat underfoot crunches, knocks, and transmits sound for kilometers through root faster than through air. It is beautiful and it is architecturally loud. Standing still you will feel subsonic creak in your feet, teeth, and sternum.

Bring:

- High-quality filtered earplugs for daytime that cut wind-drumming but let you hear a shouted warning
- Full over-ear defenders for walking between Trunks or near a market square at peak wind
- A bone-conduction headset if you have one. The city comms use them because air-conduction speech is useless outdoors

Agree on hand signals with your companions before you go up. Decide what means stop, back to a building, predator-quiet (see below), and water spill. You will not be able to improvise this at noon.

Sleep only in the thick-walled cork rooms. They are not a luxury. Sleeping outdoors, or even in a thin tent, will leave you vibration-sick, partly deafened by morning, and possibly woken to find new shoots leaning over your warm, breathing, noisy body. Buildings are curved and massively resin-sealed for a reason — flat, thin walls get pushed over in a season if the Plant likes the sound inside.

Evening, for about an hour after the wind drops all at once, is the social window. The whole plateau rings down its stored vibration in a long diminuendo, then goes comparatively still. That's when people talk at normal volume, eat together, and do business. Save questions, interviews, and delicate conversations for then. Don't waste the quiet hour with earplugs still in.

### 3. The one absolute rule: NO OPEN WATER

It is printed over every airlock. Obey it as if it were "no open flame on a munitions ship."

The Plant has not had free water in longer than records, but it has never forgotten it. A spilled cup will swell the mat within minutes. Pale, soft, water-fat shoots — the only soft pale things up there — will force up through root cords, resin seals, and in old accidents, through a building wall, grow visibly toward the wet patch, flower white and deafening for 2-3 hours, seed the wind, and then desiccate into more dry plates. A burst pipe once cracked a tower.

What this means practically:

- Drink only from a completely sealed canteen with a bite valve or enclosed straw. Never pour into an open cup outdoors. Never open a bottle to "let it breathe." Drink indoors, over a sealed drip tray
- No flasks of tea, soup, or wet food outside. All food service indoors is covered. Street food in Table City is dry, oily, roasted, or resin-candied
- Showers are dust, ultrasound, and oil. Toilets freeze-dry waste before transport. Follow the diagrams exactly, even when tired and altitude-dumb. If you don't understand the toilet, ask — locals would infinitely rather explain than call a pruning crew
- No water pistols, humidifiers, live downhill plants, cut flowers, aquariums, snow globes, or "just a little sealed terrarium." Customs in the shaft will confiscate them, and they are not being theatrical
- If you cut yourself, get indoors or press a sealed dressing on immediately and alert your host. Blood is water with extras

If a spill happens despite you: Step back, do not try to mop it, shout (this is a correct use of shouting) "WATER, WATER" and point. Everyone else will step back too. Tuners with listening spikes and pruning tools need to reach it before shoots reach stone or a conduit. Do not be embarrassed into hiding it. A hidden spill becomes a cracked street.

### 4. Sound is landscaping

The Plant does not grow toward light — light up there is too weak and diffuse. It grows toward noise. Louder, more patterned places grow faster.

Table City exists because 12,000 people, nine towers, tuned vent-streets that howl a clean A, and market squares designed to roar on purpose, make a steady noise-shape that holds the Plant in a gentle open dome. Tuners revise literal musical scores nightly, based on a network of silver node-grafts in the Trunks that can slow or turn growth at a molecular level. Those nodes are partly overgrown, partly made now by the Plant itself, and no one fully understands the oldest ones.

As a visitor you are an un-scored instrument. Therefore:

- Stay on marked streets and mat paths, especially in the city center. The mat off-path is firmer in some places and will drum your footsteps for a long way
- Walk at a steady, unhurried pace. Do not run, stamp, tap walking poles rhythmically, hum, sing, play music from a speaker, or test the echo between Trunks. A repeated rhythm is an instruction you are not qualified to give. Oil-beetles tap to ask the Plant to open resin ducts, and it obeys them. It may try to obey you too
- Do not touch, tap, knock on, or carve a Trunk. Do not cut any stem, ever. If cut it weeps a clear oil that beads and hardens to amber and never wets skin, but the wound is loud to the Plant and illegal for you
- Do not touch the silver nodes, the hair-fine filaments running into the mat, or pruning crews' listening spikes. If a new node is extruding from a bud, admire it from the path and move on
- Keep nighttime noise to your room. The nodes pulse at night, felt in molars, and coax growth down by a few percent. Unpatterned party noise undoes a night of work in your immediate neighborhood

Indoors is miraculously quiet — double cork airlocks are for decibels, not pressure. Close both doors, fully, every time. Propping an outer door open makes the whole hall a mouth the Plant leans toward.

### 5. Moving around the Table

Between Trunks you can see for miles, and distances lie. The mat is ankle-deep everywhere, corky then crunchy underfoot. Wear stiff-soled, ankle-supporting boots with soft outer soles. No crampons, no spiked soles — they pierce living cords. You will walk slower than you expect and your calves will ache from the give of the mat.

Trunk Elevators use field-lifts inside hollowed Trunks: no cables, just a lessening of weight and a pop in your sinuses. If you have inner-ear trouble, migraine with aura, or recent dental work, warn the keeper: the lift and the nightly node pulse both find your teeth. Sit if offered.

Outside the city, go with a local guide or maintenance crew in resin-hard suits. They read sound the way downhill guides read weather. Afternoon is the active time: grazers migrate, gliders hunt, crews prune shoots that lean too lovingly toward a busy kitchen. Dawn is still but bitterly CO2-frosted. Evening wind-drop can disorient newcomers — everything settles and knocks at once, and you will feel vibrations in your feet long after your ears stop registering them.

### 6. Fauna: loud, except for the parts that want to eat

Mat-grazers are long-legged, big-eared, split-hooved herd beasts the color of the mat who chew leaf-plates with grinding sideways jaws. You will often notice them first by a hole in the sound: gliders go quiet near a herd. Give herds a wide, downwind berth — their hooves drum their location into the mat, and a startled herd drums a lot louder.

Gliders — four-winged, feathered, not-quite-birds — scream constantly, not to be heard over the Plant but to steer it over generations. Nesting colonies have screamed Trunks into bending away. Do not approach a screaming colony hoping for a closer look; you are adding your noise to a century-old argument.

Small bark-city life is mostly harmless if ignored: earless burrowers that thump inside roots, iridescent oil-beetles. Do not mimic taps or thumps.

Predators are the exception to the loudness. They are huge, soft-footed on the mat, almost silent in the air. In this world silence is camouflage. If the plates, leaves, birds, and your own footfall transmission suddenly make a shaped quiet around you, stop talking (even shouting), get your back to a Trunk or building if one is close, and let your guide listen. Do not run — running is a clear rhythmic signal through the root.

### 7. Culture and small courtesies

Locals call the settlement "tuning," not Table City. They are gardeners and conductors at once, and they take scores seriously. Asking to see the nightly score, asking what note your street plays, volunteering to be quiet during a tuning operation — all appreciated.

Shouting at midday is not anger. Kindness, greetings, and law about not cutting stems are all delivered at volume in the shaft. Save your normal voice for evening and indoors; people will lean in then and talk about ordinary things while children press ears to the ground to hear grazers coming home.

You will probably dream, vividly, of rain. Natives mention this without mockery — your body remembers what the Plant is trying to forget. You will also go home slightly resin-smelling and for a few days find downhill quiet unnervingly flat. Leaf rustle will sound wrong: too soft, not ringing.

### Quick departure checklist

Filtered ear protection + backups; wind goggles; oil balm; sealed-valve canteen (empty for shaft inspection, fill only from indoor sealed dispensers); warm windproof layers with sealed cuffs; stiff, spike-less boots; sealed bags for damp clothing; headlamp — light attracts nothing, it's safe and the dim day is shorter than you think; altitude medication if your doctor advises; written hand signals; zero open-water containers, water toys, live plants, or water-based cosmetics.

Bring curiosity, bring patience for the first altitude day, and bring a willingness to spend most of the day not being heard clearly. The Table will hear you anyway — in your boots, in your heartbeat through the mat, in the little knock of your canteen against a buckle. Travel so that what it hears is steady, dry, and brief, and it will, like the city that sings inside it, make a little open space for you and let you stand in the song for a while.
