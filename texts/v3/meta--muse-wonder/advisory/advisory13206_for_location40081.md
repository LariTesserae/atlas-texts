This is not a ruin, a jungle camp, or a garden town you will visit in passing. It is a working industrial mill town that happens to be built *inside* living plants, in a drained-then-half-undrained marsh bowl, where the ground never dries, the walls keep growing, and the pumps never really stop.

An ordinary human can live there - people do - but only by doing maintenance constantly, and by obeying the local pace. Your preparation is less about braving a single hazard and more about not being slowly rotted, soaked, tripped, deafened, bitten, and molded out of function in 48 hours.

Plan for equilibrium, not adventure.

### 1. What the Place Will Do to You

**You will be wet from below, above, and the air itself.**
You drop about twenty feet from the levee road into an oxbow bowl. That drop is the weather change. On the levee you have moving air. Below, the canopy and fig walls trap heat. Water is 4 feet down in any well, running in a grid of slow brown-green canals under every lane, sweating off every iron pipe in shade, dripping off every roof long after rain, and in the morning weeping straight out of fig trunks.

Soil paths are root-mat over saturated sponge. They sigh under your feet. In late summer flood pulse, there is no flood wave - the wells all just get an inch higher at breakfast, another inch the next day, the lowest floorboards darken from underneath, hyacinth mats rise and jam against the bridges, and the pumps thump harder for weeks.

Assume: your feet will be damp all day, your clothes will never get crisp, paper will soften and curl, ungroomed leather will bloom white mold in under a week, untreated iron and rope and canvas will rot or swell or rust. Steam from the boiler house doesn't disperse, it hangs at head-height between sheds, mixed with canal mist. Lanes are in green-tinted shade from two or three leaf layers; only the central mill yard at noon is bright enough to make you squint. By late afternoon the lanes are already dark while the tin roofs are still amber.

It is loud everywhere near the center, but nothing moves quickly. Decorticators grind, belt drives slap, saws bite bamboo, trolleys clatter on a single loop track, steam vents in sudden white sighs - all under constant dripping and trickling. A hundred yards from the pumps you feel the bass thump in your soles. From the levee at dusk you won't even see the town, just darker green, a lit steam plume, window lights behind leaves, and that muffled heartbeat through the ground.

### 2. When to Go - and When to Leave

Come in the relatively drier, cooler part of the year, well outside the late-summer river rise.

If locals start checking the painted subsidence/flood marks on the pumphouse door daily instead of monthly, if your morning canal is noticeably higher than last night's, if bridges start to feel tight over swelling hyacinth, leave early. The exit is one slick grassed earth embankment and a single gravel track between banana stands. It's easy when dry, treacherous when slick, and it's the only high ground. Do not wait for an emergency evacuation - this place evacuates at root-speed, or it just lives with the water a while.

Allow acclimation days. The trapped wet heat is harder on lungs and skin than honest hot sun. Even local dogs do badly here with perpetual damp. If you have asthma, mold allergy, chronic fungal issues, immunocompromise, or trench-foot history, talk to a doctor, bring extra inhalers/antifungals in waterproof containers, and be prepared to cut the trip.

Also adjust your schedule to the mill: work is urgent and continuous, with a brief midnight pause - that's when the frog chorus erupts from the canals - and shift changes marked by a double steam blast late afternoon that shakes leaves loose. Sleep will be interrupted by pumps unless your lodging is far from the Combine.

### 3. What to Wear and Carry

Forget normal hiking gear. Think: fisherman-mechanic in a greenhouse.

**Footwear is your #1 decision.** You want boots or sturdy sandals that *drain*, dry fast, and grip wet teak, polished root, mossy stone, and algae. Heavy leather hiking boots will stay wet, grow mold, and weigh a ton. Rubber boots keep canal water out for stepping across a yard, but fill with sweat and condensation and give you fungal feet if worn all day. Most residents go with rolled trousers, bare lower legs, and shoes they can hose off. Bring:
- 2 pairs of quick-drain shoes, rotate daily
- Many thin synthetic/wool socks, none cotton - change 2-3x a day
- Antifungal powder/cream, and treat feet every night
- A spare pair kept sealed dry for the levee climb out

**Clothes:** Light, loose, synthetic or treated natural fiber that won't rot. Cotton stays clammy and mildews. Long sleeves/pants help against mosquitoes under awnings and contact with wet vegetation, but you'll roll them constantly for bridges and wet lanes. Bring a wide hat for the mill yard glare - sunglasses there, headlamp for lanes, even in daytime. Rain jacket is less useful than a small umbrella/awning hat and just accepting damp; you are getting wet from condensation and canopy drip, not just rain.

**Packing system:** Everything in dry bags, inside dry bags. Zip bags for documents, silica gel packets in bulk, hard plastic cases for electronics. Paper notebooks: use waterproof notebooks and pencil (pens bleed, paper disintegrates). Photograph / scan important papers and leave originals on the levee if you can.

Do not bring cherished leather, untreated canvas, paper books, or steel tools you are not prepared to oil daily. If you bring leather at all, bring oil. Metal: grease it, like locals grease iron with a coating that collects green dust. Expect zippers and buckles to verdigris/seize.

Add:
- Earplugs/hearing protection for mill interior; you'll need to shout near decorticators
- Work gloves - wet fiber, bamboo splinters, hot pipes (snakes coil on warm pipes in the morning)
- Mosquito repellent, plus a head net for evenings under awnings; permethrin-treated clothes help
- Waterproof headlamp with red mode (frogs, heron at settling ponds, night canal edges)
- Dry sleeping liner, not a heavy sleeping bag - you'll be warm, damp, and want something washable and mildew-resistant
- Small sewing/repair kit, spare laces that aren't natural rope (local rope is good but rots by design)

Leave your dog, and unnecessary pets, behind.

### 4. Health Prep

Before: routine vaccinations up to date, plus discuss with a travel clinic mosquito-borne illness, tetanus (rust, machetes, saw work, constantly wet cuts), leptospirosis/water-borne exposure in canals and shallow wells, and fungal skin prophylaxis. Any cut here stays wet and gets green life in it - bring waterproof dressings, antiseptic you can reapply, and take small wounds seriously.

Daily routine:
Wash with clean - not canal - water, dry feet fully despite the humidity (sit by the mess hall hearth or boiler warmth, the only really dry air), change socks, air your bedding whenever there is sun in the yard. Check between toes, groin, under pack straps for fungal rash. Hydrate *more* than you think: you sweat without noticing in saturated air and lose electrolytes; salt is a market commodity for a reason. Don't drink from the 4-foot wells unless locals tell you which are potable and it's been treated - the settling ponds behind the mill for fiber wash-water, compost runoff, latrines on pilings, and canal grid are all in the same shallow water table. Drink pumped/boiled/store water.

Mosquitoes are the real wildlife. Larger animals are rare, apart from frogs, occasional heron, and a snake relocated on a long pole. Don't reach onto warm pipes, into fig lattices, or under bales of curing fiber (they generate their own heat) without looking/gloves. If you see a snake, step back and fetch a local - killing is frowned on as wasteful; they carry them to the levee.

Also mind industrial hazards: moving belts, flywheels, trolley loop with wet heavy bales, sudden steam vents that can scald in a hanging cloud, slippery plank bridges when hyacinth rises. Walk the trolley track as if a load is always coming.

### 5. How to Move and Behave

The town is only 15 minutes across on paper, but lanes - Pump Lane, Dry Shed Cut, Second Canal - wind between breathing cane walls and fig buildings whose roots make known trip-steps everyone steps around automatically. Go slow the first day with a guide/local host. Learn the trip roots, low bamboo awnings, living bridges.

**Golden rule: You do not clear. You negotiate.**
Fig walls are structural. Banyan-type trees trained for generations on iron frames *are* the houses and shops. Aerial roots have fused through corrugated iron and concrete. Never cut, hack, or break a root, branch, or bamboo culm for a shortcut, view, or tent spot, and never lean ladders/saws against them without asking the pruner crew. Pruners are as essential as pump engineers - they thin canopy for light, cut back a root lifting a walkway, and have a written rule: never cut a structural root through. If you need space, shade change, or a path, ask them. If a house expands, they train a wall for two seasons first.

Same for infrastructure: new pipe doesn't go through a canal bank, it goes over on a bound living bamboo bridge. Respect weight limits on those, cross one at a time, don't bounce.

Other etiquette:
- One permitted hearth besides the boilers - the mess hall chimney. No campfires. Fire and wet thatch/bamboo/fiber bales are a bad mix, and smoke won't clear.
- Market is on a polished living root mat in the square, not pavement. Don't wear cleats/spikes that scar it. Small trade goods from outside are prized: salt, kerosene, machine oil, dried fish is already imported, good quality dried food, medicines in sealed containers, synthetic cord that resists rot. Ask at the company store first - this is a company mill town, not a free tourist bazaar.
- Noise carries and is contained by walls. At the midnight pause, keep quiet and listen - you'll understand the place.
- Ask before photographing inside the Combine, near belts/decorticators, or in housing on pilings connected by covered trellis walkways. Homes are 2-3 stories of timber, woven panel, salvaged sheet metal, and thoroughly semi-public but not public.
- Don't dump anything - even biodegradable - in canals. Leaf litter is dredged, composted, and spread on levee gardens in a cycle. Your plastic, oil, batteries will poison it. Carry out to the levee.

### 6. Living There Day to Day

Expect to re-oil, re-dry, re-tie daily. Hang clothes, knowing they will get soft not crisp. Shake out bedding and shoes for insects/frogs. Keep food in sealed tins - humidity, mold, and fermentation smells from soaking pits (sweet crushed green fiber) mean open food spoils fast. Local diet leans on taro grown in every wet interval, canal greens, plus imported staples.

Budget extra time for everything. A pipe repair, a wall change, a silted canal: all at root pace. If you are on a multiverse itinerary with a return window, put it up on the levee, not down in the bowl, and give yourself a buffer day to climb out, dry, and decontaminate.

**Decontamination on exit is a responsibility:** Water hyacinth, moss, fern spores, seeds from cane/banana/elephant grass, mold from bales - do not carry them to another world. Scrub shoes, shake and ideally hot-wash clothes above the levee, inspect packs for snails/froglets/seeds, leave local rope/fiber souvenirs to dry thoroughly and declare/inspect them as your home rules require. Clean and oil any gear before storing it or it will bloom in transit.

### Quick Pack Checklist

- Dry bags x many, silica gel, hard cases
- 2 drainable shoes, many synthetic socks, antifungal kit
- Light quick-dry long clothes, hat, sunglasses, headlamp
- Mosquito repellent + net, gloves, ear protection
- Waterproof notebook/pencil, copies of docs in sealed bag
- Leather/metal oil, small repair kit
- Personal meds in waterproof, wound care, electrolytes
- No irreplaceable paper/leather, no dog, no campfire gear

Come prepared to be slow, damp, and constantly maintaining both your gear and goodwill with the people who maintain the living walls. If you do, the reward is unique: to stand in a noisy fiber mill yard at noon squinting at rusted tin, then step ten feet into a lane and be in quiet green shade, pump thump in your feet, canal water plinking into an overflowing barrel, steam drifting through leaves, and to understand - in your wet socks - that the town isn't built *on* the marsh. It's a negotiation the marsh has agreed, for now, to keep.
