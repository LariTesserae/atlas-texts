For the Kettle, prepare for two incompatible places occupying the same walk.

Outside it is a new, high, freezing mountain that has not had time to become safe. Inside it is a lowland steam bath at altitude, with its own weather, its own gravity-adjacent habits, and no off switch. Most travelers get hurt not by the strangeness itself, but by dressing, drinking, navigating, or planning for only one of those places.

You do not find it by looking for it. You find it by stepping through it, between two black basalt teeth on the rim. That makes preparation doubly important: you can be in trouble one pace after you realize where you are.

### 1. The approach is a real alpine climb

Treat the mountain outside as the first hazard.

The ridge is freshly uplifted, pale, fractured, knife-edged rock with loose scree. Nothing is weathered round. Handholds cut. Fools' crampons and soft hiking shoes will shred or slip.

Bring:

* Sturdy, high-ankle mountaineering boots with stiff, insulated soles - you will need them inside, too, where the heat comes up through the ground
* Gloves tough enough for basalt edges, and a helmet if you are traversing scree slopes
* Layers for thin, wind-scoured cold, well below comfort even in sun. Clouds will be *below* you, so there is no shelter and UV is savage
* Sun protection, lip balm, and more water than the distance suggests. The wind takes your breath in short pulls and dries you without you noticing sweat

Acclimatize. Do not make the Kettle a first-day objective at altitude. Headache, nausea, clumsiness on that ridge is not a minor inconvenience - the rock is unforgiving and the scree shifts if you look at it too hard. Turn-around criteria matter more than reaching the bowl.

Tell someone outside the multiverse hop / off the mountain your window. Inside the Kettle no shout will carry the way you expect, and no weather report from the valley applies in there.

### 2. Dress for a sauna you enter in one step, and leave into frost

Inside is easily 40°C, wet heat that presses on your shirt and fills your lungs like light through bathwater. Outside that invisible line, your wet clothes will rime with frost instantly on exit.

That transition is the most ordinary, predictable way to get hypothermia or heat exhaustion in the same hour.

Pack in sealed dry bags:

1. **A complete dry exit set** - base layer, socks, hat, gloves - that you do NOT wear inside. Keep it in your pack, sealed, until you are in the southern shelter preparing to leave.
2. **Light, loose, quick-dry clothing for inside.** Cotton will become a hot, heavy swamp. Heavy alpine shells will cook you.
3. **Electrolytes and salt.** You will sweat constantly, and in a strange touch, your sweat lifts and briefly becomes weather around you. You can be dehydrating while your skin is slick and it is raining. Drink on schedule, not on thirst.
4. **A change plan.** Before you step back out, dry your skin as best you can, change, cover every bit of wet hair and neck, *then* step through. One pace is all it takes to go from steam to screaming wind.

If you start to feel dizzy, nauseous, confused, or you *stop* sweating inside - sit at the rim in the shade of a rock, not on the hot ground, sip electrolytes, and cut the visit short. The heat hums up through boot soles; the floor and the centers of the pools can scald. Never sit, kneel, or test the central pools with a bare hand for more than a second. Sit on your pack if you must rest.

Plan for 1-3 hours inside, maximum, on a first visit. It is only two hundred paces across, but you will not walk straight, it is too hot to linger, and it gets worse after dark.

### 3. Water is everywhere and not all of it is for drinking

The Kettle runs a short, frantic, closed cycle: hot pools evaporate in sheets, condense on the underside of nothing about twenty meters up, and rain back down in patches. It is always just-after-rain. Every surface is dark and wet, every weep in the wall is running.

And it is almost silent. Drips fall mute. Streams slide without sound. The seven grey pylons around the rim drink sound and cold the way a sponge drinks water. You will hear your own swallowing.

Practical consequences:

* **Do not rely on voice.** Agree on hand signals before you enter. Shouting to a partner ten meters away may feel muffled and oddly intimate at once, and outside the boundary the wind will erase it entirely.
* **Do not trust a pool by its edge.** Centers are too hot to keep a hand in. Edges, where the white and copper-green mineral crust forms, are cool enough to drink - and that copper-green is your warning. Mineral content is high and unknown. Prefer the shelter's self-filling still. If you must use pool water, take only from the coolest mineral rim, filter and treat it, and stop if it tastes metallic beyond a faint tang. The hoop shrine by one pool makes steam come out cold and iron-tasting; that is an instrument, not a tap.
* **Expect everything to get wet anyway.** Water beads *above* the pylons and hangs, trembling, then lets go all at once in a straight-down local rain. Keep electronics, paper notes, matches, and your exit clothes sealed. A drone or tall pole will hit that condensation ceiling and come down dripping and confused.
* **Watch where you wade - better, don't.** Under the largest pool something pale and eyeless sometimes turns, slow as a thought, and the crust reforms over it. Treat all pools as scalding, chemically active, and occupied. Observe from the grit.

Breathe slowly at first. The air is thick, hot in the lungs despite the altitude outside. Asthmatics and anyone sensitive to heat/humidity or mold spores - there is moss in every crack, orchids on the fig, fern spores in the steam - should test the boundary for a minute before committing. You can step back out.

### 4. Navigation inside will lie to you gently

This is worse than a place where compasses spin. Shadows are *nearly* right. Yours bends a fraction toward the center. Dust drifts sideways. Droplets beside a pylon fall in a slight arc. Flat path stones from the shelter to the central pool rearrange themselves when unwatched, and you will arrive from a direction you did not expect, always a little closer to a pylon than you intended.

So:

* **Memorize your exit gate visually before you go ten steps in.** The two basalt teeth, a notch in the rim, the shelter to the south. Turn around and look at it three times in the first minutes as light lensing shifts perspective.
* **Leave a non-electronic marker just inside your entry** - a bright ribbon on a dead stick you bring in, not tied to a pylon, not left permanently. Do not mark on pylons.
* **Do not follow the flat stones as if they were a road.** Use them as a suggestion. Check your position against the rim, the western fig tree, the house-tall fern-tree, and the shelter roof.
* **Stay off the pylons.** They are waist- to shoulder-high, matte grey, neither stone nor metal, and they get neither hot nor wet. They are the machinery. Do not touch, lean, climb, chip, or tether to them. If hanging droplets above a tip are trembling en masse, you are in a micro-rain drop zone - move sideways.
* Walk slowly. Ground is hot, gritty, mineral-crusted, and slick. Ferns fold shut when your shadow falls on them, hanging roots on the north wall spray mist when their bulbous tips contract - flora here reacts quickly and does not trust the soil. Give each plant its clearing; the bowl is lush but sparse by design. Do not trample the bare ground between plants; that bare ground is part of the system.

The fauna has learned the quiet: a basalt-colored lizard that freezes mid-stride for ten minutes, a pair of huge white moths that ride updrafts in spirals - researchers' carved tile by the shelter says there have only ever been two, for thirty years. Do not chase, feed, or try to prove the note wrong. Photograph from a distance, no flash near the pools.

Do not collect the shrine/instruments at pool edges: bowls that catch upward rain, marker stones whose shadow points downslope at noon and upslope at three. They may be offerings or sensors. Either way, moving them falsifies the experiment that is still running without its makers.

### 5. Respect the daily purge, and be gone before dark

Late in the day the Kettle exhales.

Warning signs, in order:

1. Pools begin to *tick* in your teeth, not your ears
2. Fig orchids close into fists, the fern-tree shudders open
3. All hanging water lets go at once - rain inside while dry freezing sun is visible on the outer ridge
4. Pylons show a seam of light where they meet the rock - the only time they do

Then a column of steam punches up through the center and out, blowing a round hole in the cloud below. For eleven minutes after, the bowl is merely warm, walls stop weeping, and you can faintly hear birds from far below. Then it restarts, one thread at a time.

Rules for the purge:

* **Never be near the center when your teeth start to tick.** Move to the rim, near your exit or the shelter, with rock between you and the central pool. That steam column can scald.
* Do not stand under a pylon's suspended water when it lets go - it is only water, but it arrives all at once, cold and blinding, on hot slick rock.
* Use the eleven-minute lull, if you are caught, to make your exit in relative comfort. It is the safest window to change and step out.

After dark the cycle runs faster, rain harder, and sometimes briefly *upward* from ground to sky. The shelter - low, open-sided, self-mending tiles, sleeping platform, still, three cups fused by heat into one useless object - is an emergency refuge, not a hotel. Do not plan to sleep there. Plan to be below the ridge before night makes the hot, wet, silent bowl actively hostile.

### 6. Leaving and aftercare

Leave the way you came. Check you have your ribbon marker. Change into your sealed dry set in the shelter. Eat something salty. Then step out and keep moving down for at least ten minutes before you stop to admire the faint plume where no volcano should be - standing still in rime-wet gear on that wind ridge is how fingers go numb.

Below, expect: pruned skin, salt-crusted clothes, a strange ringing quiet in your ears as normal stream-noise and wind return, and possibly mild disorientation from the lensing and sound-drinking. Hydrate again. Check feet for heat-reddening from the floor. Wash off mineral pool splash before it dries itchy.

Leave the Kettle as you found it: no cairns besides the existing markers, no food waste - there is almost no animal life to clean it up and the water cycle never leaves the bowl, so what you introduce will rain on that fig forever. Take photographs, temperature notes, the memory of stepping from summer to frost in a single pace.

If you go prepared for altitude *and* a sauna, for silence *and* for weather made indoors, and you leave before it decides to breathe out - or worse, breathe in - the Kettle is visitable by an ordinary human.

It will still not feel ordinary.
