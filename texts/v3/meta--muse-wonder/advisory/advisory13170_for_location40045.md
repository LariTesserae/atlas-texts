This is not a place that will kill you dramatically. It will wear you down, soak you through, dazzle you, keep you awake, and get you thoroughly, gradually lost if you treat it like normal ground.

It is an ancient, warm, almost perfectly flat flood-basin: cracked salt-clay pans linked by finger-deep water, thin sedges, isolated flat-topped trees, and miles of mud that looks solid and isn't. There are no roads, no buildings, no hills to navigate by, and no fast way across anything. Prepare for wet feet for the entire visit, for heat reflected up at you as well as down, and for travel measured in hours per visible landmark.

### 1. Understand the core problem: water that spreads, not flows

The danger and the difficulty here aren't depth, they're extent and tempo.

A channel can be damp earth in the morning and ankle-deep in a week. Sheet water can join pool to pool over days, turning your route back into a continuous, knee-deep mirror with no current to follow. A flood does not rush in — it sighs in. You won't be swept away. You will wake up to find your camp island is now 40 meters from dry-ish ground, and the ground you crossed yesterday now sucks at your boots for a mile.

Plan all movement for 1-2 km an hour at best when it's wet, less in soft oxbows and black mud. Distance lies. With nothing taller than a reedbed, a tree that looks 20 minutes away will stay the same size for an hour, then loom up suddenly. A reedbed that looks close may be across two pans and a hidden channel.

Never trust a sheen. Bright ahead can mean open water, or just wet clay reflecting sky. Probe with a pole.

### 2. Feet, legs, and clothing

This is the most important preparation.

You will be wading hand-depth to waist-depth, then walking on salt-crusted plates that click and curl, then sinking into chocolate clay under a dry crust. Boots that try to stay waterproof will fill and stay filled.

Bring:
- **Wading footwear that drains:** sturdy, closed-toe sandals with straps over socks, or light mesh boots designed to let water out. You need toe protection for buried pebbles, mussel shells, and cracked clay edges, and soles that grip slick clay. Do not bring heavy leather hiking boots or bare feet.
- **Two pairs of quick-dry socks, minimum**, changed and rinsed daily. Salt + fine silt is an abrasive paste. It will blister wet skin in hours.
- **A trekking pole or stout stick.** It is your depth gauge, your balance on slick pans, and your way to feel for holes, lungfish burrows, and softshell animals before you step.
- **Light, long clothing.** Long sleeves and long trousers of thin, loose, quick-dry fabric. Shorts expose you to sun, salt, reed cuts, biting insects, and mud. Light colors help with heat and let you see insects.
- **Gaiters or trouser cuffs you can seal.** Not to keep water out — to keep fine mud and grit out of your shoes.

Every evening: wash feet in treated water (not pan water), dry fully, check for cuts, salt sores, and embedded grit. In warm, silty, bacteria-rich mud, a small blister becomes an infected sore very fast. Carry antiseptic, plasters that work wet, and a spare dry pair of sleep socks kept in a sealed bag.

### 3. Sun and light, doubled

There is almost no shade. One tree per long walk throws a disc of shadow, and that is it. The sun hits you from above, then bounces hard off wet clay, salt crust, and open water. Looking down can dazzle you.

Bring a wide-brim hat with a neck flap, polarized sunglasses on a cord (unpolarized will still leave you squinting into green-gold glare under water), reef-safe sunscreen reapplied after every wade, and lip protection. Expect headache and eye fatigue on day one if you don't.

The sky takes up more of the world than the land. There is no visual shelter. Some travelers feel exposed or agoraphobic here. Clouds build by afternoon into tall white towers; their moving shadows are your only relief, and a sudden dark, pewter pool means shadow, not deeper water.

### 4. Drinking water in a place full of undrinkable water

Do not drink the pans.

That water is warm, salty at the margins, full of algae, sulfurous where it has stood, and alive with snails, fish, bird droppings, and animal tracks through it. Even the clearer pools are mineral-heavy.

Carry in what you can, and carry a two-stage system: a filter for silt/algae (everything will clog with fine clay, so bring a pre-filter cloth and a backflush tool) and purification for bacteria/viruses. Collect, if you must, from the deepest, least salty central pans after rain, let silt settle for an hour, then treat.

Despite being surrounded by humidity and steam, you will dehydrate. Warm air, sun from two directions, and slogging in mud is heavy work. Drink on a schedule, add electrolytes — you will lose salt and then walk across salt you cannot use.

### 5. Shelter and sleep

There is no reliably dry high ground, only slightly higher ground: sedge tufts, low polished domes, termite and nesting mounds. A few centimeters matter.

- **Camp island-hopping style:** Choose a rise with a tree if possible, but never in the lowest channel lines, even if they are dry that night. At dusk, mist fills the old channels first — that's the map of where water wants to go.
- **Sleep raised.** A lightweight cot, or a hammock if you are lucky enough to have two close supports (you usually won't), or an inflatable pad on a fully waterproof groundsheet with sealed seams. Standard tent floors will wick up damp through salt clay all night. In the wettest season, a small elevated platform or bivy on a mound is worth the weight.
- **Insect netting is non-negotiable.** Insects rise in clouds at dusk; swallows by day are replaced by bats by night for good reason. Frogs will be deafening from late afternoon through night — metallic peeps, jug croaks, reed rattles layered to the horizon. Cicadas own the midday from each lone tree. Bring earplugs if you ever intend to sleep.
Expect shin-high mist in the morning, condensation on everything, and a post-rain world that steams. Keep sleeping clothes and electronics in dry bags, inside dry bags. Nothing air-dries completely here; rotate damp items in sun at midday.

Rain is warm, brief, and frequent, then again. You don't need heavy cold-weather rain gear, you need a light shell you can walk and wade in without overheating, and pack covers that seal from below — spray comes up from splash as much as down.

### 6. Navigation and being found

The horizon is an almost unbroken hazed line. Landmarks repeat: tuft, pool, tree, reedbed, tree.

Bring compass and offline GPS / multiverse beacon and *mark your entry point on the highest rise you can find*. In flat mist at dawn, with frog noise disorienting you, you can walk in a circle across identical pans.

Navigation tricks that work: navigate tree-to-tree; note that cicada sound domes mark trees before you see them well; follow animal paths only until they braid out into mud (they will); leave no reliance on footprints — the mud records everything perfectly for a few hours, then water fills it.

Tell your extraction contact that pickup cannot be from a valley floor. Sheet flood can make a landing site a shallow lake for weeks. Choose a salt pan or dome that stays emergent, mark it with a tall, visible pole, and be prepared to wade to it.

### 7. Wildlife: abundant, distant, and heard first

You will hear this place before you see it. Splashes carry a mile. A crane's call arrives before the bird resolves from mist.

Most animals are wary and spread out: egrets and herons in shallows, ibis probing mud, ducks exploding up in whistling flocks, dragonflies, water beetles, turtles plopping off clods, monitor lizards hauling between pools, snakes swimming with only the head up, small antelope at dawn.

Give them the channels.

- **Stay out of the deepest eastern channels at dawn, dusk, and night.** Wide splayed tracks there are hippos. You will not out-wade a hippo in its own deep mud, and you can't see fast water to escape to — there isn't any height to climb except an occasional tree.
- **Watch where you step in reeds and at pool edges.** Snakes swim and rest there, turtles and crabs work the bottom, nesting colonies of weavers and waders in dense reedbeds are clamorous and defensive in breeding season. Don't push through a colony.
- **Do not wade bare-legged at night.** Headlamp light draws insects, and everything nocturnal is feeding in the shallows.
- **Store food sealed and away from sleep.** Not for bears — for waterbirds, monitors, insects, and damp. Everything will investigate a camp on a lonely rise.

Observe from a rise with binoculars. In this openness, you don't need to get close; you need patience. Animals appear at the edge of sight constantly if you're still.

### 8. Health beyond feet

Warm standing water + snails + mud = do not let untreated water sit on broken skin, in eyes, or mouth. Swim only if you must cross, keep head up. Wash after wading.

Insect repellent, reapplied after water, plus netting and long clothes is better than repellent alone. Check for leeches and ticks after reedbeds (less common than mosquitoes and biting flies, but margins hide them).

Bring antihistamines, oral rehydration salts, treatment for infected cuts, and medication for stomach upset. The nearest help is not "over the hill" — there is no hill, and evacuation is a slog.

### 9. Pace and mindset

Stay at least a few days in your planning, or you will think nothing happens. It changes a finger's width a day: grass greens from the base, pans concentrate as they shrink, fish crowd, birds crowd after them, frogs fall silent species by species, salt rims widen and whiten. It is a slow system.

Walk early morning (glass-still water, bird activity, coolest), rest in the single tree's shade through the cicada hours if you can, walk again late afternoon into the copper evening when every pool reflects at once. Midday travel across salt is punishing.

Carry less than you think. Every kilo sinks you deeper into clay. Pack everything you carry in either hand or on a high backpack — drag sleds and low wheeled carts will bog in the first pan.

Leave the crust as you found it. Cracked plates, nesting mounds, and salt blooms are fragile and are habitat pressed into cracks — seeds, eggs, burrowing fish wait there for the next rise. Walk on already-broken mud paths where you can.

**In brief before you portal/ride/walk in:** confirm season and water trend with anyone who knows the basin, drop in on high ground in morning light, mark it, dress for wading and glare, bring filtration and foot care as if they were food, pack sleep up off the ground inside nets, and plan to move tree-to-tree at a walking pace that in any other landscape would feel like standing still.

It rewards that preparation. Few places let you see weather, water, tracks, mist-channels, and animal life all laid out at once over miles, with sound carrying unimpeded and the sky doing most of the architecture.
