This is not a dangerous place in the obvious way. No predators, no insects, no storms to speak of, no seasons bearing down on you. Prepare instead for three things that will shape your entire visit: the water is not weather, it's infrastructure; the land is physically young and sharp; and the biology is radically incomplete.

You are going to a high, warm, permanently wet town built on new limestone by people who have never lived with animals.

### 1. Before you arrive: biosecurity is the most important thing

This world has people and plants, and as far as anyone remembers, it has never had animals. No birds, no insects, no fish, no frogs, no worms in your familiar sense. Crops that need pollination are pollinated by hand, by children with feather brushes. The marsh is silent because there is nothing in it to croak.

Do not be the traveler who changes that.

* Leave pets, service animals, live plants, fresh fruit, seeds, soil, untreated wood, leather or wool that may carry eggs, and aquarium/live food cultures behind entirely.
* Clean boots, tent pegs, trouser cuffs, backpack seams, and camera bags obsessively. A single pregnant insect, a clutch of snail eggs in mud, or viable crop disease on a boot could be an ecological catastrophe here, with no predators or competitors to check it.
* Bring no meat, cheese, honey, or other animal foods for gifts. They will not be culturally familiar, they will spoil fast in the damp, and the locals have no waste system designed for animal remains.
* Assume you will also need plant-pathogen discipline. Wash hands before helping in gardens. Don't walk from a sick terrace into a healthy one.

Pack your own protein and B12. The local diet is abundant but entirely plant-based: broad-leafed greens, tall wind-pollinated grain, hand-pollinated fruits and vegetables. It will keep you fed, it will probably be bland to an omnivore palate, and over more than a few weeks you will need supplementation.

### 2. Altitude that lies to you

It is high - high enough for thin air, sharp UV, and a two-mile view across the valley to the eastern aqueduct - but latitude makes it warm. It feels like a greenhouse courtyard after sun on wet stone, not like a mountain.

That mismatch gets travelers.

* Give yourself 24-48 hours of easy walking on arrival, even if you feel warm and comfortable. You will get winded hauling your own bag up a stepped lane.
* Sun protection matters more than warmth. The light looks ordinary, but the air is clear and thin. Hat, sunscreen, sunglasses. Sunglasses will fog constantly when you move from sun into shade over steaming stone; bring a cloth.
* Distances deceive. Because the town is spread out low and open - deliberately, to leave room for channels, tanks, terraces and gardens - a neighborhood that looks ten minutes away may be a causeway walk of forty. Everything is walkable, nothing is close.

Bring a watch or alarm. There is no dawn chorus, no roosters, no insects building with the heat. Mornings arrive with mist burning off the marsh to the same height as yesterday, and silence.

### 3. Dress for a place that never dries

It may not rain during your visit, and you will still be wet every day. Water seeps from cliff faces, stands a hand's breadth under garden soil, runs in stone channels along every street, drips from every eave, and steams off roofs and pavements in the sun.

Your enemy is not soaking, it's never quite drying, and the fungal, softened skin that follows.

Best kit: synthetic or woven-plant-style fibers that hold no water, in several thin layers. Cotton, down, untreated leather, and canvas will stay clammy, grow mildew in days, and start to smell. Wool will be an exotic curiosity.

Take:

* Two pairs of footwear you can wash clean of mud and spores. Soft, high-grip soles with shallow treads that can catch the cross-grooves cut into local paths. Hard smooth soles, leather soles, and the flat sandals that work on dry stone will skate. The center of every step and lane is worn dark and glass-slick; the edges are moss. Walk in the middle, slowly.
* A walking stick helps more than you'd expect -- on slick causeways, crossing flat stepping-stones over plaza rills, and on new limestone outside town.
* A wide-brimmed hat that also sheds a drip-line, and a light waterproof outer layer for mist and spray, not for storms.
* Seal everything vulnerable: notebooks, maps, medicines, electronics, spare clothes in waxed or sealed bags inside your pack. Copper fittings here green at the edge in weeks. Your zippers and circuit boards will not enjoy the same patina.
* Foot care: dry socks for sleeping, antifungal powder/cream, and time each evening with feet genuinely dry and raised. Trench-foot-type maceration, athlete's foot, and small cuts turning infected in the warm damp are your likeliest medical problems. Sharp limestone makes those small cuts common, so bring antiseptic and plasters that stick on damp skin.

Local houses stand on knee- to waist-high stone platforms for a reason. Do not camp on the ground, dig a tent trench, or pitch in garden soil -- your pit will shine, then fill.

### 4. Living in the water system

Learn this rule on day one and repeat it: **You do not dam a channel; you widen your section of it.**

Every roof sheds to a gutter, every gutter to a tank or street rill, every tank to an overflow someone downstream is counting on for their garden. The fountains, cisterns, irrigation basins and spillways sit at a perfect equilibrium. The water pressure comes from the rock itself, steady morning and dusk.

As a guest that means:

* Ask before you wash, empty a basin, take a bucket from a cistern, or even block a rill with your bag while you rest. What looks like wastewater to you is someone else's inflow.
* Don't introduce soap, sunscreen wash-off, insect repellent, fuel, or grey water into a street channel unless you've been shown exactly which basin is meant for it. Downstream is a kitchen garden.
* Don't dig, don't divert, don't build a little dam of stones in a stream for fun. Foundations here are not dug -- platforms are raised and water is allowed underneath through loose-stone culverts. Copy that logic in miniature.
* Drink where locals drink -- spring lines and household tanks -- rather than from marsh pools or algae-filmed cisterns. The water is mineral-clean from limestone, but standing water films green quickly in the warmth.
* Expect plumbing to be gravity, copper, clay and stone, open and audible everywhere. Privacy is visual, not acoustic in the way you'd expect: wet stone and moss swallow echo, but with no animal noise, human voices carry cleanly. Keep yours low. A loom in an open doorway, a gate, your own feet are loud sounds here.

Limewash: houses are recoated not mainly for beauty but because lime slows moss. Fresh wash can irritate skin and eyes. Don't lean against bright-white, chalky walls in damp clothes.

### 5. Outside town: new rock, no forgiveness in the landscape

Up close, the cliffs are beautiful in the same way a fresh fracture is beautiful -- pale facets, straight joints, V-cut gullies, waterfalls dropping thin and white straight from cracks into narrow basins that have not yet widened.

Do not treat them like well-worn hiking country.

* Rock is sharp enough to cut hands and shred footwear. Gloves for any scrambling.
* Joints are unweathered and can be brittle. No established scree, no soft runouts below falls. Waterfall basins may be much deeper, and the current much more vertical, than they look.
* Vegetation is lush but not cushioning jungle. Ferns in wall joints, moss on shaded steps, reeds in channels. Pollen from wind-pollinated grain and spores from ferns and moss can be heavy in the still weeks. If you have hay fever or mold sensitivity, bring medication -- there will be no break from it.
* You will not get bitten or stung. You do not need repellent, nets, or snake precautions. You do need to shake out bedding for damp, not for creatures.

Locals move stone, tile and greens on hand-carts pulled in pairs on wooden rollers. Offer to pull, don't look for a beast to hire. Pack light enough that you or two people can move your luggage. Wheeled suitcases will suffer on grooved stone, causeways, and stepping slabs.

### 6. The psychological part no packing list fixes

The quiet here is total in a way most travelers have never experienced. No birdsong in the morning. No drone in the grass. No dogs greeting you. It is not eerie to the people who live there, and the town does not feel abandoned -- markets, building yards scraping stone on stone, hand-pollination, looms, conversation -- but outsiders often find the first evenings unsettling. Your own breathing, swallowing, and footsteps on damp plaza stone become foreground.

And nothing changes tempo to reassure you. Springs run at the same rate, gardens replace leaf with leaf, grain is cut and stands again, new walls turn fern-green at the mortar in weeks and look a year old when only their corners are still sharp. The place feels finished, like a basin at level.

Bring something to mark time and make small human noise respectfully -- a journal, an instrument played quietly indoors, work to do with your hands. Plan deliberate conversation. People who arrive expecting adventure and discovery sometimes leave restless; people who arrive prepared to observe balance, maintenance, and repetition usually settle in.

Walk out past the last houses in late afternoon if you can. Terraces mirror, fields steam, the marsh shines at the south end, the aqueduct crosses on low arches, warm air rises smelling of green leaf, clay, lime and clean stone, and below the rim the cloud-country hides the bottom of the world. You'll understand then why they build so widely, talk so quietly, and never hurry the water.

Prepare to be clean, dry-footed at night, light-packed, protein-supplemented, acclimatized, and silent-footed -- and to leave no living trace of your own world behind you.
