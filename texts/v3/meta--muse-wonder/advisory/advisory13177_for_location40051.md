This is not a place you swim in. This is not even, in any practical human sense, a place you dive in.

It is an overhead environment with no shore, no surface, no light, and no direct way up — a drowned valley sealed by tens of kilometres of jammed sea ice, filled top to bottom with supercooled, moving seawater full of ice crystals and silt.

An ordinary human body lasts minutes here unprotected, and only a little longer in ordinary diving gear. Your preparation has to start from that assumption: you are not visiting water you can escape by swimming up. You are visiting the inside of a cold, dark, pressurized machine, and up is a ceiling.

The only sane ways to go are, in order of preference:

1. By remotely operated vehicle, while you stay on top of the ice.
2. Inside a small, ice-rated submersible at surface pressure.
3. As a highly trained technical ice/cave diver, tethered, for a very short, very planned penetration at slack tide — and even then, treat it as an expedition, not a dive.

Do not plan to arrive in open water. If your method of travel lets you choose an arrival point, arrive inside a vehicle or a pressurized habitat already in place. A portal, door, teleport, or airlock opening straight into this basin means an inrush of water at about -1.8°C / 28.8°F, under pressure, full of frazil ice. You will cold-shock, inhale, and be carried away from your entry point before you can orient yourself.

### 1. Understand what will actually kill you

**The cold is not normal cold.**
This water is at or below the freezing point of fresh water, kept liquid only by salt and motion, and in places supercooled by being dragged under the ice. Exposed skin freezes almost at once. Eyes, lips, and airways are not viable surfaces.

Worse, supercooled water wants to become ice on contact with anything it can nucleate on. Frazil — millions of tiny platelets and needles — will form on and in your equipment. It collects on masks, valves, zippers, tether lines, propellers, and bubbles. A regulator that works perfectly in ordinary ice diving can ice up and free-flow or seal shut here. A bubble rising from you does not rise clean and fast; it gathers crystals, grows spiky and sluggish, and freezes into the ceiling.

**There is no up.**
Above you is not air and light. It is first a spongy layer of platelet ice, metres thick in places, hanging under the true ice like a second, loose roof. Above that are pressure keels hanging down 10-20 metres like an upside-down mountain range.

If you lose buoyancy control and ascend, you do not surface. You hit ice, possibly in a crack between keels, in the dark, and become trapped under a moving ceiling. This is cave diving rules: no direct ascent is possible, ever, except through a specific maintained hole.

That hole, if you are lucky, will not look bright. At midday in deep winter there is only a faint blue-grey dimness for a little while; 50 metres down even that is gone. A hole is only "a slightly less dark place in the dark."

**Visibility is an arm's length on a good day.**
Frazil and suspended glacial silt backscatter light exactly like headlights in a blizzard. Turn on a powerful white floodlight and you will see glittering murk and nothing else. Anything dropped to the bottom raises a plume that can hang for an hour.

Lights are for close work and signalling, kept low and angled. Your real senses here are touch, tether tension, sonar, and instruments.

**The current will take you.**
It is quiet, but it is not gentle. It enters over a shallow sill, reverses twice a day with the tide, lifts sediment off a flat floor, and can carry a diver, a fish, or a lost rope kilometres. There is no surf or roar to warn you. You feel it as a steady tug on your body and tether and a pressure in your chest.

Nothing falls straight down. Nothing drifts where you expect. Navigation by "I came from that way" is useless after one tide change.

**There are almost no landmarks.**
The bottom is an immense, flat plain of grey glacial mud — soft for a few centimetres, then like wet stone — interrupted only by patches of egg-smooth cobbles, dissolving shells, sunken kelp ribbons, and the odd feather star on a stone. Compass work is poor this far north and in this iron-dark; GPS and ordinary radio do not work underwater or under thick ice. If you have not placed your own references, you are lost in a featureless room the size of a valley.

### 2. Prepare the exit before you prepare the entry

No one goes under until the surface team has:

- Drilled and is actively maintaining at least two, preferably three, access holes through the ice, far enough apart not to share the same keel or crack system. Holes freeze and fill with platelet slush constantly; they need saws, augers, scoops, and a watch kept on them.
- Installed acoustic pingers and strobes *in the water under each hole*, plus a physical guideline from the hole down through the platelet layer. You find a hole by sound and touch, not by looking for daylight.
- A heated shelter, rewarming gear, oxygen, and a rescue diver/vehicle team on the ice. Rewarming is not comfort here — wet, cold divers coming up through slush can collapse after exit.
- A current, tide, and ice-drift plan. The pack above is moving and grinding. A hole that was over the basin this morning may have drifted, ridged over, or closed. Surface GPS must be logged continuously, and underwater transponders must be re-surveyed.

Coordinate with local hunters if any use this ice. Their hand-lines — wooden reels, stone sinkers, bone or iron hooks — are sometimes the only human presence. They know where the ice holds, where leads refreeze thin, and where the tide runs strongest. Their lost gear is already on the mud down there. Don't add yours carelessly.

### 3. If you go in a submersible — the recommended human method

Choose a vehicle rated for under-ice work, not just deep water:

- Upward-looking sonar to map keels and find holes, forward sonar for the black ahead, and a Doppler velocity log to hold position over flat mud.
- No exposed, unheated viewports, intakes, or thruster guards that can pack with frazil and platelet ice. Everything external will accrete ice; you need heaters, shakers, or mechanical clearing, and the power budget to run them.
- Redundant ballast and a plan for getting *heavier*, not just lighter. Ice building up on the hull changes buoyancy. Accidentally positive under this ceiling is an emergency.
- A physical or acoustic link to the surface hole at all times. Free-swimming under tens of kilometres of pack and hoping to find a hole later is suicide.
- Internal heat, CO2 scrubbing, and batteries kept warm inside the pressure hull. Cold-soaked external batteries lose capacity fast. Heat inside a 1-atmosphere cabin is the single thing that makes this visit survivable for hours instead of minutes.
- Manipulators rather than hands for any sampling. The bottom sediment is easily ruined for visibility, and the animals — hand-sized isopods, skates, a rare passing Greenland shark, tiny crustaceans in the ice overhead — are slow, cold-adapted, and easily harmed. The shark is not a meaningful threat; you are the threat to it.

Stay off the bottom. Hover well above the mud, move slowly, and never set down except on a cobble patch you have surveyed. Touching mud blinds you and everyone behind you.

### 4. If you absolutely must dive it

Treat this as technical cave + ice + deep + current diving combined, and shorten everything.

You will need, at minimum:

- Full-face mask with thermal protection, no exposed skin at all. A standard regulator in an open mouth is a failure point.
- A closed-circuit rebreather is preferable to open-circuit — less bubble-frazil, less gas loss from free-flows — but only if it is specifically cold-proofed and its sensors and valves cannot ice. Many expeditions will choose the opposite for simplicity; either way, carry fully redundant, cold-rated gas and regulators tested *in supercooled flowing water*, not just in a test tank.
- A drysuit with active electric heating, heated gloves and undergarments, multiple independent power supplies worn inside, argon or suit heating that cannot depend on a single battery. A wetsuit is not a slower option; it is not an option.
- A continuous, unbreakable tether/guideline to the entry hole, tended from above, with line signals rehearsed until they are reflex. Wireless comms may fail; the line does not.
- Chemical and low-output lights, backup lights, cutting tools reachable by either hand for line, kelp, and platelet slush, and a slate/instrument console you can read by touch.
- Inertial/acoustic navigation, not just a compass. Depth, time, tide state, and distance-from-hole must be known minute by minute.

Dive only at slack water, when the tide is turning and the basin briefly hesitates. Set a hard turn time well before the current rebuilds. Your gas plan, thermal plan, and decompression plan all end at the hole — if you miss it, there is no other exit to decompress under.

Train beforehand in three separate environments and then together: under solid ice with no exit, in a cave with zero visibility, and in strong reversing current in the dark. If any one of those frightens you into task-fixation, do not combine them.

Screen out anyone with cardiovascular risk, Raynaud's, cold-induced asthma, claustrophobia, or panic in darkness. Cold shock and the sight of that inverted mountain range overhead — white and blue-grey blocks kilometres wide, groaning faintly through the ice — cause perfectly competent divers to bolt upward. Upward is the worst direction there is.

### 5. What to carry, and what to leave behind

Carry: sonar over eyesight, touch over vision, redundancy over elegance. Extra heat. Extra ways to cut free. An acoustic locator. A way to mark where you have been that the current will not immediately erase.

Leave behind: anything buoyant and loose, anything that depends on GPS/radio/sunlight, standard snorkels and recreational computers not rated for this depth and cold, and any plan that depends on finding natural features — a wreck, a wall, a reef. There is nothing built here, nothing has ever survived being built here, and the bottom litter — a stone sinker with its knot still on, a rust-furred knife in the silt — is just litter being slowly digested.

Pack food, water, and medicine for the *surface* camp, not the dive: high calories, hot drinks in insulated systems that won't freeze, and a multi-day margin for storms and ice movement trapping you on top. Being stranded on the pack is a separate survival problem from being under it.

### 6. Mental preparation

Prepare for emptiness in motion. Most of a watch here is black water streaming sideways with crystal, silt clouds boiling up when the tide turns, a skate stirring a plume, marine snow that never quite lands. Sounds are small and wrong: the tick and chime of crystals hitting each other, a soft hiss of silt on silt, and very far away a groan as a ridge grinds in the pack, transmitted terribly clearly and then gone.

It is not hostile. It is not weird. It obeys ordinary physics completely — pressure, cold, and reversal — and that is exactly why it is so unforgiving. Nothing here will make an exception for you.

Go briefly, go tethered to a maintained hole, go warm inside a machine if you can, and let your instruments do the looking. If you come back up through the platelet slush into that faint, less-dark circle, with ice in your gear and the line still in your hand, you have done it correctly.
