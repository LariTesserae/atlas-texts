This is not a lake you visit. It is three incompatible places stacked on top of each other, and you have to prepare for all of them at once:

1. A 4,000-meter alpine summit
2. A violent, cliff-bound ocean shore
3. A deep-water dive

An ordinary human who prepares for only one of those will be hurt by the other two. Treat the whole trip that way from the beginning.

### 1. Understand what you are actually going to

You will not find it from below. There is no valley view, no stream leading up to it, no beach. The mountain is obscenely young, still rising and splitting, made of unweathered black rock with edges sharp enough to cut leather. There is effectively no soil, no shelter, and almost no life to orient yourself by.

You will hear it before you see it — and only after you have climbed high enough that the air is too thin for sound to carry that well. A contained roaring and crashing will start coming up through the soles of your boots, through your knees, into your teeth. A hundred meters out, shouting stops working.

The entrance is a slit in a fold of the summit, barely shoulder-wide. Inside is a bowl perhaps 12-15 paces across, open to the sky through a ragged eye far above. It is brim-full of clear, cold salt water in permanent, violent motion: knee-high waves generated in the middle, a wandering vortex, undertow, spray thrown ten feet up the walls, and a fast tide that drops and surges every 40-50 minutes regardless of the moon.

And underneath that little bowl is depth and pressure that do not belong there. A few meters down your ears will scream like you are on a real ocean dive. A dropped tool will fall, shrink to a speck in blue-green water, and vanish. Sometimes there is a pale, soft, newly-formed mineral floor close under the surface. Sometimes there is not. It is being made and unmade constantly down in the churn.

Do not let its size fool you. You are not visiting a tarn. You are visiting a piece of deep sea that has been misfiled in the sky.

### 2. Who should not go

Do not go if you have:

- Chronic sinus, inner-ear, or eustachian tube problems — you *will* need to clear your ears repeatedly if you enter the water, even wading deep enough for it to close over your head briefly
- A heart condition, arrhythmia, panic disorder triggered by vibration / loud sound, or respiratory disease that makes altitude and cold spray dangerous
- Bad knees, poor balance, or no experience on exposed, sharp rock
- Any expectation that you can swim, float, explore, fish, or "go see the bottom"

There is no bottom you can rely on. There is no swim. At best, there is a brief, roped, supervised entry at the edge.

Go with a local tender/guide if at all possible. The rope, pegs, platform, and ladle have been replaced and maintained generation after generation by people whose bodies know the rules. Hire that knowledge. If you are arriving by multiversal means, do **not** materialize directly inside the bowl or on the summit. Arrive well below, acclimatize, and climb up like everyone else. Teleporting straight from sea level to 4,000 meters *and* then dropping into deep-sea pressure is a good way to be incapacitated in the one place nobody can carry you out of easily.

### 3. Physical preparation, weeks before

**Altitude:** Spend at least 2-3 days ascending in stages. Sleep lower than your highest point of the day where you can. Learn your own altitude-sickness signs. On the final push you will be breathing hard in thin air, deafened, wet, and cold — a combination that masks early hypoxia.

**Ears and diving:** Practice gentle ear-clearing on land — Frenzel or soft Valsalva — until you can do it without force. Practice in a pool: descend only 2-3 meters and clear repeatedly. If you cannot clear reliably in a pool, plan to keep your head above water here, full stop. Never force a clear against pain in this place; the pressure escalation is not linear and not honest.

**Cold and grip:** Cold-water exposure training helps. So does scrambling with gloves on wet rock. Your hands will be wet, cold, and holding a hide-and-fiber rope over sharp stone.

**Sensory load:** The noise is not just loud, it is percussive and bone-conducted. People report their pulse being pulled off rhythm, hands shaking after an hour, and phantom crashing in quiet rooms for up to a week after a three-hour stay. Plan a hard time limit *before* you go in — see below — and have someone outside the slit keep it, because inside you will lose track of time and speech will not work to argue about it.

### 4. Gear

Pack for summit, sea-cliff, and dive simultaneously. Tether *everything*.

**Climb:**
- Broken-in high boots with aggressive soles, plus a spare pair of laces and gaiters. Expect cuts to leather. Bring a second pair of gloves — one pair *will* be sliced.
- Cut-resistant work gloves for the upper ridge, separate waterproof gloves for the rope descent.
- Helmet. The rock is still splitting as it rises. Loose, new slabs and falling grit are normal.
- Layered insulation plus a fully waterproof shell. Even in midsummer it smells of iron and snow, and inside the chamber it rains spray constantly. You will be soaked at altitude in wind on the way down if you don't have dry clothes sealed outside the slit.
- Sun protection for the approach, and a headlamp even for a noon visit — the slit itself is shadowed, spray kills visibility, and weather at 4,000m turns fast.

**Sound and communication:**
- Foam earplugs *plus* over-ear defenders if you can tolerate them on the rope. They will not stop the vibration in your chest and teeth, but they preserve some hearing.
- Pre-agreed hand signals with your partner: STOP, UP, DOWN, LINE TIGHT, I NEED OUT, TIDE SURGE. You will not be able to shout intelligibly inside. Write them on a card and rehearse.
- A simple waterproof timer/watch set to vibrate at 20 minutes and again at 30. That is your visit. Not an hour.

**In/near the water:**
- A climbing harness and your *own* modern rope/prusiks as backup. Inspect the traditional hide rope by hand along its whole reachable length, but do not replace or "improve" it unless the tenders ask you to — it is maintained that way for a reason, and metal hardware dropped here is gone for good. If you bring carabiners, use them clipped to you, tethered, and never over open water unclipped.
- A waist line, always. If you wade even to your knees, tie off to a peg or your partner on the platform. The undertow is described by locals as practiced, and they are not being poetic. It takes ankles first.
- Wetsuit or at minimum thermal base layer you don't mind being salt-soaked, plus neoprene socks. This is northern-tide cold in a windless bowl; hypothermia starts in minutes once you are wet and then have to climb down in thin air.
- No loose metal you need again. No loose anything. Cameras, tools, bottles: wrist tethers or leave them on the platform. A stone hammer dropped here falls for an uncomfortably long time. Yours will too. If you must bring instruments, prefer wood, plastic, copper, or stone — everything ever traditionally used here is antler, basalt, lashed wood, or hammered copper no thicker than a fingernail — and expect electronics to suffer from spray, salt, vibration, and inexplicable pressure effects if submerged.

**Supplies:**
- All your drinking water, carried up. Do not plan to live off the bowl. The water is salt, though a ladleful reportedly leaves a fresh, almost sweet aftertaste. If the tenders offer you a drink from the copper ladle, a small sip is the customary courtesy; do not fill bottles from it, do not move the ladle for any other purpose, and do not treat it as a freshwater source.
- Food, altitude medication you have tested before, basic first aid for cuts, ear pain, and altitude headache, and an emergency bivvy. There is no soil to pitch in and the lean-to outside the slit has collapsed and been rebuilt half a dozen times — do not count on shelter existing.
- A single smooth, water-worn pebble carried up from far below, if you want to follow local custom. People have carried incongruous pebbles up for cairns for a very long time. It is a better offering than anything you could build.

Leave behind: fishing gear, collecting jars, metal sampling tools you intend to drop, drones (the updraft through the eye and the spray will take them, and then the water will), and any plan to camp inside. The platform holds two people crouching. That's it.

### 5. The approach

Climb in the morning in stable weather. The rock holds almost no vegetation — a thumbprint of white moss in a lee, three blades of straw grass, dried-blood lichen only on the underside of stones. Do not step on what little lives there; it is holding on by turns and giving up by turns, and your boot will end the argument.

Follow the cairns if you see them, even though the path seems obvious. They mark the line that doesn't need marking — which usually means they mark the line where the rock is least likely to cut you or shed under you.

When speech starts to fail, stop trying. Switch to signals. When you can feel the roar in your teeth, slow down. Your breathing will start to feel like it is happening inside someone else's chest; that disorientation is normal here and is your cue to check your partner, your line, and your timer before you go any further.

Do not enter during a surge if you can time it. Watch from the slit if possible: every 40-50 minutes the surface sighs down a hand's breadth, then comes back higher, flooding the north platform and sucking at the rope. Time your descent down the rope for the calmer part of that cycle, and be off the rope before the next return.

### 6. Inside

Descend the notched slit hand over hand. The notches are polished dark by wet hands; they are shallow, not stairs, and they are slippery. Three points of contact. No jumping the last bit.

Once you reach the platform:

1. Clip/tie in immediately.
2. Stay low. Spray and breaking waves will hit you at knee height where you are standing.
3. Keep your visit to 20-30 minutes. Twenty is better for a first time. Set the person outside the slit — where they can still hear themselves think — as your official timekeeper.
4. Do not trust any pale surface under the water as a floor. It may be soft mineral skin hours old. Do not stand on it, do not push off it, do not aim for it.
5. If you enter the water, enter roped, backwards, and slowly, clearing your ears from the first moment your head goes near submersion. Expect pressure far beyond 3 meters of depth. If an ear will not clear, abort. A wave will submerge you briefly whether you plan it or not, so if you are not prepared to be underwater, stay on the platform and keep your entry to wet boots only.
6. Keep a hand on something fixed whenever the vortex stutters. The reprieve that lets the translucent shrimp-like things uncurl in the mid-depths and, very rarely, the long eyeless eel-thing show itself, lasts perhaps twenty seconds. Then the rhythm reasserts itself hard. Watch, don't reach. Do not try to catch, kill, or feed anything. There is never enough life here to take, and it tastes, by all accounts, of deep cold and copper anyway.
7. Leave the tripod and ladle alone except to drink if offered. It bangs against the wall adding a bright bell-note to the roar; trying to still it just puts your hands where spray and surge can trap them.
8. Expect rainbows on gold evenings and, if you are quiet in your own head under the noise, a ticking, popping, fizzing from below — new rock audibly growing. It is beautiful. It is also your reminder that the container is still being sealed around this water while you are inside it. Do not linger to listen.

No shouting for joy, no singing, no experiments dropping things to "see how deep it is." The bowl is a drum; your voice comes back as vibration. Save your throat and your ears.

### 7. Getting out and after

Climb out before your timer forces you. Your hands will be weaker and shakier than you expect, the rope will be wetter, and the tide surge can make the lower notches treacherous.

Outside, change into dry layers immediately, put in fresh earplugs or simply get distance until normal conversation is possible at normal volume, and check each other: ear pain, nosebleed, headache, nausea, unsteady pulse, hands still trembling after 20 minutes. Descend at least several hundred meters before you rest for the night. Do not sleep on the summit fold; rock is still moving there and the sound carries.

For the next few days, expect ringing, phantom crashing in quiet rooms, vivid dreams of pressure, and a strange aftertaste of salt and sweetness. Rest your ears — no loud music, no diving, no flying/multiversal hops involving pressure changes for at least 24-48 hours if you submerged your ears. If ear pain persists, or hearing stays muffled beyond a day, get checked for barotrauma; you have, physiologically, been on a dive, whatever the altitude said.

### 8. Etiquette that is really safety

- Do not stay long.
- Do not trust the shallow appearance.
- Do not drop what you need.
- Do not take life from it.
- Do not move the tenders' marks — the wandering waterline notches, the cairns, the pegs, the ladle — to make them "better" or straighter. Their failure to impose order is information about the place.
- Leave nothing except, if you wish, your carried pebble on a cairn outside. Take out every scrap of modern material you brought in; salt water here preserves nothing kindly and the depth disposes of nothing honestly.

If you prepare for a summit, an ocean, and a dive — and give the place half an hour instead of a day — you have a chance to come down with the memory intact: a cup of sea held at the top of the world, bright as a courtyard at noon, loud as a foundry, cold as a northern tide, furiously empty and furiously full, still trying to get its bearings while the young mountain grows shut around it.

That is quite enough.
