This is not a ruins site, and it's not a wilderness in the normal sense. It's a working landscape whose working parts have dissolved into the water, air, and soil.

Prepare for it the way you would prepare for a cold tidal marsh, a white-noise factory floor, and a low-grade spatial anomaly all at once. The danger is not one dramatic event. It's cumulative disorientation, damp, bad footing, and trusting your normal senses in a place where local ecology has already learned not to.

An ordinary human can walk it. You just cannot walk it by ordinary rules.

### 1. Understand what you're entering

The basin is about 20 km across, rimmed by old, weeping hills. Inside: peat, reed beds, alder thickets, black pools, channels, and knee-to-waist mist under a permanent pearl-grey cloud deck.

The old climate/hydrology lattice is not gone. It is *everywhere* - nanoscopic actuators in the soil water, membranes and fields in the air, suspended material in the pools. It has been malfunctioning for so long that plants and fungi have evolved around it. Expect:

- Water flowing uphill, in rectangular channels with no banks, or falling up from pools into the clouds
- Reflections that are almost correct: no mist in them, fast clouds, sunlight that isn't in the real sky, your reflection finishing a gesture early or late
- Distance that changes with humidity - a reed bed can be 3 steps or 10 minutes away
- Gravity that pools and drifts slowly through the valley during the day, like invisible shallow basins of extra pull. Your inner ear, loose hair, hanging cloth, and water will agree on a slope that your eyes deny
- Air that folds. Sound, especially your own voice, can come back subtly altered when this happens
- A continuous low roar from underground streams, amplified through peat and hollow reeds. You will feel it in your teeth. You have to shout at arm's length.

This is settled, not actively collapsing. Do not try to fix, sample aggressively, or "trigger" it. The fungal mats under the peat that pulse when the pools flash may *be* the lattice now, partly eaten and partly incorporated.

### 2. Team, timing, and limits

Do not go alone. Minimum three: one to navigate, one to watch footing/time, one to watch the other two for confusion. People get quietly wrong here before they notice it themselves.

Plan short incursions from the rim, not a heroic traverse on day one. Distance is not reliable, so time is not reliable. Set a hard turnaround time by a mechanical watch and obey it even if your goal looks 50 meters away.

Best entry is what passes for morning, when the cloud lightens from slate to pearl. By evening cool air slides off the hills, mist rises to meet it, channels reverse, up-rain columns form and dissolve, and the booming drops half an octave. Night is not dark or quiet - pools hold faint light longer, mist beads tremble in unison on every surface, and it's easier to get lost. Don't plan to navigate out after dark unless you have a physical tether line back to the rim.

Leave a full itinerary outside the basin, with an overdue trigger. Rescue by straight-line search will not work well in here.

### 3. Clothing and personal gear

You will be damp within minutes and will stay damp. It's not sharp arctic cold, it's a deep penetrating cool - breath shows, cloth never dries. Hypothermia and trench foot are your most mundane serious risks.

- **No cotton.** Synthetic or wool base layers, sealed-seam waterproof shell, and proper marsh waders or waterproof boots with gaiters. Peat will try to keep a boot.
- **Layers you can vent.** You'll be working hard in bog, then standing still while you wait for air to unfold or a gravity pool to drift past.
- **Gloves, hat, spare socks in dry bags - plural.** Change socks on schedule, not when you feel like it.
- **Eye protection.** Mist droplets carry charge and fine suspension. It won't melt you, but long exposure irritates eyes and lungs, and pools flash like an eye opening when light catches them.
- **Respirator or at least a fitted filter mask for long stays.** The mist is instructed water. Short visits with a mask you can pull up in thick mist-walls are wiser than breathing it for 12 hours because locals-who-don't-exist would.
- **Ear protection with a communication plan.** The roar is constant and loud at conversational distance. Bring foam plugs for rest periods, and agree on hand signals, touch signals, and written slate/pencil communication *before* you enter. Bone-conduction headsets sometimes help, sometimes pick up the ground hum worse - test at the rim. Do not rely on shouting instructions in an emergency.
- **Headlamp with a very diffuse, low mode.** A bright beam just lights mist white and kills your ability to see reflected light off pools and sedges, which is what local life uses. Red light is less useful than low, wide, dim white.

Seal all electronics in waterproof cases with desiccant. Fields that tune refractive index, surface tension, and local inertia are not kind to fine sensors, touchscreens, drones, GPS, or inertial navigation. Bring them if you want, assume they will lie politely. Bring analog backups: mechanical watch, paper notebook, pencil, compass - and expect even compass behavior to be suspect near concentrated lattice activity. Your most reliable instruments are low-tech: dropped pebbles, a plumb line, a strip of loose cloth, a small bottle of water you can watch slosh.

### 4. Navigation - learn the local rules

Forget maps as truth. A map of channels and pools will be wrong by lunch; a peat bank can slump a hand's breadth and open a new pool already complete with reflection.

Use these instead:

1. **Go where the reeds lean away.** They lean away from drifting weight. That is your best gravity compass.
2. **Never navigate toward something you see in a pool.** Reflections have different weather and slightly different timing. Navigate to the real thicket, not its double.
3. **Trust downhill in your body over downhill in your eyes.** If hair, cloth, water, and inner ear vote together for a slope, step as if that slope is real. Expect pebbles to roll, hesitate, and roll back in bad spots - test uncertain ground with a pebble toss and a weighted pole.
4. **Stop when your voice comes back altered.** That means the air ahead is folding. Do not push through talking loudly to "stay in contact." Sit, tether together, wait the few minutes it takes to unfold. Continuing during a fold is how groups get separated by a distance that wasn't there.
5. **Loud ground is living ground. Never step into perfectly silent water.** Silence means the lattice is concentrating under the surface, doing too much to spare any for sound. Back away, mark it with a reed bundle on *dry-ish* peat, go around widely.
6. **Walk the roar.** Pitch changes matter. Where fungal mats are thick the booming changes pitch. A sudden lowering, a patterned undertone - five beats, pause, five beats - or reeds shivering with no wind on your face is a cue to stop, get together, and observe, not to investigate the source.

Keep a physical breadcrumb system that can't be reflected or drifted: a tether line from the rim for first forays, or bright, anchored markers driven deep into alder root-knots, not just stuck in peat. Photographs help less than you think; the mist in the photo may not match your memory.

Test each step with a pole. Alder roots knot up out of water like hands, sedge fans interlock, moss covers turtles, fallen wood, and holes with the same green. Nothing that looks solid is guaranteed to be at the depth it appears.

### 5. Water, food, and exposure

Do not drink the water. Do not fill bottles from pools, even still clear ones, even after filtering for particulates. It's black, iron-rich, peat water with a functional suspension in it. Bring all drinking water in sealed containers from outside. Same for washing wounds - use your own supply.

Do not wade bare-skinned if you can avoid it, and cover cuts before entry. If you fall in a pool - and assume someone eventually will - get out, rinse with clean water, change base layer, watch for unusual tingling, vertigo, or delayed/precocious sense of movement for the next few hours.

Eat sealed food. Humidity gets into everything, peat gets onto everything. Eat on schedule; cold damp suppresses appetite and judgment at the same time.

After exit: full decontamination at the rim. Rinse boots, waders, poles. Bag peat and seeds separately - sedges and fungal threads belong to a closed engineered ecology, and other worlds do not need it in their drains. Do not bring home a charming bottle of luminous pool water. It is not a souvenir, it is active medium. Shower, change, and keep worn gear quarantined until dry and inspected. If respiratory irritation, persistent vertigo, or sleep disturbance lasts more than a day, log duration and pattern of exposure and seek medical review with this description in hand.

### 6. Wildlife and invisible events

You will see very little fauna, and what you see is instructive.

The ash-grey wading birds lift each foot high and test whether it's landing in the same day as the last step. Imitate their caution literally.

Eel-like things can surface where their tail does not quite connect. Don't reach for animals. Don't approach moss-backed turtles - they are slow, not safe handholds.

Once or twice a day something unseen crosses at great speed - a displaced pressure wave, mist tearing, reeds flattened in a line. The birds freeze. You freeze too. Drop low in sedge if you're caught in the line, stay tethered, don't run for a landmark. Wait until the sound decays back into the general roar.

Nothing nests where gravity pools drift. Take the hint: don't make camp, don't leave gear sitting still for long, don't sleep on open peat. Moss covers anything that holds still for a season, pools can open under saturated banks, and sleeping flat puts your inner ear and circulation in a drifting gravity basin for hours. If you must overnight, use a raised platform or hammocks securely tied between living alders above the water line, three points of contact, each person tethered, watches rotated. Expect to wake damp, disoriented, and unsure which direction is downhill. Re-orient with plumb line and reeds before anyone unties.

### 7. Psychological preparation

This place reliably produces derealization if you fight it.

Your reflection will move wrong. Sunlight will cross water that never crosses the sky. A view will take too long to reach. Your voice will be handed back to you slightly wrong. That is not a personal haunting and it is not a message for you.

Prepare by:

- Narrating facts out loud in short bursts when safe: "Pool on left, loud. Reeds leaning east. Turnaround in 40 minutes." It anchors the team to shared observations.
- Keeping a timed paper log. When memory disagrees, trust the log with timestamps, not the vivid impression.
- Calling a stop for any member who starts following a reflection, arguing with a slope their body feels, or laughing about how close something "obviously" is. Those are early navigation failures, not jokes.
- Not interpreting patterns. The lattice may pulse east to west like a thought across a mind. It is still a landscape doing old bookkeeping. Anthropomorphizing it leads people to wait for permission, or follow it.

### 8. Abort signals

Turn back via your tether/markers - not by shortcut - if any of these happen:

- Silence spreads around you instead of staying in one pool
- Your plumb line, water bottle, and inner ear start disagreeing with each other wildly
- Reflections stop lagging and start leading consistently by more than a second
- A mist wall thickens enough to lean on and your voices alter on both sides of it
- The roar drops out under your feet, or the patterned undertone repeats and does not decay
- Anyone cannot tell, after rest, which way the reeds are leaning

Leave dropped gear. The basin will moss it, incorporate it, or pool under it. It is not worth retrieving in a fold.

---

In short: dress for a cold swamp that will never dry, equip for a loud industrial site, navigate like a wading bird, and think like a sailor in shifting currents - except the currents are in water, air, distance, and weight itself.

Go in slow, loud-ground only, tethered, timed, and prepared to wait doing nothing while the valley finishes a calculation. That patience is the main piece of safety equipment.
