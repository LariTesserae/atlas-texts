This is not a lake district. It is a young, high-altitude ocean sitting on top of an unfinished volcanic plateau, warm as blood, filled with a drowned forest that has decided not to die.

You will not visit it like a beach, a hike, or a dive site. It is all three badly combined, at altitude, on knife-edged rock, in water so plant-choked that swimming, walking, and getting lost become almost the same activity. Prepare for slowness, sharpness, warmth below and cold above, and almost total ecological emptiness.

### 1. Your body first: altitude is the gatekeeper

Far below is a plain of cloud. You will be living above it.

The air up here is thin, dry, bright, and windy. The water is warm. That contrast is the central physical fact of the place. You can be perfectly comfortable submerged to the neck and be getting wind-chilled, sunburned, and altitude-exhausted on your scalp at the same time.

Before you go:

* **Acclimatize as for high mountains.** Arrive expecting headaches, broken sleep, breathlessness on trivial exertion, and stupid decision-making for the first 2-4 days. Do not plan to swim passes or bottom-walk on day one. If your portal / drop point can be staged at intermediate altitude, do it.
* **Train for long, slow endurance, not speed.** Weeks of gentle swimming and wading, hauling yourself onto rock, pushing through stems. At altitude, sprint capacity will desert you. Panic is your main drowning risk.
* **Get freshwater and altitude diving instruction, even if you never intend to "dive."** You will spend whole days with your head under, walking the drowned forest floor at 10-20 feet. Altitude changes your decompression and buoyancy maths, the water is warm enough to make you forget exertion, and the surface is often hidden by leaves, floating mats, and trapped debris. Shallow does not mean safe. Learn to navigate up when up is covered in a ceiling of rotting leaves.
* **Sun protection is serious.** Thin air, hard noon light, reflection off water and white travertine. You will burn at the waterline — neck, ears, scalp, backs of hands — while feeling cool. Wide hat that can get wet, reef-safe mineral sunscreen reapplied constantly, light long sleeves for above-water travel.
* **Expect prolonged warm-water exposure problems.** Blood-temperature water for hours softens skin until basalt and silica-grass shred it, encourages ear infections, and masks dehydration. You will sweat into the lake and not notice. Drink on a schedule, use ear drops / drying routine nightly, and get out to dry completely every day.

### 2. Sharpness: nothing here has been worn down yet

Erosion has not had time to help you. Basalt fins go straight into the water, faceted and split. Obsidian rubble lies underwater in angular fans, perfectly visible and perfectly lethal to bare feet. The plants themselves are mineralized: grass blades trap bubbles and cut, reed stems are like bundles of green glass rods, horsetail groves are hollow and rigid.

Do not bring beach gear. Bring armor for incidental contact.

* Hard-soled, closed water boots with ankle support — never sandals, never barefoot, even in shallows that look like glass. Obsidian cuts are deep, clean, and bleed freely in warm water.
* Shin guards, knee pads, and tough gloves. You will be pushing between stems that are nearly touching, bracing on trunks furred in algae, climbing over submerged passes.
* A full thin wetsuit or dive skin is useful not for cold — you will overheat in a thick suit — but as a cut suit. 1-2mm, reinforced knees/forearms, worn even when snorkeling at the surface.
* Serious wound kit: irrigation, closure strips, antiseptic, pressure dressings. A small cut here stays wet, warm, silicified and dirty. Clean it when you get out, every time.
* Land shoes for islands as well. The mountaintop islands are not beaches, they are emergent ridges. Landings mean scrambling from water directly onto wet, knife-edged rock.

Walk everywhere as if the bottom drops without warning. It does. Shallows are clear to ten feet, then craters turn green-black and sudden. A meadow can end in a drowned valley.

### 3. Moving: swim the passes, walk the bottom, do not expect to boat

There are no shores to launch from, no open water free of vegetation, and no current to carry you. Currents move meters per day. A rockfall wave at one end of a crater will arrive the next morning as a long, breathing swell.

Your options:

**Surface swimming** — Best for distance between valleys. You move through storey after storey of lily discs, floating leaf rafts around tree waists, reed beds clicking in water that should be still. Wear a mask even on the surface; you need to see submerged trunks and vines before they are at your throat. A tow float in a bright color helps a partner find you in green discs, but snags constantly. Agree on a snag protocol: stop, back up, never thrash.

**Bottom walking** — In shallower groves, with a snorkel or light scuba / surface-supplied air, you can walk the old forest floor in warm dimness, pushing gently between trees. It is silent enough that your heartbeat feels loud. This is magical and disorienting. Vines descend from aerial branches, root underwater, and re-ascend. Lily stalks rise in steps. Leaves fall *upward* and collect under the surface. Bubbles lodge in the canopy for weeks and grow green skins, so looking up you see a silver, inverted meadow that is not fruit and not the surface.

Never bottom-walk alone, never under a floating mat without a marked exit hole, and always run a line if visibility will close. Mats can be woven so tight rain pools on them an inch deep — a second, tiny lake above the real one. They will not reliably hold you, and from underneath they look like solid ground. People drown under much thinner mats on Earth.

**Climbing out** — Every exit is a scramble. Bring a short rope, gloves, and a way to haul packs in dry bags that float but don't blow away. There is nowhere to beach a boat, and inflatables will be punctured by basalt, obsidian, and silica reeds within days. If you bring a craft, make it a tough packraft for open crater centers only, and expect to portage it like a suitcase.

Navigation: Compasses help; landmarks betray. At late afternoon the water mirrors the sky so exactly that basalt islands appear to float, completed below by their reflections. At noon, underwater light is green-gold; by depth the forest is already in dusk. Mountaintop islands are your only fixed points. Mark passes by emergent tree crowns, white travertine terraces, and particular ridge shapes — photographed from both above and below water. GPS, if your multiverse kit allows it, is worth it; plant growth can close a channel you swam last week.

### 4. Heat from below

This sea is warm because the mountain is still making itself. Sun-warmed shallows, geothermal seeps, and hot springs building chalk-white travertine terraces outward in real time.

* Travertine at the rim is soft as wet plaster. It will collapse under a boot and can be scalding just underneath. Test terraces with a pole, walk only on hardened, older white stone behind the rim, and never camp on a terrace — the plateau tilts, levees build, pools rise by inches a season locally, faster near a new spring.
* Vents release bubbles that grow under leaves and detach with a soft *bloop*. A stream of large, accelerating bubbles, milky water, or a shimmering column means water well above blood temperature nearby. Do not swim over vents you haven't sounded from the side. Scalds at altitude heal poorly.
* At night, listen for ticking cooling rock and distant rockfalls. Keep camp away from steep dry ridges. Earth tremors may be minor and still send a slow swell through linked lakes or slump an obsidian fan.

### 5. Water, food, and shelter: bring almost everything

Animals are rare to the point of eeriness. A single ash-grey wading bird picking its way over travertine. A pale lunged thing, forearm-long, surfacing under the lilies maybe once an hour to roll an eye above water. Transparent crustaceans grazing algae. No herds, no flocks, no insect hum, no fishery.

Do not plan to live off this place.

* **Food:** Pack all calories. The orange fruit ripening underwater, splitting to release sinking seeds in mucilage parachutes, is unpicked for a reason — you don't know its chemistry, it is loaded with silica/minerals like everything else, and removing it damages a system with almost no dispersers. Treat all local plants as inedible unless exhaustively tested.
* **Drinking water:** Lake water is mineral-rich, warm, algae-rich, and near springs potentially sulfurous, iron-heavy, or dangerously hot. It will not refresh you safely straight. Filter and purify, or better, collect rain. Rain here arrives strangely: cloud drifts level through the crowns, droplets thicken until air and lake merge, and for an hour you are standing submerged in wet air. Set out clean catchment during those events — that is your cleanest water. The inch-deep pools on floating mats are full of plantlets and microorganisms; filter them too.
* **Camp:** There is almost nowhere flat, dry, soft, and safe. Options ranked: (1) a high basalt island, with a sleeping pad thick enough to insulate from faceted rock and a tent that can take dry wind above and constant humidity below; (2) a hammock slung between emergent trunks — check that the tree has both its aerial and submerged canopies healthy and is not newly drowning; (3) a floating platform you bring and anchor carefully away from exits. Never camp near a waterfall lip. At the rim, water pours over basalt and disassembles to mist halfway down, feeding the clouds below — the current near the lip is deceptively slow until it isn't, and there is no downstream to swim to.
* **Dryness is maintenance.** Above water, wind dries quickly. Below and in cloud, nothing dries. Bring clothing in sealed bags, a dry set reserved only for sleep, and accept that gear will grow algae films if left in the water. Metal corrodes, natural fibers rot. Rinse volcanic grit off zips daily.

### 6. Weather is a change of state, not an event

You will not always hear rain start. Humidity builds, cloud sinks to surface level, droplets become too heavy to suspend, and visibility falls to your knees. In that state you cannot tell cloud from lake, up-current from down, trail from channel.

When it thickens: stop moving. Sit with your feet in clear water below the murk, mark your position, wait. Lightning at this altitude on wet isolated ridges is a real risk, and so is getting turned around in a reed saddle in five minutes.

Evenings are your best travel window for orientation — level amber light, emergent crowns glowing, water and sky separating again — but underwater dusk comes early. Plan to be out, or at a known exit, before that green dark closes.

Nights will be cold in the air, steaming on the water, and profoundly quiet. Dripping trees, a distant rock tick, a bubble working loose. Earplugs won't be needed. A good headlamp with a green/red mode helps preserve underwater vision and doesn't blind a partner at the mirrored surface.

### 7. The most important rule: do not seed this place

This is a perfect, unfilled habitat. Hollows in silt where nothing has nested. Feeding grounds no mouth has found. Plants already putting out dark water-leaves *above* the current surface, on speculation for a flood their grandchildren will see.

An ordinary traveler is an invasion vector.

Decontaminate boots, suits, packs, and boats before arrival — no soil, seeds, insect eggs, snails, microbes from other lakes. Do not release aquarium water, bait, food scraps, or grey water into the sea. Pack out everything, including human waste from island camps; the mineral water preserves more than it breaks down, and there is almost no fauna to process it. Do not plant, feed, transplant, or "help." Your restraint is the difference between visiting a world being made from the top down, and accidentally finishing it with Earth weeds.

### 8. A minimal pack list

Altitude medicine & sun kit; 1-2mm cut suit; hard water boots, shin/knee protection, gloves; mask/snorkel + light altitude-rated scuba if trained; compass, waterproof maps/photos, line reel; dry bags, rope; packraft optional; tent/hammock + thick pad on an island; rain catchment + filter/purifier; all food; wound/ear infection kit; pole for testing travertine and depths; headlamp; sealed dry sleep clothes; decontamination brush.

### 9. How to be there

Go slow enough to be recalibrated. Currents here travel meters per day; fruit takes an afternoon to sink ten feet; leaves open and close over days. Weeks to cross flooded valleys is normal. A single doubled call late in the day — low, from across a crater — will make the whole plateau feel attentive because there is nothing to compete with it.

Hang at the boundary sometimes. Face at the surface, scalp in thin dry wind, ears in warm motionless water, looking down over a forest floor you can swim above. That is the place teaching you its logic: the bottom is a forest, the surface is a ceiling, leaves fall up, bubbles stay.

Prepare as mountaineer, diver, and swamp-traveler at once, carry everything, cut nothing, introduce nothing, and leave the emptiness empty.
