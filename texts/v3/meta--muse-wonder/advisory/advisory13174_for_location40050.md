At the top of the mountain there is a sea, and the first thing to prepare for is that almost nothing about getting ready for a mountain, or for a sea, will quite prepare you for both at once.

You are going high, into cloud, to a warm lake. You are going to be wet permanently, in dim light permanently, at altitude. Your body, your clothes, your plans for the day, and your sense of how fast a conversation should happen will all have to adjust.

### Before You Go: Train for Slow Water, Not Rough Water

This is not a place of storms, currents, or cold shock. It is a place of entanglement, heat, depth, and waiting.

Learn to swim well, but learn to swim *slowly*. Panicked thrashing will get you wrapped in copper-green kelp in the drowned streets, and the kelp does not need to be fast to be strong. Practice floating, treading water with minimal movement, duck-diving and rising calmly, and freeing a leg by hand without kicking.

Practice breath control, but do not expect modern diving. The local divers go down weighted with a net of stones, breathing through a reed tube tended at the surface by a child on a float. There is no redundancy. If you cannot stay unhurried at depth, with low visibility and sound arriving as a thud in your teeth, do not go below the first drowned storeys. Ordinary lungs are welcome here, but only within ordinary limits.

Get basically fit for a steep volcanic climb. The cone is young, black, sharp where lava folded, and uncarved by rivers or proper paths. The ascent outside is the hardest physical part of the visit. Inside, almost all exertion is poling a boat, wading, hauling baskets, and climbing ladders between levels of a house.

Acclimatize to altitude if you can. The caldera rim sits *in* the weather. Exactly how high will vary by world-shadow, but expect thin air on the climb, wind, and abrupt cold in cloud — followed, once you cross the lowest pass, by bath-warm, saturated air that feels like breathing inside a greenhouse. Give yourself a day on the rim if your head aches, your sleep goes strange, or you are panting out of proportion. Do not descend straight to swimming and diving.

### What to Pack, and What to Leave Behind

Pack for rot. Everything will be damp within hours and never fully dry again.

Bring:
- **Two or three sets of light, quick-drying clothes only.** One to wear wet, one to wear damp, one sealed away for the climb out. Wool that stays tolerable when wet, loose woven synthetics, or whatever your home world has that does not chafe, mildew in a day, or hold a kilogram of water. Cotton will live sodden against your skin. Leather will soften, grow, and fail.
- **Waterproofing that is itself waterproof.** Sealed dry bags, resin-coated pouches, waxed wraps inside other wraps. A merely “water-resistant” pack will become a bag of warm lake.
- **Footwear for razor stone and ankle-deep causeways.** Sturdy sandals or soft shoes with thick, grippy soles you can swim and wade in. The basalt teeth on the rim, new glassy stone near the vents, pumice blocks, slick wet logs — all will cut bare feet, and a cut in warm mineral water is not a trivial thing. Bring a second pair. Mooring posts sprout here; your shoes will try to become gardens.
- **A hammock and a light sleeping mat that floats or drains.** You may be offered a bed on a raft-house floor, a tower room, or, in the hot season, a hammock strung in a half-drowned lower storey just above the internal waterline. That is not an insult; it is the coolest, most comfortable sleeping place in town. Sleep with your sealed bag hung above the thermal tide line.
- **A blunt, non-metal knife if you must have one, and otherwise stone/bone/wood tools.** There is very little metal in town — a few inherited bronze knives, cold-hammered and precious. Metal is too rare, and too loud. Do not arrive clanking with buckles, carabiners, bells, zips, and cookware to show off. You will sound violent in a place that prizes softness, and you will make yourself a target of envy or theft without meaning to. Replace toggles with wood, cord, and knots where you can.
- **Ear plugs for sleeping if you need them, ear care for living.** You will have water in your ears daily. Bring whatever your healer recommends for preventing swimmer’s ear, and dry your ears gently and often. Warm, mineral-rich, plant-thick water loves an ear canal.
- **Trade goods that are useful, quiet, and non-invasive:** good cordage fibre, hardwood, shell, resin, salt if they will accept it, worked stone, waterproof cloth, stories, skills. Hands are the best currency. Help pole, weave, carry stone baskets from the rim, tend roof gardens, carry air bladders down to the temple. That is remembered longer than objects.

Leave behind, or seal away and never use:
- **Candles, lighters, and anything that needs flame below water.** They will not burn in the underwater rooms, and fire on reed-and-palm houses that lean eaves-to-wall is a town-ending risk. Light below is living light: jars of pale phosphorescent algae from the roof gardens. At night they are hung along the canals, green and faint. Bring an eye that can adjust, not a torch to ruin everyone else’s.
- **Sunglasses for the town itself.** You may want them on the bright climb. Inside, light arrives dimmed twice — cloud above, steam above warm water — silver-grey, late-afternoon even at noon, no shadows with edges. Below the surface it goes green, then bottle-brown, then warm darkness. Glare is not the problem. Depth misjudgment is. The water is so clear, with almost no sediment, that the drowned town twenty metres down can look disarmingly close on a still day. It is not.
- **Any live plant, seed, insect, or animal from home, however harmless.** This ecology is sparse and strange: one pale, nearly blind forearm-length fish, small brown roof frogs that call like a fingertip on wet glass after heavy rain, one tall grey wading bird. No biting insects, because the water discourages them. Do not be the traveller who imports the first biting fly, the first fast weed, a fish disease. Clean your gear before the rim pass. Eat what is offered rather than planting what you brought.
- **A rigid schedule.** Markets open when the thermal tide is low and the pumice causeways are up, and close when the water covers them. A conversation begun across a canal in the morning may pause for the whole midday and conclude in the evening, and no one will think this odd.

### Getting In

You enter the way everyone enters: over the lowest pass in the rim, through cloud.

Dress for cold wind on the black flank, then expect to strip layers at the pass. Visibility can drop to a few metres; the rim is broken basalt teeth, eight kilometres of crater lip holding back a lake that comes to within a few metres of spilling. Stay on the worn line, go with a local guide if one will take you, and do not wander the rim “for the view” in cloud. There is no view, only a sudden awareness of which side falls outward and which side falls to water.

Once inside, hire or borrow a boat and a poler for your first days. Boats are poled, never rowed hard. Canals are one boat wide. Houses, bridges, chicken runs, trellises, and gardens are all continuous with one another; what looks like a shortcut is someone’s roof, someone’s bean frame, someone’s floating island that took ten years to become land. Elders can tell you whose abandoned garden each southern island was. Treat every mat as property, and every surface as possibly alive, possibly someone’s floor.

### Living With the Thermal Tide

The tide is not lunar. Twice a day the caldera floor breathes heat in a long pulse, the water expands and cools, and the whole lake rises and falls by about a handspan.

That handspan runs the town. Doors, steps, walkways, and moorings are built for it. Learn, each morning, which causeway will be ankle-deep by evening and which will be dry. Ask. Watch where people hang baskets, where they move chickens, where a bridge becomes impassable without anyone commenting. Tie your boat with slack. Hang anything you care about well above the high line — and then check what “high” means in that particular house, because internal waterlines in the drowned lower rooms are their own geography.

It does not get cold at night. The cloud holds the heat. It does not get bright at noon. Your circadian rhythm may drift; use meals, market low-tides, and the hanging-out of algae jars at dusk as your clock.

Rain will not wake you. Falling through saturated air onto saturated water, it stipples rather than drums, almost soundless. Steam rises in the cooler mornings. The smell — faint sulphur, wet stone, crushed leaves, iron — will get into everything you own. Accept this. By day three, you will smell like a local and stop noticing until you leave.

### The Underwater Town

Do not treat the drowned storeys as ruins. They are cisterns, fish traps, cool storage reached by ladder through a hole in the floorboards, sleeping rooms, and, in the temple, holy space.

Go down first with an experienced diver, in daylight-through-water terms: early, when the green is clearest. Know the hand signs and wall-tapping patterns *before* you descend, because voices do not descend at all. A wooden clapper on a hollow log is a soft internal thud felt in the teeth. If you cannot communicate silently, you are deaf and mute below and must behave accordingly — stay close, touch nothing without invitation, leave when tapped to leave.

In the submerged temple, air is kept in great inverted clay bells hung from the ceiling, wider than a person. You tip your head up inside and breathe air that tastes of green plants and other people’s lungs. It is replenished daily with bladders carried down — animal skins, sealed seed-pods. That air is labour. Do not waste it talking for pleasure, do not take a bell to yourself, and offer to help carry the next load down. Prayers are done in chest-deep water and thin green algae-light because the ancestors are held to live at the depth where they died. Guests should be quiet, bare of metal, willing to be wet to the neck, and content to find a face by phosphorescence rather than see a room clearly.

Never enter a fully underwater room alone because it looks “kept.” Many are kept on purpose. Some are storage. Some are shut for reasons no one owes you. Nothing in this town is ever demolished, only added to, tied alongside, floated off, or surrendered storey by storey. That includes doors that now open under water.

### Real Dangers, In Order of Likelihood

1. **The hot places.** Across most of the lake the water is body-warm and lovely to trail a hand in. Over the vents in the depths it is hot enough to cook in. Those places are marked by strings of wooden buoys. Buoys are not decoration and not a suggestion. Do not swim across a string, do not dive “just to look,” do not follow the patient thread of bubbles rising from the centre. Near the middle, where the drowned temple streets converge, the floor is doming — a black cone a few metres high, growing by millimetres, glassy, razor-edged, filmed iridescent, with only a white bacterial mat rippling on it. In a few hundred years it will break the surface. Keep off it entirely. It is measured at new moon with knotted cords by people who know how close is close enough.

2. **Kelp and lily roots.** The kelp forests the drop-offs around the drowned streets sway slower than a sleeping breath and tangle with unhurried strength. Lily leaves broad enough to briefly hold a giggling child crowd the canals; their roots grope into houses. If you feel a pull, stop. Signal. Work the stem down to your ankle with your hands. Never let a boat pole push you deeper into a bed to save time.

3. **Infection and slow softening.** Warm water all day softens skin, opens small cuts, invites ear and foot trouble. Cover broken skin before swimming, rinse in the cleanest water available, dry what you can, watch for heat and redness. The sulphur-and-iron smell is faint and normal; a sudden strong rotten-egg reek, unusual bubbling, or a rapid change in water temperature under your boat is not — pole away and tell someone.

4. **Falling between things.** Walkways are lashed logs, bridges are house extensions, houses lean together. Everything moves a little, sinks a little at high thermal tide, and is slick with moss and steam-condensation. Look where the next foot goes, especially carrying baskets hand-to-hand across gaps, which is how goods move here.

5. **The mountain itself.** It has not finished being born. Tremors, new bubble streams, cloud that suddenly smells stronger, birds abandoning the shallows — take your cue from locals instantly and without debate. Your exit is that one low pass through cloud. Know where your sealed climbing clothes are at all times, even weeks into a stay. If the rim ever broke, the sea would fall; it does not break, and people live calmly on that fact, but a traveller should know the path uphill the way sailors know lifeboats: unworried, and exactly.

### Etiquette That Matters

Be quiet. Metal is not rung here, voices do not carry in water, rain makes almost no sound, children are hushed for the grey wading bird at dusk. A loud arrival stays loud in memory long after you have learned to pole gently.

Do not eat, touch, or chase the pale fish. Seeing one is good luck; eating one is forbidden, and there are very few. Do not “helpfully” clear vegetation: mats drift a few metres a day and are poled back as a normal chore, but an untended mat this year is footing, in ten years an island. Ask before you cut, move, tie, or stand.

Contribute labour in the rhythm it is offered. Carry stone down from the rim in baskets. Weave reed while it is wet. Tend taro in its submerged beds, beans climbing straight out of the water, fruit trees in pumice barrels with roots dangling into the lake. Construction is constant and all hand-work — stone wedges and rope, reed cut and dried on roofs, tools of stone, bone, shell, fire-hardened wood. A traveller who expects to be served in a town that is personally holding itself above water, generation by generation, will be politely starved of welcome.

Eat what grows in wet warmth: taro, beans, fruit, garden greens, stored things from cool drowned rooms. Drink what locals drink, where they draw it — lake water is clear and mineral and not every part of it is for every use, and houses have their own cistern logic in their drowned ground floors. Follow the house, not your assumptions.

### Leaving

Plan to leave at a low thermal tide, in the steadiest part of the day, with your sealed dry set saved for the rim. You will go up through cloud from steam-warm dimness into wind that feels shocking, down black slopes that have never known a river, smelling of sulphur and iron and crushed leaves.

Give yourself a day afterward of bright light, dry air, and loud ordinary sounds before you judge how you feel. Many travellers find the return of hard shadows, cold nights, demolished buildings, and conversations that are expected to finish in one sitting stranger than anything in the caldera.

Go prepared to be wet, slow, quiet, and useful. Go prepared to live in a town that began at the bottom of a dry bowl, rose a hand’s breadth a year with the water on its own floors, and simply never left — building upward, floating sideways, praying underwater, and gardening on land that may one day drain away over the slopes of a mountain still being born beneath it.
