Ilvarra will not hurt you the way most strange places hurt a traveler. It will not starve you, freeze you, chase you, or surprise you. Its danger is gentler and, for an ordinary human body, more disorienting:

It will never give you evening.

Prepare not for an emergency, but for the slow loss of all the cues you normally use to know where you are in a day, in a week, in your own life. In Ilvarra, you have to manufacture those cues yourself — politely, quietly, and mostly indoors.

### 1. Before the pass: decide how long you are *really* staying

Do this outside the valley, in a place where clocks and sunsets still agree with each other.

You will lose track in Ilvarra. Not because you become foolish, but because there is nothing to count. No dawn to wake after, no dusk to work toward, no weather front, no harvest turning, no worn stair getting more worn, no barrel filling or emptying. A household will feed you "when the household eats" and offer you sleep "when your shift sleeps," and after a while that will feel completely sufficient.

So before you climb:

* **Set an external extraction date.** Tell someone on the other side of the pass, or your transport, or a companion who is *not* entering, to come back for you on a specific date. Leave it written down outside the valley. Inside, "three more days" has a way of becoming an aesthetic preference rather than a plan.
* **Keep your own count.** Bring a paper journal and a pencil — paper gets damp, but it works — and make one page per sleep. Not per "day." Per sleep. Mark it *before* you sleep, while you still remember if you've slept. A mechanical watch or a silent vibrating timer helps for the first few sleeps, but don't rely on looking at it like a talisman. The locals have no clocks, nothing that ticks or combusts, and a glowing screen checked obsessively at a communal table will mark you as exactly what you are: a person waiting for the light to do something.
* **Plan for circadian collapse in advance.** You *will* get weepy, wired, nauseated, euphoric, suddenly exhausted, or suddenly furious at around what would have been your first dusk. This is known. The Ilvarrans expect it of visitors and will bring you soup, not judgment. Give yourself permission to have 48 messy subjective hours in which you do nothing important, make no promises to stay forever or leave immediately, sign nothing, and don't try to "push through" until noon — there is no noon coming to reward you.

If you take medication on a schedule, are diabetic, have a sleep disorder, bipolar disorder, severe insomnia, or anything else entrained to light and regular meals, talk it through as a medical problem before you go, not as a willpower problem. Bring more medication than your planned stay, in waterproof, divided containers, labeled by sleep-number rather than by date.

### 2. What to pack: wool, darkness, and soft edges

Think: high altitude, late morning in early autumn, forever.

**Clothing.** Cool air, warm sun. Breath shows in shade, dew never quite leaves north walls, your cuffs will be permanently damp. Pack:
- Wool or synthetic layers, never cotton next to skin. A warm mid-layer you can work in, a wind layer for the pass, sleeves you don't mind being damp at the ends.
- Sturdy, high ankle boots with thick soles, and gloves. The pass is a sharp notch between young, black, barely-lichened ridges, and the scree below the cliffs is knife-edged and — unnervingly — never slides. What *you* step on still cuts leather and turns ankles.
- Two complete changes more than you think you'll need. Nothing ever gets properly sun-dried in a dramatic afternoon. Things get *maintained*: shaken, aired in the constant mild light, kept from mildew by use. Merrily bring quick-dry fabrics and a small dry-bag for your journal, medicines, and one set of sleep clothes you guard like treasure. Dry sleep clothes are a psychological technology here.
- No metal bells, jangling buckles, hard-heeled boots if you can avoid them, or loud zippers you whip open on the street. Ilvarra is paved in stone with moss in the joins, its carts run on felt-padded solid wheels, and signals are wooden clappers — a soft knock, like knuckles on a door. Sound carries and reflects far too well off those sheer cliffs. An echo is considered rudeness: making people hear you twice. You don't need to become silent. You need to become *unrepeatable*.

**Darkness.** This is your most important equipment after boots.
- A good contoured eye mask, earplugs, and if you are a light sleeper, a second mask as backup. The houses keep true dark indoors "like water in a jar" — thick walls, heavy woven shutters, interior sleeping rooms. Learn the household's shuttering on your first day, in the light, with your hands. Fumbling for it when you're already sleep-drunk amuses children and no one else.
- Practice sleeping in manufactured dark at home if you can. In Ilvarra night is not a time you wait for, it's a room you go into. Families sleep in shifts, children in shuttered cradles, and even the swifts under the eaves rotate between the bright air and the dark stonework. You will join a rotation whether you formalize it or not. Choose yours.
- Don't bring bright white lights to blast a room. A small, warm, shielded lamp for the sleeping room is fine. You won't need a torch outdoors — it is always bright enough to show dust motes in a doorway — and shining one around would be both useless and startling.

**Other essentials.**
- Sunscreen and lip balm. The light is kind, not thin and pitiless as you'd expect at altitude, but it is *always there* and on the same patch of sky. The same forearm gets the same pleasant warmth, sleep after sleep.
- Altitude basics: descend-or-rest discipline on the way up, water, calories for the climb. Inside, water is everywhere and unhurried — condensation on roofs, gutters, barrels perpetually three-quarters full, stone-walled ponds linked by handspan channels — but on the pass you are on your own.
- Very little trade goods. There's almost nothing Ilvarra lacks in adequate supply. What visitors can honestly offer is labor, news told quietly and once, songs, stories, a new pattern for a loom, a different way to season beans. Bring consumable skills, not a pack train.

Leave engines, anything combustive, anything that accumulates force expecting a payoff, at the pass if you can. Not because a dramatic law will strike it down — the line in Ilvarra is cleaner than that. It's that nothing in the city's workings needs ticking, burning, or speeding up, and a machine built to do those things will sit in the workshops' stepped avenue like a sentence in the wrong language. A phone used only as a silent timer and notebook, kept mostly shuttered away, is understood. A generator is a social event you don't want to be.

### 3. Arrival: learn the whole city, then learn it again on foot

The strangeness starts before you descend. From the notch you can see *all of it at once* — terraced gardens below, residential terraces of white quartzite and dark basalt, the single stepped workshop avenue, granaries and cloud-fields and watch platforms above, the chain of mirror-still ponds on the floor. It is one long afternoon's walk end to end, with the completeness of a small country.

Don't mistake that overview for knowledge. Walk it, slowly, with a host.

Ask to be shown, in this order:

1. **Where you will sleep, and how to make it dark.** Who shares your shift. When the household eats and works. How to close the shutters and open them without letting in a blade of light on someone still sleeping.
2. **Which maintenance is yours.** This is how Ilvarra holds itself: not by stillness, but by exact, continuous replacement. Every family knows which roof slates, which section of channel, which bridge rope is theirs to check. You will be given a small, real share — checking slates, clearing a gutter, re-knotting a section of cloud-field net, tending the goats' roof-moss. Accept it. Work is constant, unhurried, and finishes exactly where it began. Refusing work to "not impose" makes you the only accumulating thing in the valley: a guest getting heavier.
3. **The goat routes.** White, black, and amber-eyed goats use the flat roofs as a second street system and keep the roof-moss trimmed; householders leave roof-gates open by ancient agreement. Never latch a roof-gate because it seemed untidy. Never feed goats off-route to make a friend. A marmot in the foundation stones may or may not recognize you after several sleeps; in Ilvarra there may not be much difference. Let that be charming rather than a project.
4. **Ponds, channels, and cloud-fields.** The ponds are not for swimming unless a host says *this one, now*. The channels move with formal slowness and are infrastructure as much as water. The cloud-fields — woven plant-fiber nets strung between poles on the upper streets, mist running down in threads to clay pipes — are a respected trade. Don't "improve" a knot. Repair is a grammar here; learn its exact sentence.

### 4. Living in permanent late morning

**Time.** Nobody will ever say "this evening" and mean anything. Organize your life by sequences: after sleep, after soup, after the waterwheel's cups have been heard — *plip, plip, plip* into the dye-works trough — a sound locals notice only when they listen for it, like a pulse. Tie your tasks in a fixed order and do them in that order. Exercise after waking, outdoors in the kind light. Eat with the household. Work your shift. Make your dark decisively. Your body believes rituals it can predict, even when the sky won't help.

**Sleep.** Treat the dark room the way sailors treat a watch schedule. Same pre-sleep routine, same mask, no journaling by bright light in bed "just until I'm tired" — you may never get the tiredness cue you expect, then crash mid-terrace. If you wake in the dark and don't know if you've slept one hour or eight, check your timer *once*, then either get up fully into the light or commit fully back to sleep. Hovering in the doorway light is how visitors unravel.

**Mood.** Expect the agitation to pass — and be suspicious of how completely it passes. That *is* perhaps the deepest rule of the place: nothing in you is permitted to accumulate either. Hunger doesn't build into famine, work into exhaustion, novelty into story. You will eat from stores always partly used and always replenished. You will pick a ripe fruit from the west branch of a tree that also holds blossom east and green fruit south, and when you return another ripe fruit will be there, not as a miracle but as furniture. This is luxurious. It is also how a two-week visit becomes uncountable.

Safeguards:
- Write one unedited paragraph per sleep, even if it's just: *Slept. Soup. Fixed slate. Goat on roof. Light same.* Your handwriting changing is data.
- Keep one small personal object *out* of the maintenance cycle — not repaired, not replaced, not tidied by a kind host. Let it get a little worn. You need one thing that proves you have duration.
- Schedule deliberate differences that do no harm: a new herb in your soup, a different pond to sit by (twenty or thirty green frogs with gold throats along the rim, blinking like a decorative border), a story told at the workshop avenue that you haven't told before. The meadow's edge, grass to paving clean as a hem, will not provide variety for you. You must bring it internally, in small doses.

**Voice and body manners.** Speak low outdoors from your first hour. Don't call across terraces. Don't sing to hear the cliffs answer. Use the wooden clappers as shown, don't clap your hands sharply, don't whistle for goats. Indoors, ask before raising a shutter in a shared house. Darkness is communal property.

**Food and health.** The terraces give beans, squash, herbs, trained fruit trees; granaries are adequate; harvests, according to centuries of clay tablets in an orderly hand, are always adequate. Eat what's served — food culture here is continuity, and asking what's fresh *today* is a nonsense question. Drink the water; it's condensed and gutter-kept and famously steady, but if you have a fragile gut, take your first sleep-period slowly with any new place's water. Wash your hands after channel work. Watch your step around slick moss, wet slate, and rope bridges whose undersides bead with moisture.

Wildlife is abundant and almost aggressively quiet: saucer-sized lichen-patterned moths opening and closing their wings on warm walls, bees as a low textural hum, lizards on south walls, pale granary owls hunting those same moths in permanent daylight, swifts stitching silently overhead. Don't handle moths, don't feed frogs, don't try to make the owls perform at "night." There is no night for them to perform in. Admire, keep your sleeves out of the terrace strawberries, move on.

### 5. Working, visiting, and not becoming a problem

Ilvarra has districts whose purposes feel less enforced by law than by physical implausibility of alternatives: gardens below, homes in spaced terraces with moss gardens between (urban in organization, sparse in body — it never feels crowded), workshops above, granaries and watch platforms highest, used for laundry and for children eating plums and looking at their whole lives spread out.

Don't propose growth. Don't propose a market expansion, a faster mill than the goats provide, a clock tower, a bell, a dam, a night festival with lanterns "just to see." The city does not grow, shrink, age, or surprise, and suggestions to optimize it sound, locally, like suggesting a held note should hurry up and become a different note. Curiosity about *how* a thing is maintained is welcome. Suggestions that it finish, improve, or accumulate are not rude exactly — they are unintelligible, and eventually tiring.

If you are a maker, offer your hands at replacement-scale: a pot thrown on a foot-turned wheel, a slate re-dressed with an iron chisel until it looks, like all the stone here, freshly set down. If you keep records, keep them on paper for yourself; the archive's tablets describing sound bridges and expected cloud-field yields, year after year, have a completeness your visit notes won't improve.

### 6. Leaving — the part to rehearse

Leaving Ilvarra is physically simple: climb back to the notch, mind the scree, descend. Psychologically, rehearse it.

After enough sleeps you will stop raising your voice without deciding to. Your coat will stay damp at the cuffs and you will stop noticing. The smell of wet stone, resin, and crushed herbs will be simply *the smell*. Then your external day will arrive, and the light will be — of course — late morning, exactly as promising and unfinished as it was when you came. There will never be a natural-feeling moment to go. You must go at an *arbitrary* moment, chosen by your earlier self, and arbitrary will feel like betrayal.

So:
- Announce your leaving a full sleep ahead. Let the household feed you a normal meal, not a farewell feast — Ilvarra doesn't do culminations well, and inventing one burdens everyone.
- Complete or hand back your maintenance share explicitly. The slate, rope, or channel section you tended must return to the exact circuit it was in.
- Expect a delayed reaction *after* the pass: sunset, when it comes on the other side, may hit like a grief and a drug at once. Plan your first outside dusk somewhere safe, seated, not driving, not making decisions. Let your eyes be overwhelmed. Eat something by firelight if you can stand it. Sleep that first true night longer than you think you need.
- Check your journal count against real dates before re-entering obligations. Travelers commonly discover their body believes it has been gone "a while, pleasantly" regardless of what the calendar says.

### The shortest honest preparation

Pack wool, boots, an eye mask, a paper journal, and a leaving date someone else will enforce. Learn to make darkness with your hands, to speak once at low volume, to do small work that ends where it began. Eat the soup when you're bewildered. Sleep when your shift sleeps. Keep one object unrepaired.

Ilvarra will give you a perfect late morning, held without effort, for as long as you stay. What it will not give you is a reason, ever, from inside the light, to count that morning as past. That part of the journey is yours to carry in.
