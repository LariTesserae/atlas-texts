Vael-Mora will not hurt you quickly. That is the first thing to understand, and also the trap.

It is warm, quiet, dim, humid, and solicitous. It anticipates thirst and shade and a shorter way home. Almost nothing there shouts, bites, falls, or rushes. And for exactly that reason, an ordinary human traveler — with ordinary circadian rhythms, ordinary expectations about roads, water, gravity, and cause happening before effect — can get profoundly, gently lost in it without noticing until they are exhausted, dehydrated, disoriented, and politely standing in a meadow that was a path yesterday.

Prepare accordingly. You are not preparing for a wilderness. You are preparing for a city that behaves like weather.

### 1. Reset Your Sense Of Time Before You Arrive

There is no night in Vael-Mora, and no noon. There is a held amber dusk under the lattice, a fractional brightening between roughly the tenth and fourteenth hours, and a fractional dimming when the towers switch to their greenish stored glow. The sky is pale copper. Shadows are faint to absent. Everything is visible, but nothing is ever fully lit.

Your body will hate this within two days.

You will not feel properly awake. You will not feel properly sleepy. You will start napping at strange angles and waking convinced it is early evening, which it always is.

Bring:

* **A reliable watch or timer that does not depend on the sun, local networks, or GPS.** Local "folded" transit and the lattice can make satellite time and positioning unreliable. A simple mechanical or shielded electronic watch is ideal.
* **A sleep mask that is truly blackout, plus earplugs.** The towers are wonderfully quiet — the ceramic is grown to swallow sound — but light is constant, and your brain needs an artificial night.
* **A small, warm-white personal lamp with a timer.** Use it to fake a day for yourself: bright-ish (but see etiquette below) when you wake, dim an hour before sleep. Keep it inside your room. Vael-Mora's light is metered by physics, not law, and blasting white light outdoors is the equivalent of shouting in a library that can throw your voice back at you later.
* If you use sleep aids or melatonin at home, bring your own supply. Do not assume a tower pharmacy stocks human circadian drugs, or that "night medicine" is a legible concept there.

Set an artificial cycle on arrival — say, 16 hours up, 8 down — and keep to it even when the city does not. Locals live on the lattice cycle, not yours. Agree meeting times in numbered hours, not "morning" or "later this evening." It is always later this evening.

### 2. What To Wear On A World That Is Damp But Rarely Wet

It will feel like a forest floor in late summer: warm, still, mineral-smelling, green. Warmth pools in the low places. Hollows steam by mid-cycle. Basalt fins and clay plates radiate stored heat back. Then, once per cycle, the lattice releases its heat: the temperature drops by about two degrees for exactly eleven minutes, and condensation blooms everywhere.

Two degrees does not sound like much. On saturated air, it is a rain that falls sideways out of nothing onto every surface.

Pack:

* **Loose, pale, quick-drying clothes with coverage.** Locals wear a weave that tightens/loosens with humidity. You won't have it at first — buy or borrow a set in your first tower if you can; it's not fashion, it's building technology you wear. Until then, linen-like synthetics or light natural fibers that don't chafe when damp. You will be damp most of the time.
* **A light outer layer you can put on in under a minute.** For the condensation hour. Buildings sheet water down their skins, leaves curl open spilling yesterday's cups, fins weep. You will get misted, dripped on, and walked through tower "exhalations." Then it stops and you are warm and wet again.
* **Sturdy, closed shoes with real ankle support and thick soles. Two pairs if you can.** Do not plan to go barefoot outside, and be careful barefoot inside. The basin floor rose from the sea less than a century ago. Floors and paths are inlaid — sometimes deliberately left exposed — with perfectly preserved, razor-sharp white shells of unnamed extinct things. Basalt at the rim is knife-edged. Clay plates are sharp at the seams. This land has not been worn smooth yet, including metaphorically.
* **A hat will not help much** — there is no direct sun to block — **but a small towel will.** For condensation on skin, gear, glasses.

Seal electronics, paper, medication, and anything you need dry in waterproof pouches. Intake vanes on newer towers produce frost on one side in warm air; your unprotected gear will do a less controlled version of the same trick every cycle.

### 3. Arrival And Choosing A Base

Vael-Mora is sixty kilometers across and spread impossibly thin. A tower is a town. Between towers: fern forest with leaves as broad as doors, plum-dark orchards, wet meadows, domes half-buried in vines, a black-glass landing field nothing seems to land on. From the ridge you can see all of it and think it's wilderness.

It is not. It is urban at regional scale. That means distances lie.

Do not try to "stay centrally." There is no center. Pick **one tower or dome district as your base** for your first several cycles, learn its cisterns, markets, dew-rooms, arch timetable, and condensation logic, and make day trips from it. Each tower has food, water, sleeping vaults, and cool halls low down. Trying to walk tower-to-tower on foot on your first day is how you end up spending your artificial night in a steaming meadow listening to roots click through stone.

Arrive, if you can, via the hills and a guide — not by trying to interpret the landing field. Have your host tower's name, its *current* water-name, and its nearest stable arch mark written down in local script and phonetically. Names change to follow water: if a cistern dries, the district renames until it fills. Old maps are kept as poems to former arrangements, not navigational documents. A name you were given three months ago may be an affectionate historical reference now.

Ask on arrival, explicitly:

1. What is this district called *today*?
2. Which arch marks are stable this week?
3. When is the next condensation drop, in numbered hours?
4. Where do *people* drink here?

Write the answers down on paper. Update daily.

### 4. Getting Around: The Folded City

Transit is arches — freestanding, made of a metal that is only sometimes reflective, with a rotating etched mark. Face the mark for the Eastern Cisterns and walk through, and you are twenty kilometers away with pressure in your sinuses, a stronger smell of rain, and your shadow arriving a fraction before you do.

They do not call it teleportation. The city is "folded, politely, in a few places."

Rules for ordinary humans:

* **Never stand in the arch itself, especially on a humid day.** Pause before, decide, then walk through in one steady motion. Loitering in the fold is uncomfortable and, on bad days, temporally smearing in small, nauseating ways.
* **Read the mark out loud with a companion or guide before stepping through** your first dozen times, and confirm it hasn't rotated while you were talking. Marks rotate on their own schedule.
* **Expect your shadow to precede you, and other people's shadows to precede them.** Do not lunge, greet, or apologize to a shadow-person. Wait the second. Locals do not apologize for arriving like this. You shouldn't either.
* **Do not rely on an arch existing tomorrow.** An arch that served a neighborhood for a decade may unfold one morning into a flat bridge, retired, while a new one buds knuckle-like from the ground two streets away. Always learn the *second* way to your destination — usually a path — before you need it.
* **Paths also move, politely.** A branch heavy with unripe fruit growing across yesterday's orchard path overnight is a suggestion from the district that a root main is being replaced. Take the long way. Do not duck under, climb over, or eat the fruit to assert dominance. Maintenance crews negotiate with buildings by standing very still and waiting for a wall to decide; that is the civic tempo. Match it.

If you are prone to motion sickness, sinus trouble, migraines, or inner-ear issues, tell your host. The pressure pop is mild for locals and novel for you. Sit down for a minute after your first few folds, even if you feel fine. Disorientation there is delayed and cumulative.

### 5. Water: The Real Architecture, And The One Real Danger

Do not drink from the meadow pockets.

They look perfect: shallow, mirror-bright, clean, reflecting the veil and the upside-down towers. They are not for people. They are kept — evaporating and replenishing — for the few deliberate animals that remain. Drinking from them is both a health risk you can't assess and a serious etiquette breach, like drinking from a bird bath in a temple.

Drink only from:

* Tower cisterns and roof-harvest systems (older towers) or air-harvest reservoirs (newer ones — follow the drip).
* A family's dew-room, if offered tea. A dew-room is a small cold chamber where water condenses onto a beloved object — a stone, a copper bowl, a preserved grandmother's hand. You may be served water collected from that object. Accept or decline the tea, but **do not touch the object**, comment on its nature unless invited, or treat it as a curiosity. It is beloved. That is the relevant fact.
* Your own sealed bottle, filled at the above.

Carry water even though the air is wet. Humidity is not hydration, and the steam in the hollows will make you sweat without noticing in the dim light. Refill at every tower you visit. Condensation water sheeting off a tower skin is being drunk *by the building* — admire it, don't cup your hands under it unless a local does first.

Rain as you know it almost never falls. If you see open sky water, you are probably near the rim or outside the lattice's full effect. Treat it as weather, not as the city's water system.

### 6. Food, Markets, And Things That Grow Audibly

Tower lower levels open into markets and cool vaulted halls. Food will be abundant, local, and unfamiliar in timing more than ingredient: things harvested at condensation, things that opened in the steam hour, orchard fruit that must be eaten the cycle it ripens or not at all.

Guidance:

* Eat in markets at first, point and ask, rather than foraging. The plum-dark orchard trees are engineered, their roots clicking as they grow; fruit blocked across a path is unripe and the tree is signaling. Forest plants grow fast enough to hear — a soft papery sigh of ferns unfolding — and fast growth in rich volcanic soil does not equal human-edible.
* Tell hosts about pollen allergies. Vael-Mora keeps a warm glass moth fauna that feeds on excess pollen; their wings, collected only after natural death, make the tower window lenses. Pollen load is managed, but not zero, and a traveler with hay fever in a perpetual twilight spring will know it by hour six. Bring antihistamines.
* Stay away from the three herds of low, pale, almost hairless grazers in the southern meadows. They are not livestock for petting. They crop fast grasses and their body temperature regulates a parasite no one wants reintroduced. That sentence should be sufficient. Admire from the path.
* Do not collect moth wings, shells, algae, or "loose" ceramic petals from a tower in condensation bloom. The petals are the building opening. The wings are a materials harvest. The shells are someone's floor and also a fossil record.

### 7. The Three Gravity Districts And Other Physics That Is Municipal

In three older districts — built during what histories call the Experimental Generosity — local gravity is a service. Mostly aligned, sometimes not. Where it isn't, there are handrails and warnings inlaid in **scent**.

Learn this one on day one: **a sudden smell of iron means the floor is about to become a suggestion.**

If you smell iron where you didn't a step ago: stop, find a handrail, widen your stance, and either back out the way you came or wait for a local to cross first and mirror them. Children from those districts test each step in normal neighborhoods and get teased for it; you should imitate the children, not the teasers.

Do not run, jump for fun, stack heavy objects, or sleep on an upper bunk in those districts until you've spent a full cycle there with a guide. Ordinary human inner ears assume one down. Vael-Mora mostly agrees, with exceptions that are marked if you know how to smell.

Other local physics to internalize:

* **Sound returns.** Do not shout — not because it's forbidden, but because the city will return your shout to you several hours later, quieter, in a place you don't expect to hear your own voice. This applies, to a lesser and less predictable degree, to loud arguments, private conversations held at normal volume in odd acoustics, and singing in the wrong hall. Assume anything said loudly will be replayed out of context to future-you. Keep confidences quiet and indoors.
* **Light does not travel far.** The towers' green stored glow is local. A lit window does not light a path. Carry your small lamp, dimmed, and learn to navigate by scent gardens, temperature changes (theater actors are identified by the temperature of air around them; whole gardens are planted in shades of scent), and sound. Your eyes will adapt to identifying color and phosphorescent-trigger painting in dimness if you give them a few days and don't keep ruining it with bright white light.
* **Heat registers in teeth at the Foundry Terraces.** They print tower segments atom by atom in chambers of held lightning, in a hum you feel more than hear. If you have dental work, pacemakers, or sensory sensitivities, ask before visiting; "very quiet" there does not mean low-impact.

### 8. Etiquette For A Solicitous City

Vael-Mora is not formal. It is *attentive*, and expects attention in return.

* Speak quietly. Five thousand people in a market hall produce a murmur like wind in reeds by building design. Match the room.
* Wait. Maintenance, buildings, plants, and hosts all operate at negotiation speed. Standing still for a long stretch is often participation, not idleness.
* Ask before recording, photographing with flash, or using bright light on art, gardens, or theater. Many works are *meant* to be seen by the phosphorescence they trigger, smelled, or felt as warmth. A flash doesn't just annoy; it erases the piece temporarily.
* With wader birds in the cistern marshes: the long-throated, mostly silent birds who stand motionless for a day are, per local children, listening downward through the water into the city below the city. Whether or not you believe that, stand still, don't wade toward them, and don't tap the water to get their attention. They are one of the few animal fixtures and are left alone.
* If a place has changed since yesterday — renamed, re-pathed, re-arched — do not insist on the old arrangement loudly. Note it, update your paper map, and treat the old name as you would a former name of a person: usable in history, rude as a correction.

### 9. The Edge Of The City

Beyond the last tower the country continues unbuilt: sharp mineral flats, hot springs ringed with red algae that are pockets of the old sea still escaping upward, and a forest that has not been instructed in any way.

People go there to be unaccommodated. Understand what that means before you romanticize it: the city anticipates you; the forest does not. No condensation logic, no arches, no scent warnings, no paths except those worn by wader birds moving between pools.

Do not go alone, do not bathe in hot springs unless a knowledgeable local explicitly invites you into a specific pool (heat, mineral content, and algae are all variable and the land is young and hot in places), and set a turnaround time by your watch. In perpetual dusk with no shadows, it is very easy to walk an hour farther than you meant to.

The spot between the last arch and the first unmodified tree is, nonetheless, worth standing in. You'll feel the settlement breathing behind you: condensation, opening, drinking, closing. Someone may arrive — shadow first — and stand with you, and point at a pocket of water that wasn't there that morning.

Agree with them. That's the real city. You are living in the part around it.

### 10. A Minimal Packing List

* Watch/timer independent of sun/network
* Sleep mask, earplugs, timed warm lamp
* Two pairs sturdy closed shoes, thick soles
* Loose pale quick-dry clothing, one fast-on condensation layer, small towel
* Waterproof pouches for electronics/paper/meds
* Personal bottle (empty on arrival — fill at a cistern)
* Paper notebook and pencil — for names, arch marks, scent warnings — updated daily
* Personal medication including allergy and sleep/circadian supplies
* Dim, shielded walking light (green or warm, low)
* Patience, deliberately practiced

### Final Warning

The danger in Vael-Mora is not that it is hostile. It is that you will adapt to it just enough — folding between districts, drinking dew-room tea, learning that iron means gravity, falling asleep to water ticking into leaves — to forget that your body is still keeping score: of light it never got, sleep it never anchored, kilometers it thinks it didn't walk because an arch erased them, water it saw everywhere and didn't drink.

Keep your artificial night. Keep your bottle full. Keep your map in pencil.

Leave when you planned to, even if, by afternoon, the clearest pool has vanished and the spot where it was is the greenest thing for meters around. Especially then.
