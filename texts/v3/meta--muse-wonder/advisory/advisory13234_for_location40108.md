You have to go down to it, and that descent is your acclimatization. Don't rush it, and don't arrive at midday.

From the plain, it looks empty. A rectangular wound in cream limestone, sixty meters deep, a spiral service ramp cut into one wall. By the time you're halfway down that ramp you will understand the town better than any briefing can teach you: the wind stops. It scrapes overhead and misses. The air below isn't moving air that has heated up — it's stored air, damp, loud, smelling of hot iron, wet stone, diesel, algae, and somebody's fried onions. It's already holding the heat of yesterday.

An ordinary human can live there. Three hundred people do. But they live there the way you live on a boat: by a hundred small, constant courtesies paid to water, heat, and other animals. Prepare to do the same.

### Time Your Arrival — And Your Whole Day

The pit keeps its own time, lagging hours behind the sun.

**Coolest:** just before dawn. "Cool" means you will still sweat through a sheet.
**Morning to ~11:30:** dim, thick, workable. Permanent late afternoon under the pit's own shadow. This is when people, swifts, and dragonflies are most active.
**Noon, for about 90 minutes:** the sun finds the angle and drops straight in. Everything — corrugated roofs, concrete blocks, pipes, sumps — flashes white and murderous. Do not be on the ramp, in a gravel lot, or between buildings then if you can help it. Sunglasses are not enough; you want shade, a hat, and preferably indoors. Sunburn and reflected glare off pale limestone can hit you in minutes.
**Afternoon:** shadow returns, but the rock is now giving back its heat.
**Hottest: around 7 p.m.** This is the trap most visitors fall into. It *looks* like evening. It feels like a furnace. Do not plan your climb out, any heavy carrying, or any exercise for then.
**Night:** lights come on early in the dimness. A second shift begins — moths, geckos, frogs, mosquitoes. The machines never stop.

Arrive on the rim at first light, descend while you still have strength, and spend your first noon inside watching how the locals do it: under awnings, under tarps, doing nothing strenuously.

Coming from the blinding, silent, empty plain, bring sunglasses for the rim and be ready to take them off below. Your eyes will need ten minutes to adjust to the gloom, the steam haze, and the sheer amount of movement in it.

### What to Wear, What to Pack

Forget ordinary desert gear. This is not dry heat. It's a hot, wet hole.

**Clothing:** Loose, light-colored, long sleeves and long trousers, despite the heat. Mosquitoes rise off the sumps in visible columns, dragonflies patrol, but biting insects own dawn and dusk. Shorts and a t-shirt are an invitation to wake up covered in bites. You want fabric that is breathable, dries fast, and doesn't hold salt — synthetics or thin cotton blends. Everything will get damp and stay damp. Bring at least twice as many socks and underwear as you think you need; fungal infections, chafing, and trench-foot type misery are real risks when the ground is never quite dry.

**Shoes:** Sturdy, closed, waterproofed walking boots or wellies you can hose off, with grip. Terraces are gravel, mud, algae-slick concrete channels, and limestone that weeps dark fans of seepage. Sandals are for inside your room only — and shake *any* shoe, boot, or slipper before you put your foot in it. Every morning. No exceptions. Beetles, cockroaches, frogs, and worse use them as warm overnight shelter.

Pack a second, dry pair to sleep near and change into. Salt bloom from the walls will eat leather, corrode zips, buckles, electronics, and camera gear astonishingly fast. Put everything vulnerable in sealed dry bags with silica packets. Wipe metal down nightly.

**Essential kit:**
- A fine-mesh mosquito net that you can tuck in completely, plus repair tape. Nets with a tear are decoration.
- Insect repellent (reapply after sweating, which is constantly), after-bite cream, antihistamines
- Earplugs, several pairs. The pumps throb underneath *everything*, plus power hall fans, compressors, idling trucks — no one turns an engine off for fear it won't restart in the heat. At dusk the frogs in the drains start, fist-sized and throat-deep, until individual calls merge into a grinding pressure. You will either sleep with plugs or you will not sleep.
- Headlamp with a red/dim mode. White lights attract moths so thick they soften the beam and get in your eyes, mouth, and food. Red attracts less chaos.
- Sun hat, high-SPF sunscreen, refillable bottle, and electrolytes. You will sweat constantly and underestimate loss because the air is already wet.
- A small broom or brush for your threshold, a torch for checking ceilings/walls, tweezers, a basic first aid kit with antifungal powder, antiseptic, plasters that actually stick in humidity (most don't — bring tape).
- Waterproof cover for your pack. Condensation drips without pause off cold pipes, tank undersides, and ceilings.

### Water: Everywhere, Not To Be Trusted

Water is not scarce here. It is *near*. It condenses, seeps through limestone, stands in the unfinished reservoir at the bottom in a chain of warm, green, knee-deep sumps linked by mud channels and reeds. Pumps work day and night to keep those sumps from joining up and taking the lowest street — they fill a centimeter a day, are pumped down a centimeter a night.

Do not drink, swim in, or casually wade in that water. It's warm, algae-rich, frog- and tadpole- and mosquito-filled, and contaminated with diesel, salt, animal waste, and whatever has washed off roofs and pipe runs. Assume leptospirosis, bacterial skin infections, and gut parasites are a real risk. A cut foot in a sump is a clinic visit.

Drink only what the clinic/market/power hall tell you is treated. Even then:

**Never leave a cup of water uncovered.** Not for a minute. Same for food — cover it the *second* it is set down, not when you get around to it. Insects, geckos dropping from ceilings, sparrows from the market roof, and ash and moths will be in it instantly. This is the first courtesy you will be judged on.

Wash hands obsessively. Buildings sit on low plinths for a reason. Stoops have drains. Lower walls bloom salt and mold. If you're offered a room, take one on a higher terrace, in the pink workers' blocks if possible, not on the lowest street. If it rains hard on the plain above — rare, but catastrophic in a pit — you want height and a clear route to the ramp.

### Sleeping and Living Indoors

Apartment doors have double doors. Use both, every time. Shut the screen door properly and fast — you will hear a child being told this ten times a night, the bang, the geckos scattering. That door is the difference between a room and an ecosystem.

Before bed:
1. Shake bedding, check ceiling corners. Geckos on the ceiling at night are normal and beneficial — they eat mosquitoes — but you don't want one dropping on your face.
2. Tuck the net in all the way around the mattress. Test it.
3. Earplugs in.
4. Sweep the threshold. If you don't, by morning it will be blocked by beetles seeking the cool seam under the door.
5. Keep food, damp clothes, and rubbish sealed or outside. Cockroaches own the warm spaces behind electrical panels, red ants make highways from scrap to scrap, rats travel the overhead pipe runs like streets. Don't leave an open bin to teach them your address.

The feral cats, officially discouraged and unofficially fed everywhere, are why the rats are merely bad. Don't chase the cats. The slow stray dogs asleep in the clinic's cool exhaust will ignore you; step around them, don't try to move them into the sun.

In the morning, bats pour back into the cliff fissures above the top terrace in a thin black smoke. Don't stand under those fissures, don't explore them, and don't handle any bat, alive or dead. Guano dust and rabies are not souvenirs.

### Moving Around Town

It's small — four streets on blasted terraces, a market arcade, clinic, school with mesh on every window, power hall — but it's dense with infrastructure: overhead pipe racks, cables, valves, back-up tanks, culverts, scaffolding that has been up so long swallows nest in it and it's become structural.

Ask before you go. Everyone (all ~300) knows which pump is knocking differently today, which stairwell currently has wasps, which lot is ankle-deep after a seep burst. Asking is not just safety; it's politeness. You are a very obvious guest in a place that works by everyone noticing small changes.

Watch your head for pipe runs and your feet for drains — frogs sit in them, snakes could, and the grates are slippery. Lizards on sunlit walls and swifts shrieking between blocks will startle you at first. Pigeons crowd every ledge not strung with wire; don't eat under one.

Don't turn anything off. Not an idling truck, not a pump, not a fan that seems redundant. Systems here are backed up by habit, not logic, and a silent pump by evening can mean a flooded street by midnight.

Vegetation has mostly failed — leggy tomatoes under shadecloth get eaten to stems overnight, there's algae, reeds, and one unkillable oleander by the clinic. Don't rely on local fresh produce. Food is brought down, preserved, cooked fast, and eaten covered. Bring any specific medication, sun, or dietary supplies with you from above; resupply is irregular. The reservoir was postponed, then postponed again; everything else follows that schedule.

### Health Precautions for an Ordinary Human

Before you go if your multiverse route allows it: be up to date on tetanus, and talk to a medic about mosquito-borne disease, typhoid/waterborne cover, and rabies pre-exposure — you'll be living close to bats, dogs, cats, and rats with limited rapid evacuation except up that one ramp.

While there: treat every bite that swells hot, every scrape that was in muddy water, every fever, and every gut upset early at the clinic. In this heat and damp, small infections bloom like the salt on the walls. Keep feet dry when you can, change socks midday, air boots at night (inside the net or up high, or something will move in).

Air quality is poor low down: stagnant diesel fumes, steam, mold spores from damp concrete, limestone dust. If you have asthma, bring your inhaler and consider a light mask for the power hall / lowest terraces. Headaches that get worse near idling vehicles are a warning — move up a terrace into slightly better air.

### The Mental Part

Prepare to be outnumbered. This town is overwhelmingly lived-in, just not primarily by people. Sparrows live inside the market roof. Sparrows, pigeons, ants, roaches, moths around every yellow work light and blue porch bulb — life found its way down and stayed and prospered because the pit is a perfect refuge on a barren plain.

You can't spray, stomp, or tidy that away, and trying will mark you as dangerous and foolish. Learn to cohabit: brush a gecko off the wall rather than kill it, step over an ant highway, let a dragonfly have the seep.

Also prepare for no horizon, no wind, no true darkness, and no quiet. The pit is hottest when it looks like evening, dimmest when it's technically morning, and loudest when you're trying to sleep. People adapt by keeping routines small and repetitive: seal a wall, pump the sumps, open a stall, do the same work again. Follow their rhythm for a few days and your body will stop fighting it.

Plan your exit before dawn on a cool-ish morning, well-watered, with a hat for the rim. Halfway up the ramp you'll hit moving air again, then sudden, silent, blinding plain. Stop at the top by the sparse, thorny acacias leaning *away* from the pit. Let your eyes and ears adjust before you travel on.

Below you, out of sight almost immediately, the pumps will keep throbbing, the frogs will be waiting for dusk, and the town in its unfinished foundation will go on being hot, damp, half-built, and completely alive.
