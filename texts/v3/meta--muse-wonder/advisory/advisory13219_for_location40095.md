You enter it by going down, and you should treat that descent the way you would treat going underwater or underground. Once you are off the rim, weather, light, water, food, sleep, and exit are all controlled by the pit and the plant. You cannot improvise your way out quickly — it's nine minutes across, and up to an hour from bottom to top by the permitted route, in 40-degree heat, on stairs.

Prepare to be hot, wet, dim, lost, and socially clumsy for days.

### 1. Your body will be the limiting factor

At the rim it may be windy and ordinary. At the bottom it is 39 to 44°C, still and saturated, and it shifts with the boilers. The air does not move unless a machine pushes it. In air like that, sweat does not save you the way it should. Hurrying makes it worse: you generate heat faster than you can shed it.

Before you go:

* **Heat-acclimatize if you can.** Spend your first 24-48 hours on the upper and middle terraces, not the floor. Work down a few levels a day. If you arrive and immediately tour the boiler yards, you will be a medical problem for people who have very little spare capacity.
* **Get stair-fit.** Fourteen levels counting pipe galleries and roof-warrens, switchbacks, ladders, shoulder-width alleys that turn three times. You will climb with everything you own on your back, often sideways. Train for an hour of slow climbing in heat, not a short sprint.
* **Plan to move slowly and speak low.** Everyone does, because raising your voice feels like work. Shouting, running, and pushing in the galleries at shift change mark you as a visitor and can genuinely make you ill.
* **Learn heat illness cold.** Headache, nausea, confusion, skin that stops sweating, a feeling of pressure in the teeth from the plant that suddenly becomes disorientation — sit down in shade *that you have been given permission to use*, get wet, get up-level if you can, and tell someone. People live here by noticing each other stop. At shift change, when the felt-wrapped bell is struck once, everyone stops. Use that pause.

Do not sleep on the lowest floor during venting week if anyone will trade you up. During a vent, deep shafts sigh out and every loose sheet of tin lifts and settles in sequence. It is not just hotter down low then; the air is worse.

### 2. What to wear, what to carry

This is a dry place that is never dry. Your clothes will never quite dry and never quite stay wet. Paper goes soft in drawers. Salt turns to stone. Metal at noon becomes untouchable.

Pack for a sauna built out of a construction site:

**Clothing:** Light, loose, fast-drying synthetics or very thin natural fabric, in layers you can wet down. Long sleeves and trousers despite the heat — every ledge is sharp black glassy rock, every fallen fragment is angular as broken crockery, and sun and hot pipes burn faster than skin can learn. No heavy cotton, no waterproof rain shell that seals you in. It will become a personal steam bath.

**Feet:** Sturdy, closed shoes with thick, insulated, grippy soles. Walkways are wet iron, streets in the drip-zones are always dark and stippled, moss grows in pipe joints, and at noon metal ticks and expands. Sandals will cut you, cook you, or slip you off a gallery.

**Hands and head:** Thin work gloves and a hat/hard covering you never take off near the walls. Rock here doesn't crumble, it cracks clean and loud like a rifle in another room and falls sharp. Gloves are also how you survive handrails — some are rails, some are pipes, some are hot enough to be neither. Learn which is which before you lean.

**Umbrella / drip hood:** Not for sky-rain. Rain here mostly never lands: it condenses under the rim and cold-water loop, falls, and evaporates halfway down in the rising heat. You need cover for specific, predictable leaks — a particular joint in a drip-street that is always raining in a three-square-meter patch. A small folding umbrella or hooded cape is perfect.

**Light:** A headlamp with a warm, dim setting. Noon gives about 40 minutes of hard white direct sun on the floor. The rest is shade from the walls plus work light: sodium orange, green indicator lamps, blue welding stutter through steam, amber household bulbs. Your eyes will adjust slowly. Visitors keep ducking, expecting a ceiling. A harsh white torch blinds everyone and ruins that adjustment.

**Sealed storage:** Everything in waterproof pouches with desiccant if you can get it. Documents, electronics, medicines, salt, matches. Concrete sweats, cool cisterns sweat into hot alleys, condensation forms in pockets where you least expect it. Assume anything left open will be damp, salt-caked, or mildewed in a day.

**Water bottle and electrolytes:** Bring your own full bottle down from the rim for the first day, and a way to carry at least a liter hands-free while climbing. After that you will be on pit water — see below — but don't arrive thirsty and start stealing from chained buckets while you learn.

Leave behind: scented products, open food, anything that assumes animals will eat scraps or insects will be a nuisance. There are none, and the absence matters.

### 3. Water is plumbing, food is plumbing

Do not drink the first thing that drips on you.

Real drinking water is born at the top, where hot plant breath hits the shade-net and cold-water loop under the rim and condenses. It's caught where it forms, under pipes and overhangs. Everywhere else is sweat off a cistern, a doorway breathing damp, a joint leaking machine taste.

The local rule is exact:

1. **First drip after a cooling cycle: throw it away.** Iron and machine oil.
2. **Second: washing.**
3. **Third, if you are patient and your pipe is clean: drinkable.**

If someone shows you *their* pipe, *their* bucket — buckets are chained to railings by address — and offers you a third-drip cup, accept exactly that and no more. Taking water from an unattributed bucket is theft, and in this town water rights are compensation, inheritance, and currency. Likewise shade: shade is surveyed, deeded, inherited, fought over in court up at the rim. Never simply step into a good shadow and settle. Ask: "May I stand here a while?" People will usually move you three feet to a spot that is not someone's.

Food comes down sealed: tinned, dried, frozen, into cold boxes straight from the freight lift. There are no open food stalls, no flies, no rats, no pigeons — nothing survives long in the lower heat and whatever sweet chemical rides the plant steam, not even the dog they tried in the early years. Expect mess-hall food that you cannot picture ever walking. If you have allergies or medical dietary needs, bring sealed supplies for your whole stay and guard them like the locals guard imported soil sacks.

What grows here grows in lines along leaks: pale, stringy beans in troughs under reliable cold pipes, hand-pollinated with a paintbrush passed house to house; black-green fern like wet leather in shaded cracks; bright moss in pipe joints made into bitter tea and poultices. Do not pick, move, or "improve" any of it. Gardens are planted in plumbing, not soil. Moving a pipe can kill a neighborhood's crop; rerouting a cooling line creates a new green seam three levels down that someone will claim within a month. If you see a planter bolted under a drip, treat it as a farm fence.

Don't offer the moss tea a polite try if you are on medication you care about — you don't know its strength, and you didn't come here to test poultices.

### 4. Navigating a hamlet with city density

Four thousand people live where a village should be, stacked in iron and poured concrete against the walls. Houses share walls with cisterns, transformer sheds, coolant manifolds. A main steam line runs through the second floor of the laundry and out a bedroom window behind it. That's not sloppy; that's rent and heat arranged sensibly.

* **Stay on the permitted route unless invited off it.** Some ladders go through other people's kitchens. Some alleys are private by custom. A guide for the first day down and the first day up is worth whatever they charge in water or sealed food.
* **Expect vertical addresses.** "Three levels up from the laundry, past the green-black copper line, left where the alley turns twice" is a real direction. Write nothing on paper you need to keep crisp; memorize landmarks by temperature and smell — the sauna pocket by a sweating cistern, the boiler-yard orange, the always-wet stair.
* **Time your movements.** Avoid bottom-to-top climbs in the noon disk. Everything metal is untouchable, everyone goes under tin, the town ticks and pings as it expands. Also avoid shift-change galleries if you can; they are shoulder-to-shoulder, and the stairs never truly empty because half the town sleeps while the other works, behind wet curtains.
* **Tools down = danger at noon.** Follow the unwritten temperature rules slavishly: don't leave tools — or your bottle, buckles, camera — where the disk will find them; don't lean on sunlit iron; don't block a vent path with your pack; don't sleep low when you could trade up.

The engines are not roaring, they are enclosed and lagged — note the asbestos cloth and sheet iron and do not touch, scrape, or lean against lagging — but you will feel them as pressure. The loudest sounds are dripping, a walkway creaking under weight, a crane chain taking strain link by link. Keep your own noise small.

### 5. Social preparation

Rules here aren't posted. They're learned by taste and touch: locals can tell by their roof water if the north boiler is running lean. You can't, and pretending you can is worse than asking.

Ask before photographing inside homes, before sitting on a balcony at the cool hour — middle terraces in the evening are where people sit among bean troughs and listen to water bead overhead, and that hour, when rain falls a little further before vanishing and the heat is almost bearable, is private even when it's outdoors. Ask before helping scrape moss, move a partition, or hold a rivet gun. Houses expand six inches into dead air shafts; a new gallery over an alley takes someone's daylight, and they are compensated in water rights after negotiation, not by a visitor being handy.

Also prepare for the emptiness. Nothing moves that is not a person or a machine. No wings cross the light. Nothing scurries when you lift a crate. In a crowd that dense, that cleanliness feels wrong, and many visitors get quietly unsettled at night. Bring something human to do — mending, a sealed instrument, letters — and don't bring a pet under any circumstances. The story of the dog ends the way everyone expects.

### 6. Arrival and exit plan

Arrive at the rim in the morning, check in with whoever controls shade/water/lift rights up top, and get your descent slot. Confirm: where you will sleep (level, and who owns the shade/water there), where your drinking pipe is, when the freight lifts and crane run (the crane may move container houses at 3 a.m.), and who to find if you feel ill.

Keep an exit reserve: a full bottle, electrolytes, gloves, and headlamp on your person at all times, even for a "nine minute" walk across. That walk can become an hour up, in a vent or a cooling cycle when middle terraces shine with condensation and stairs are slick.

Finally, leave the pit slower than you entered it. Sit a while at the rim and let real wind feel strange again. Down below at night it will still be glowing faint orange and green, making its own weather in the dark, rain falling that never lands, a cupped-hand town keeping itself alive on recycled water and heat. You will smell of iron, machine oil, and damp concrete for days — that's normal. If you don't, you probably never really went down.
