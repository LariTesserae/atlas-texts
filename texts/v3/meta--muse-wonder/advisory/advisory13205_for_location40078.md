This is not a town you visit by walking into it. It's a town you enter, the way you enter weather — slowly, wetly, and on its terms. From the caldera rim it will look almost deserted: black rock, snow that never quite melts, a small green-gold tarn full of lily pads and reed clumps, a waterfall roaring in at the north end. Put your face under the surface and it opens up at once — streets, gardens, houses, fish processions, bubbles rising in silver threads along the roads.

An ordinary human can go there safely for a short visit. You cannot live there the way the locals do without months of adaptation, and you should not try to fake it. Prepare as if for high-altitude cold-water diving, a stay in someone's living house, and a visit to a place where almost every building material is alive, all at once.

### 1. The three physical facts that govern everything

**1. It is high and cold.**
The air is thin, the wind is thin, the sun is strong, and the water is snowmelt cold even in late summer. Local people are used to it in their skin. You are not. Hypothermia, not drowning, is your most likely day-one problem — it creeps up while you are distracted and feeling fine in a warm air-bell with your body still trailing in the lake.

**2. The water is *almost* breathable. For you, it isn't.**
In daylight the moss, pondweed, and root-mass oxygenate the water so heavily it pearls with bubbles. Locals work the lower rooms — fish stores, root cellars, old stone sleeping nooks — on long, practiced dives. They are still holding their breath. If you inhale the water expecting it to sustain you, you will drown in very clear, very pretty water. Treat this as breath-hold swimming between air stations, always.

**3. Sound does not fade underwater here, it accumulates.**
The inlet fall, a thousand frogs, hand-drums, wooden clappers, market haggling in the big bell, a clear bell-note out of the lake itself — you will hear all of it, layered. In an air-bell voices are startlingly loud because the curved leaf throws sound back and the water walls it in. Speak softly, expect headaches at first, and do not be surprised by sensory exhaustion by evening.

### 2. Before you go: train and choose your season

Go in late spring to late summer if you can. That is when the oxygenation is strongest, the bells are warmest, the terraces are producing, and storms — while violent — are brief hailbursts rather than prolonged cold.

For 4-6 weeks beforehand:

* Practice calm breath-hold swimming in cold water, not heroic apnea. You need a relaxed 30-45 second working swim, surfacing unpanicked, over and over. Panic is what kills visitors in places with frequent air.
* Cold-acclimate gradually: cold showers, short cold swims. You are training your shock response, not trying to become a local.
* Get comfortable walking slowly on a slippery bottom while buoyant. Life there happens either swimming a hand's breadth above the pale causeways, or in that slow, dreamlike bottom-stride.
* Learn to clear your ears gently and often. Most of the town is only a fathom or two down, but you will be up and down into bells dozens of times a day.
* Spend a night or two at altitude first if your portal drops you straight at the rim. Thin air + cold water + breath-holding is a bad combination on day one. Arrive at the rim, camp dry, sleep, *then* enter the lake on the second morning.

Get your ears, sinuses, lungs, and any skin breaks checked. A small ear infection becomes a major problem when your head is under water all day. Do not go with a chest cold.

### 3. What to pack

Everything will be wet. Pack for that, not in spite of it.

**Wear / bring:**
* A close-fitting thermal swim layer that does not shed fibers or trap much air — locals wear woven fibre and skin; you will want insulation. A modern wetsuit is acceptable if you rinse it clean of detergents before arrival, but a thick, buoyant suit will fight you on bottom-walking and in bells. A 3-5mm suit or thermal top + leggings is a better compromise.
* Mask. The water is clear at the edges but your eyes will sting and blur after an hour. A simple low-volume mask lets you see the streets, posts, and bubble-paths.
* Soft fins for surface transits, *and* soft-soled water shoes with grip for the causeways. The old stones are crowded with freshwater mussels — sharp, living, and not to be crushed. Bare feet will be cut.
* A weight that you can drop instantly — a stone in a fibre sling is local style — if you want to walk the bottom rather than float over it. Never weight yourself so you cannot swim up to a bell unaided.
* Nose clip if you are prone to sinus flooding when you flip up into a bell.
* Dry-bags that actually seal. Keep one set of *completely dry, warm clothes and a blanket on the rim* with your exit partner. Nothing you take into the town will stay dry.
* Sun protection for the rim and for surface floating: hat, cover-up, mineral-only sunscreen applied well before entering. Chemical sunscreen, soap, insect repellent, and shampoo wash straight into a closed lake that people drink, farm, and breathe around. Leave them behind. For insects at dusk, use clothing and smoke, not spray.
* A small, blunt tool only — no saw, no adze, no axe. You will not be cutting anything. Ever. See etiquette below.

**Do not bring:** pumps, drains, hoses, herbicides, poisons for leeches, metal fish hooks to use on your own, outside seeds, live plants, live animals, or any food that could seed or rot in the water. This lake is a closed system. Disinfect and dry all swim gear before arrival — you can carry fish disease, weed fragments, and snail eggs between worlds without ever seeing them.

Bring trade as labor and small useful things that do not become pollution: your hands for morning sluice-clearing, mending fibre, hauling root-felt. If you bring objects, bring bone, stone, wood, woven fibre; ask first about metals and plastics. Money means very little in the market bell. Help, dried rim-herbs from *this* caldera only, and interesting news do.

### 4. Arrival and first entry

Walk the rim first — about an hour above water. Look down. Memorize it from above while you can: the pad-roofs, the reed clumps marking houses, the bubble streets. From in the water, with green-gold light, it is beautiful and disorienting, and north becomes a guess.

Agree with a local guide *before* you get wet on:

1. Which bell is your home bell, and which bells are public (the big market bell over the old circular market is).
2. Your bell-to-bell route. Never plan a leg longer than half your comfortable breath-hold. The bells are the true rooms — pockets of cottage-sized air trapped under a huge trained leaf, head out of water, body in. You swim up into one from below.
3. The exit point and the sluice at the outlet. That lashed-log sluice is cleared by hand every morning and must never be widened. Do not touch it except as invited labor. Even a little draining kills house roots first.

Enter slowly at a shallow terrace, not by diving off the rim. Sit in the shallows until your breathing settles. Put your face in. Listen. That first roar-frog-drum-bell wash is normal.

### 5. Moving, breathing, talking

* Follow the bubble threads and pale scoured causeways and their worn posts. They are roads. Swimming a little above them keeps you off house walls, gardens, and fish pens.
* Do not pull yourself along by stems. A giant horsetail stem as thick as a thigh is someone's wall. A lily rhizome is a beam. Breaking one is not "snapping a plant," it is knocking a hole in a house. Use posts, ropes, and stone.
* Surface into bells head-first, slowly. Break the surface, exhale, breathe, let your eyes adjust to warm, plant-smelling air. Keep your voice *down* to a near whisper at first — it will sound like shouting to everyone else.
* Rest in bells often, even if you do not feel winded. Cold steals breath capacity without warning.
* Ducks will commute straight through rooms, trout will procession past your knees, newts will sit in the warm bell margins. This is normal and not an invitation to chase, feed, or grab. Fish in under-house pens are stored food and also sheltered stock — enough are taken, enough escape. Take nothing unless it is handed to you.
* At night, do not navigate by memory alone. Frogs take over, light goes, bubbles catch the last sun and then vanish. Sleep where locals put you: usually a hammock slung in a bell right at the waterline. If you are a restless sleeper, ask to be tethered loosely or to sleep on a surface platform — locals' children are rocked in those hammocks from birth; you can roll out and under in your sleep.

If a hailstorm hits while you are visiting — pads hammering, every bell ringing at once — go *down*, not up. Under two fathoms the water barely stirs and the old stones hold. Wait it out in a lower room on short breaths between bells, with your guide.

### 6. Eating, drinking, sanitation

Food is water-chestnuts shelled in the evening circle, trout, wild rice and arrowhead from the shallow terraces, cress from the cold inflow, snail-pond produce. It is fresh and usually cooked where smoke can escape through reed chimneys at the surface. Tell hosts about allergies early — shellfish-like snails and mussels are everywhere in the environment even when not on the plate.

Drink what locals drink, where they draw it — usually inflow side, not in the middle of town. Do not add anything to the water. No soap-bathing in the lake, no urinating near gardens or bells if you can possibly climb out — ask, discreetly, what the custom is; every closed lake has one, and following it matters more than your embarrassment.

Leeches are — as someone will cheerfully tell you — the tax. Wear close leggings, check in a bell after each long swim, especially ankles, waist, between toes. Do not tear a feeding leech in a panic in the water; surface, let a local remove it, let it bleed cleanly a bit, cover it. Keep cuts covered when out of the water and watch for infection.

### 7. Social etiquette in a town made of houses that grow

* **A new house is begun for a child and is ready when she is grown.** Repairs are plantings. Compliment growth, not construction. Never suggest cutting a main stem to "open up" a room, straighten a wall, or improve a view.
* Privacy exists even though water has no doors. A family circle with heads together in a bell, bodies trailing below, is a closed room. Approach along the causeway, make a soft clap or drum-tap at a distance, wait to be beckoned. Do not swim up into a private bell unannounced — your head will arrive in their living room at face height.
* Market is in the largest bell and is a pleasant din. Haggle there, by voice, at close range — do not shout across it with clappers unless you are calling a shoal and know the signal. Striking wooden clappers idly can call fish, and people, who did not want to be called.
* Herons are watchmen. People note where a heron chooses to stand before setting a trap. Do not shoo a heron off a ruin to get a photo or a swim-through. Walk around.
* Ask before photographing/recording in bells, if your device is even waterproof and silent. The resonance makes recording equipment click and hum painfully loud underwater.

Your body in the water is not considered rude — everyone lives trailing below the surface — but staring, grabbing at passing swimmers to steady yourself, and blocking a causeway while you adjust gear are.

### 8. Leaving

Plan your exit before you are tired and cold, and leave while you still have reserve breath. Climb out at the agreed shallow, sit in the wind until a companion checks you for leeches, then change immediately into the dry clothes you left on the rim. You will be colder after exit than in the lake.

Thank hosts with work, not damage: help clear the outlet in the morning, carry root-felt, shell chestnuts badly and laughably in the evening circle. That circle — heads in warm leaf-air, talk thrown back by the curve, hands busy, bodies cool below — is the town at its most itself. If you are invited into it, you have been given the real visit.

Do not promise to "come back and build." A house takes a childhood. Promise instead to come back and see how much a canopy has thickened in ten years. That is the right timescale here, and they will understand it as respect.

**Bottom rule for an ordinary traveler:** You are a warm-headed guest moving between pockets of air in a cold, living lake. Swim short, speak soft, touch stone and rope and never stem, take nothing living that was not offered, add nothing chemical that was not already there — and the town that looks like an empty tarn from above will, under the surface, be sparse, ordered, noisy, and alive in every wall.
