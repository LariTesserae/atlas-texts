Sessra is not dangerous in the way a battlefield is dangerous. It is dangerous in the way a half-built house is dangerous, at very high altitude, in bad light, where the house is still being built by something that is not the builder.

Prepare as if you are visiting a remote industrial port town — cold, damp, expensive, slow, dependent on imported everything — that happens to be sitting on top of an active geological event. Then add a second layer of preparation for the fact that altitude, air, time, and architecture do not stay where they were put.

### 1. Arrive By The Lift. Do Not Arrive Directly On The Plateau If You Can Avoid It.

The funicular takes two days. That is not wasted time. It is your acclimatization, your orientation, and your only reliable way to learn what "up" means here.

Places below have already started putting Sessra at the wrong altitude on their maps. If you portal, teleport, drop, or otherwise insert yourself directly onto the plateau, you have three immediate problems:

1. **You may arrive in thin air.** Breathable air in Sessra does not form a uniform atmosphere. It pools. It lies chest-deep in market squares, fern-pits, lower terraces, and sheltered quarries, and three streets over it can be effectively absent. A "safe" arrival point on a map may be standing in what the locals call a moth-height — air only the great pale moths tolerate.

2. **You may arrive in a place that wasn't finished yesterday, or was finished differently.** Districts shift on screw-jacks. New rooms extrude. Streets near the spire can arrive pre-cobbled. Your coordinates are less trustworthy than your guide.

3. **You will have no bell, no local context, and no one who saw you come up.** That matters for echoes. See below.

Take the lift. Sleep at the way-stations bolted to the cliff. Watch the weather fall away below you, watch the sunlight get *dimmer* instead of harsher, and get used to the blue-grey dusk before you have to navigate in it. Buy your bell at the last way-station if the lift operators don't issue you one. They should. If they don't, ask. Asking marks you as sensible.

Arrive, ideally, in daylight — such as it is — into a low, populated square in one of the established districts. Not the northern rim. Not near the spire. Not between districts.

If you are traveling with children, seriously reconsider bringing them all the way up. And if you do, the spire is absolutely forbidden to them. Not because of falls. Because children near fresh rock can be *finished* — subtly, asymmetrically, in ways that don't reverse when you go back down.

### 2. The Bell Is Not Optional

Every resident wears a small brass bell. Get one, wear it where it will sound when you move naturally, and keep it sounding.

This is not for fog signaling. It's so your echo cannot follow you home unnoticed.

In Sessra, echoes can lag, pool, and — especially in newer construction or near the spire — arrive early. You may hear your own footsteps a half-second *before* you take them. You may hear your own voice giving an instruction from an upstairs room that is sealed and unoccupied.

The city has a legal standard for this, and you should adopt it as a personal standard: **a voice that sounds like you is not you, and is not to be obeyed, unless it also knows the bell-code.**

The bell-code is local, practical, and taught without ceremony. It's a pattern of ringing — a way residents identify themselves to each other and, importantly, to themselves across time-slips. Ask your landlord, lift operator, or the first district warden you meet to teach you the current code. Write it down physically. Codes change after Leveling Festivals and after major emergence events.

Practical rules:
- Never willingly silence your bell except during the official hour of the Leveling Festival. If you are visiting during a Leveling, stay indoors, with people who know you, for that hour. It is the only true silence Sessra ever has and residents find it terrifying for good reason.
- If you hear your footsteps and your bell isn't in them, stop moving. Ring deliberately. Wait for locals.
- If something follows you with your gait but no bell, do not lead it to where you sleep.
- Do not buy an antique bell with someone else's pattern still filed into the clapper. Get a plain new one and have your own code cut.

Also bring earplugs for sleeping, but sleep with the bell within reach. The city is never quiet otherwise: the great forge-hammer in the Lower Foundry falls every twenty-two seconds, day and night, for sixty years now, through your teeth and into the puddles. Foghorns blow from watchtowers for reasons that have nothing to do with ships. Tram bells, piston engines, chain lifts, bird flocks in the turbines — you will either adapt or you will not sleep at all. Dim light helps. Most people find Sessra restful, mentally, once their ears give up.

### 3. Breathing: Read The Animals, Not Your Instruments

Do not trust an altimeter or a simple oxygen gauge up here. The altitude is moving, the mountain is rising a few centimeters a year, and air behaves like a liquid in hollows.

Learn this hierarchy on your first hour:

- **Sparrows and mixed low flocks skimming the cobbles:** Air is good at ground level. Breathe normally.
- **Choughs, pigeons, starlings at roof height, but nothing low:** Air is pooling lower than you are standing. Crouch, go down a terrace, get into a market hollow.
- **Only the great pale moths, wings like dinner plates, beating very slow:** Hold your breath and hurry. This is not metaphorical. Your ears will pop, sound will go strange, your own steps may come back to you early. Get low, get indoors, get to a fern-pit. Do not try to "push through" a long moth-street.
- **Translucent frogs singing:** That's in the cisterns, and they mimic tram bells perfectly. Do not navigate by sound alone anywhere water is stored.

Carry a small personal oxygen supply if you are an ordinary, unacclimated human — not a full mountaineering rig, just a bridge for crossing between districts or caught-out moments. The forty-minute tram rides between districts cross bare, smoking rock fields with almost nothing built. If a tram breaks down out there, you want to be the person who can breathe while waiting, sitting low in the carriage.

Move slowly. Even industry moves slowly here. Furnaces take a month to fire and a week to cool. Herds take a season to cross the plateau. Your body will want that pace anyway.

Watch for delayed effects if you've been near fresh rock: one ear hearing things a few seconds early, headaches that arrive before the forge-hammer's boom, dreams in the wrong order. Report them to the Office of Emergence. They have heard stranger and they will not laugh.

### 4. Dress For A Wet Foundry On A New Volcano

Forget alpine gear catalogs. Sessra is not snowy and picturesque. It's cool, damp, sharp, and metallic.

You need:

- **Boots that can be resoled, with thick, replaceable soles and ankle protection.** Fresh basalt at the plateau edges cuts leather like glass. The stone comes up in black columns and glassy folds, still warm in the fissures. Step where locals step.
- **Waterproof outer layers that can take constant condensation.** The ceiling — and it is a ceiling, about a mile above the plateau, breathing slowly, dripping, glowing faintly at night with badly remembered daylight — rains condensation all day. Under market roofs it patter down warm and metallic. Railings bead and run black. You will be wet from above even when it isn't raining.
- **Gloves.** For sharp rock, hot pipes, cold iron, and chalk.
- **A headlamp, preferably with a blue electric setting to match tram lamps, and spare power.** At noon it's twilight. At midnight it's not much darker, which ruins circadian rhythm faster than darkness would. Keep a regular sleep schedule by clock and by meal, not by light. Bring whatever you normally use for light-deprivation mood effects back home — long-term residents will tell you the dimness is why they stay, that it's "dim enough to think in," but newcomers often go flat and strange in the second week.
- **Layers you can remove near steam vents, furnaces, and exhaust grates.** The yaks sleep in perfect circles around those grates for a reason. Pockets of lush heat sit right next to pockets of thin cold air.

Also bring chalk. Several sticks, in a waterproof tin. You will understand very quickly.

### 5. Counting, Doors, And Not Being Finished

This is the part visitors think is superstition until their third staircase.

**Count stairs aloud. Always.** Even in your own lodging, even when embarrassed, even at 3 a.m. Residents do it without thinking and they will respect you for doing it.

A staircase that had fourteen steps in the morning can acquire a fifteenth by evening, in fog, in newer districts. That extra step leads to a landing with a door that was not let to anyone.

If this happens to you:
1. Stop. Do not open the door. Do not look through the keyhole to be clever — air and time on the other side are not guaranteed.
2. Mark it with chalk, clearly, on the wall and on the step.
3. Go back down counting aloud.
4. Notify the Office of Emergence.

A crew will come with rivet guns and seal it politely from your side. This is normal municipal service. Tipping is appreciated but not required; accurate reporting is required.

Other architectural rules that will keep you intact:

- **Never enter a house the mountain has started but finishers haven't cleared.** Near the spire you will see recognizable arches, window openings with the glass already in, condensation sweating on the *inside*. Streets that arrive already cobbled. Low walls that bud and branch. The finishers' job is to persuade a building to have only the rooms it needs. Until they've done that, a building is a suggestion with load-bearing opinions.
- **Do not cut vines casually.** In older districts, vines and roots thread scaffolding and become structural. Demolition crews regularly find a tenement held up more by thirty years of roots than by its beams. If something green is growing through a wall, assume the wall needs it until a local says otherwise.
- **Do not expect straight lines near the spire.** Builders deliberately avoid straight load-bearing walls there because formation gets ahead of intention. Your lodging should be in a screwed-down, older district, on screw-jacks, re-anchored regularly — not in a charming new extrusion with a view.
- **Do not remove survey stakes, telegraph markers, or chalk marks between districts.** That emptiness is actively being watched.

Keep your own records on paper. What floor you sleep on, how many steps to your door, where your window faced yesterday. In Sessra this is not paranoia, it's housekeeping.

### 6. Getting Around A City That Is Nine Towns Pretending To Be One

Sessra is twenty kilometers of raw stone with nine or ten dense industrial knots scattered across it. Within a district: walk, count, stay low, stay among birds. Between districts: take the tram, take the causeway rail, do not walk the rock fields alone to save money.

Inter-district rules:
- Travel in daylight-dusk if you can, in a group, with bells audible.
- In the unfinished northern rim, surveyors report tall, shy things that stand perfectly still when a lamp is turned on them and are several streets closer when the lamp is turned off. Do not test this. Do not play with your light. Keep it steady, keep moving toward lit districts, and do not go there as a tourist at all.
- Rock goats ride freight platforms. Dwarf yaks migrate slowly across warm ground and will block tracks for hours. Parrots, starlings, swallows and choughs nest in machinery overnight out of wire, blue glass and human hair. Transport delays are biological and geological, not personal. Build half a day of slack into any crossing.
- Meet people by place and event — "by the forge-hammer side of Long Market when the vendors switch to evening lamps" — rather than by precise clock time. In Long Market clocks run slow on wet days; vendors sell by weight rather than by the hour for this reason. Bring a decent mechanical watch for your own body schedule, but expect local time to pool and thin like the air. Confirm appointments in person or by telegraph, and reconfirm.

Maps are useful for district relationships, useless for exact altitude, stair counts, or new streets. Hire a local for your first trips between districts. It's cheap insurance compared to imported everything else.

### 7. Food, Water, Dirt, And Ordinary City Life

Soil is wealth. Real earth is hauled up the lift at ruinous cost and guarded like flour; the rest is crushed slag, ash, and compost made by the city. Do not waste soil, do not dump foreign soil or seeds casually, and do not bring in off-world plants to "see what happens." Life here rushes into any shelter and overdoes itself: ferns in steam vents, acid-yellow and arterial-red mosses colonizing new rock within weeks, fig trees in greenhouse terraces that should die instantly, night-blooming flowers six feet across pollinated by cat-sized bats that arrived in banana crates fifty years ago.

That exuberance cuts both ways. Something you introduce may thrive structurally.

Water comes largely from the dripping ceiling, collected in condensation cisterns over months. Filter and boil it. There are things in the cisterns — pale frogs, and in the flooded, not-yet-plumbed undercrofts of the newest district, eels. Foxes with six toes attend shift-changes for dropped lunches. Assume all standing water has residents.

Food is hearty, foundry-town, and import-dependent. Eat in markets where the air pools and the condensation rains on corrugated roofs. Try the lowland greenhouse produce while you can; accept that bread, earth-grown root vegetables, and anything not adapted here will be expensive.

Money works, trade works better for small favors, and punctual labor is respected. If you want goodwill quickly, carry messages between districts on a tram you're already taking, spell someone counting steps during a move, or help turn screws at Leveling time if — and only if — you're invited and properly briefed.

### 8. Etiquette That Reads As Competence

Residents can tell a visitor who will be fine because they do these things without being asked twice:

- Bell on, code learned, stairs counted aloud.
- Chalk carried, extra doors reported, not opened.
- Watch the sparrows, respect the moths.
- Don't mock the Office of Emergence, the finishers, or the silence at Leveling.
- Don't take cuttings, rock shards, or "souvenir glass" from un-finished windows near the spire. The stone is sharp, warm, and sometimes not done deciding what it is.
- Don't follow a voice that knows your name but not your bell-code. Don't give orders back to it, either, until you've checked with a human present in the room.
- Ask before photographing or recording in newer districts. A recording that plays back a few seconds early is unsettling and occasionally, legally complicated. The council has ruled on whether a voice from a sealed room counts as an instruction. You do not want to generate case law.

People stay in Sessra because nothing has been worn down yet, not even grief. They are not quaint. They are practical people living inside an ongoing creation event with municipal services. Treat their rituals as engineering, not folklore, and they'll reciprocate.

### 9. Leaving

Leave the way you came, by lift, in company, with your bell on until you're well below the ceiling. Check, before you depart, that your lodging's stair count is back to normal and report any discrepancy on your way out — the next tenant needs to know.

On the way down, count things again: your luggage, your companions, yourself in mirrors. If you hear your own footsteps on the funicular platform a moment before you make them, stop, ring, and let a station keeper walk you the last stretch. Echoes thin out as the air thickens. By the forests, by the country that gets the altitude wrong, you'll be yourself in the usual order again.

Except, maybe, for the dreams. Visitors often dream of dim streets for a while afterward, and of windows opening on the plateau twenty seconds ago. That's common. If one ear keeps hearing things early a month later, tell a doctor — and, by post, the Office of Emergence. They keep records, politely, from their side, and they would want to know what the mountain tried to finish in you.

Pack chalk. Wear the bell. Stay low where the sparrows are. Count out loud.

Sessra will do the rest, slowly, generously, and whether you are ready or not — so be as ready as an ordinary human can be.
