This is a place that will trick you, because the two facts about it pull in opposite directions.

Geologically it's dead, old, soft, and warm. Meteorologically and physiologically it's extreme. It's 4,000-5,000 meters up — thin-air altitude, equatorial sun — but it's not a summit. There's no peak to reach and descend from. You just live up there, for days, on a rolling grass plateau under a wind that never stops, in a landscape where everything is miles apart.

Prepare less like you're climbing a mountain, and more like you're going to sea on land.

### 1. Don't arrive at the top

If you can choose your entry point, choose the lowlands.

Teleporting / portalling / dropping directly onto the plateau will hit you with instant altitude sickness: headache, nausea, pounding heart, stupid decisions, steps that feel "light and wrong" in a dangerous way. The locals come up for days through valleys for a reason.

Plan 3-5 days of gradual ascent, sleeping a little higher each night. Walk even if you have transport. At altitude:

* You will be out of breath doing trivial things, then feel weirdly fine, then crash.
* Sleep gets broken and vivid. Judgment gets confident and bad.
* Alcohol, sedatives, and heavy first-night exertion will make it much worse.
* Dehydration is invisible — the wind drinks water off your skin, your breath, your wet clothes, by noon. You will lose far more water than you sweat out.

If you develop a worsening headache that doesn't respond to rest and fluids, confusion, clumsiness, or breathlessness at rest, the only cure is to go down a valley. There is no nearby clinic. The shepherds are seasonal and scattered half a day apart.

Acclimatize until you can walk uphill in wind carrying a pack and still hold a thought.

### 2. The wind is your main environment

Not cold, not rain, not altitude. Wind. Continuous, west, deafening roar with eddies that reverse around every dome.

It will flap clothing until seams sting, rip unsecured objects a literal valley away, make normal conversation impossible, and leave you with ringing ears and a headache from noise alone. At night it does not drop.

**Clothing is a wind system, not a warmth system:**

It's warm in direct sun — hot, even — and instantly cold in shadow or blast. You will be sunburned and wind-chilled on the same cheek.

* A hard, close-fitting windproof outer layer, hood that cinches around the face, cuffs that seal. Nothing flappy: no loose scarves, open jackets, wide skirts, ponchos, unzipped vents. Flapping fabric will exhaust and bruise you.
* Layers underneath that are warm when damp: wool or synthetic. Cotton will stay wet from dew, squalls, and bogs and then get stripped of heat.
* Weighted or tied everything. Locals sew small stones into hems. You should have chin-straps on hats, retainers on sunglasses, lanyards on gloves, tools, map, cup, everything. Never set anything light down "for a second" unweighted. Put a rock on it, or tie it to you.
* Full face and eye protection: wraparound sunglasses / clear goggles for grit, a buff for nose and mouth. Wind-driven grit, grass seed, spray and insects are constant in the afternoon, and UV at equatorial 5,000m is brutal through thin, clear air. Sunscreen exposed skin and lips repeatedly — the wind removes it.
* Hearing protection you can live in: good earplugs for sleeping and for long walking hours, plus earmuff-style defenders for the worst passes. You need to be able to hear a shout mouth-to-ear occasionally, but chronic exposure up there will damage hearing and sanity. Plan for noise fatigue: irritability, exhaustion, inability to think.

Learn to communicate before you go. Shouting doesn't work. Travel partners need: forehead-to-forehead close talking, a set of 10-15 big arm signals (stop, come, down, danger, water, shelter, hurt, go west/east), and a whistle or ground-drumming code if you're splitting between camps. Sound sometimes carries impossibly clearly from an empty ridge and then cuts off — don't trust a heard voice or bell as proof of location or distance.

Drones, kites, umbrellas, loose tents, paper maps unfolded in the open: leave them behind. A paper map should be in a sealed case, tethered, and mostly memorized.

### 3. Shelter must be low, heavy, and boring

There are no trees upright, no cliffs, no sharp talus to hide behind. Only low domes, boulders, and hollows.

If you're not invited into a shepherd's hut — round stone, waist-high door, sod/felt roof netted down with rope and boulders — you need a mountaineering tent re-thought for sustained horizontal wind, not snow.

* Low-profile, geodesic or tunnel aligned to the west wind, as short as you can tolerate. Pitch it in the lee of a boulder or in a hollow, never on an exposed rise, even if the view is better.
* No reliance on thin pegs in peat. The ground alternates between sponge moss, black peat that sighs under boots, and polished old rock. Bring wide deadman anchors, stuff sacks you can fill with smooth stones and bury, and extra cord to tie out to boulders. Expect to weight the whole perimeter with rocks.
* Double-secure rainfly, zips, and doors. The flapping at night will shake everything inside.
* Inside, expect condensation like a rainforest. Racing clouds drag their bellies across the ground. By morning everything — grass, wool, metal — runs with dew. Keep sleep system in waterproof sacks, vent when you can in sun, and accept that by noon the wind will dry what morning soaked.

There is no wood. Fuel up there is dung and root peat, burning hot and quick in a sheltered hearth. If you bring a stove, it must have a full windshield and be usable inside a vestibule dug into lee (with ventilation — carbon monoxide kills faster in a sealed, wind-battered tent), and fuel bottles tethered and weighted. Cold won't kill your stove; wind blowing the flame sideways will.

### 4. Walking, navigating, and not getting bogged

The plateau looks gentle. The walking is tiring in a specific way: springy tussock grass knee-high, cushion moss that weeps water when pressed, hidden pans, peat bogs in flat basins where boots sink, and smooth stone everywhere. No sharp scree cuts boots, but also no edge ever gives purchase.

* High, waterproof boots + gaiters. Your feet will be wet most days from moss, bogs, dew-soaked grass, and sideways squalls, despite it "never properly raining." Bring more dry socks than you think reasonable, rotate them, dry them on your body/in sun at noon halts.
* Trekking poles tethered to wrists help enormously in gusts and bog-testing. Probe suspect flat black ground before committing weight. Bogs aren't usually bottomless, but extracting a leg while being battered and at altitude is exhausting, and a fall soaks you completely.
* Navigation is hard because everything is the same soft ridge repeated to the horizon, cloud shadows race, mist can drop instantly, and magnetic landmarks are subtle. Use cairns — the man-high smooth-stone ones hum in the wind gaps — compass bearings between valleys, sun position, and wind direction (relentlessly west, a free compass, until local eddies fool you around a dome).

Do not rely on streams or lakes for wayfinding. Streams run bright for 100 meters and vanish into cracks. Lakes are mirror-thin, no inlet, filled by condensation overnight and can be mudflat with white salt by evening. A water source on your morning map may be spray by afternoon.

Singing boulders are real landmarks: hollowed, fluted boulders produce distinct steady tones in strong wind, felt in your teeth. A field of them is a broken chord you can navigate by note, if a local or previous traveler has told you which note means which slope.

Walk early if you want relative clarity, but know squalls are visible for miles as grey veils racing in. When you see one: seal gear, turn your back/side, protect eyes, wait 3-10 minutes. It will needle sideways, fill pans, and be gone. Afterward wet grass steams in the sun.

Afternoon is for dust devils, boiling clouds spilling from the plateau edge, and insects tumbling in lee pockets. Keep mouth closed walking through a devil; they deposit sorted drifts of seed, spray, and small shining insects, and will steal hats and loose layers.

### 5. Water, food, and small living

Water is everywhere and nowhere reliable. Carry capacity for at least 2-3 liters per person, and a filter/purifier — peat bogs, sheep dung (you will smell warm peat, herbs, wet stone, sheep dung everywhere), ground-nesting birds, and transient flocks mean even bright moss streams need treatment. Top up at every moss seep and morning lake, before the wind drinks it.

You are burning calories from altitude, wind resistance, and uneven tussock. Food should be dense, protected from damp, and edible with gloves on without cooking. Cooking is a luxury for hut lee and lulls that may not come.

No shade, no trees, no place to sit that isn't damp grass, bog, or rounded rock. Bring a closed-cell sit pad tethered to your pack. Rest in lee pockets behind boulders — that's also where fat bees and butterflies shelter, and where lichen maps grow on rocks. Don't crush cushion moss unnecessarily; it holds the plateau's water and rebuilds very slowly, and locals depend on the pasture.

Fauna is scarce and skittish. Don't chase hares or approach ground nests — those birds literally get thrown by wind to return to them and waste precious energy. The huge scavenger hanging motionless a thousand feet up is doing fine; you don't need to signal it.

### 6. Mind and manners

The emptiness is mental preparation, not just physical. Sky takes three-quarters of the world. Between huts, cairns, flocks there are miles of only grass waving sideways like water. You can walk hours seeing nothing move but cloud shadow. That scale plus constant noise does things: time stretches, you talk louder even alone, you get lonely in company because you can't hear each other.

Build in quiet rituals inside shelter: earplugs, hot drink, checking feet, writing notes (tethered notebook). Approach people like the plants: bent into wind, anchored, forehead-close, patient. Offer to weight, tie, or carry before you offer conversation. Drums through ground between distant huts mean people have long-range systems you won't hear with ears — ask before assuming a valley is empty.

Leave stones as found. Cairns and windbreak Vs (opening away from west) are navigational and survival infrastructure, not souvenirs. Rounded "egg" stones travel; taking one removes a weight someone will need.

**A minimal pack mindset:**

Windproof shell system with all ties, insulating mid-layer, wool base, hats with straps (sun + warm), goggles + sunglasses, earplugs, high boots + gaiters + 4-5 sock sets, low tether-everything tent with stone-anchor kit, stove with full windscreen, water filter + wide capacity, high-calorie no-cook food, compass + sealed route notes, signaling arms/whistle, dry sacks for everything, sit pad, tethers/lanyards/cord in absurd quantity, sunscreen/lip balm, basic altitude medicine discussed with a clinician beforehand.

If you prepare for a cold sharp alpine peak, you'll be overdressed, exhausted, and deafened. If you prepare for a warm, old, endless windy sea of grass that happens to be at 5,000 meters — acclimatize slowly, tie yourself and all you own to the ground, protect ears and eyes, carry water while standing in it, and learn to move low and close — you'll start to hear, underneath the roar, the whistle in the lee of rocks, and the individual notes of the singing stones.
