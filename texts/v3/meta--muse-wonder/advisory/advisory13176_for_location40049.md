This is not a ruin, a natural pool, or a place with anything living in it to meet. It is a brand-new, man-made plunge tank under a wash-house floor — one small, flooded, vaulted cellar that does exactly one thing, very intensely, for a few hours around noon.

Preparation is mostly about timing, heat, glare, and not accidentally breaking a system that is still curing.

### Go at noon, and only at noon

Outside midday this place is diminished. The light comes from a square shaft built straight up through the roof above, lined with white limewash and hand-hammered tin reflectors. Only when the sun is almost overhead does it fall in as a solid column, hit the water, and shatter.

That is when the room becomes itself: turquoise-white, almost shadowless, everything trembling with reflected light. The sun-patch then moves about an inch an hour across the tiles, climbs the far wall, and goes. If you arrive in the morning you will get a dim, milky tank. If you arrive in late afternoon you will mostly get a wet cellar.

Arrive in the wash-house above by late morning. Give yourself time to pause halfway down the stairs — the sound reaches you before the light does, and your eyes will need a minute to adjust both ways.

Plan for a short visit. Twenty to forty minutes in the water is plenty for most people. This is not a place to settle into for an afternoon.

### What you are actually entering

You duck through a low hatch in the wash-house floor — it's thrown back against the wall, and it warps a little more in the humidity every day, so it has to be kicked at one corner, not politely lifted. Someone local should show you which corner. Leave it open while you are below, and ideally leave a companion up top. It is the only way out.

Nine steep steps go down. There is no earth, no rock, no old stone. The walls are new hand-laid brick under thick, fresh lime plaster. The ceiling is a low vault a tall person can touch. The tank takes up four-fifths of the room. Around three sides is a ledge just wide enough for bare feet. In one corner: a low wooden stool and a clay ewer. That is all the furniture there is.

Warm water pours in constantly at head height on the east wall, from a black-painted cistern baking on the roof since dawn. By midday it is bath-hot, thumb-thick from a flattened bronze spout, and it does not stop. In a tiled vault that small, a pour becomes a roar. Overflow gurgles out the opposite wall, drips fall from the ceiling, the drain sighs underfoot. You will have to raise your voice to be understood by someone standing right beside you.

Agree on hand signals before you go down: *up, out, I'm fine, help*. Do not plan on conversation down there.

The air is steam, caught in the light shaft, condensing on the cooler vault and raining back down in fat, irregular drops. Everything you take down will get wet even if it never touches the tank.

### Dress and what to leave above

Leave almost everything in the wash-house.

Wear swimwear you don't mind exposing to warm, slightly alkaline water — the fresh mortar is still leaching, and for the first hour after a refill the water can be faintly milky and smell like wet chalk warmed in sun. It stings broken skin, freshly shaved skin, and eyes. Don't open your eyes underwater more than a blink; underwater everything looks bleached and magnified anyway, and it isn't worth the burn.

Go barefoot, but go carefully. The plunge is lined with pale, unglazed terracotta tiles, fired unevenly, with corners sharp enough to catch a knee or elbow badly. No running, no kneeling, no bracing yourself against the edges as you turn. The broad steps *into* the water are the safest place to sit and acclimatize. Once you're in, you can stand chest-deep in the middle and turn slowly without touching anything. Intend to do exactly that.

Do not bring electronics, paper, leather, or anything that hates steam down the steps. Condensation rains indoors here. A towel, dry clothes, drinking water, and anything valuable stay up top on dry ground. There is nowhere dry to set them below — the ledge is for feet, the stool is for scrubbing, not storage.

Wash off before you enter. Sunscreen, lotions, insect repellent, hair products, and street sweat will film a tank that is kept clean precisely because it's too hot and too constantly replaced for anything to live in it. The morning routine is a stiff brush and a wooden paddle, no soap in the water itself. Use the ewer and facilities above, then go in clean.

### Heat, water, and your body

Hydrate before, not from the tank. Do not drink it — roof cistern, warm storage, fresh lime. It's for plunging.

Ease in. Sit on the submerged steps first and let the heat come through the soles of your feet. By noon the inflow is genuinely bath-hot and the tank has been warming all morning. Chest-deep immersion will raise your core temperature faster than you expect, especially with steam preventing cooling above the surface.

Get out if you feel light-headed, nauseous, or your heart is pounding. There are only those nine steep, wet steps between you and cooler air, and fainting on them is the main real danger here. Move slowly, use the wall, watch for slippery lime and tile. Tall travelers: mind both the low vault and the spout at head height on the east wall.

Keep immersion short if you have eczema, sensitive skin, asthma aggravated by steam, low blood pressure, or claustrophobia. The room is small, low, loud, bright to the point of glare, and the hatch is out of sight above you. It feels larger because it's empty and echoing, but it isn't.

Consider bringing sunglasses for the first minute at the top of the shaft-adapted stairwell when you come back up, or simply close your eyes and pause. Going from shadowless turquoise glare to ordinary daylight and back is disorienting.

### The one rule you must not break

**Keep it flooded, keep it warm, keep the spout running.**

That was the mason's instruction, and it's structural, not ritual. The plaster must stay wet while it cures or it will crack. If someone shuts the wooden sluice-gate above by its rope pulley and lets the room cool and empty, by morning the tiles tick as they contract, the surface dulls and films, and damage is done.

So: do not touch the sluice. Do not drain it to "see the bottom better." Do not stop the inflow because the noise is too much — if the noise is too much, leave. The normal state of this place is fill, heat, overflow, condense, drip, repeat. The water level even rises and sighs down an inch when the overflow clears. That is not a malfunction, and there is nothing under the drain sighing back at you.

Likewise, leave the first life alone. There are three tiny maidenhair ferns, fronds no bigger than coins, where the inflow pipe sweats through the plaster; a thread of green algae barely visible from an angle in the shadiest bottom corner; and one reed on the ledge, roots in a crack, leaning for the light. Do not pull them, plant anything, release anything, or bring in soil, food, or animals from another world. Nothing lives here yet on purpose — the fresh lime discourages insects, the flow discourages everything else. Your job as a visitor is to keep it that uncolonized.

Do not lean hard on fresh plaster, knock the tin reflectors out of alignment, or set anything on the lip of the light shaft above. This was finished only this spring. The edges haven't softened.

### Getting out and after

Climb out slowly, in reverse order of your entrance. Your legs will be warm, a little unsteady, and the ledge is narrow. Dry above, not below. Rinse off with clean water from above as soon as you can — that faint alkaline film feels tight as it dries. Moisturize, dry your ears well after all that roaring water and humidity, drink water with a little salt or food, and sit somewhere cool and dim for ten minutes. People commonly underestimate how much heat and glare they absorbed because nothing *happened*.

Nothing happening is the visit. Stand in the middle, don't fight the noise, watch heat-shimmer rise off the bottom if you stay still, watch the hard square of sun inch across new tile. There is nowhere else to go inside it and nothing else to meet, and travelers who come expecting a creature, a ruin, or a secret passage will be disappointed.

Travelers who come prepared for a small, newly-built hollow holding heat and light and sound all together for an hour under an ordinary floor — and who leave it still running, still full, exactly as they found it — will have seen it correctly.
