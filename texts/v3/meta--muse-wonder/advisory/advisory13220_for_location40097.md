This is not a town you visit so much as a current you step into. It will not pause for you, dry out for you, or quiet down for you. Prepare for three facts that will govern everything else:

1. **You will be wet, all the time, in layers.**
2. **You will never be alone, or in silence, or in a space that is only human.**
3. **Nothing stays where you put it** — not your shoes, not your route across town, not the market stall you were just talking to.

An ordinary human can do very well here, and people clearly have for centuries, but only if you stop expecting to keep things dry, clean, private, and finished.

### Before You Arrive: Pack For Rot, Not For Rain

A single rainstorm is not the problem. Constant humidity is.

Don't bring paper books, untreated leather journals, cheap steel, or anything electronic you need to survive. Iron and steel rust visibly fast here, leather mildews, paper softens to pulp, glue lets go. If you bring metal tools, oil them daily and expect them to be politely pitied — locals treasure a good steel knife precisely because keeping one is so much work. They trust stone, bone, wood, flint, copper, bronze, and knots.

Pack in small, separate bundles wrapped in oiled cloth or waxed canvas, not one big bag. You *will* drop something in a channel. You want to lose one bundle, not soak everything.

Clothing:
- **Wool, not cotton, for your base layers.** Wool smells strong when damp — everything here does — but it stays warm when wet. Cotton will stay cold against your skin from the stair climb to the day you leave.
- Two complete changes, minimum, and three sets of socks and underclothes. You will rotate them constantly: one on you, one drying (never fully) over a balcony rail, one sealed dry for sleeping and for the descent. A truly dry set of sleep clothes, kept in a double-wrapped oiled sack you only open in bed, is the difference between discomfort and getting sick.
- A wide-brimmed hat and a hooded outer layer of oiled cloth. Umbrellas are useless in lanes four feet wide with balconies overhead, goats on stairs, and wind that circles the bowl instead of blowing through it.
- **Footwear is your most important decision.** The approach stair is an hour of dished, wet slate with water running across it, algae in the hollows, and moss at the edges. Smooth soles will kill you here. Bring boots or heavy sandals with deep wooden, rope, or heavily textured tread, a snug ankle, and drain holes. Bring a second pair. A shoe left by a door overnight will be mossy by morning and may have a duck investigating it. Shake out and inspect shoes every time you put them on — for water, frogs, spiders, and rot. Never go barefoot: lanes are packed earth, animal dung, sharp flint chippings, and open water channels.

Bring earplugs, but only partial ones. And a sleep mask. And tolerance.

Bring gifts that make sense here: good bone needles, strong cord that doesn't rot easily, copper wire, salt in a sealed pot, dried foods in wax, high-quality steel *if* you present it oiled and as a luxury. Don't bring seeds casually — this place grows aggressively, and introducing a new runner could be a real social offense.

Get fit for stairs before you come. The climb is not technical, it's relentless, wet, and shared with a near-continuous line of laden donkeys coming down as you go up. There is nowhere wide to rest. Travel up in the morning, after the gate is opened and the first herds have moved, but before the afternoon shower makes the steps a stream.

### The Arrival

You will hear it long before the gate: waterfall, bells, frogs, geese, wooden clappers, voices shouting in ordinary conversation. Don't mistake volume for anger. Here, everyone talks at what would be a shout anywhere else because the waterwheels never stop.

The gate is a gap in a living hedge, shut at dusk not to keep people out but to keep stock in. Arrive after dark and you will be sleeping outside it, wet, with the animals who also missed curfew. Arrive before noon if you can.

Once inside, stop just past the gate and look — not at houses, at water. Learn the braided channels. Every lane, courtyard, and argument in this town is organized around who is upstream from whom. The spring at the upper end, under the black overhang, is the only place to drink untreated. That water is achingly cold and clean. Downstream it is duck bath, washing stone, frog pond, pig trough, and millrace all at once. Fill at the spring, or drink what has been boiled for tea/cooking. Never cup water from a handy runnel in the market, however clear it looks. Ten feet above you a child, a goose, and a basket of mushrooms are in it.

Ask immediately, out loud and to a specific person, whose channel-edge you can store your pack on, where visitors sleep, and who is below you. Those are not separate questions here.

### Living in Density

There is no empty space. Every lane is also a duck patrol route, a sheep drive, a balcony, a laundry line, and a vine bridge. Every roof is a meadow with a tethered goat on it. Every eave has swallow nests. Kitchens have chickens. Doors have a resident toad who is not a pet so much as staff — do not move it, feed it scraps only if the household does.

Practical rules to internalize on day one:

- **Yield to animals, always, without making a drama of it.** A lane will wait for a single file of ducks. Geese on the market steps *are* the authority; do not try to shoo them, walk around where they indicate with hisses. If a herd seals a street for fifteen minutes, that street is closed. Life moves to the second story — balcony planks, hand-to-hand baskets. Learn those crossings from children and old people, who use them without thinking, but test planks before trusting your weight; wood swells, ropes darken and stretch.
- **Do not touch the infrastructure without asking.** Don't cut a vine off a window, trim a house-corner willow, move a sluice board, or clear a channel into someone else's yard. House-trees require a whole lane's agreement to fell, with a replacement already knee-high. Each household must keep its stretch of channel clear — and the household *below* you has the legal right to walk through your yard to clear it if you don't. If you're lodging with someone, offer twenty minutes each morning with a wooden rake. This is the local equivalent of paying rent and saying good morning.
- **Never build, block, or pile gear over the main water, and never go on the mill roof.** Goats can graze any other roof, not that one. Respect it.
- **Expect to be moved.** After a 2 p.m. shower — brief, violent, announced by the big bronze bell at the spring to clear the lower bridge and open the second sluice — channels rise three inches in ten minutes. A flotilla of leaves, feathers, cups, and lost toys goes through town. The market reassembles two feet from where it was, under a different goat. This is normal. If you hear that bell, get off low bridges, move anything of yours uphill from a runnel, and help snatch goods under awnings. The snap of oiled cloth unrolling from balconies is your cue.

Your sense of privacy needs recalibrating. Balconies almost touch. Babies wake each other house to house. Dogs — dozens, all employed — run informal messages and will follow you from house to house herding you and any nearby ducks. Conversations are window-to-window. Assume you are overheard, smelled (wet wool, woodsmoke, pig pens that are also retaining walls, bee skeps under balconies on warm afternoons), and watched by humans, goats from above, and pigeons from below the eaves. Dress and wash accordingly — at designated washing stones, upstream of drinking troughs, downstream of the spring, ask where.

If you have allergies to feathers, animal dander, pollen, bee stings, or mold, medicate seriously before coming. Bees drift over the square, blossom blows in drifts, thatch harbors mice and fat spiders, damp stone grows algae and moss everywhere. Bring antihistamines and, if bee-stung badly before, whatever your world uses for that and tell your host household where it is. A toad by the door eats flies; spiders in the rafters do real work. Don't clear them.

### Eating, Sleeping, Staying Healthy

Bread softens almost as soon as it's out of the closed ovens. Food is made constantly in small batches and eaten quickly. Don't hoard fresh food in your pack — it will mold and attract mice in a night. Eat cooked, eat hot, eat with people. Market waste goes to pigs; don't throw food in channels, ducks notwithstanding, without watching what locals throw.

Cold is subtle here. Air stays cool even at noon high up, mist comes both up from the ground and down from the rim at night, lamps become amber dots in white. Wet + cool + wind circling the bowl is a recipe for a chill you don't notice until you're shivering in damp bedding. Change out of day-wet clothes before sleep, even if the dry set is only *less* wet.

Sleep will be broken. Waterwheels slow but don't stop. The fall at the notch roars. Frogs after rain make a physical pressure. Owls, pigs grunting in wall-pens, babies answering babies, roofs dripping onto full barrels. Use light earplugs that still let the spring bell through — that bell is a safety system, not a church decoration. Expect to wake damp and to the dawn eruption of thousands of swallows and pigeons changing places under the eaves, sounding like water poured overhead. That's your alarm; the gate opens when the dogs wake, which is everyone.

Foot care is medical care here. Dry feet as often as you can, rotate socks, inspect for cuts — every cut will be in manure-rich water within the hour. Clean any nick with spring water and boiled water, cover it, and keep it out of channels. Watch for persistent cough from mold or trench-type foot rot if you're staying more than a few days.

### Moving Around and Leaving

Move slowly. Flat stones in lanes are set in root mats and slick with algae. Roof meadows are not walkways. The notch at the lower end where water pours out as a white fall into cloud below is spectacular — you can stand with rain falling from clouds level with your boots — and it's wet rock with no rail worth trusting. Go with a local, in morning light, and don't carry anything you can't afford to drop into weather.

Plan departure for a dry-ish morning window, which here means "not actively raining." The descent is more dangerous than the climb: dished steps, water threads, donkeys, your own tired knees. Wear your best-tread shoes, keep hands free for the cliff-side roots, and seal everything you want to arrive dry below. Expect nothing to stay dry anyway.

Leave your lodging better negotiated than you found it: channel raked, a wall re-woven if you were taught how, thatch checked, your dry sack space empty of forgotten damp clothing that will be a moss farm by next week. Ask if your visit used water someone below needed. Pay in labor and useful, rot-resistant goods more readily than coin.

If you prepare for a picturesque mountain village, you'll be miserable. If you prepare for living briefly inside a rapid — old, balanced, beautiful, and never for one minute still — you'll understand why people stay.
