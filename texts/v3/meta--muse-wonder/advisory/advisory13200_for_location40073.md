This is not a dangerous place in the usual way. It is not dark, cold, deep, or full of things that have learned how to kill you.

That is, in fact, the preparation problem.

You are visiting a room where most of the rules have not finished being decided — light, shadow, sound, time, hunger, geology, even what your own body is made of — and so you cannot rely on ordinary cave sense. Prepare less like a spelunker and more like a guest in a nursery: go softly, go briefly, go reversibly, and do not teach anything there anything permanent.

### 1. Understand what you are entering

From above, there is almost nothing: a shallow dip in cool grass on a hill, ground that gives slightly underfoot, roots and loose chalk, and a belly-width opening. You will expect a dark burrow.

Instead you wriggle into a pocket about twelve slow paces across and maybe four paces high in the center. It is entirely underground, with no shaft or crack to explain the light, and yet it is brighter inside than outside — a clean, white, midday light with no heat and no source. It does not fall so much as gather, pooling most strongly in the empty center.

The stone is soft and undecided. Chalk like cheese, sandstone that crumbles to warm sand and then grains itself back together, new crystals you can dent with a thumbnail. The whole room breathes over about an hour — ceiling lowering a finger's width, sighing, rising again. Ledges change relative height because the floor politely sinks or swells. Pebbles measurably grow.

It is crowded with provisional life around the edges: translucent salamanders who haven't decided about losing their gills, white crickets that land a moment *before* they jump, pillbugs that roll up perfectly and then forget how to unroll, chalk-colored frogs, a fully grown mouse with sealed eyes that navigates perfectly, bats entirely unbothered by the brightness that hang in a row and periodically all open their mouths at once, silently.

Sound arrives late, or elsewhere, or not at all. Predation is practiced rather than performed. Webs hold light, not flies, and flies walk through them and come out fatter. Bats spiral through the central column with mouths open, drinking light, their bellies glowing faintly afterward.

Nothing finishes. That is the deepest rule to prepare for.

### 2. Before you go: set up your anchors outside

Do not make your only way home *inside* the room. Anchor everything on the hill above, in finished reality.

* **Tell/bring someone who stays outside.** This is non-negotiable. Your shadow will not follow you in — you will be able to see it, dark and patient, flattened against the dirt at the entrance, waiting for you to come out. Your voice may not reach the surface on time, or at all. Your sense of elapsed time inside will not be trustworthy. You need a person in ordinary time whose only job is to watch the dip in the grass, keep the entrance clear, and pull you out on schedule whether you signal or not.
* **Agree on a hard time limit in advance.** One hour for a first visit. Less is better. Not because something will attack you at hour two, but because the place's unfinishedness is cumulative and subtle: fingernails soften, a scratched knuckle un-bleeds and reconsiders, the fern has opened a hundred times and stayed young. We don't know what a long stay rehearses in a human. Teeth, bone, old healed injuries, habits, intentions — treat them all as potentially negotiable if you linger.
* **Set two independent outside timers** and one simple mechanical watch you carry in. Do not rely on phones, GPS, or any portal/compass/navigation that assumes space and time hold still. Dust motes in there drift and reappear a hand's breadth to the left. Your instruments may record that honestly and be useless.
* **Decide your single purpose before entry.** Observe the bowls. Watch the bats. Sit by the moss. Pick *one*. Inside, tasks have a way of slumping before they set, like the little mud towers. Travelers report the mental version of that: you will find yourself having started three things and gently revised all of them. Write your purpose on your wrist in ink.
* **Eat, drink, and use the toilet beforehand.** Bring nothing edible in. See below.

### 3. What to wear and carry

Think soft, small, clean, and retrievable. You are crawling on your belly through roots and chalk, into a place where hard things are still being invented.

**Wear:**
- Old, close-fitting clothes you don't mind returning chalk-white. No loose straps, dangling cords, or wide packs — the entrance is just wide enough to wriggle through.
- Soft shoes or barefoot socks, not rigid boots. The floor is soft, absorbs footsteps, and is actively growing. Boots wound it, and it heals over wounds in loose, undecided shapes you don't want to be standing in.
- Light layers for a cool cellar damp, but be ready for the damp to be intermittent, as if the room is only trying out the idea of moisture. The light gives no heat despite its brightness.
- Sunglasses or a hand ready to shade your eyes. Your pupils will be prepared for underground dark. The shock is brightness.
- Gloves you can remove. You will want bare fingers to understand how soft things are, but cover any open cuts *before* entering. Broken skin is an unfinished thing, and the room has opinions about those.

**Carry (pocket-sized only):**
- A length of soft, bright cord tethered to your outside spotter, with plenty of slack. Check it with a tug every few minutes — not because the room is malicious, but because rock can heal over a line and pebbles can grow around it while you're looking elsewhere. Never tie it tightly around a limb.
- A mechanical watch, a pencil, and a small paper notebook. Pencil marks are provisional enough to work. Expect photographs to overexpose in the center, show a ceiling that isn't there, or show you with four faint disagreeing shadows.
- Your own sealed water in a soft flask, for emergency only.
- A small hand mirror or polished token on a cord. Useful in a place with no reliable shadow for judging where light/space are pooling, and for checking bowls without leaning over them.

**Do not bring:**
- Drills, hammers, chisels, cement, specimen jars, nets, traps, or collecting tools of any kind.
- Mature seeds, cuttings, soil from elsewhere, food, or live bait. The vegetation there is a first draft: one pale fern that uncurls in the morning and carefully curls shut by afternoon, three almost-transparent grass shoots leaning homesick toward the entrance, one palm-sized patch of moss that *moves* a few inches a day leaving a damp trail, eating shadow where the wall curves. A single finished, competitive plant or microbe colony from outside could teach the whole room a final answer it wasn't ready for.
- Loud equipment, bright lamps, or fire. It needs none of these, and sudden finished noise/heat is the rudest thing you can introduce.
- Anything you cannot afford to have gently revised — softened, grown slightly, un-finished, or left a hand's breadth from where you put it.

Clean your shoes and clothes beforehand and arrive free of pesticides, insect repellent, and strong scent. Everything in there is learning what to eat by eating things it shouldn't.

### 4. Entry protocol

1. Your spotter marks the dip in the grass discreetly from above and tests the ground gently — remember, it gives like the soft spot on an infant's skull. Do not widen the opening. Do not dig it into a proper doorway. Enlarging it may be the decision that finishes it.
2. Go in head-first on your belly, arms ahead, tether trailing, slowly. Pause halfway when the brightness hits and let your eyes adjust with them closed first, then squinted.
3. Once inside, stop just past the entrance and look back. Confirm three things and say them aloud for your own memory (your spotter may hear you seconds later, or from the wrong direction, or not at all): the entrance is open, your shadow is waiting there, your tether is loose. Noticing the shadow calmly the first time prevents a very human panic later — inside, your hands may cast no shadow, or four very faint ones. You have not lost something essential. It is simply not allowed in yet.
4. Stand up slowly in the lower part of the room. The center domes highest, but the ceiling breathes down over the hour. If you are tall, keep a soft awareness above your head.

### 5. How to move and behave inside

**Keep to the margins, keep the center empty.** Every living thing there does. The center is where the light pools in a column, where the bats feed, where motes vanish and reappear displaced. Walking through it occasionally seems to do no harm, but do not stand in it to eat, camp, set up equipment, place a portal, or hold a long conversation. It is reverently unoccupied for a reason we don't understand, and your body displacing that much gathered light is the largest experiment you can run.

**Move as if sound will betray you by arriving late.** It will. Your footsteps will be absorbed. Dripping water makes no tick. A cricket's scrape may reach you seconds later from the far corner, as if the noise had to walk across the room. So:
- Use visual signals with any companion inside: raised hand = stop, hand to chest = I'm leaving now, both hands up = pull the tether.
- Never rely on a shouted warning, a whispered plan, or a creature's noise to judge its distance. The groan of rock shifting may be felt in your molars rather than heard.
- If you drop something, look a little to the left of where you expect it.

**Touch like a nurse, not a geologist.** You may press the new crystal clusters gently — they are wet-looking and pliable. You may let crumbled sandstone regrain in a pile if it happens in your palm. But do not carve initials, pry samples, stack the pebbles, or complete the little structures around the edges: the miniature arch spanning an inch-wide gap to nowhere, the hollow mud towers with one round doorway each too small for even the salamanders, the nest woven to the scale of a much larger remembered bird, the three flat stones near the entrance stacked into steps that the rising floor made unnecessary. Do not finish them for them. Do not knock them down, either. They are being built and rebuilt as they slump. That is the work.

**Give all animals the respect of fellow beginners.** They are not tame; they have not learned fear correctly yet, which means they will not reliably flee OR reliably stay away.
- The pale coin-sized spider's webs hold light and spill brightness when disturbed, nourishing the flies that walk through. Don't clear them away as you move.
- The salamander hunting a cricket will likely close its mouth an inch to the side, and both will look surprised. Don't "help" either party. Introducing successful predation is still an intervention.
- The bats, when they wake at what should be evening (it never dims enough to call it anything but day inside), fly slow spirals drinking light. Duck softly, keep mouth closed in the center column, and let them pass. Their bellies glowing afterward is normal.
- Do not feed anything. The frogs snapping at motes and moss eating shadow are metabolisms still being drafted. A crumb from a finished world could become a permanent lesson.

### 6. The three bowls: specific cautions

In the floor are three shallow bowls grown or worn into the rock. Treat all three as instruments you don't know how to read.

1. **The almost-always-dry one** with a darker stain in the shape of water — observe only.
2. **The one holding a cupful of clear liquid that reflects a ceiling that is not the one above it** — do not touch the surface trying to fix the reflection. Use your pocket mirror beside it if you must compare. Photographing it is the likeliest way to bring home an image you will not enjoy explaining.
3. **The one that fills only when you are not watching it**, brimming and still with no ripple, surface faintly warm despite the cool air — this is the one travelers are tempted to drink from because it looks offered. **Do not drink, taste, bottle, or dip fingers in any liquid in the room,** including this. A place where water drops can slide sideways, hang trembling below the ceiling, and evaporate mid-fall has not finished deciding what water does in a body. Your own sealed water is for emergency; sip it with your back to the bowls to avoid absent-minded confusion.

If you find a bowl fuller or emptier than your last look, note the time on paper and move on. Checking it repeatedly to catch it changing appears to prevent the change and, anecdotally, makes travelers stubbornly waste their whole hour.

### 7. Body effects to monitor

Check yourself every fifteen minutes — set your watch to vibrate or your spotter to tug the cord twice — for:

- Fingernails or toenails feeling softer; rings feeling looser or tighter as small pebble-like growth/revision happens to skin.
- Old scars itching, a recent scratch looking less decided, any sensation of a healed thing *reconsidering*. If a wound reopens or, stranger, begins to un-heal in a way that worries you, leave.
- Disorientation from shadowlessness. Without a shadow your brain loses a quiet anchor for where you and the light are. If you feel vertigo, sit with your back (lightly) toward a wall, close your eyes, and name five marginal sounds and when they actually reach you.
- Temporal drift: the feeling that you have "plenty of time." You do not. That feeling is the room's courtesy, not information. Obey the outside timer, not your impression.

Leave immediately if: the entrance looks smaller when you check it, your tether goes slack *and* taut in a way you can't explain, the bats all open their mouths and you begin to feel rather than hear a pressure building, the light in the center pools bright enough to wash out the far wall entirely, or you cannot remember your written purpose. None of these necessarily mean hostility. They mean a revision is underway and a full-sized finished human is a complicated sentence to revise around.

### 8. Exit and return to finished reality

Plan to leave before you feel ready. Back out the way you came, belly-first, while your spotter keeps the cord from snagging on growing ledges. Expect a moment of real emotional strangeness as you pass through the entrance and your waiting shadow reattaches — travelers describe it as being rejoined by a patient pet, or by their own capacity to block light. Sit in the real grass afterward until your shadow behaves singly and promptly.

Then, outside:

- **Account for time.** Compare your mechanical watch, your notebook times, and your spotter's timers. Record discrepancies without trying to reconcile them.
- **Check your body in daylight** — nails, knuckles, old injuries, teeth with your tongue. Most softening firms up within hours in finished air; note anything that doesn't.
- **Do not wash chalk/sand off into a garden or houseplant soil right away.** Brush off dry over a tray, seal the dustings and any damp clothing in a bag for a day. This is partly ecological courtesy (you may be carrying provisional spores, crystal seeds, or a few inches of ambitious moss) and partly because piles of that sandstone have been known to re-grain themselves together in overnight heaps. It's harmless. It's better watched on a tray.
- **Debrief by hand, on paper, the same day** — but accept that some details will slump before they set. Write the firmest facts first: entry time, exit time, who was spotter, any body changes. Leave blank space for the parts that were revised.
- **Wait at least a full day before returning,** and never visit twice in one day to "check if the bowl filled." Repeated, watching human attention is itself a kind of finishing pressure.

### A final note on attitude

Bring curiosity without ambition. This room may someday sigh shut and harden into a proper black, cold, dripping cave with final eyeless fauna, or sigh outward and unroof into a small valley that lets real weather in. Either ending is its own business.

Your job is not to preserve it forever, excavate it, rescue it, or complete it. It is to enter a bright place under a hill where the world is still quietly trying things out — light that gathers, water that hasn't learned to fall, animals rehearsing being animals — and come back out with most of your finishedness intact, having taught it as little as possible, and having let it teach you, briefly, that not everything has to have decided what it is before it can be alive.

Go in soft-shod, short-staying, shadow-expectant, and prepared to leave unfinished things unfinished.
