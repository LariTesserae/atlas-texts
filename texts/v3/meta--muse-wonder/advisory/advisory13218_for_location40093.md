This is not a cave, a forest, or a ruin. It is a single, living, cold-generating organism that is also a computer, a nursery, and an unfinished geological system — about thirty paces across, sunk below a frozen plain, made entirely of interwoven root, leaf, stem, and wet.

Prepare as if you are visiting a slow, intelligent reef that lives in negative heat. Most survival gear assumes the environment is passive. This one is not. It will read you, reroute itself around you, sample you, and very politely begin to find a use for you. The danger is not that it will attack. The danger is that it will accommodate you until leaving feels unnecessary.

Do not go alone, do not go un-tethered, and do not go without a pre-committed extraction.

### 1. The Three Rules to Internalize

**1. The plants are the cold.**
Above ground, cold is weather you insulate against. Below, cold radiates directionally outward from leaf veins, roots, and stems the way heat radiates from a stove. Frost forms on living, supple tissue. Brine beads on leaves and does not freeze. Cold will come at you from below through the mat, from the sides at knee and shoulder height, and it will be steady, not gusty. Standard winter layering works, but your feet and hands — in contact with the source — will chill far faster than your core.

**2. Nothing moves fast, and soon neither will you.**
This is physiological/chemical, not just psychological. Fronds recoil over minutes. Insects take off one separated wingbeat at a time. Drips swell and hesitate. After 10-20 minutes you will find you have been reaching for something for longer than you intended. Your attention becomes deliberate. Your joints accept the local tempo. This feels peaceful. It is the main trap.

You must set your time limit *before* descent, by mechanical timer, and give your surface team authority to haul you out even if you radio down that you are fine and want to stay a little longer. You will mean it. That is the problem.

Recommended first visit: 30-45 minutes below the ice line, maximum. No first visit over an hour. No sleeping, no sitting for more than a few minutes, no lying down — ever, and especially not in the empty hollow on the far side (see below).

**3. Sound is amplified, layered, and meaningful.**
For a cold, wet pit, it is shockingly loud. Leaf-drums pitched by dripping water, roots creaking around the whole bowl so the sound returns from behind you, ice singing inside hollow stems, thumbnail frogs clicking in synchronized quadrants, and a subsonic hum you will feel in your molars and the small bones of your feet before you hear it.

Bring soft ear protection you can vary — not total plugs, you need situational awareness, but dampers. Shout once and you will regret it; voices come back before you finish speaking. Plan to whisper, and better, to use slow hand signals agreed in advance. Fast, percussive noises — a dropped metal tool, a camera shutter bank, a drone — will be enormous, disorienting, and will ripple through the whole system. If the frog-clicks and drips suddenly entrain or stop, leave. You have become a loud event in their conversation.

### 2. Gear: What to Wear

**A smooth, sealed, sacrificial outer suit.** Hair-fine roots will find boot seams, cuffs, zippers, and fabric weave within minutes, not aggressively but curiously. They are probing whether you are weather. Wear a one-piece, low-texture shell over insulation with taped seams, integrated gloves and hood. After exit, that outer layer is to be peeled off in the sponge/ice transition zone, bagged, and quarantined — do not bring it back to your home dimension untreated. Self-repairing moth pollen, spores, luminous lichen mites, and smaller things on the mites will be on you.

**No crampons, spikes, cleats, or hard lug soles.** There is no floor. You will stand on woven thigh-thick roots, rope-braids, and trembling membranes of mat and leaf. Point loads cut and wound the computer. Wear broad, soft, snowshoe-like root shoes or heavily padded overshoes that spread your weight. The mat will, eerily, begin to reroute a footpath around your boot *before* you fully weight it — pause half a beat before each step and let it. Do not stomp to test solidity. If you push a hand forearm-deep you will only find denser, wetter growth. There is no bottom to find.

**Closed-circuit breathing, if you can manage it.** Your breath does not rise there. It sinks and is harvested in real time by thousands of mouths on frond undersides. The grove will return filtered, resin-and-ozone air, and breathing open is not immediately toxic. But open breathing is *participation* — you are feeding it CO2, water vapor, heat, and a chemical read of you. For a short, least-interference visit, use a low-noise rebreather with a full face seal. It also protects you from pollen and spore load. If you do breathe open, expect dry mouth despite saturated air, and expect the plants nearest your face to uncurl toward you.

Bring your own sealed water. Do not drink from the cupped black leaves. Do not trail fingers in pools. The pools are inhabited (translucent frogs, other looking things) and have an optical wrongness — your reflection assembles after a delay, slightly displaced. Whether that is processing, predation, or perception lag, treat all standing water as a sensory organ. Do not eat anything while below. The closed loop — warm worms excreting heat and ammonia, fronds drinking it, frogs eating mite-eating lichen, clicking shaking spores loose — has no surplus designed for a mammal, and introducing outside food waste will be enthusiastically incorporated.

**Very dim, diffuse light only.** It is already lit, reluctantly: a blue-green suffusion from the sky column in the center, veins that light along a stem and go out, berries that brighten when you move, lichen that glows only on its far side so you see halos, never sources. A headlamp is a weapon here. It will bleach your night vision (which will keep adjusting for your entire stay; shadows lag behind objects), trigger reading-berries for meters around, and probably be recorded. Carry a single shielded, deep-red or dim green light, pointed down at the mat, and use it in seconds-long bursts. Prefer to wait and let your eyes work.

Leave behind: heaters, heat packs in boots (they create a thaw that fauna will rush and that reads as a false worm-signal), open flames, ultrasonic repellers, fast drones, metal sampling tools, soil corers, plant presses. Anything cut here is not a sample, it is an amputation from one body.

### 3. Descent and Entry

You enter through a sink in snow hard as fired clay, then blue ice full of trapped air, then a dark, warm-smelling sponge band that is neither ice nor soil. That band is the threshold — alive, giving under gloves.

Be lowered by winch/rope from the surface, do not climb free. The transition layers are fragile and are literally the roof the grove is precipitating into future stone; mineral is still soft curd and jelly among roots. Use a padded harness, go feet-first, slow. Keep the line taut but not sawing against edges. Your surface team stays up in the crude, fast wind — they are your clock-keepers precisely because they are *not* in the tempo.

On first contact with root, stop. Stand still for two full minutes. Let fronds finish recoiling from your shadow, let berries read you and fade, let hair-roots test your overshoes, let the mat warm slightly under you. Sudden movement in the first minutes causes the whole weave to spend energy rerouting. Speak your entry call *before* you are fully below the ice — your voice will arrive strangely down there anyway.

Note the center column of overcast sky. That is your only reliable orientation. Walls are vertical tangles braced against unformed mud, not stone. Compasses and sensitive electronics cannot be trusted over a superconducting root-mat where signals move instantaneously and without heat. Carry a mechanical watch, a mechanical compass as a curiosity only, and a physical guideline back to your drop line. Do not wander by following interesting glowing clusters — they brighten *because* you approach, which creates a path that follows you.

### 4. How to Move and Behave Inside

Move at about one-quarter your natural speed, *deliberately*, before the place enforces it. Economy is safety. Plan each handhold — but expect stems to fold *a breath before* contact, not after. Do not grab in surprise if support withdraws; sit back onto the mat rather than clutching. It will cushion a shape that it predicts.

Stay out of leaf-pools, do not step into cupped leaves, skirt the thaw-trails left by the warm segmented worms. Those worms are little furnaces and other organisms time their lives around those brief thaws; stepping there is stepping into a queue. Pale frost moths blunder intentionally to spread pollen — do not swat them, they clatter softly and will cover you anyway, hence the sacrificial suit. The bird-shaped things do not fly so much as fall controllably and call once with voices too large for their bodies; duck slowly, do not duck fast.

Do not try to decode. Frost by your knee will resolve into branching diagrams, fractal vascular patterns, things like lung diagrams, then shiver back to frost at a frog click. Berries lighting in train, packets of bright motes sorting at stem junctions, sap that carries information — this is thought moving through frozen tissue at speeds sound cannot match, and the dripping/ticking/humming is overflow. Staring and pattern-matching accelerates the tempo capture. Note, photograph only if you brought a slow, quiet, low-light mechanical camera (flash is prohibited), and keep moving.

**The Sleeper Hollow:** Near the far wall the growth parts around nothing, a curled-sleeper-sized empty space. Air is coldest there, hum clarifies to a matchable tone, and condensation hangs in empty air in a drifting bead-lattice that rearranges when unobserved. Things are kept there in potential — seeds, unneeded fauna, the next grove. Do not enter it. Do not put a hand, tool, or sensor into it. Do not lie in it to rest. It is the one place the grove has explicitly kept clear, and the invitation you will feel in your joints to curl there is the strongest manifestation of the place wanting to store you while it decides what you are for.

### 5. Exit and After-Effects

Begin ascent while you still feel you could easily stay another half hour. That feeling is your cue you are already late.

Be winched up at a steady, slow rate through sponge, ice, snow. On the surface, the wind will seem crude, fast, and oddly silent. Do not immediately operate vehicles, fly, climb, or do precision work. Your hands will continue at grove tempo and your trust in their speed will lag for hours. Sit with your surface team, warm up *gradually* (outside heat applied fast to frost-rimed tissue patterns is painful and confusing to nerves trained down there), and do a verbal debrief while memory of grammar-like intervals in creaks and clicks is fresh — you will lose it as you speed up.

Quarantine hard, both directions. You were sterilized as best as possible going in to protect a closed, tightening ecology that had no mammal diseases. Coming out, assume you are dusted with engineered pollen, spores shaken loose by specific click frequencies, mites and sub-mites, and root hairs in seams. Bag suit and overshoes, shower/sterilize per biological-computer protocols, and hold all gear for inspection. Check *yourself* over days for slow responses, for finding loud dripping in ordinary rooms, for dreams paced at one wingbeat at a time.

Above all, remember the grove would have kept you well. Fed air, cushioning, dim green loud peace, small warm things investigating your thaw in their slow way — for a very long time. Preparations here are not mostly about surviving hostility. They are ropes, timers, and friends in the wind whose job is to remind your body that it belongs to a faster world, and to pull before the slower one finishes adopting you.
