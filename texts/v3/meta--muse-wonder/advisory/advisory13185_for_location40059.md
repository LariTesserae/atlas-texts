From a distance, it looks like nothing. Up close, it will be the loudest, loneliest city you have ever visited — three million people spread across fifty miles of young jungle and broken rock, living in isolated vertical towns, almost never speaking aloud, and timing their lives around bursts of artificial fog.

You, as an ordinary unmodified human, will not be adapted to it. The locals are. Plan accordingly.

### 1. The main hazard is not the jungle. It's the sound.

This is not "noisy" in the city sense. It's a continuous physical pressure you feel in your chest, teeth, sinuses, and behind your eyes. The canopy insects, frog colonies, flying predators, resonant reeds, and the subsonic pulse under everything combine into a roar that will damage unprotected human hearing very quickly and make normal speech outdoors effectively impossible.

Before you go:

- **Get fitted for serious, tuned hearing protection.** Ordinary foam earplugs are a backup, not a plan. You want custom-molded plugs *plus* active over-ear cancellation rated for sustained low-frequency and infrasonic environments, ideally tested by whatever transit clinic is sending you. Bring at least two complete backup sets. Filters clog with humidity, batteries die in the warm air, seals fail when you sweat.
- **Do not count on getting local ear-shunts.** Local children are fitted within days of birth with cartilaginous shunts grown from their own tissue. That is not a tourist procedure. It takes time, medical supervision, and biology they don't fully explain. For a short visit, mechanical protection and indoor rest periods are your substitute.
- **Assume your ears, microphone, and voice recorder are useless outdoors.** Phone calls won't work. Voice notes won't work. Alarms won't be heard — set everything to visual flash and vibration, and then realize vibration itself matters here (see below).
- **Get a medical check focused on ears, sinuses, teeth, and vestibular system.** Fillings, sinus congestion, ear infections, inner-ear sensitivity, migraines, and balance disorders will all be amplified. If you are prone to tinnitus, motion sickness, or panic in loud/enclosed conditions, talk to a doctor before you consider the trip. Doorway transit also hits the inner ear hard.
- **Plan an acclimation schedule.** Do not arrive and spend an afternoon on a terrace sightseeing. First day: mostly indoors, brief outdoor exposures with full protection. Build up slowly. You will be exhausted in a way that feels disproportionate — constant loud sound is fatiguing even when it stops consciously registering.

Locals will seem calm outdoors because their implants filter the roar to a hiss and their shunts protect the mechanism of hearing. Those implants occasionally fail. If you see a local suddenly weeping, grinning, hands clamped to their head, guide them — silently, by touch — indoors. Do not shout at them. They are getting the full basin.

You should have the same plan for yourself: if your protection fails outdoors, don't try to fix it in place while talking. Point to your ears, point to the tower, go inside.

### 2. Learn to communicate without speaking before you arrive

Nobody speaks aloud outdoors. Not as a fashion, and not primarily as politeness — they literally cannot be heard, and trying marks you.

Conversation is:

1. A local sign language, fast and highly developed
2. Touch-alphabet traced/spelled on the forearm
3. Text projected through eyelid implants

You will not have the implants. Before visiting:

- Learn at least survival sign: *water, inside, help, pain, ears, danger, stop, go, doorway, guide, thank you, I don't understand.* Locals are patient with slow signing and very impatient with shouting tourists.
- Arrange a **HUD visor or glasses with real-time captioning/text display** as your implant substitute. Make sure it works offline, in humidity, and in dim amber light without audio input. Bring a simple waterproof writing slate and stylus as the no-power fallback — genuinely useful here.
- Learn the etiquette of the touch-alphabet *before* you touch anyone. It's normal communication locally, but it's still forearm contact. Offer your own arm first. Ask, in sign, permission.
- Practice being silent. You will instinctively raise your voice when you can't hear yourself. That does nothing except hurt your throat, attract attention, and — literally — alter the local ecology. The reed beds grow toward sustained sound for a season. A shouting visitor leaves a visible record.

Indoors is different. Inside the double-skinned, frequency-cancelled tower walls, people do speak, and the quiet is so absolute that first-time visitors often panic, hyperventilate, or feel their own heartbeat as an intrusion. Transition gradually if you can. Your first quiet-room experience should be short, seated, with someone nearby. Locals pay by the minute for silence the way desert cultures pay for water, and emerge looking shaken and reverent. Don't treat quiet-rooms as a novelty ride, and don't book a long session on day one.

### 3. Pack for warm, dim, intermittently soaking wet dryness

It almost never rains. It is also not dry in any simple sense.

- **Light that never becomes daylight.** The sky-sails a mile up filter everything to permanent smoked amber late-afternoon. Bring a headlamp with a warm, dimmable setting for night (which is brown, not black) and for interiors away from galleries. Don't expect sunrise to reset your body clock; set strict sleep/wake alarms on visual/vibrating timers, and consider melatonin/circadian advice from your clinic.
- **Clothing:** light, breathable, quick-drying, full-coverage. You will be drenched without warning and then steamed dry. Bring a waterproof outer layer you can get into in under thirty seconds, waterproof covers for electronics and documents, and shoes with serious ankle support — the soil is still angular clods, the gullies have knife edges, the basalt is freshly fractured and bright where it split. Sandals are indoor footwear only.
- **Eye protection:** not for brightness, but for particulate, insects the size of thumbs in uncountable numbers, and the faintly electric condensate drops (below). Clear sealed goggles for doorway platforms and fog-bursts are worth it.
- **Water:** carry more than you think you need, in *sealed* containers. The basin is water-scarce between events. Do not plan to drink from the beautiful black mirror pools in the fracture basins — they are mineral-heavy, crowded with alien life, and can vanish within a week. They're ecosystems, not wells. If you must use local water, use tower cistern water that locals are drinking, treated by whatever protocol your travel clinic specifies for this world.

### 4. Understand the weather, because locals schedule everything around it

Water arrives as events, not weather systems:

**Tower exhalations.** When a tower's cooling cycle ends, usually around dawn, it exhales a warm fog-burst down its flanks. For about an hour everything is drenched, gardens fill, cisterns top up, ground-plants unfurl in minutes — entire meadows opening purple-white and loud with bees, then folding away. Locals time laundry, baptisms, curing, celebrations, and sex to this. Learn your host tower's exhalation schedule on arrival and plan around it: you will get wet, visibility drops, sound changes, and doorways used during an exhalation can deposit you damp in someone else's weather forty miles away. Travel light through doors at that time, secure everything loose, and waterproof anything that matters. There are documented, heavily fined cases of travelers arriving a few seconds before they left.

**Sky-sail weeping.** The vast translucent membranes drifting unsupported overhead condense moisture for a long time and release it irregularly as fat, concentrated, tingling, faintly electric drops out of clear dimness. When locals suddenly move under the door-sized leaves or indoors, copy them immediately, without asking why out loud. Do not catch this water on your tongue for fun. Do not stand open-mouthed under a sail. If your skin tingles or your hair lifts, get under cover.

Drops are a warning sign, not rain. There is no storm coming to wash the air. It's just the concentrated sky coming down.

### 5. Movement: there are no roads, and that is load-bearing

Do not look at a map and plan to walk, ride, or drive between towers. The towers stand kilometers apart with unbuilt jungle between them *by physical necessity*. The doorway lattice only works across unexcavated, unbuilt ground, grounded through raw young rock. Build or dig in the gaps and doors begin opening onto wrong years, wrong heights, or solid stone.

Therefore:

- **Travel tower-to-tower only by doorway, with local authorization.** Stepping through feels like a frame humming at the edge of pain, then instantaneous arrival with ringing ears and someone else's weather on your clothes. Sit down afterward if you need to. Don't schedule tight connections. Don't carry open liquids, loose papers, or unsecured hats/tools. Freight moves in sealed pods that arrive with a concussive *whump* — never stand in a freight arrival zone, even if it looks empty and the animals are ignoring it.
- **Never camp, build, dig, trench, or even drive a serious tent peg in the gaps.** No fire pits, no latrine digging, no "temporary" shelter foundations. This is law, superstition, and physics at once. Sleep in towers. Use tower sanitation. Carry out everything.
- **Do not attempt a cross-country hike as tourism.** The jungle is loud, trackless, ridge-cut, and populated by an ecology that runs on sound. Your noise, your devices, your voice will pull reeds, vines, insects, and their predators toward you. If you must be in the gaps at all, go with a local guide, in a small silent party, with a defined tower destination, sealed water, and a return plan — and understand that locals will consider this mildly insane.
- **Standing on a terrace, you will see almost nothing of a city of millions:** perhaps six distant lights in green murk. This produces a real psychological effect — agoraphobic loneliness in a populated place. Keep scheduled contact with your host/guide. Isolation here is structural.

### 6. Living in a tower

Each tower is a complete town of ~10,000 — markets in lit galleries around the middle, plus schools, manufactories, temples, vertical farms, reservoirs, and cemeteries, all stacked in a spiraling column of pale ceramic, living timber, and a translucent self-healing material that sweats in warm air.

Rules of thumb:

- **Treat the building as alive, because for practical purposes it is.** Don't cut, carve, nail into, or take samples of the sweating translucent material or living timber. It heals cracks overnight and in warm wet weather sometimes dreams in faint patterns of light across its surface. Don't flash-photograph the patterns at close range or tap on them to see if they respond. Ask your host what that section is for before touching walls at all.
- **Budget and book like a local.** Indoor quiet space, cistern water, and doorway priority are managed resources. Quiet-rooms are paid by the minute. Markets, schools, and services are vertical — confirm *which floor and which spiral arm*, not just which tower. "Meet me in Khar-Tower" is like saying "meet me in town."
- **Expect total self-sufficiency and total dependence.** A tower has everything its people need, because it has to. It also means if your host tower can't provide something, getting it from forty miles away is instant by door and impossible by road. Plan medication, batteries, and spare hearing-protection parts accordingly. There is no courier road as backup.
- **Cemeteries, temples, farms are all in the stack.** Behave as you would in any dense community where you will keep passing the same people in the galleries. Privacy is managed by custom and scheduling, not by distance.

### 7. The one emergency drill you must memorize: a tower goes silent

You will feel it before you understand it — a sudden hole in the pressure on your chest, as if a note under everything has stopped.

When that happens:

1. **Walk away from the tower across open ground.**
2. **Do not use its doors.**
3. **Do not run, shout, or try to investigate.**

Silent towers are considered to be thinking, repairing themselves, or listening to the rock settle. Locals do not speculate loudly and do not re-enter on their own schedule. The last tower silent for more than a day came back with a new floor nobody built — furnished, lived-in-looking, dust already in the corners. Nobody will find that as reassuring as they pretend to.

This is the one situation where a guided walk through the gap is expected. Carry your boots, sealed water, hearing protection, and slate with you in a grab-bag by the door of your room. Follow locals in single file. Stay signed-in with your guide by sight or touch.

Other emergencies:

- **Doorway feels wrong** — hum rising past pain threshold, frame light uneven, attendants signing *stop*: step back. Don't argue schedule. A wrong door is not a delayed door.
- **Loose stones beginning to tremble, puddles forming standing rings:** the infrasound is peaking. Move off fractured slopes and ridge edges; young rock sheds. The ridges tick and contract at night too — never shelter at the base of a fresh basalt cliff.
- **Meadow unfurling around you during a fog-burst:** beautiful, and suddenly full of bees/analogues. Stand still, cover your face, let locals lead you out. Don't swat broadly; you'll only make more noise and vibration.
- **Four-winged hunters overhead:** iridescent, membranous, screaming as they stitch between towers. They eat frogs and insects, not people, but any large predator following prey through a crowd is a collision hazard. Get under a leaf awning or gallery roof when locals do.

### 8. Don't damage the loop

Half the flora here is resonant. Twenty-foot hollow reeds lean toward towers and pump condensation to their roots using infrasound; cicadas cluster on and amplify the reeds; epiphytes grow in the reeds and drip; frogs breed in the drip-basins; hunters eat the frogs; guano fertilizes the next explosive round of green. Noise is this ecosystem's sunlight.

That means:

- No portable speakers, no drones with whining motors, no ultrasonic pest devices, no generators left running in the open, no celebratory shouting or horn-blowing outdoors. You are not just being rude; you are redirecting water and growth.
- Keep unavoidable device vibration to a minimum outdoors, and never leave a vibrating device on the ground in a reed bed.
- Don't feed wildlife, don't collect the thumb-sized cicadas, don't tap reeds to "play" them. Clearings visibly grow toward new sound sources, vines questing toward a humming doorway frame, leaves turning like sunflowers toward noise. You can redirect a season's growth by carelessness.
- Follow alien-biology quarantine from your travel clinic strictly: sealed footwear cleaning on tower entry, no release of Earth insects/microbes/seeds, no taking local life — however abundant — off-world in your luggage. Everything here is loud, fertile, and primed to colonize a niche made of vibration.

### 9. Night is not a respite

The dimness deepens to warm brown, the sails overhead glow faintly with stored light, the pools steam, the rock ticks, and the sound *changes key* rather than lessening — nocturnal fauna trading shifts with diurnal, towers cooling, reeds playing lower notes. Ear protection stays necessary outside after dark. Sleep indoors, where the cancellation lets your nervous system finally stand down; many visitors sleep pathologically deeply the first nights and wake disoriented by the absence of pressure. That's normal. Set a visual alarm.

### A minimal pack list

- 2–3 sets tuned hearing protection + foam backups, cleaning supplies
- Captioning HUD/slate/stylus; pre-learned survival sign
- Waterproof everything-bag, fast-on rain layer, clear goggles
- Sturdy ankle boots, broken in; indoor soft shoes
- Sealed water bottles, electrolytes (you'll sweat in warm fog without noticing thirst in the noise)
- Headlamp, visual/vibrating timer, offline tower map and host contact in writing
- Personal medication for the whole stay + spares divided between bags, ear/sinus medication as cleared by your clinic
- Grab-bag by your bed: boots, water, protection, slate, light — for the silent-tower walk
- No tent, no shovel, no speaker, no drone, no plans that involve digging

Come prepared to be a deaf, mute, slightly dazzled guest in a civilization that solved those problems generations ago by growing new organs and building its cities as far apart as physics demands. Indoors, accept the terrible holy silence slowly. Outdoors, keep your protection on, keep your voice down, watch the locals and copy them a half-second early — under a leaf, away from a cliff, back from a door.

And if the pressure suddenly leaves your chest and the tower in front of you goes quiet, start walking, and don't look for a road.
