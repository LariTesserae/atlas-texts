This is not a lake on a mountain. It is a factory on a mountaintop that has been deliberately flooded, kept flooded, and planted until the plumbing and the forest are the same system.

From the outside it looks almost harmless: a pale concrete plateau where a peak used to be, a small steel-and-glass nave, white and safety-yellow tanks, condensate towers, pipe bridges. Inside, the water fills the building almost to the high windows. You will spend most of your visit wading, swimming, pulling yourself along ropes through warm bright water full of kelp, ferns, mangroves, algae, turbines, and heat exchangers that are still running.

Prepare for three different environments stacked on top of each other:

1. **High, cold, windy, thin air outside**
2. **A slow airlock and locker room**
3. **A warm, steamy, permanently wet interior where there is essentially no dry ground**

Do not arrive inside the building if your method of travel lets you choose. Arrive on the outside plateau, in daylight, in good weather. Arriving directly into the nave would mean arriving waist- to neck-deep in water among machinery you cannot see clearly.

### Before You Go

**Get used to altitude first.**
You are above the clouds. The air is thin enough that the building has to wring its water out of it drop by drop. Swimming is hard work at altitude, even in warm water, even for a few minutes. If you have just come from sea level, give yourself at least a day lower down the mountain if you can, and take the service gondola for the last pitch rather than trying to climb.

Headache, nausea, breathlessness, a racing heart, and stupidly poor decision-making are real risks here, and they get worse once you are wet, warm, and exerting yourself in slow motion against water. If you are already feeling altitude-sick on the plateau, do not go in. The interior will not help.

You do not need to be an athlete, but you do need to be a calm, competent swimmer and comfortable putting your face in water, treading water while holding a pipe or rope, and hauling yourself up onto a grated catwalk that may be slick with algae. If you cannot swim, stay on the upper surface gallery with a crew member and treat it as a viewing visit only - and even the gallery is wet, humid, and in places ankle-deep.

**Expect a sterility procedure.**
There is no animal life in there. No fish, no insects, no birds, not even in the warm wet air above the water where you would expect midges. That absence is maintained. You will be treated, politely, as a contamination source.

That means:
- Shower thoroughly before entry. No sunscreen, insect repellent, perfume, lotion, hair product, or makeup that can wash off into the recirculating water. That water is kept for years, filtered through roots and membranes, and a fraction of it becomes someone's drinking supply downhill.
- No outside soil. Clean boots, clean clothes, nothing with mud, seeds, or plant matter in cuffs and tread. You will probably change completely at the lockers anyway.
- No food inside, no crumbs, no live plants, cuttings, pets, or untreated natural-fiber gear that can shed.
- If you have a cold, a stomach bug, an open cut, a skin infection, a fresh tattoo, or conjunctivitis, reschedule. Warm, still, plant-rich water that is recirculated for years is not a place to be casual about biology.
- Declare medication, inhalers, and allergies. There is underwater pollen here - pale waxy flowers open below the surface and release fine treated puffs that hang visible in the sun. It is engineered, but if you have asthma, hay fever, or plant allergies, assume it can find you.

Leave anything that must stay dry outside the inner door. Paper swells, phones drown, leather rots, ordinary cameras fog instantly and stay fogged. If you need notes, bring a waterproof notebook and a pencil in a sealed pouch, or agree to remember and write it up afterwards.

### What To Wear And Carry

Outside, dress for an exposed summit: windproof shell, warm mid-layer, hat, gloves, sunglasses, and sturdy shoes with good grip. The plateau is artificial - poured concrete and cast aggregate, no natural shelter, and the wind will try to stop you walking the perimeter. The sun is hard and ordinary and misleading; at this height you burn fast, even through cloud glare.

Inside, you will strip that all off at the lockers.

The building keeps thin wetsuits hanging dripping by the airlock. Use one even though the water is bath-warm. You will be brushing past steel, ceramic planting blocks, root masses, valve housings, and kelp racks. A wetsuit protects your skin, keeps you from shedding as much into the water, and makes long immersion bearable when you stop moving and hang in the current. If you have your own thin suit that is scrupulously clean and fits well, ask if you can bring it - a bad fit is exhausting when every corridor may require swimming.

Also bring or accept:
- Mask or good swim goggles - the far wall is visible, but the water is cloudy enough to breathe, aesthetically speaking, and you will want to see intakes, roots, and the bright unrusted tools on the bottom grating before you put a foot on them.
- A snorkel if you are confident with one. Much of the nave is best seen with your face just under, drifting. There is no scuba system for visitors, and you do not need one - the surface is always a short reach up, often to a mangrove crown, catwalk, or pipe. Do not assume the aeration bubbles are breathable air for you.
- Ear protection or a plan for your ears. You will surface and submerge repeatedly, ears clearing under crane rails and pipes. Warm water sitting in ears at altitude, then cold wind outside, is a recipe for pain and infection. Dry them carefully afterwards.
- Swim cap or tied-back hair. Hair catches in fern, felt planting mats, and rotating brush housings, and loose hair contaminates filters.
- A small dry bag for the locker area only, a fast-drying towel, and a complete warm change of clothes waiting for when you come out. Coming out wet into thin mountain wind is the fastest way to get dangerously cold on this entire trip, despite having just been in a bath.

Do not bring a heavy pack inside. In water it becomes a sail and a snag hazard, and there is nowhere dry to put it down.

### Arriving

Take the gondola up the last sheer pitch. Service runs are slow, weather-dependent, and may stop for wind that feels trivial down below. Build slack into your day; if the gondola is not running, there is no casual alternative route, and you should not invent one up a sawn-off mountain.

On the plateau, move slowly at first. Look at the building dry before you see it drowned: note the clerestory windows, the south wall where the mangroves are, the turbine row down the center, the filtration bays, the crane rails. Then accept that your memorized dry schematic will only be half true inside. Plants block and reroute passages month by month. A heat pump that was a corridor last visit may be a thicket now. The guide ropes are the real map.

The airlock cycles very slowly. Do not rush it, do not try to help the doors, and do not treat the wait as wasted. Use it to breathe, let your heart rate settle, and listen. When the inner door opens, the first step is not onto a dry floor. It is onto grating already ankle-deep, then knee-deep down a ramp.

Enter exactly there, nowhere else. Hold the rail. Algae makes everything velvet-slick.

### Inside: How To Move

**Slow is not etiquette here, it is physics.**
If you kick, thrash, or stride, you will tear leaves and stir up fine particulate that takes an hour to settle and clogs the very sensors and intakes the sterility crews spend their lives scraping clean. Move as if you are trying not to wake the water. Pull hand-over-hand on ropes and pipes. Trail a hand ahead of you to feel for root masses, racks, and glass sight-glasses with leaves pressed flat from the inside.

Keep to the maintenance path unless a crew member takes you off it. Watch specifically for:
- **Intakes and pumps.** A pump the size of a small house turns, sighs, and stops for a long minute. That stillness is not safety. Suction and sudden starts are transmitted through water as a soft clunk and a new, almost imperceptible current that rearranges kelp by millimeters. Valves open on the hour. Give machinery clearance even when it looks overgrown and dormant - especially then. Plants have grown around pipes so tightly that a trunk may now be part of the pressure system.
- **Electrical cabinets and control panels.** Some were meant to stay dry and didn't. Moss makes continents on them. Do not open, lean on, or use them as handholds. If indicator lights are disappearing under the daily tide line, that is information, not an invitation.
- **The daily tide.** The whole interior sea rises and falls about twenty centimeters over a day with the storage cycle. A band of slick steel, a fringe of roots, a row of lights will be exposed, then covered again. Your exit catwalk at 10 a.m. may be deeper at noon. Note the waterline marks when you enter and check them when you turn back.
- **Heat.** The water is body-warm, the air above it is a steamy jungle, condensation rains gently and constantly from the roof trusses. You can become overheated and dehydrated while completely immersed, and altitude makes it less obvious. Sip water *before* you go in - from the crew's dry supply, not from the nave - and come out if you feel flushed, headachy, or oddly chilled in warm water.

Above the surface, where heads can be up among mangrove crowns and broad leaves pushing into cabinets, people whisper. Follow that. Sound carries wrong across water and glass, shouting is useless below the surface anyway, and the quiet is functional - crews listen for ticks, sighs, clunks, and changes in the felt hum of the turbines that you perceive in your teeth more than your ears. Agree on hand signals with your companion before you submerge: stop, up, problem, look.

Stay with a buddy and keep visual contact. In bright green midday water, with shafts of sun and caustics moving across everything, it is surprisingly easy to lose orientation among identical racks of kelp swaying in a current too slow to feel. If you stir up a cloud, stop moving entirely and hold a rope until it settles. Panic is the only fast thing that works in there, and it works badly.

Do not prune, pull, clear, or "help" unless you are handed tools and a specific task. Crews prune constantly - cutting growth back from intakes, crane rails, and sensors - and then deliberately let it grow again. What looks like a blockage may be a filter. What looks like a vine in conduit may be serving as a baffle. Leaves are baffles. Root masses are filters. If a flower is growing out of a boiler seam, leave it alone and admire the ambiguity.

The best moment is noon, sun straight through the high glass, outside all glare and wind. Inside it is clear and cloudy at once, completely full. Give yourself time just to hang at the surface under a crane rail, hold a pipe that feels indistinguishable from a branch, and notice the absence of animals. It gets louder the longer you stay. That stillness is not an oversight to fix by being lively in it.

### Getting Out

Retrace by rope, not by memory, and surface slowly enough for your ears to clear. Back in the airlock, wait for the full cycle again. You may be asked to rinse, to check wetsuit, skin, hair, and goggles for fragments of leaf, rootlet, or seed-like material, and to leave all of it behind. Do not carry this ecosystem downhill in your towel. These are first- and second-generation engineered plants, unchecked by herbivore or winter. They do not belong in a mountain stream.

Once outside, towel off aggressively, get into warm dry clothes immediately, and get out of the wind. Evaporative cooling at altitude after a warm soak can drop you fast. Drink something warm and salty if the crews offer it. Sit down before you walk the perimeter or board the gondola down. Many people feel euphoric, sleepy, and slightly deaf for an hour; that is normal, and it is not a good state in which to descend a sheer cable pitch in worsening weather.

If the gondola crew says you wait, you wait. Weather here is not dramatic - it is simply hard, thin, sunlit wind over artificial rock - and it decides the timetable.

### Who Should Not Go Deep

Stay on the gallery or skip this visit if you are pregnant and overheating is a concern, have uncontrolled asthma or severe allergies, have a heart condition affected by altitude and exertion, have chronic ear or sinus problems that prevent clearing, have any open wound, or are uncomfortable being submerged in warm water among machinery with no dry exit except back the way you came. There is no shame in a gallery-only visit; from above the water you will still see the crowns breaking the surface, the condensation rain, the warning signs half-hidden by fern, and the bright drowned factory breathing below you.

Come expecting not ruins, not wilderness, but something new and not yet finished learning what it is: paint still unpeeled, concrete barely tide-marked, tools on the bottom still bright, plants still exuberant because nothing has had time to rot properly. Move slowly, touch little, breathe shallowly at first, and let it be full without you.

That fullness is the whole point.
