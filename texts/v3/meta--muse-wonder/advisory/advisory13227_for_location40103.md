This is not a place you visit. It is a place you cross, quickly, on someone else's terms — and the first thing to prepare is your expectation.

You will not talk there. You will not hear birds, or wind in the normal sense, or your companions, or yourself. You will not find water when you are thirsty, shelter when you are tired, or a season when it is easier. On the high equatorial saddle, at 5,500 to 6,000 meters, the wind through the eastern gorge has blown at the same velocity, in the same direction, through the same fluted rock, for ten thousand years. Everything you bring has to be redesigned around a condition that never pauses, never gusts, never relents.

Prepare as if for high altitude, desert sun, arctic shade, and industrial noise injury — all at the same time, all at noon.

### 1. Your Body Is the First Problem: Altitude

The air has less than half its proper weight. You will never acclimatize enough in a day, and no one lives up there to acclimatize permanently.

Stage below for at least 7-10 days if you can. Sleep progressively higher in the valleys where there is still soil and shrubs — 3,500m, then 4,200m, then 4,800m — with day hikes higher and nights lower. Walk slowly even when you feel fine. On the plateau, your heart will work constantly, cuts will clot slow, nosebleeds will start without warning and crust almost instantly.

Do not cross if you have a cold, sinus infection, ear infection, recent dental work, a heart or lung condition, or a history of altitude sickness. The vibration settles behind the teeth and in the sinuses; loose fillings, inflamed sinuses, and fluid in the middle ear will become agony, not discomfort.

Talk to a medic beforehand about altitude prophylaxis in whatever world you're coming from, about iron and hydration in the weeks before, and about the signs of cerebral and pulmonary edema. There is no rescue by shout, horn, or radio call that competes with that roar — if you collapse a day from the rim, your party carries you or you do not leave. Turnaround criteria must be decided *below*, in words, while you can still hear them: persistent vomiting, ataxia, confusion, breathlessness at rest, a companion who stops responding to touch signals. No arguing on the plateau. There is no language to argue in.

Plan to be up there less than 24 hours. One night is survivable. Two nights, even plugged, and your hands will tremble at a frequency not your own, your sleep will fragment, your ears will ring for days. Three is dangerous. The guides who crossed too often in youth are stone deaf below the rim for the rest of their lives.

### 2. Hearing Protection Is Not Comfort — It Is Armor

Unprotected ears are damaged in an hour, ruined in a day. And ordinary foam plugs alone are not enough, because much of the sound arrives through bone — through your jaw, your sternum, the soles of your feet.

Fit your protection *below* the cairn-line where the herders stop talking:

* **Inner plugs, custom-fitted if possible:** The locals use greased wool, resin, and wadded grass, worked deep and sealed. Modern soft plugs, properly rolled and inserted, are the equivalent. Carry three pairs per person — they will get dusty, bloody from nosebleeds, and lost.
* **Outer muffs over the top:** Hard, sealed earmuffs, strapped so the wind cannot lift an edge. Double protection is mandatory.
* **A mouthguard or a strip of leather to bite:** Not for sport. The mid-range howl makes loose teeth ache and makes you clench involuntarily for hours. A guard spreads the vibration.
* **Never remove them above the line.** Not to hear a signal, not to sleep more comfortably, not for a photograph. Sleep in them. If a plug works loose, stop, turn your back to the gorge, crouch in the lee of your pack, and have a partner replace it with gloved hands. That thirty seconds matters, cumulatively.

Accept that even with all this you will *feel* sound constantly: a low roar in the ribs, a high shriek that seems to originate inside the skull, pebbles jittering into the same patterns under your boots. Headphones, music, voice recorders, alarms, whistles, drums, and drones are useless weight. Sound-based navigation, sonar, and audio recording equipment will overload or be torn apart physically. Bring tactile alarms — vibrating timers strapped to the wrist — if you need a wake-up.

People with tinnitus, Ménière's, migraines triggered by pressure, or panic triggered by not hearing their own breathing should not go. You will not hear your own breath at noon in the middle of the bowl. That absence unmoors people more than the loudness does.

### 3. Learn a Language That Does Not Use Air

Speech at arm's length does not exist. Shouting tears words apart before they leave your mouth.

Before you go up, your entire party — ideally 3 to 6 people, never alone — must rehearse a small, stupidly simple touch-and-sight system until it is reflex:

* Two fingers tapped on the ground: *stop / attention down / danger felt through ground*
* Tap on the sternum: *me / I am in trouble / I need check*
* Tap on a companion's wrist: *look at me / are you okay?* Squeeze once for yes, twice for no, continuous shaking for *no, help now*
* Flat palm on rock together: *feel this* — the foxes, rodents, and guides all read tremor this way, and a falling stone or a thumping rodent colony is real information

Add a dozen broad visual signals for daylight: both arms raised = turn back; arm circling = gather close; hand flat, pushing down = crouch, wind-borne debris; mirror-flash patterns for distance. The swifts communicate by flashing white wing undersides. You will do the same with a polished signaling mirror — it carries fifty kilometers in that clarifying light when a shout carries zero meters.

Carry an etched slate or wax tablet on a lanyard inside your coat, and a stylus. Paper blows, tears, and vibrates illegibly. Write only in pre-agreed short codes; at altitude, in cold and vibration, your handwriting will degrade fast.

Stay in line of sight at all times, and within touch distance in the gorge mouth itself. Tethering together with a short, flat rope in the highest-wind section is sensible — keep everything lashed flat to your body anyway, because proud, flapping gear will be worried to pieces and will drag on you for hours without pause.

Hire a local guide. Not as a luxury. Navigation is by single distinguished stones, a split rock shaped like a tooth, a memorable boulder — cairns the wind would otherwise scatter. Distance lies murderously: a cliff that looks an hour away is a day; your shadow at noon is a small black puddle and gives you no scale. The guide will also know which cracks hold permanent moisture, which slab piles hold rodent burrows that collapse, and when your party is syncing its tremor to the rock and needs to get down *now*.

### 4. Eyes, Skin, and Temperature: Burn and Freeze Simultaneously

There are no seasons, and the day is always twelve hours. The sun climbs nearly straight up. In direct light your face burns in minutes, lips crack and blister; in the shadow of one boulder your fingers go numb.

* **Slit-goggles of polished bone, or the darkest glacier glasses you can get, with side shields, on a strap.** The light off pale granite and pumice is merciless; shadows are black cuts, not grey. Ordinary sunglasses will give you snow-blindness without any snow.
* **Full face cover:** A tight-weave, light-colored balaclava or cloth wrap, with a mouth opening. You will breathe through your mouth in the thin air — cover it with cloth to warm the air, filter fine dust that hangs in standing ripples near the gorge, and prevent your teeth and lips from desiccating and cracking. Reapply fat-based lip and nose salve every hour by touch, not by schedule you can hear.
* **Layer for two climates worn at once:** A completely windproof outer shell, sealed at wrists, neck, and ankles — wind, not cold, is the tearing agent. Under it, insulation you can vent in seconds when you step into sun. Gloves: thin liners for dexterity with signals and plugs, plus windproof over-mitts tethered to you. You will lose fine motor control in shade and sweat in sun ten steps later. Manage it constantly or you will get frost-nipped fingers under a sunburned nose.
* **Boots stiff and ankle-high,** with soles thick enough that ground-thump signals read as clear taps and jittering pebbles do not exhaust your feet. Everything living there reads the ground; you will too, whether you want to or not.

No umbrellas, no wide hat brims, no loose scarves, no tall pack frames. Tall things are torn, desiccated, and shaken apart.

### 5. Water: Carry Every Drop

There is no free water to speak of. What exists is a stolen half-hour: frost laid at night on lee sides of stones melts two hours after sunrise, beads, runs a few centimeters into natural cups no bigger than a saucer, and is gone by afternoon. A few deep pits in the southern gorge walls hold permanent black moisture and a skin of ice at noon, rimmed with salt and green algae — and with swift guano, fairy shrimp, midge larvae, and a whole daily ecology that assembles and disassembles in hours.

Do not plan to drink from them. Carry in all water for the crossing — at altitude, in wind, breathing through your mouth, you will need 4-5 liters per person for a hard day's crossing, more if you overnight — and carry it in flat, hard bladders close to the body so it does not freeze at night or vibrate open. If you are reduced to the pits in an emergency, collect in that brief morning window, filter, and purify thoroughly, and take as little as possible: those cups are the entire water supply for cushion plants a century old and for animals that will die without that day's melt.

Food likewise: no fire will stay lit, no stove will be audible or manageable in that sustained blast. Bring cold, dense, fatty food you can eat with gloves on, in small bites, without crumbs that require water to swallow. Eat on a schedule felt by a vibrating wrist timer, because hunger and thirst signals get drowned by everything else.

Carry everything out, including waste. The wind will sort dust back into its old patterns in a day, but it will not remove your tins, cloth, or feces from a water pit. Leave nothing. Take nothing except by arrangement with the people who harvest there sustainably — swift feathers, a little lichen for dye, guano for fields far below. Those lichens map damp tracks year to year unchanged; a boot scuff through a sulfur-yellow crust may be a decade's growth. Walk on bare rock and scree tails, never on cushions, rosettes, or the fringes of pits. Do not lift loose stones: translucent scorpions, centipedes, and trip-line spiders live under almost every one, and rodent burrows honeycomb the slab piles.

Do not reach into gorge cracks at night feeling for swifts unless you are with locals doing a traditional harvest. Do not chase photographic closeness to foxes, vizcacha-like rodents sitting with ears permanently sealed flat, or the great pale owl at dusk. They hunt and warn entirely by sight and ground tremor; your stomping approach is deafening to them in the only channel they have.

### 6. Shelter and Sleep

No one builds here, and your tent should not try to be the first exception. A tall tent will flap until it tears, and the flapping will transmit through the poles into your body all night.

If you must overnight, do it once, low: behind a house-sized boulder on its lee side, in a bivouac sack or a shelter dug a hand's depth into scree and roofed flat, entrance away from the gorge. Anchor everything with stones. Condensation will form on anything metal and on your windward side while your other side stays bone dry; keep plugs, goggles, and water inside your sleeping layers. Set a tactile watch rotation — one person awake, hand periodically on each sleeper's sternum to check breathing — because you cannot hear apnea, vomiting, or a call for help.

Expect sleep to be shallow and full of the sense that the ground is trying to say something it never finishes. That is normal. It is also the warning sign: if by morning your ears ring through plugs and your hands will not steady, descend that day regardless of plan.

### 7. A Sensible Crossing

Start from the southern rim before dawn, already plugged and goggled at the cairn-line, fed and watered below. Climb breathing through your mouth, move in the cold blue before the sun hammers straight down. Collect your bearings at noon only briefly — there is nowhere to hide from light then, but the white flashes of swift colonies a mile away and the clarity of the bowl are your best navigation check. Get to the far side or to your pre-chosen lee camp well before dusk, when the owl hunts by sight and temperature falls off a cliff without the wind changing at all. Stars up there are whetted to points; headlamps on foreheads, shielded downward, will preserve a little night vision and will not carry as sound would.

If anyone loses a plug irretrievably, loses goggles, develops a bleeding nose that will not stop, or cannot feel a wrist tap — turn. The place will be exactly the same tomorrow, next year, in ten thousand years. That constancy is its beauty and its indifference. It is not waiting for you to conquer it. It has already negotiated truces finer than yours: plants pressed to stone living on a half-hour of melt, animals born deaf routing language through rock, birds flashing instead of singing.

Go up plugged, covered, silent, carrying your water, following a guide's hand on the ground, and come down quickly with your shadow small and your footprints already being sorted away. That is a successful visit.
