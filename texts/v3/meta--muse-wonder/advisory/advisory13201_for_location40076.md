This is not a place that will try to kill you dramatically. It will do it by accumulation: a little too little oxygen, a little too much cold water in your boots, a little too much wind, a flat grey light that makes you misjudge how far that lake edge really is — until you are exhausted, wet, hypoxic, and lost on a plateau with no buildings, no roads, and nowhere to shelter that you didn't bring yourself.

Think of it as a very high, very young Tibetan plateau / altiplano salar that is still being built. 4,500-5,000 meters in effect, wet, treeless, geologically raw, alive for a few summer weeks, and worked by crawling factories that are legally required to leave no trace.

An ordinary human can visit it, and it will be extraordinary. But you cannot day-trip in cold. Preparation is mostly altitude, wet-cold, and self-sufficiency.

### 1. The first rule: Do Not Portal Directly In

The air is the country. A short climb up rubble leaves a fit lowlander standing mouth-open, tasting iron, waiting for their lungs to catch up. If your multiverse transit lets you choose, do not arrive on the plateau.

Arrive at least 1,500-2,000 meters lower, in the country below that supposedly "might as well not exist," and spend 4-7 days staging up. Walk uphill a little each day, sleep a little higher each night, and come back down if you develop more than a mild headache.

If direct arrival is unavoidable:

- Sit down immediately. For the first 2 hours, do nothing more strenuous than breathing and drinking water.
- For the first 48 hours, no climbing for a view, no helping move gear, no "short walk to that lake." It is never short. Flat light with no shadows lies about distance, and the ground between you and it will be half sharp basalt, half ankle-deep peat sponge.
- Expect broken sleep, vivid dreams, periodic breathing where you stop and then gasp, a racing heart at rest, and a headache behind the eyes. This is normal to a point.
- Not normal, and means descend / get oxygen / get to a processor crew *now*: headache that painkillers don't touch, vomiting, stumbling as if drunk, confusion, cough that produces pink froth, lips turning blue while sitting still.

Talk to a doctor before you go about altitude prophylaxis like acetazolamide. Do not use alcohol, sleeping pills, or other sedatives to sleep the first nights — they suppress the breathing you desperately need. Eat carbs even when you have no appetite. Altitude kills appetite and digestion; small, frequent, warm food beats one big meal.

Engines are derated here, batteries are lazy, water boils at a temperature that won't properly cook rice without a pressure cooker. You will be derated exactly the same way.

### 2. When To Go

Go in the brief high summer, and only in that window.

That's when the sedge meadows green, the cushion plants flower white, the geese and shelducks and black-necked cranes nest, the herds come up, and the lakes are liquid enough for the processors to work. It's also the only time a human without serious polar expedition kit has a margin for error.

Even in summer, plan your day around this rhythm:

**Morning:** Ice skin on every stone melts by mid-morning. Best travel window. Cold but calmest.
**Midday:** Pale, spread-out dimness like evening. No hard shadows. Deceptively cold.
**Almost every afternoon:** Cloud thickens, temperature falls out from under the day, and a short violent squall of sleet scours sideways. Streams swell and re-braid. Saturated rock faces shed slabs with a crack like a rifle shot. Stop moving, weather-vane into wind like the processors do, wait it out.
**Evening:** Everything drips, then freezes as if by decision. Seeps ice shut, peat stiffens, breath fogs close. Brutal, beautiful, and very cold very fast.

Outside summer: don't. The pools that don't freeze are on the back of industrial machines, and they are not for you.

### 3. Clothing: Cold, Wet, Wind, Sharp — All At Once

There is no dry cold here, and no warm wet. It is cold water in the air, underfoot, condensing on rock at night and running again by day. Hypothermia can happen well above freezing because you will be wet and wind-blown and hypoxic.

You need a system, not a coat:

- **Base:** Merino or synthetic, no cotton ever. Cotton will stay wet and kill heat.
- **Mid:** Fleece / light synthetic puffy you can wear while moving slowly.
- **Heavy insulation:** A real high-altitude down or synthetic parka for stops, evenings, squalls. You cool instantly when you stop gasping your way up a slope.
- **Shell:** Fully waterproof-breathable jacket and pants, with a hood that cinches around an oxygen mask. Sleet is horizontal.
- **Head/face/hands:** Windproof balaclava, warm hat, sun hat (see below), liner gloves + waterproof insulated mittens + spare gloves. You will lose dexterity fast. Anything with tiny zippers will defeat you.
- **Sun protection despite the gloom:** At this altitude UV goes through thin cloud. No shadows does not mean no sunburn and no snow-blindness. Category 3-4 sunglasses with side shields, SPF lip balm and sunscreen, reapplied. Grey glare off milky lakes and salt crust is brutal.

**Feet are the whole expedition.** The ground is two hostile materials alternating without warning:

1. Fresh slabs of basalt and glassy granite, edges sharp enough to cut a boot sole, unstained, slippery when iced.
2. Black peat and root mat that gives under you and releases a bubble of cold water around your ankle with each step.

Wear stiff, high, waterproof mountaineering / alpine trekking boots with a sole you would trust on broken glass, broken in beforehand. Bring gaiters to the knee. Bring a second, completely separate dry pair of camp shoes and 4-5 pairs of socks, kept in waterproof bags. Change socks at midday. Wet feet at this altitude go numb, then blistered, then frost-nipped before you notice because the thin air dulls everything.

Trekking poles are not optional. There are no real valleys or trails, drainage hasn't decided where to go, and braided sheets wander across gravel flats and vanish. Poles test peat, save knees on rubble, and keep you upright in squalls.

### 4. Water: Everywhere, Almost None Drinkable

This will confuse your instincts. Water weeps from cracks, runs in sheets, fills lakes without outlets.

Do not drink the lakes. They concentrate lithium, borates, salts, and ochre minerals. The milky blue color, the salt fringe where they've shrunk — that's chemistry, not purity, however thirsty-gasping you are. The warm clean discharge behind a processor is industrial process water; ducks may use the waste-heat pool, you should not bathe in or drink it without the crew explicitly clearing it.

Peat water is acidic, full of organic matter, and shared with dense animal colonies. Meltwater from a fresh seep above any meadow is your best wild source, and even that must be filtered *and* purified — boiling takes longer and uses more fuel at altitude; a pump filter + chemical/UV backup is standard.

Carry 2-3 liters personally, drink constantly in small sips. You lose huge amounts of water breathing hard dry thin air, and you won't feel thirsty when cold. Dehydration mimics and worsens altitude sickness. Add electrolytes.

Your crew arrangement should include a guaranteed daily resupply of potable water. There are no shops, wells, or streams you can count on staying in the same place tomorrow — floods move them seasonally, sometimes daily after a squall.

### 5. Shelter, Sleep, and the Leave-Nothing Rule

There are no buildings, foundations, fences, or power poles, by practicality and by law. The ground heaves centimeters a year, cracks in thaw, floods in new places. Anything fixed would be destroyed and would be illegal.

You sleep in what moves with you: a high-quality 4-season expedition tent on a footprint, or berthing arranged on / alongside a processor convoy's support vehicle.

Pitch only on high, dry, saturated gravel — never on peat meadow (you'll sink, flood, and destroy the turf that everything feeds on), never in a dry braid channel (it won't be dry after the afternoon squall), never close to a lake margin that can expand. Stake *and* weight with rocks you return to place; pegs pull out of heaving ground.

Bring:
- A sleeping bag rated at least -15 to -20C and an insulated pad with high R-value. Cold comes up through wet stone faster than down through air.
- A pee bottle. Getting fully dressed and oxygen-masked at 2 a.m. in sleet to stagger over sharp ice is how people fall.
- Everything for human waste pack-out. In cold peat, nothing decomposes. If you leave it, it's still there next season. The entire local ethic — machines that crawl so they can leave, routes erased by frost-heave — depends on visitors doing the same. Pack out all trash, all hygiene products, all grey water solids. Even "biodegradable" is too slow here.

No fires. There is no wood, no shrub worth burning, and lichen/cushion plants grow millimeters a year. Bring a reliable multi-fuel or canister stove *with windscreen and pressure cooker*, and extra fuel: stoves labor just like diesel engines in thin air.

### 6. Moving Without Roads, Trails, or Shadows

You navigate by GPS, compass, and landmarks you learn to read: a new cliff scar, a particular milky lake, a processor's amber running lights in fog. Do not trust compass alone near basalt; do not trust eyes alone ever — black rock, grey water, brass-colored grass all flatten to the same tone at 200 meters.

Rules of movement:

- Walk absurdly slowly for the first days. "You can outwalk a processor easily if you weren't gasping" — that is the pace of life here. If you can't speak a full sentence, stop.
- Never cross a meadow in a straight line because it's shortest. Go around wet peat where possible; stepping through it destroys root mat, soaks you, and disturbs nesting birds hidden at ankle height.
- Cross braided streams in the morning when they're lowest, poles out, boots on. An inch of rise after sleet changes the course around a fallen block.
- Stay off fresh scree below rock faces in the afternoon and early morning thaw. Slabs shed without warning.
- Tell someone your route and return time, and carry a satellite communicator / PLB. Cell service is irrelevant. A service drone might find you, a wolf might find you, but a twisted ankle on sharp granite in sleet gets hypothermic in under an hour if you're alone.
- Carry a bright panel / light for flat whiteout. Sound carries a mile in thin air — a whistle is genuinely useful. So is silence: you'll hear a processor's low thrum and conveyor clink long before you see its plume, and marmots whistling are telling you something is moving.

### 7. Wildlife: Crowded, Concentrated, and Not Tame

For a kilometer: nothing. Then a flat opens and it's all movement. That concentration is along the meltwater green line, which is exactly where you'll want to walk, camp, and collect water.

Give that line to them first.

- **Wild asses (20-30 in strings), gazelles:** Let them cross. They queue unhurriedly even for processors, and by law machines stop and idle. You stop further back. Never get between a herd and water.
- **Geese, ruddy shelducks, black-necked cranes:** Nest in impossible numbers at marsh edges, invisible until they explode up with a clatter that fills the basin. If birds flush, you are too close — back out the way you came. If sedge is flowering and geese are down, whole lake sections are skipped by industry that year. You skip them too. Cranes are paired, territorial, and federally sacrosanct here in practice; drones literally cut power and drop rather than cross their lane.
- **Marmots and pikas:** Adorable, whistling from scree, stacking sedge. Do not feed, do not approach burrows. They are prey base and potential disease vectors, and attracting them attracts foxes.
- **Foxes, wolves, ravens:** Foxes work meadow margins by day, wolves work in early dark. You are not preferred prey, but you are slow, loud-breathing, and smell of food. Store all food and scented items sealed in your vehicle/tent vestibule in hard containers, never sleep with food, and never leave scraps. Ravens will open an unguarded pack in minutes and scatter your trash across a protected meadow — which then becomes your legal problem.
- Keep 50m from large mammals, 100m+ from nesting colonies and cranes if possible. Use binoculars. The pleasure here is watching behavior undisturbed: heads coming up together, cranes calling once or twice across water at evening.

No dogs. They would be a disaster for ground-nesting birds and herds, and altitude is cruel to them too.

### 8. The Walking Factories: How Not To Be A Problem

A brine processor is a complete plant on wide, low-pressure tracks as tall as a person: intake pumps at the front drinking from a lake margin, settling tanks and evaporator stacks, diesels coughing in thin air, a discharge arm laying warm clean water behind. Crews are 2-3 people. They work kilometers apart, following lithium/borate concentrations that shift week to week.

If you are visiting, you will almost certainly be attached to a fleet's permit, guide, and logistics. You cannot just wander up and freelance — there is no infrastructure to support you, and the environmental permit is theirs to lose.

Etiquette and safety:

- Never step in front of a moving processor, intake, or conveyor, and never walk in its fresh track expecting firm ground — it's churned, wet, and hides nests the crew was avoiding.
- Intake pumps sit in shallow lake edges where birds feed. Follow the crew's marked path exactly, or don't go.
- Exhaust condenses instantly to white plume, stacks tick hot after shutdown, discharge pools steam and may be near-freezing at edges or hot at outlet. Ask before approaching the stern pool for that famous dawn duck photo.
- When outside near a machine, crew wear oxygen-assisted masks for exertion. You should have a personal supplemental oxygen bottle / mask for heavy work and for emergency; know how to use it before you need it.
- Drones carry samples, filters, pumps. They are fast, quiet after takeoff, navigate around colonies automatically, and are programmed to drop if a crane crosses. If a drone drops or cuts power near you, freeze and look up — don't catch it, don't continue under its lane.
- If asses cross, if a nest is found in path, if a squall hits: everything stops. Build slack days into your plan. The schedule is weather and wildlife, not you.

The machines dim to a running glow at night — three lit smudges in vast dark from a high slab. That is your size reference. Don't wander from camp at night without a headlamp, buddy, and a GPS waypoint for your own tent.

### 9. Medical and Emergency Reality

There is no hospital, no road out, no helicopter that can reliably fly in afternoon squalls and thin air at this altitude. Evacuation is slow tracked vehicle to a lower staging point, weather permitting, possibly days.

Bring a personal kit and know it: blister care, wound care for sharp-rock cuts (they are dirty, deep, and slow to heal hypoxically), rehydration salts, your prescriptions in double supply kept warm, altitude meds discussed beforehand, antibiotics / etc. only as directed by your travel doctor. Crew will have oxygen and a more serious kit — locate it on day one and ask what their descent trigger is.

Decide your turn-around rules in advance, while you can think clearly: e.g., persistent vomiting = descend; can't walk heel-to-toe = oxygen + descend; spO2 reading (bring a fingertip pulse oximeter, keep it warm) trending down despite rest = descend. Hypoxia makes everyone stubborn and euphoric about bad ideas.

Also protect against the mundane: cold-induced asthma, cracked lips/hands (bring heavy balm), constipation and dehydration, sunburned nostrils from reflected glare.

### 10. A Practical Pack List, Compressed

- Documents: fleet permit / guide contract, ID, satellite comms registration, medical info card. Cash/currency is mostly useless up here — arrange payment, trade, and resupply before.
- Altitude: pulse oximeter, personal O2 for exertion/emergency, prescribed meds, pressure cooker in cook kit.
- Clothing as above, duplicated socks/gloves, repair tape.
- Shelter: 4-season tent, high-R pad, -20C bag, waste pack-out system (WAG bags).
- Water: 3L capacity, filter + purification backup, electrolytes.
- Food: high-carb, freeze-dried + pressure-cookable, +30% extra for altitude appetite weirdness and stuck days.
- Navigation: GPS with offline topo and extra batteries kept in inside pocket, compass, headlamp with red mode, whistle, signal panel.
- Tools: poles, boot repair, multi-tool, duct tape around a card, spare batteries — cold kills them.
- Wildlife: binoculars, long lens instead of approaching, hard food storage.
- Leave-no-trace: trash bags, trowel is *not* sufficient here — pack it out.

### 11. The Mental Adjustment

What unsettles most visitors is not cold, it's unfinishedness. Colors are flat, horizon is torn new ranges, streams haven't chosen courses, a rock face becomes a new scar overnight, a factory track is already being erased by frost. You are used to landscapes that are *done*. This one isn't.

Move at its speed. Sit on a high slab (carefully, test for ice) and watch three distant lights crawl slower than stars. Listen for ravens a mile away, marmot whistles, crane calls at dusk. Notice cushion plants tight as felt, lichen in rust and sulphur starting on rock that fell that morning. The plenty here is narrow and brief — a green line for a few weeks — and every animal and every human machine has learned to share it by never settling.

If you prepare for altitude first, wet-cold second, and absolute self-removal third, you won't just survive it. You'll understand the bargain: you can stay here only insofar as you can leave, and leave nothing behind but a track the next thaw will take.

When in doubt, ask the processor crews. They live that bargain, two or three to a machine, watching gauges and watching for nests in the same glance. Do exactly what they do, only slower.
