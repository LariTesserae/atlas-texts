# Inhabitants of the Sunken District

The Sunken District harbors life as uniquely adapted as the environment itself—a community of resilient beings that exists between worlds.

The most visible inhabitants are the Waders, humans who refused evacuation when the waters rose decades ago. Now numbering perhaps three hundred souls, they've constructed homes in the upper stories of the sturdiest buildings, connected by hand-built catwalks of salvaged metal and wood that crisscross between structures fifteen feet above the waterline. Unlike the shift workers in their corporate-issued heat gear, Waders wear lightweight clothing of treated fabrics—loose pants cut off at the knee, sleeveless tunics, and wide-brimmed hats woven from reclaimed synthetic fibers.

Their skin tells the story of their adaptation: a ruddy-brown tint from constant exposure to the mineral-laden mist, with many bearing elaborate patterns of vitiligo where the water's chemicals have altered pigmentation. They've evolved a distinct gait—a measured, pendulum-like movement perfected for traversing their narrow walkways and wading through chest-deep water. Children learn to swim before they can walk.

The Waders sustain themselves through fishing, mechanical repair work for the remaining corporations, and salvage. Their distinct floating markets appear at the district's edge during the monthly low tide, drawing curious visitors from the conventional city beyond. Here they trade salvaged industrial artifacts, unique metal crafts formed from recovered materials, and their prized "mineral pearls"—crystalline formations harvested from certain buildings' submerged chambers.

More mysterious are the Echotrawlers, reclusive figures rarely glimpsed by outsiders. They navigate the deepest parts of the district in retrofitted submersibles cobbled together from industrial drums and machine parts. Some say they're researchers studying the water's unusual properties; others insist they're the original flood engineers' descendants, perpetually working to recover lost technology. Their submersibles emit distinctive low-frequency hums that reverberate through the water, giving them their name.

The district's avian population has evolved with remarkable specificity. Most notable are the Smokestack Herons—once normal gray herons whose plumage has developed a distinctive russet coloration from minerals in their diet. They've learned to nest inside the partially submerged smokestacks, using the structures' remaining heat to incubate their eggs. At dawn, they perform elaborate fishing displays, diving from great heights into precise spots where currents channel fish through submerged doorways.

Perhaps most extraordinary are the Copper Skimmers—crab-like creatures that evolved from common shore crabs but now grow to the size of dinner plates. Their shells have incorporated copper and other metals from the water, giving them a striking blue-green patina that camouflages perfectly against the oxidized surfaces of submerged machinery. They move in organized colonies, systematically harvesting the bacterial mats while avoiding territorial disputes through complex clicking communications. The oldest specimens display intricate patterns of mineral accretion that Waders consider omens of good fortune.

The district's most elusive residents are the "Breathmakers"—an amphibious mammal species that some biologists believe evolved from muskrats trapped when the waters rose. Now the size of small otters, with semi-translucent skin and enlarged lungs, they create and maintain air pockets within submerged buildings. These creatures methodically clear sediment from specific chambers, then repeatedly surface and dive, carrying bubbles of air trapped in their specialized facial fur until viable air pockets form. Whether this behavior is purely instinctual or represents a higher intelligence remains hotly debated among visiting scientists.

As darkness falls, the Ink Bats emerge—tens of thousands of them spiraling from the higher floors in undulating columns. Their fur has developed a deep indigo hue from their diet of mineral-affected insects. When they skim the water's surface to drink, the oils on their fur create expanding rings of geometric patterns across the water, a phenomenon locals call "night writing."

In this unlikely confluence of industrial ruin and adaptation, life hasn't merely persisted—it has transformed. The inhabitants of the Sunken District don't simply survive; they've forged an identity inseparable from the half-drowned world they call home, reading meaning in the tides, tracing patterns in the mist, and finding communion in the rhythm of water against weathered stone.
