# The Inhabitants of Hollow Works

The people of Hollow Works are as specialized as the instruments they create, a community evolved in isolation like the blue-tailed lizards that make these buildings their home. Around one hundred and sixty souls live here permanently, with perhaps two dozen temporary residents at any given time.

Most visible are the craftspeople—men and women with hands that tell their stories. The clockmakers have fingertips stained with tiny amounts of brass and oil, perpetually carrying magnifying loupes in breast pockets. The glassblowers are recognized by their forearms—strong but delicate, bearing the occasional small burn mark like badges of honor. Meteorological instrument makers test their barometers constantly, tapping glass faces with fingernails worn smooth from the habit.

The Reeves family has overseen operations for four generations. Matriarch Eliza Reeves, now in her seventies, still makes morning rounds through every workshop, her footsteps as precisely timed as the metronomes they export. With silver hair pulled back in a practical bun and eyes that miss nothing, she carries a brass pocket watch that hasn't lost a minute in thirty years. When she speaks, which isn't often, workers listen. Her granddaughter Meridian, at twenty-nine, gradually assumes more responsibilities, combining traditional knowledge with innovations learned during her rare ventures to universities beyond the valley.

The oldest inhabitants are the Masters—three men and two women in their eighties who no longer work daily shifts but whose expertise is sought in moments of particular challenge. Master Thorne can diagnose a faulty gear mechanism by its sound alone. Master Weller knows the exact composition needed for glass that can withstand extreme pressure changes.

Children grow up in Hollow Works with unusual freedoms and unusual constraints. They scramble up shortened practice climbing routes on the limestone walls, but also learn to sit perfectly still while holding delicate components. By age twelve, most have already gravitated toward their future specialties. The Yamamoto twins are inseparable from the hydroelectric station, while young Eli Blackwood shows uncanny skill with calibrating instruments.

Not all are originally from the valley. A small but steady stream of apprentices arrives each year, drawn by Hollow Works' reputation. Perhaps half will stay permanently. Those who remain speak of the moment they knew—often describing a peculiar sensation when the valley's mist first enveloped them, a feeling of being recognized and welcomed.

Less conventional inhabitants make Hollow Works their home as well. Old Sam—no one remembers his surname—arrived thirty years ago as a delivery driver and simply never left. He sleeps in a converted storage room and serves as unofficial historian, mail carrier, and storyteller. Three academic researchers have permanent accommodations, studying everything from the valley's weather to its unique ecosystem.

The blue-tailed lizards form their own society paralleling human activity. Workers insist the creatures understand the production schedule, as they appear precisely when warming machines create ideal basking conditions. Some craftspeople form attachments to particular lizards, recognizing them by patterns of blue on their tails, saving choice insects for their favored companions.

Hollow Works follows no conventional religious practice, but holds to rituals uniquely their own. At each equinox and solstice, work pauses for a day. Precision instruments are placed throughout the valley to measure light, temperature, and air pressure. In the evening, tables are arranged between buildings for communal meals where forecasts are made for the coming season, both meteorological and metaphorical.

Most distinctive is how time affects the inhabitants. Newcomers notice it first—the people of Hollow Works age differently. Not slower, precisely, but with peculiar constancy. Their faces weather like the limestone walls, gradually and evenly, without the sudden collapses that afflict others. Their memories remain precise, catalogued like the components in workshop drawers. Some attribute this to the clean air or the mineral-rich water. Others suggest that those who spend their lives measuring time develop a different relationship with its passage.

Visitors sometimes remark that the population seems too small for the operation's output, yet production never falters. Ask about this, and residents exchange knowing glances before changing the subject. Only the longest-staying guests glimpse the night shifts, when workshops hum with activity by minimal lamplight, operated by hands that move with practiced efficiency in near-darkness.

The people of Hollow Works speak their own dialect of technical terminology, weather predictions, and mechanical metaphors. They describe emotions as having gears, relationships as requiring calibration, and problems as pressure systems that will inevitably change. Their manner is reserved but not unfriendly—like well-crafted instruments themselves, precise in function, elegant in design, revealing their true nature through long observation rather than immediate disclosure.
