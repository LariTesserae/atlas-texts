# The Quiet Ones of Alvamist

They call themselves the Tidiiri, though visitors rarely hear this name spoken aloud. Communication in Alvamist happens primarily through a language of deliberate gestures—fingers tracing specific patterns in the ever-present water, palms pressed briefly against stone surfaces, subtle shifts in posture that convey complete thoughts. When they do speak, their voices emerge as hushed whispers that seem to blend with the gentle gurgling of the streams.

The Tidiiri appear human at first glance, but closer observation reveals their uniqueness. Their skin bears a subtle translucence, with faint blue veins visible just beneath the surface, creating patterns reminiscent of the plateau's waterways. This skin never fully dries—it maintains a slight sheen, neither quite wet nor completely dry. Their eyes are large and remarkably dark, reflecting light like the surface of deep pools. Most striking is their breathing—so measured and slow that an observer might count fifteen seconds between each inhale.

Every Tidiiri bears intricate patterns of raised skin across their foreheads and down their necks—patterns unique to each individual that become more elaborate with age. These are not tattoos or scarification but natural formations, like the lichen patterns on Alvamist's stone walls. Children are born with simple lines that gradually branch and spiral as they mature. The eldest display patterns so complex they resemble detailed maps of the plateau itself.

The Tidiiri do not count years as others do. They measure time by "flows"—cycles measured not by the sun but by subtle changes in the mineral content of the springs. Their histories speak of only seven such flows since the first awakening, yet artifacts in their oldest buildings suggest a civilization spanning thousands of years. When questioned about this discrepancy, they simply dip a finger in the nearest water and say, "Time moves differently where the stone weeps."

Their diet consists primarily of specialized fungi cultivated in the dark chambers beneath the raised buildings, supplemented by the starchy roots of water plants and rare minerals harvested directly from certain springs. They consume these in small, precise portions, often dissolving mineral tablets in water vessels that are passed communally. They require remarkably little sustenance, sometimes going weeks between proper meals during what they call their "quiescent phases."

No Tidiiri has ever been observed to leave the plateau. Visitors who remain long enough begin to notice that the inhabitants seem to emerge from and disappear into the community's seven central springs. The Tidiiri neither confirm nor deny this observation, though they treat these spring chambers with reverent care, maintaining small shrines of stacked stones around each one.

They practice no burials. The eldest Tidiiri, when they sense their final transition approaching, walk alone to specific chambers carved into the northern rock face. These chambers are sealed with fitted stone after each occupancy. Yet the population never decreases—new Tidiiri simply "arrive" periodically, fully formed adults emerging from the central springs during certain mineral-heavy flows, already fluent in the community's ways.

The lack of animal life is no mystery to the Tidiiri. Their ancient stories tell of a covenant with the mountain itself—the plateau offers perpetual water and perfect equilibrium, but demands the exclusion of chittering, flying things that would disturb its contemplation. The Tidiiri serve as tenders to this meditative ecosystem, their careful movements and quiet speech part of an ages-old agreement.

Their craftswork reveals their true nature. The stone blocks they shape fit together with impossible precision, requiring no mortar yet never allowing water through the seams. The reeds they weave create textiles that remain dry on one surface while channeling water along the other. Their most accomplished artisans create intricate glass-like structures—not blown or poured, but somehow grown from the minerals in the water itself, forming over decades into dwellings, tools, and ceremonial objects.

The Tidiiri's relationship with visitors is cordial but distant. They provide shelter and guidance but never urge travelers to stay. Those rare few who have remained for months report dreams of underwater cities and conversations with the mountains themselves. Most leave before these dreams become too vivid, carrying away the unsettling sense that the Tidiiri are not caretakers of the plateau but are, somehow, caretakers of something much older and larger that merely manifests as the plateau.

In the deepest chambers beneath the central springs, where no visitor has been permitted, the Tidiiri gather in circular formations to place their hands upon strange columns of the same glass-like material as their finest artifacts. There, it is said, they commune with the mountain's memory—accessing knowledge from before the rocks thrust upward, from when all was dark and fluid and time had no meaning. What passes between mountain and tender in those moments remains their most guarded secret.
