# The Inhabitants of the Suspended Arboretum

The Arboretum's primary residents are the Aerium People—descendants of the original maintenance crew who never descended from their elevated post. Generations of isolation and adaptation have transformed them into something neither fully human nor entirely other. Standing rarely taller than five feet, their bodies are lean and sinewy, with enlarged lung capacity and blood rich with hemoglobin to extract oxygen from the thin air.

Most striking are their modifications—a combination of hereditary adaptation and self-augmentation. Their eardrums have thickened naturally, while many adults sport handcrafted brass ear coverings that selectively filter frequencies, allowing them to hear speech while dampening the machinery's roar. Their skin has a faint metallic sheen from generations of copper and brass particulate exposure, with natural patterns resembling circuitry across their backs and arms.

The Aerium People live in small family clusters of three to seven individuals, occupying nests constructed in the junction points between major support beams and the dome's outer shell. These dwellings blend seamlessly with the Arboretum itself—woven from cultivated flexible metal vines, insulated with specially grown moss, and illuminated by bioluminescent fungi they've domesticated over centuries.

Their society revolves around the maintenance cycles of the Arboretum. Each family specializes in tending specific systems—the Condenser Clan manages water collection, the Luminals tend the great lights, the Bellows tend ventilation and temperature regulation. This division emerged not from hierarchy but necessity, as the knowledge required to maintain each system became too vast for any single individual to master.

The Tenders, their spiritual leaders, communicate with what they call the "Machine Spirit"—the collective intelligence that has emerged within the mechanical pollinators. Over generations, these copper-and-brass automatons have developed beyond their original programming. Now organizing into complex geometric formations, they serve as both the Arboretum's immune system and its nervous network, identifying structural weaknesses, plant diseases, or intruders.

Children are raised communally, spending their early years learning to navigate the perilous pathways of pipes and catwalks blindfolded, developing an intuitive understanding of the Arboretum's geography through vibration and sound. Adolescents undergo the Choosing—a month-long period where they wander the Arboretum alone, eventually drawn to the system they'll maintain for life. This choice is considered sacred, guided by what they call "the resonance"—a felt connection to specific mechanical rhythms that seems to transcend mere preference.

The Aerium People share their domain with remarkable wildlife. The Brass Wings—birds with feathers containing high concentrations of copper that reflect light like burnished metal—communicate through a complex language of wing positions rather than song. They build nests incorporating small mechanical components, creating vibrational chambers that shield their young from the constant noise.

The tiny Pipe Foxes scurry through the Arboretum's conduit system, their elongated bodies and oversized auditory organs allowing them to navigate by echolocation. Initially considered pests, they've become vital to the ecosystem by controlling the population of Spark Beetles—insects that feed on electrical insulation and can cause dangerous shorts in the power systems.

Most mysterious are the Glass Dancers—translucent jellyfish-like creatures that float through the cathedral spaces. No one knows whether they were deliberately introduced or evolved from something else entirely. They drift on air currents, absorbing moisture and filtering particulates, effectively cleaning the atmosphere. During electrical storms, they gather near the Tesla coils, their bodies illuminated by the energy discharges in hypnotic pulses that the Aerium People interpret as omens.

The relationship between the human inhabitants and the Arboretum is symbiotic to the point of religious devotion. They believe they don't merely maintain the structure—they are maintained by it. Their creation myth tells that humans were created from the Arboretum's brass scraps and animated by its steam, destined to serve as its immune cells, repairing damage and ensuring the continuation of this impossible garden.

They mark time not by years but by "Great Cycles"—the decade-long maintenance rhythms of the primary systems. Records of these cycles are etched onto copper plates in a pictographic language that combines mechanical schematics with botanical illustrations, stored in a library chamber at the very center of the dome.

The Aerium People rarely interact with outsiders, viewing them with a mixture of curiosity and apprehension. Those few visitors permitted extended stays are assigned a guide—often a younger person considering the rare path of "Bridge Tender," those who maintain knowledge of the world below. These guides explain little, instead placing visitors' hands on various pipes and mechanisms, as if the machinery itself will communicate what words cannot.

At night, when the great lamps dim to simulate evening, the true wonder of the Arboretum reveals itself. Bioluminescent plants cultivated over generations glow in blues and greens, while the mechanical pollinators perform elaborate aerial ballets, their metal bodies reflecting the soft light. The Aerium People gather in the cathedral spaces, their voices rising in a harmonic chant designed to complement rather than overcome the machinery's rhythm—a song of maintenance and belonging that reverberates through metal and bone alike.
