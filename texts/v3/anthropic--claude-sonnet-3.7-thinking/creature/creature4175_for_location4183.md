# The Gardeners of Misthollow

They call themselves the Mistkin, though outsiders might name them differently: tree-singers, water-weavers, or simply "the green folk." There is no physical uniformity among them—their skin tones range from deep umber to pale ivory, with hair in shades of autumn leaves, forest moss, or silvery lichen. What marks them as one people is not appearance but movement.

The Mistkin move differently than lowlanders. They possess a fluid economy of motion, a barefooted grace developed from navigating their vertical home since childhood. Even the eldest among them—some nearing their twelfth decade—ascend the living stairs and cross swaying bridges with the casual confidence of squirrels. Their toes are spread wide and strong, their forearms rope-muscled from climbing, their hands perpetually stained green from work with plants.

Look closely at their skin and you'll notice unexpected things. Many bear fine patterns of blue-green that at first appear to be tattoos but are actually symbiotic algae living in the upper dermal layer—commemorations of significant life events cultivated through applications of specially prepared pastes during coming-of-age ceremonies. These living markings glow faintly in darkness, illuminating gatherings when night falls.

The Mistkin speak three languages simultaneously: their melodic human tongue, rich with liquid consonants and elongated vowels; a language of subtle hand gestures for communication across terraces when wind carries away voices; and most significantly, what they call the "green speech"—a system of whistles, clicks, and guttural sounds that mimic and communicate with the birds, insects, and small mammals of their home. Children begin learning these languages before they walk.

No Mistkin exists in isolation. Their social structure revolves around "flow-families"—fluid groupings that transcend blood relation. These units form around shared work, complementary skills, or simply natural affinity, shifting seasonally as needs change. A Mistkin might belong to the Harvesting Flow during spring, the Teaching Flow during winter, and perhaps the Building Flow during storm season. Some flows persist for generations; others form and dissolve within a season.

The eldest and most respected among them are the Keepers of Green Memory—those who can communicate directly with the grandfather trees through a meditative practice involving specific breathing patterns and hand placements on living bark. These elders, rarely more than seven at any time, carry the settlement's history in memorized oral traditions, but they also access deeper knowledge from the ancient trees themselves—weather patterns from centuries past, the movements of underground water, warnings of earth tremors to come.

Daily life in Misthollow follows no fixed schedule but rather what the Mistkin call "the telling currents"—patterns dictated by immediate needs, weather conditions, and seasonal imperatives. Dawn brings the Water-Watching, when channels are inspected and flow adjusted by the day's appointed water-tenders. Afterward, the population disperses to their various tasks: tending the vertical gardens, harvesting medicinal moss from the grandfather trees' northern sides, reweaving damaged sections of living architecture, fermenting preservation foods in stone chambers carved into the mountain face.

Mistkin technology reveals elegant adaptations to their environment. Their botanical knowledge is unparalleled—they cultivate over thirty varieties of fungi for food, medicine, and material. Their primary tools are ceramic and wood, fired in small kilns built into thermal vents where the mountain breathes warm air. Their few metal implements are ceremonially important, many made from skystone (meteoritic iron) passed down through generations. 

Most remarkable is their fiber craft. The Mistkin harvest specific strands from the inner bark of younger trees (never killing them), then spin these into thread of astonishing strength. Woven using techniques known only to their most skilled crafters, this living cloth continues to strengthen over time rather than deteriorate, becoming supple armor that protects against the occasional predator from higher elevations.

Their clothing reflects practical needs—loose-fitting garments with numerous hidden pockets that don't restrict climbing movement, layers that can be added or removed as mist conditions change, and wide-brimmed hats woven from waterproof reeds that channel rainfall away from the face. Nothing is dyed artificially; all colors come from natural variations in the plants themselves, giving the Mistkin a palette of greens, blues, russets, and grays that helps them blend seamlessly into their surroundings.

The Mistkin possess unique physiological adaptations from centuries living in their misty domain. Their lungs have developed extraordinary efficiency in the thin mountain air, giving them exceptional breath control that lowers the children particularly utilize in diving games in the deeper collection pools. Their vision has adapted to see clearly through shifting mist, discerning subtle variations in fog density that would appear as solid gray to outsiders. Most striking is their hearing—attuned to the subtle harmonic relationships between water sounds, wind through leaves, and animal calls, they can detect approaching weather changes hours before any visible signs appear.

Outsiders often misinterpret the Mistkin as primitive or simple, but their complexity lies in relationships rather than possessions. Their knowledge system tracks thousands of interactions between species—which plants purify which water-borne contaminants, which insect populations must be maintained at what levels, which fungi strengthen which tree roots. Children learn these relationships through songs, games, and stories long before they understand the ecological principles behind them.

The Mistkin's spiritual life centers on what they call "the threading"—their understanding that all life weaves together in patterns of mutual sustenance. They practice no formal worship but maintain deep reverence for the processes of growth, decay, and renewal. Their ceremonies mark seasonal transitions and life passages with quiet gatherings where story-seeds (literal seeds imbued with memorized tales) are planted to grow into story-trees that will later be tended by those who inherit the memories.

Few outsiders ever witness Misthollow's most celestial residents—the luminous Mist Dancers. These translucent beings, neither fully physical nor merely spectral, appear only when conditions of light, moisture, and air currents achieve perfect balance—usually at dawn or dusk during seasonal transitions. Appearing as humanoid forms composed of concentrated mist with inner bioluminescence from specialized algae that exist nowhere else, they perform complex aerial choreographies between the terraces, their movements influencing weather patterns in ways the Mistkin have learned to read as predictions for coming seasons.

The Mistkin themselves debate whether these dancers are separate entities or projections of the collective consciousness of Misthollow itself—the gardens' dreams made visible. When asked directly, most Mistkin simply smile and say, "They are the breath between words, the pause between heartbeats," an answer that explains nothing to outsiders but everything to those who have grown up watching the dance between solid and vapor that defines their home.

Where ordinary humans end and Misthollow begins has grown increasingly difficult to determine. Through generations of symbiosis, the people have become as much a part of the gardens as the gardens are a part of them—not separate caretakers but integrated participants in an ongoing creation.
