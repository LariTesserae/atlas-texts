# The Mirovas - Keepers of the Mist

The people of Mirova are as much a part of their environment as the stone and water that define it. They call themselves simply "the Mirovas," though in their own language—a soft, consonant-rich tongue—the word carries connotations of both "caretakers" and "children."

Physically, the Mirovas stand taller than one might expect, with slender frames and elongated limbs that move with fluid economy. Their skin ranges from pale alabaster to deeper slate tones, often bearing a subtle iridescence that becomes apparent in certain light—an adaptation to centuries of living in constant moisture. This slight sheen is most noticeable across their broad foreheads and along the backs of their hands.

Their most striking feature is their eyes—large, widely set, and invariably the color of water in different states: some clear as morning dew, others deep as cistern shadows, still others with the milky translucence of fog. These eyes rarely blink, adapted to the constant misty environment, protected by unusually long lashes that catch condensation before it can reach the sensitive orbs.

The Mirovas' hair grows remarkably slowly but never stops, resulting in elders with pale tendrils reaching below their knees. They wear it typically in intricate braids interwoven with carved bone beads that softly click together during movement—one of the few deliberate sounds they create. The hair itself absorbs moisture from the air, slightly darkening and lightening with humidity changes, serving as a natural hygrometer.

Their society functions through what outsiders might call "permeable hierarchy"—leadership roles flow between individuals based on need rather than permanent status. The most respected among them are the Cistern-Keepers, who can read water quality by taste alone, and the Path-Finders, who navigate the vast expanse of Mirova without markers visible to others.

Mirovas communicate as much through gesture as through speech. Their language contains over forty distinct terms for different types of moisture and water flow, but lacks entirely any word for "waste" or "ownership." Conversations often include long, comfortable silences that outsiders find disconcerting. When they do speak, their voices carry a distinctive melodic quality—words flowing into one another like water finding its path.

The Mirovas practice "water-memory"—a tradition where important events are commemorated by adding a drop of water collected during that moment to specific ceremonial cisterns. These waters are never consumed but serve as living archives; skilled Memory-Readers can dip their fingers into these collections and retrieve impressions of events hundreds of years past.

Their clothing reveals their most intricate craftsmanship. Multiple layers of moisture-wicking fabrics are woven from fibers harvested from specialized moss colonies. These garments change slightly in response to humidity—tightening in heavy mist, loosening in drier periods. Outer layers feature embedded quartz fragments that catch light and improve visibility in fog. Pockets and small pouches contain personal water collections—tiny vials of liquid from significant places or moments.

Children are raised communally, moving between family groups until they "find their cistern"—a coming-of-age process where young Mirovas discover which area of the city resonates most deeply with them. This might take years, with youths apprenticing across different districts before settling.

They mark time through a complex calendar based on mist density patterns and the migrations of the great plateau birds. Seasons are recognized not by temperature but by subtle changes in water composition and the emergence of different amphibian species from their hibernation pools.

The Mirovas believe that consciousness itself is a form of water—flowing, adapting, connecting all living things. Their spiritual practices involve meditation beside specific cisterns, where the acoustics of dripping water create patterns thought to synchronize with mind patterns. They have no creation myth; they believe they have always been emerging, always condensing into being, just as the mist does.

Death is viewed as "re-dissolution"—a return to the water cycle. Bodies are placed in special open-air chambers where moisture gradually reclaims them. The process takes years, but friends and family visit regularly, considering the slow dissolution a continued conversation rather than an ending.

Perhaps most remarkable is their relationship with time itself. Mirovas can enter a state they call "drift-mind," slowing their metabolism and perception until they experience the world at the pace of condensation forming. Elders can maintain this state for days, watching droplets form with the attentiveness others might give to swift action. This practice is both meditative and practical—allowing them to understand water patterns that would otherwise be imperceptible.

In all things, the Mirovas seek balance and flow. They are neither conquerers nor victims of their environment, but participants in its endless cycles. In their understanding, they are not separate from Mirova—they are its consciousness made manifest, just as the stone is its body and the water its blood.
