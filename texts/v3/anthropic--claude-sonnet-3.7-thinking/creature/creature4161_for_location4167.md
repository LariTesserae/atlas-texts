# The Caretakers of The Verdant Works

They are called the Verdant Folk by outsiders, though they refer to themselves simply as Tenders. Neither fully natural nor entirely industrial in their character, they have adapted to the amphibious existence their home demands.

The most obvious Tenders are the human workers—forty-three in total—whose families have maintained the Works for generations. Their skin bears a faint green undertone, particularly visible along the veins at their wrists and temples. This is no illness but a symbiosis; microscopic algae dwell in their dermal layers, offering protection against the fungal infections that would otherwise plague those in such constant dampness. 

Their fingers are long and nimble, toughened by work yet sensitive enough to gauge the health of machinery by touch alone. They can detect vibrations that signal misalignment long before metal fatigue becomes catastrophic. Most can barely tolerate dry environments now, finding ordinary air "scraping" and "empty." When they must venture into Fallowbrook proper, they carry humidifiers—brass contraptions worn like backpacks that emit small clouds of mist around their heads.

The eldest Tender is Marissa Groke, whose 103 years have seen her transform from a robust machinist to the current Oracle of Pressure—a position that requires her to interpret the complex rhythms of the Central Boiler. She has not left the Works in 37 years. Her eyes have adapted to the perpetual mist, developing nictitating membranes that sweep horizontally across her pupils when the steam grows too thick. The youngest workers watch her with quiet awe, for her communion with the systems of the Works seems more mystical than mechanical.

Less visible but equally essential are the Skinfish that inhabit the water channels. These translucent creatures—somewhere between amphibian and crustacean—consume the mineral buildup that would otherwise clog the hydraulic systems. Their flat bodies pulse with visible organs that glow faintly blue-green in darkness. They reproduce asexually, budding smaller versions of themselves that detach when mature. The Tenders feed them copper filings once monthly, which the creatures require for their exoskeletons.

The Whisper Moths are perhaps the Works' most beautiful residents. Wing-spans reaching up to eight inches, they bear patterns that mimic the mechanical diagrams of the machinery they prefer. They lay their eggs in the warm felt packing of the machine joints, and their caterpillars consume the lubricating oils, processing them into a superior substance that they secrete as they move. The Tenders have learned to farm them deliberately, allowing certain machines to run dry so the moths will be attracted to them. Their presence is considered a blessing; a machine without its complement of moths is said to be "soulless" and prone to breakdown.

In the deeper recesses dwell the Rootmen—a colloquial name for the mycelial network that has achieved a peculiar sentience over decades of exposure to the chemical outputs of production. These fungal colonies cannot speak but communicate through pheromonal signals that the oldest Tenders have learned to interpret. They exchange information about structural weaknesses in the foundations and are rewarded with careful feedings of specific minerals. They can extend hyphal tendrils through almost any substance in the Works, creating living sensors that monitor conditions too subtle for instruments.

The Night Tenders—those who work the skeletal night shift—have developed exceptional night vision and prefer the quieter hours when the machinery hums rather than roars. They tend to be shorter than their daylight counterparts, with larger ears that swivel slightly toward sounds of interest. They speak less but whistle more, using a complex tonal language that carries well over the mechanical background without disturbing the sleeping Whisper Moths.

Children born to Tenders begin their apprenticeship at age seven, starting with the cultivation of the "starter gardens"—plots of specialized plants whose properties support the Works' operations. These children can identify dozens of moss species by touch alone and know which ferns indicate unhealthy metal content in the groundwater. They carry small pruning shears from their first day, learning the eternal balance between allowing growth and maintaining function.

Most curious are the Pipe Singers—neither fully human nor entirely other. Three generations ago, a family of Tenders became so attuned to the acoustic properties of the pipe network that their descendants now demonstrate an uncanny ability to communicate through the hollow metal veins of the facility. They can put their mouths to one pipe junction and have their voices emerge anywhere in the complex. Over time, their vocal cords have modified to produce the perfect resonant frequencies for different pipe diameters. They serve as the Works' communication system, their voices distinguishable from the mechanical sounds only to those who have learned to listen for them.

The oldest residents are perhaps not alive in any conventional sense. The Brass Growths—metallic accretions that form slowly on certain machines—are studied carefully by the Tenders. These formations, somewhere between crystal and callus, incorporate metal, mineral, and organic matter into structures that grow a few millimeters per decade. The oldest is seven feet tall and predates the current buildings. The Tenders leave small offerings of powdered minerals at their bases, though none will explain exactly why.

Together, these beings—human and otherwise—maintain the delicate communion between growth and purpose that defines The Verdant Works. Neither masters nor servants of their strange home, they have instead become its circulatory system, its nervous system, its stewards and its children simultaneously. They age slowly, work steadily, and rarely leave. Why would they? The outside world has nothing comparable to offer them.
