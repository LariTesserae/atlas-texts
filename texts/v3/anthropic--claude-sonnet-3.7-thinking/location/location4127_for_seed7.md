# The Forgotten Hothouse

Descend through the narrow shaft, past the cracked concrete collar and rusted ladder rungs, and you'll find yourself in what was once called Auxiliary Chamber 17. The industrial complex spreads no wider than a cathedral nave, contained within a cavern hollowed out perhaps forty years ago, its walls still sharp and defined where human tools carved into the living rock.

The darkness is nearly absolute except for the faint amber glow of emergency lights that somehow still function on backup power, casting long shadows across the chamber. They illuminate in patches and flickers, revealing the true masters of this space: plants. Not ordinary plants, but specimens engineered to thrive in this artificial environment.

The heat is oppressive—a constant 95°F that radiates from pipes running along the ceiling and walls. These pipes, part of an experimental geothermal tap, leak steam at their joints, creating pockets of intense humidity that collect on the ceiling and fall as artificial rain. The moisture patterns are visible on the walls—dark streaks of dampness emanating from the leaking infrastructure, forming miniature watersheds that culminate in small pools where the floor has settled unevenly.

Massive tanks of brushed steel stand in the center of the chamber, once containing nutrient solutions for hydroponic experiments. Now they host wild colonies of the engineered flora—thick vines with leaves the size of dinner plates cascade from their openings, reaching toward the sparse light. The plants have a peculiar luminous quality, their veins glowing faintly blue-green in the darkness, an embedded bioluminescent trait that was part of their design.

The vegetation thrives in specific zones where moisture, heat, and the remaining artificial light converge. Between these pockets of lushness, bare concrete and metal create deserts of industrial sterility. The contrast is stark—vibrant green tangles surrounded by the abandoned geometry of human engineering.

No animals move among the foliage. The original design eliminated them as variables in the controlled environment. The silence is profound, broken only by the occasional ping of cooling metal, the subtle drip of condensation, or the almost imperceptible hiss of a distant steam valve adjusting itself automatically.

A control panel against the far wall still displays scrolling numbers on cracked screens—measuring temperature, humidity, CO₂ levels—its mainframe continuing its programmed monitoring long after anyone remains to read the data. Next to it, a partially collapsed workstation contains notebooks with faded diagrams of plant cellular structures unlike anything found in nature.

The air tastes metallic and green simultaneously. Spores float invisibly, seeking new territory in this contained world. The plants grow with deliberate slowness, their movements imperceptible to the human eye yet inexorable. They've begun to infiltrate the machinery, their roots probing delicate components, gradually claiming the technology that created them.

In one corner stands a sealed glass chamber, its purpose unclear. Inside, a single plant species grows in perfect isolation—a spiral structure with no leaves, only a twisted stalk that seems to absorb moisture directly from the air. Its purpose, noted on a faded label as "Atmospheric Reclamation Prototype VII," suggests this entire facility may have been designed for creating self-contained ecosystems for extreme environments.

The facility has its own heartbeat—the low, rhythmic thrum of machinery deep below the floor, maintaining this pocket world through automated processes designed to outlast their creators. The boundaries between technology and biology blur here, as they were always intended to, though perhaps not in this abandoned, evolving form.

This small world continues its slow, quiet transformation—not dying, not thriving, but adapting, forgotten but functioning in its own peculiar equilibrium.
