# The Ascension of Ob'Sidara

Pillars of black glass reach toward the void, their surfaces warm to the touch despite the surrounding darkness. You stand at the edge of Ob'Sidara, a vast expanse of newborn mountains still writhing into existence, their obsidian spines cracking and groaning as they push ever skyward.

The air is surprisingly warm, carrying the scent of hot minerals and subtle electrical charges. You breathe deeply, noticing the thinness that comes with extreme altitude. The oxygen here is scarce, yet somehow sufficient—as if the laws of atmosphere bend to accommodate this impossible place.

No stars shine above. Instead, the darkness above is absolute, a velvet curtain punctuated only by occasional flares of deep crimson lightning that illuminate the landscape in brief, stark moments. These flashes reveal the true scale of Ob'Sidara—a mountain range still being born, stretching beyond sight in all directions, peaks rising impossibly high while new formations thrust upward from below.

The ground trembles constantly underfoot. Not the rhythmic pulse of earthquakes, but the irregular spasms of creation itself. Sometimes the vibrations remain subtle for hours, merely a hum beneath your boots. Then without warning, the very crust might buckle, sending new crystalline formations erupting upward in silence before shattering with a sound like distant thunder.

Water is but a memory here. The few depressions that might one day become lakes remain perfectly dry, their basins lined with geometric patterns of salt and mineral deposits that glow with faint bioluminescence—not true life, but something adjacent to it, a proto-organic chemistry still deciding what it might become.

Scattered across the endless obsidian plains and razor peaks stand curious structures, their purpose as enigmatic as their origin. Some appear as perfect hexagonal columns of an amber-like substance, unmarked by tools yet clearly not formed by nature alone. Others resemble half-melted spires of copper and silver, their surfaces inscribed with symbols that shift when not directly observed. None appear planned or purposeful—they stand isolated and irregular, like thoughts abandoned mid-conception.

The landscape is largely devoid of movement save for the geological birth-throes. No birds circle overhead; no insects buzz or crawl along the warm stone. The occasional dust devil spins across a flat expanse, picking up glittering particles that dance briefly before settling once more into new patterns.

Sound here behaves strangely. The constant geological activity—the grinding of new mountains forcing their way upward, the cracking of cooling stone, the occasional avalanche of crystalline debris—all these occur in muted tones, as if heard through thick fabric. Yet certain harmonics carry for impossible distances, so that the delicate tinkling of a stone sliding down a distant slope might reach your ears with perfect clarity while the mountain collapsing beside it registers as barely a whisper.

Most unsettling is the behavior of the shadows. During the brief illuminations from the crimson lightning, they don't simply appear and vanish as shadows should. They linger, stretching and contracting across the glassy surfaces, moving against the light rather than with it. Some gather in pools of absolute darkness from which emanate faint vibrations that might, with generous interpretation, be called whispers.

In the valleys between the tallest formations, curious dust storms gather and disperse with unpredictable frequency—not composed of typical particulates, but of fine crystalline material that seems to organize itself into complex, spiraling patterns before collapsing back into chaos. These storms generate no sound but produce waves of heat that distort the air, creating mirages that seem to reveal other landscapes layered beneath or behind this one.

The few flat plateaus that interrupt the jagged topography bear what appear to be impact craters, but closer inspection reveals them to be perfect inverted cones, their surfaces lined with concentric ridges spiraling inward. At their centers, small mounds of obsidian pebbles arrange and rearrange themselves continuously, as if sorting through possible configurations.

This is Ob'Sidara—a landscape caught in the act of becoming, its features still negotiating with physical law, its nature still undecided. It is a place of potentiality rather than actuality, where the boundary between creation and destruction vanishes into meaninglessness. In the constant warm darkness, the mountains continue their silent, violent ascension, indifferent to observation yet somehow aware of it.
