# The Undercity of Calderum

Descend through the narrow maintenance shaft, and the heat hits you first—a thick wall of it, pressing against your skin, forcing sweat from every pore. The ladder rungs burn your palms despite the fine layer of condensation that slicks their surface. You've entered Calderum, the subterranean industrial quarter that few surface-dwellers ever witness.

The main gallery unfolds before you, a cathedral of industry carved directly into the bedrock. Ceiling height varies dramatically—in some places barely clearing your head, in others soaring to three or four stories where ancient gas pockets once existed. Sharp, unweathered edges of recent excavation catch what little light there is. The rock itself seems almost fresh-cut in places, with tool marks still visible.

Dim illumination comes from scattered electric bulbs housed in brass cages, their yellow light barely penetrating the perpetual haze. Supplementing this are the occasional geothermal vents—deliberately tapped in some areas, unexpectedly breaking through in others—which cast an eerie reddish glow when their superheated steam catches the light just right.

Buildings crowd every available space, constructed from whatever materials could be brought down: corrugated metal, repurposed shipping containers, locally quarried stone. They press against each other like desperate strangers seeking shelter, leaving only the narrowest of passages between them. Some structures rise vertically, stacked three or four levels high where ceiling height permits, connected by precarious metal walkways and ladders. Many have simply been carved directly into the walls, creating warrens of living and working spaces.

The hottest areas near the thermal vents host the foundries and forges, where metals are worked in conditions barely tolerable to human endurance. Workers move slowly here, conserving energy, their movements economical as they tend to machinery that seems perpetually on the verge of breaking down. Ancient conveyor systems creak and groan, transporting raw materials from the deeper mines to processing stations.

Water is present but precious. It weeps from certain walls where underground seams have been tapped, collected in elaborate catchment systems of gutters and pipes. Moisture condenses on cooler surfaces, forming drops that fall with hypnotic regularity, each one measured and accounted for. In the eastern quarter, a small underground river runs through a carefully maintained channel, its flow regulated by a series of hand-operated locks.

Vegetation exists only by deliberate cultivation—hardy fungi grown in climate-controlled chambers, strange heat-resistant mosses that have adapted to the conditions and now carpet certain walls with their pale green growth. In one remarkable corner, a gardener has established a small grove of stunted, twisted trees that somehow survive in this hostile environment, their leaves unnaturally waxy and thick.

The sounds of Calderum are oddly subdued despite the constant activity. The thick rock absorbs noise, creating pockets of near-silence interrupted by the occasional hiss of steam, clang of metal, or low hum of machinery. Conversations are kept hushed, as if the residents fear disturbing something that sleeps within the deeper rock. Communication happens more through hand signals and the rhythmic tapping of metal tools that carries through the stone better than voices.

What's most unusual about Calderum is its relationship with time. With no daylight cycle, work and rest periods have evolved into a complex system unrelated to the surface world's schedule. Different sectors operate according to their own internal clocks, creating a perpetual staggered activity cycle. A metalworking shop might be in full production while the adjacent residential section is in its designated sleep period, residents sheltered behind thick curtains in windowless rooms.

No animals survive here—not even rats or insects—yet the place teems with movement. It's all human industry and mechanical motion, a closed system of production and consumption that has developed its own strange equilibrium in this hot, cramped underworld.

As you move deeper, you notice how the newest excavations reveal strange crystalline formations in the rock—formations that seem to glow faintly from within when the lights dim, though most residents pretend not to notice this unsettling feature. These areas are quickly covered with construction, as if to hide whatever secrets might lie within the ancient stone.

Calderum isn't meant to be beautiful, yet there's a certain harsh elegance to its efficiency, to the way its inhabitants have adapted to living in a place that should, by all rights, be uninhabitable.
