# The Silent City of Condensing Mists

From this height, the vastness unfolds beyond comprehension—a sprawling highland plateau that stretches to horizons so distant they blur into the perpetual ring of clouds circling the realm. The cool air carries moisture that never quite falls as rain, but neither does it fully evaporate. Instead, it condenses, collects, and flows in a thousand meandering paths.

This is Mirova, the City of Ten Thousand Cisterns, though "city" hardly captures its true nature. It is an endless expanse of human habitation—stonework structures built not upward but outward, spreading across the plateau like a vast living organism. From above, one sees the intricate lacework of channels, reservoirs, and collection basins that capture every drop of moisture from the air.

The structures are primarily of stone—quarried from the plateau itself over centuries—creating a curious union between what is built and what remains natural. Dark granite and milky quartz form much of the architecture, worn smooth by time and constant moisture. Buildings rarely exceed two stories, instead spreading laterally, connected by covered walkways that shelter travelers from the morning and evening mists.

Water is everywhere yet nowhere abundant. It seeps from walls, collects in carved basins, trickles down carefully engineered channels that run alongside every footpath. The sound of water—gentle dripping, soft flowing, the occasional splash as droplets fall from overhanging eaves—forms a subtle background murmur that paradoxically enhances rather than disturbs the profound quietness of the place.

Vegetation is precious and purposeful. Hardy mosses cultivated for their water-retention properties carpet north-facing walls. Pale blue-green lichens spread across rooftops, their subtle colors creating a patchwork visible only from certain angles when the light catches their surface. In courtyards and alongside the more important waterways, carefully tended ferns and low-growing herbs provide both sustenance and medicine. Nothing grows tall or wild; everything is managed, cultivated, respected.

The inhabitants move with deliberate slowness through their vast home. They are not many considering the size of their domain—perhaps this contributes to the silence. They wear layers of finely woven fabrics in colors that echo the landscape: slate blues, misty greys, and the occasional accent of copper or bronze. They carry carved wooden vessels to collect water from communal cisterns, and their footfalls on the worn stone paths make almost no sound.

There are no machines here, no contraptions more complex than the system of water collection itself. Tools are simple—stone, wood, bone, and occasional metalwork for specialized implements. Yet the engineering of the water systems reveals a profound understanding of gravity, flow, and the behavior of moisture in air.

Rare birds with wingspans stretching wider than a person's height ride the thermal currents that rise from the plateau's edge. They're seen as omens—neither good nor bad, simply messengers from whatever lies beyond. Small amphibians thrive in the constant damp, their metallic skins flashing occasionally as they dart between shadows. Fish swimming in the larger cisterns are watched rather than caught—living indicators of water purity.

The weather moves across Mirova in slow, predictable patterns. Morning brings heavy mist that condenses on every surface. Midday sees thin sunlight breaking through, causing momentary rainbows where light strikes water droplets. Afternoons bring winds that sweep across the plateau, carrying moisture to the collection systems on the western edge. Evenings see a gentle cooling that starts the cycle of condensation anew.

Time here is measured in the filling and emptying of cisterns, the slow erosion of stone steps, the gradual polishing of handrails by countless palms. Nothing is rushed, but nothing is truly static. The city breathes—inhaling mist, exhaling quiet footsteps and whispered conversations.

From edge to center and back again would take many days of walking. Some inhabitants spend their entire lives exploring just one sector of Mirova, learning its rhythms, contributing to its maintenance. Others walk the long paths as couriers or traders, carrying news and goods from one distant quarter to another.

In Mirova, there is no wild nature to conquer—only balance to maintain. The plateau existed before the first stone was placed, and the people understand they are merely temporary stewards of something far greater and more enduring than themselves.
