The plateau begins above the weather.

Its rim is a wall of black-green stone rising almost vertically from the lowlands, not a sharp young escarpment but an old, settled face: terraced by mineral seams, rounded at every exposed edge, upholstered wherever roots can hold. Water threads down it in thousands of silver lines. From a distance they seem motionless. Some are waterfalls, some merely wet rock reflecting the sun, and some flow upward so slowly that a person can watch for an hour without being certain which way the droplets have moved.

At the summit there is no bare alpine cold. The air is hot, heavy, and startlingly deep. Moisture condenses on skin and leaf even at noon. Clouds form beneath the rim as well as above it, leaving the high country suspended between two weather systems. The upper surface extends for many days’ travel: a shallow basin of rain forest, reed marsh, warm lakes, and broad shelves of dark stone. Peaks rise within it like islands, but all belong to the same immense elevation.

The heat should be impossible here. So should the forests.

Trees grow densely along the basin’s waterways, their trunks pale and smooth, splitting twenty or thirty meters above the ground into baskets of glossy foliage. They do not sway much. Their crowns interlock and brace one another, so that whole groves respond to wind as a single, slow body. Vines hang in heavy cables between them. Broad epiphytes gather rain in layered cups, each cup hosting larvae, translucent snails, floating pollen, and small fish brought up from the marshes by storms—or by some less obvious means.

Between the groves lie open wetlands bright with reeds. Their saturated soil is almost black, though the standing water is clear enough to reveal mats of red root and pale mineral nodules below. Footsteps release bubbles with a metallic smell. Rain falls often but without violence: large warm drops, widely spaced, continuing for hours. Afterward the entire plateau exhales. Mist lifts waist-high over the marshes, and every exposed surface begins to bead with water.

Much of the water is held near the surface rather than allowed to escape. It moves through the country in wide, nearly level sheets, divided by low natural levees and ancient stone ribs. Streams wander, rejoin, and wander again. Where they reach the plateau’s edge, they hesitate in hanging swamps before spilling over. Orchids and mosses grow to the brink, trailing into the white emptiness below.

The lakes are long and narrow, fitted into folds in the underlying rock. Their shores have been stable for ages. Trees lean over them without being undermined; beaches consist of smooth green-black pebbles arranged in bands so regular they resemble survey marks. On still afternoons, reflections in the water are slightly displaced from the objects above. A branch may appear a handspan to the left of itself, or the reflected sun may continue glowing several seconds after a cloud has crossed it. Animals drinking there ignore this.

The landscape is loud, though little in it moves quickly.

Insects maintain a continuous layered drone, rising from the marsh and descending from the canopy. Large tree frogs produce low pulses that can be felt through the soles of the feet. Colonies of reed-dwelling birds call back and forth across whole valleys, passing a note along so slowly that one call may take half an hour to cross the basin. Water sounds everywhere: dripping, pouring, bubbling under roots, ticking into stone pools, hissing at the cliff edge. Beneath these is a deeper sound, steady and almost too low to hear—the plateau’s long resonance, like machinery operating many kilometers underground.

There is machinery, though no one agrees where it ends and geology begins.

Across the basin stand scattered structures: narrow towers of a dull pearl material, low archways half buried in moss, and windowless pavilions placed on isolated shelves of rock. They are too few to dominate the land. Many days may pass between sightings. None has a road leading to it. The towers taper upward and vanish into permanent caps of condensation, their surfaces neither wet nor dry. Lichens avoid them by a finger’s breadth.

At irregular intervals a tower emits a soft flash—not bright, merely comprehensive. For an instant, every pool in sight becomes perfectly flat. The insect drone drops by a fraction of a tone. Loose droplets lift from leaves, pause, and settle again. Nothing else seems to happen.

Yet the heat remains constant.

The prevailing explanation, among the few people who live here, is that the structures do not produce warmth. They alter the plateau’s relation to altitude. Within the basin, height does not correspond cleanly to atmospheric thinning or cold. Pressure, gravity, and distance are gently edited around the towers, not enough to disturb daily life, but enough to sustain a hot wet forest several kilometers above the lowlands. Climbing a hill may add elevation without increasing one’s distance from the center of the world. A falling object reaches the ground at the expected speed, unless it falls into open water, in which case its descent may be postponed.

No controls are visible. Broken fragments cannot be cut, melted, scanned, or carried far from where they lie. A shard moved downslope is usually found upslope the next morning, resting in damp moss with beetles beneath it. The technology is not active in any familiar sense. It behaves more like a law of nature that was installed and then left to weather.

Its weathering is exceedingly slow.

The pavilions have rounded corners and shallow channels worn into their thresholds. Their interiors are cool but never cold. Some contain benches grown from the floor; others contain only a vertical oval of air that appears denser than the room around it. Through such ovals one sees another portion of the plateau—sometimes nearby, sometimes impossibly distant, sometimes as it looked centuries before. Passage is unreliable. A hand pushed through may emerge dripping with lake water while the view shows dry forest. People use the ovals chiefly for observation and leave offerings of fruit, salt, and polished stone nearby, less as worship than as courtesy.

Human habitation is similarly dispersed. Houses stand on high root-islands or against stable cliff shelves, built of woven reed, dark timber, and scavenged panels that regulate their own temperature. Roofs are steep, with gutters feeding cistern gardens. Villages are rarely more than six or seven buildings. Elevated walkways cross the wettest ground, their posts furred with moss and nesting insects. At night, dim marker lights appear along certain paths even where no lamp has been placed. The inhabitants follow them unless the lights are blue. Blue paths are said to lead to locations that exist but are not presently attached to the basin.

Fauna fills every layer.

Long-legged grazers move through the marsh in loose herds, their hides patterned like wet bark. They browse on floating plants and carry bright cleaning birds along their spines. Arboreal animals with six gripping limbs travel through the joined crowns, seldom touching the ground. Their warning calls resemble stones struck together. Kite-sized insects circle above the lakes, glassy wings flexing rather than beating, and fish surface to take them with slow, deliberate mouths.

Along the cliff edges live broad-bodied gliders. They bask on hot rock with membranous sails folded tightly against their flanks, then drop into the cloud below. Hours later they return from above, descending out of clear sky. Whether this is migration or merely a consequence of the plateau’s altered geometry is unknown.

The largest animals are heard more often than seen. Something passes beneath the marsh at the pace of growing roots, raising a low ridge of mud that may advance a few meters in a day. Reed beds bend ahead of it. Frogs fall silent as it nears and resume behind it. Old maps mark several such routes, all curving toward the towers.

Predation is present but rarely sudden. Many hunters wait rather than chase. A flower may take weeks to close around a nesting colony. A pale catlike animal follows a herd for a season before selecting a weakened member. In the northern lakes, ribbon-shaped creatures herd fish by changing the reflective angle of their scales, confusing the water’s displaced images until prey swim into the real mouths hidden among them.

Decay is rapid in the heat, but nothing disappears cleanly. Fallen trunks become gardens. Bones collect minerals and turn green. Shed shells fill with rain and breed midges. The dense abundance is held in balance by countless small exchanges, few of them hurried. Even disease moves ceremonially: leaf blights travel in visible rings, crossing a grove over decades, followed at a precise distance by new growth.

There are storms, but they arrive slowly. Pressure gathers for days. Animal calls deepen. The tower flashes become more frequent. Then cloud rises from below the cliffs and pours over the rim like a pale flood, joining the cloud above. Thunder continues almost without interruption, rolling around the basin until direction loses meaning. The rain itself remains warm. It fills every hollow, submerges the walkways, and turns the reed valleys into connected lakes.

During these storms the landscape’s stranger properties become obvious. Waterfalls reverse. Fish appear briefly in puddles atop boulders. Distant pavilions seem close enough to touch, while nearby trees recede behind kilometers of rain. The laws do not break dramatically; they loosen. Distances become negotiable. Gravity develops preferences. Sound arrives by routes unavailable to bodies.

When the storm passes, the water drains with equal patience. No catastrophic torrents gouge new canyons. Ancient channels accept the surplus. Roots tighten the soil. Cliffside vegetation bows and rises. The plateau returns to the shape it has kept for longer than any local account measures.

At sunset, ordinary gold light crosses the basin. It catches on wet leaves, tower surfaces, bird wings, and innumerable suspended droplets. There are no lurid alien colors, no constant spectacle. The strangeness is embedded too deeply for ornament. A herder walks a familiar causeway. Frogs begin their low percussion. Steam lifts from sun-warmed stone. Somewhere beyond the trees, an unseen waterfall continues its long ascent.

Then the towers make their nearly inaudible adjustment, and the whole high country settles a little more firmly into its impossible place.
