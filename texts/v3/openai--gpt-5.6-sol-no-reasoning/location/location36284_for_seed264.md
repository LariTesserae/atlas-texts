The land is a warm, low basin of pale stone, broad enough that its far margins dissolve into ordinary daylight. Nothing grows. There is no scrub in the cracks, no lichen on shaded faces, no buried seeds waiting for rain. The ground has the settled nakedness of something that reached its final form long ago.

Across the basin stand scattered mineral outcrops: blunt stacks of iron-dark rock, shelves of yellow limestone, and rounded pillars glazed with old silica. They are far apart, with large floors of compacted silt between them. Erosion has finished its dramatic work here. Edges are softened, channels established, loose slopes already fallen. Even the rubble lies in stable aprons sorted by size, each fragment lodged as if assigned a permanent place.

Moisture survives without making the country green. Shallow riverbeds braid through the flats, holding only intermittent ribbons of warm, metallic water. Beneath overhangs, condensation collects steadily and runs down polished grooves into small basins. There are pockets of black water in sinkholes, their surfaces filmed with copper-colored minerals. Rain occurs occasionally—a mild vertical rain from an unremarkable gray cloud deck—but it does not seem to alter anything. The same drops strike the same hollows. The same channels fill to the same depth. The same water withdraws beneath the ground.

The basin is loud.

A continuous industrial thunder comes from below: the deep cycling of immense pistons, the hammering of pressure valves, the drawn-out groan of loaded bearings. At intervals, a sharper detonation travels through the bedrock and returns from the escarpments as a perfectly repeated echo. The stone beneath one's feet trembles with the steady rhythm of machinery at full production.

Yet there are no machines.

No tower, pipe, road, cut block, wire, spoil heap, or foundation interrupts the mineral plain. The cliffs show only sedimentary bands and ancient faults. The vents in the ground have irregular lips. The shafts are natural sinkholes, winding rather than drilled. Even where the noise is strongest—at a field of narrow fissures surrounded by rust-red crusts—there is no visible mechanism below, only darkness, heat, and the smell of wet iron.

The industry appears to be geology itself.

Deep under the basin, strata compress and release in precise cycles. Aquifers act as hydraulic lines. Cavities resonate like turbine housings. Columns of dense brine descend through one set of faults while steam rises through another, turning hidden wheels that may not be wheels at all. Electrical potential accumulates between mineral seams and discharges in long, measured pulses. The entire basin functions as a factory without builders and, perhaps, without products. It consumes no apparent fuel. It leaves no waste. Its motions have reached such complete equilibrium that production and erosion cancel one another exactly.

Along the riverbeds lie low crusts of salt and manganese arranged in parallel ribs. They resemble cooling fins, though each is demonstrably deposited by water. Hexagonal plates of dark crystal occur around certain vents with the regularity of manufactured grating. When warm rain touches them, current passes from plate to plate in pale blue threads. The threads never branch differently. Every discharge follows a channel burned smooth over ages.

Time does not visibly accumulate here. Dust is shaken from the rocks as quickly as it settles. Condensed water replaces what evaporates. Mineral growth matches mineral dissolution. A chip struck from an outcrop would seem an act of violence against the basin's balance; one suspects that, left unattended, the fragment would be carried through a sequence of floods and tremors until it returned to the exact scar from which it came.

The light is plain and sufficient. There are no theatrical suns or colored auroras, only warm daylight filtered through high haze. It reveals the basin honestly: ocher flats, gray-white shelves, black water, reddish seams. Shadows remain soft and short. At night, if night occurs, it leaves no evidence by morning. The subterranean rhythm does not slow.

Life is almost absent. In some condensation wells, a translucent film thickens along the waterline. It has no green pigment and gives off no odor. Whether it is microbial growth or a self-organizing mineral membrane is uncertain; under vibration it contracts into evenly spaced beads, then spreads again when the pressure cycle ends. Rare pale animals emerge from flooded cracks—soft-bodied, eyeless things no longer than a finger. They lie against the warm stone and pulse in time with the engines below. They do not feed in any observable way. After several minutes they withdraw, always before the same low-frequency concussion.

Far out on the flats, larger movement is sometimes visible: a solitary, many-jointed silhouette crossing between stone pillars. It moves slowly, pauses during each subterranean impact, and leaves no tracks in the damp silt. It is too infrequent to become part of the scenery. One may watch for hours and see nothing but the slight quivering of water in the sinkholes.

The place has rules, though no signs announce them. Water repeats its routes. Echoes return at fixed intervals. Warmth is evenly distributed, neither building toward eruption nor fading into dormancy. The underground production cannot be approached, because every promising passage narrows, floods, or curves back toward daylight. No opening is artificial, yet all seem designed to deny access.

Most unsettling is not the mechanical noise but its lack of consequence. The pounding should fracture cliffs. Steam should exhaust reservoirs. Pressure should rise or diminish. Something should wear out. Instead, the basin has been weathered into compatibility with the process. Every rock face is tuned to the vibration; every cavity participates in the resonance. What would elsewhere be disruption is here the permanent climate.

The traveler crosses an empty mineral region under normal skies while an invisible industrial civilization seems to labor beneath every step. But there is no civilization—only a landscape that has, over immeasurable time, become indistinguishable from a working machine.

It is not waiting. It is not progressing.

It is operating.
