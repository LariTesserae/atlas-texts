The city lies across a warm plain only a few meters above the reach of an unseen sea. It is not crowded. From any tower, the greater part of it appears to be absence: dark boulevards a kilometer wide, parks without paths, empty foundations sunk among reeds, and districts separated by long tracts of luminous foliage. Buildings gather in small constellations rather than blocks. Five towers here, a market vault there, a row of low dwellings half hidden under broad leaves—then nothing inhabited for a great distance.

Yet the place is deafening.

The noise comes first through the feet. A continuous subsonic pressure travels beneath the plain, too deep to hear cleanly, shaking dew from the plants and making loose stones creep in minute circles. Above it rises the metallic roar of the city’s transit system. Vehicles do not move along the roads; they are drawn from one district to another through engineered discontinuities, vanishing with a report like sheet lightning and returning several kilometers away amid a clap of displaced air. Every few seconds, somewhere beyond sight, the atmosphere booms.

Animals answer the machinery. Flocks of black, translucent-winged creatures lift from the canopy by the tens of thousands, producing a dry thunder. Marsh insects pulse in synchronized waves. Hollow-throated grazers call from the vacant avenues, each note amplified by their branching antlers until it resembles a harbor horn. In the ornamental forests, unseen predators crack membranes in the air to confuse their prey. Silence occurs only locally, suddenly, in perfect round pockets. These pockets drift like weather. Stepping into one is startling: the city’s violence continues visibly—the beating wings, trembling leaves, distant flashes—but no vibration crosses the boundary.

No one has determined whether these quiet zones are failures in the world or functioning pieces of its infrastructure.

The plain itself is young. A few generations ago it was a level expanse of pale mineral foam, extruded overnight from fractures beneath the old coast. It has not had time to soften. The ground breaks in fresh-edged plates, black on top and nacreous within. Shallow fissures run arrow-straight for kilometers before dividing abruptly into geometric deltas. In places the crust has folded into low ridges sharp enough to cut footwear, though creepers already pour over them in glossy green cataracts.

Water is scarce in volume but ubiquitous in trace. Rain comes perhaps twice a month, warm and brief, falling in enormous drops that leave circular bruises in the dust. Most moisture arrives as condensation. At dusk, the undersides of leaves cool beyond what the air should permit. Water films gather there, travel against gravity along the veins, and drip into suspended root cups. Buildings sweat as well. Their shaded walls pearl with clean water, while gutters carry it not downward but sideways, distributing it to cistern gardens far across the city.

There are no permanent rivers. Instead, narrow channels wake after rainfall and run for an hour or two through the lowest quarters. Their beds are lined with silver scales, dormant organisms that uncurl when wet and swim upstream in flashing schools. Before noon the channels are dry again, leaving fishlike shapes sealed under a skin of transparent clay. Larger pockets of water persist in collapse basins: dark, perfectly still ponds crowded with lilies, larvae, and long-legged amphibians. These ponds are deeper than the geology allows. Soundings return inconsistent results, and objects dropped into them sometimes fall from low cloud days later.

Vegetation has taken the new land with astonishing speed. The city does not stand against wilderness so much as provide a trellis for it. Warmth, condensed water, and mineral-rich dust support immense leaf mass despite the general dryness. Ferns with copper undersides occupy the roads. Rope-vines span the gaps between towers, sagging beneath colonies of sleeping animals. Pale trunkless trees grow as branching sheets, each one a vertical fan rooted along a hundred-meter line. When the wind shifts, these trees slowly turn themselves edge-on.

Much of the greenery is dim-adapted. The sky remains covered by a permanent veil of high particulate matter, violet-gray by day and almost black by afternoon. There is no bright noon. Plants use broad, near-black leaves to gather what little light reaches the ground, and many supplement it through stranger means: feeding on electrical gradients, on vibration, perhaps on probability. Around the loudest transit stations, moss grows meters thick. Its surface ripples whenever a vehicle arrives. Cut into it, the moss reveals concentric rings corresponding not to years but to repeated frequencies.

Flowers are uncommon and conspicuous. They open in the city’s moments of silence. Whole fields may remain closed for weeks, showing only matte green bulbs; then a quiet pocket passes over them and tens of thousands unfold at once, exposing interiors that glow a subdued blue. Pollinators rush into the mute zone from every direction. When sound returns, the flowers snap shut hard enough to launch their seeds above the canopy.

The fauna is abundant beyond easy counting, though little of it is large. Life fills surfaces rather than space. Beetles tile shaded walls. Warm condensation pipes contain entire ribbonlike ecologies. Tiny grazing animals travel in orderly columns down empty tramways, their fur carrying spores from district to district. In the plazas, six-legged browsers crop the resilient groundcover. Their tall, narrow bodies resemble walking knots of root, and they freeze when the subterranean pulse changes.

The sky belongs to flocking things. Some have feathers, some membranes, some rotating vanes like seed pods. They avoid the transit corridors with exquisite precision, dividing around an arriving vehicle a heartbeat before the air erupts. At night, aerial predators descend from the particulate veil. Only their mouths are visible: curved rows of bioluminescent marks moving through darkness without an apparent body.

The city was planned for a population far greater than the one that inhabits it. Or perhaps it was planned for beings that require more room.

Its structures are low, warm-colored, and deceptively simple. Most resemble terraces of porous ceramic, grown rather than assembled. Up close, the walls are busy with microscopic apertures. They inhale at night, separate moisture and useful gases, and exhale warm mineral scents at dawn. Their interiors reconfigure slowly around occupants. Doors migrate. Rooms bud from corridors. A neglected house may close itself entirely, digest its furniture, and become a dense seed-shaped object waiting for another resident.

Above these dwellings rise a few impossibly thin towers. They have no windows, antennas, or visible entrances. Each bends slightly away from anyone who approaches. During rain they produce vertical shafts of darkness extending into the clouds, within which droplets rise. The towers are believed to stabilize something—not the weather, exactly, nor the land, but the agreement between measurements. When one ceased functioning, the surrounding district developed three conflicting elevations. Stairs led both upward and downward to the same square. Residents abandoned it, but animals flourish there.

Infrastructure is everywhere and almost invisible. Street surfaces heal cuts before the blade can be withdrawn. Waste is carried away by colonies of engineered mites. Lamp posts emit no light; instead, they alter nearby eyes, making certain paths perceptible as faint amber bands. Visitors who remain too long begin seeing these paths even outside the city, sometimes leading across open air or into sealed ground.

Despite all this capability, the inhabited districts are sparse. In the evening, one may walk for hours without meeting anyone. A café glows under a grove of water-gathering leaves, but there is only one table. A station the size of an airport receives two passengers. Vast apartment terraces tend gardens for absent households. Public announcements roll through empty plazas at overwhelming volume, spoken in layered voices and in tenses that do not quite exist: warnings of events that might already have happened, welcomes addressed to citizens not yet instantiated.

The residents accept distance as a civic necessity. No two sleeping people are permitted within three hundred meters of one another. Certain thoughts, if dreamed close together, are said to resonate and acquire mass. Old maps show dense neighborhoods from before the rule. In those places stand smooth, heavy forms with the approximate dimensions of buildings but no openings. Birds will not land on them. The ground nearby sinks a fraction lower each year.

So the city spreads itself extravagantly over the plain. Its emptiness is not neglect but spacing: a design principle, a quarantine, perhaps a form of politeness.

After dusk, the warmth lingers close to the ground. Condensation ticks from leaf to leaf. The particulate sky hides the stars, though occasionally one appears below the cloud layer, moving slowly between towers. Transit detonations continue. Creatures call, wings churn, membranes drum, and the buried pulse presses upward through every foundation.

Then, without warning, one of the silence pockets crosses the plain.

The roar vanishes along a moving boundary. Trees enter stillness branch by branch. Animals crowd behind it, following the hush. Blue flowers burst open in sweeping arcs. For a few minutes, an observer can see the true scale of the landscape: the low black ridges newly born from the earth; the intermittent silver channels; the towers leaning away from unseen pressures; the scattered inhabited lights; and between them immense gardens breathing in the dark.

Far out, where there are no recorded structures, something answers the silence by switching on.

It resembles a second city, very distant and very large. Its lights appear one after another beneath the ground.
