Beneath the winter sea, inside a split no wider than a hand, there is a city.

It occupies the sheltered seam between two slabs of black stone. The slabs have only recently begun to part. Their inner faces are raw and dark, almost without scratches, as though the rock were not broken but still deciding how to become two pieces. Cold mineral water seeps through hairline faults, adding thin clouds of milk-white precipitate to the surrounding brine. These clouds settle slowly, building ledges, chimneys, arches, and hollow bulbs. Nothing here is old enough to have become ruin. Even the ruins are still growing.

From outside, the place looks like a pale stain in a crack. Within, it has avenues.

The avenues are narrow channels kept open by traffic: processions of transparent shrimp, flatworms with rippling rims, seed-sized crabs, and pale amphipods moving shoulder to shoulder. Their bodies brush constantly against the soft walls. Where currents divide, the inhabitants divide with them; where currents return, they converge into quiet, dense knots. No one excavated these roads. Repeated passage prevents the mineral dust from settling, and so movement itself makes the plan of the city.

Buildings rise on either side, though none were constructed by tools. Tube worms have grown in clusters so regular they resemble organ pipes. Sponges occupy the intersections, their porous towers drawing water through chambers and releasing it in slow, nearly invisible jets. Bryozoans spread branching balconies between them. Limpets tile the lower walls. Above, colonies of translucent tunicates hang like communal lanterns, each individual no larger than a grain of rice, sharing a single gelatinous architecture.

Everything is inhabited by something smaller.

The tube worms’ casings shelter mites. The mites carry spores. Bacteria coat the spores. Minute flagellates graze the bacterial films, and viruses move among them in numbers too great to give the water any meaning as emptiness. The city is dense beyond crowding, but its density is layered by scale. A creature may cross a plaza without realizing that the plaza is the feeding surface of another creature, which in turn lives on the jaw of a third.

There are few plants. True leaves cannot live in this depth and dimness. Strands of dark red algae persist only near the crack’s upper mouth, where a diluted green twilight reaches down from the ice-covered surface. They grow sparsely, as fine as hair, and bend in the tidal breath. Deeper within, vegetation gives way to bacterial gardens: cream, rust, violet-black. These films feed on the young stone’s chemistry. They form borders, fields, and terraces wherever cold seeps bring dissolved minerals from the rock.

The water is barely above freezing. It presses into every chamber and corridor, heavy with salt. Fresh meltwater sometimes slips downward from far above and gathers against the roof in inverted pools, lighter than the sea around it. Small animals enter these lenses and hang upside down along the boundary, feeding where two waters meet. To them the underside of the freshwater is a second sky—a trembling ceiling through which shapes pass distorted and enormous.

The city has no fire, no metal, no cut stone, no ownership, and no devices. Its infrastructure is appetite.

Sponges pump the water. Worms stabilize the walls. Burrowing bivalves open drains through soft deposits. Grazers keep bacterial avenues from choking shut. Predators enforce a shifting geography of danger. A hunting snail’s passage empties a district. When it leaves, scavengers arrive, then grazers, then settlers that attach to the newly bare surfaces. Every vacancy is temporary.

The largest animal here is a juvenile wolffish, though even it could curl inside a cup. It waits beyond the crowded center in a cavity lined with shell fragments. Its breathing is slow. At intervals it thrusts its blunt head into an avenue and the avenue vanishes into side passages: shrimp spring backward, worms fold into tubes, amphipods pour through holes in the walls. The fish takes one creature and withdraws. Within minutes, the traffic restores the road.

Other disturbances are less sudden. The crack opens by a fraction. No sound accompanies it that a human ear could hear. Yet the city registers the movement everywhere. Mineral towers lean. Membranes tighten. A bridge of bryozoans breaks and drifts downward in white pieces, each piece carrying its living occupants into lower neighborhoods. New seepage begins along the fresh fracture. Bacteria find it first, appearing as a dull sheen. Then come the grazers; then the tube builders; then the hunters following them.

Thus urban growth proceeds: not outward across a landscape, but inward along newly possible surfaces.

The light is dim and indirect. Most of it descends as blue-green residue from the remote day above. Some is made locally. Tiny crustaceans flash when disturbed, their bodies giving off brief cold sparks. A comb jelly sometimes squeezes through the upper slit, comb rows trembling with faint spectral color. Certain polyps shine steadily enough to mark the busiest junctions, though they shine for themselves or for prey, not for navigation. Nevertheless, animals gather around them, and luminous neighborhoods emerge without intention.

There is almost no sound. Water carries distant pressure: ice shifting far overhead, the blunt pulse of waves, the minute crackle of feeding limbs against shell. But inside the seam these arrive softened into sensation rather than noise. The loudest events are intimate—the snap of a shrimp’s joint, a shell closing, grains sliding down a fresh mineral slope. Even these are quickly absorbed by the bodies, tubes, films, and soft deposits packed into the fissure.

Silence does not mean stillness. Cilia beat in billions. Water passes through pores. Mouthparts comb surfaces. Eggs loosen and rise until they collect beneath overhangs like beads of clear rain. Larvae drift through districts their adult forms will never leave. Predators follow chemical trails. Waste becomes food before it travels the length of a body. The whole place circulates, consumes, precipitates, hatches, and molts.

Its strangest law concerns size. The deeper one travels into the city, the less dependable scale becomes. A sponge pore opens into a branching interior more extensive than the avenue outside it. Parasites live within those chambers, and smaller chambers open within the parasites. Meanwhile, the crack itself is only one pore in a reef hidden beneath the sea floor, and that reef may be only a pale crust on a stone.

No inhabitant sees the city whole. There may be no scale at which it is whole.

At the lowest end of the fissure, where light fails completely, the new rock is still soft. Mineral skins inflate from seep water and collapse under the touch of passing animals. Pale chimneys grow for hours, break, and begin again. Here the city has not yet chosen its streets. Life crowds the unfinished surfaces, testing each provisional arch and dissolving wall. Construction and erosion are the same young process, and both are performed by water.

Above lies the cold ocean, immense and dark beneath its roof of ice. Below lies unbroken stone. Between them, concealed in a crack that could be covered by a palm, the crowded settlement continues to make itself—quietly, blindly, and faster than the landscape can harden around it.
