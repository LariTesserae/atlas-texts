The land is low, warm, and almost entirely hidden.

From above—if anything could remain above it long enough to look—the region would appear as a dark green plain extending between young ridges of black stone. It has no continuous river, no lake large enough to shine. Water occurs in fragments: shallow channels that run for an afternoon and vanish by nightfall; warm pools gathered in stone hollows; condensation beading perpetually on the undersides of leaves. Rain comes rarely but violently. Between rains, the forest lives on what it can catch from its own breath.

The ground is new.

Long seams of basalt have pushed through the soil in recent generations, splitting root mats and lifting whole groves onto tilted shelves. The rock has not yet been softened into rounded forms. It breaks in blades, steps, and fresh glassy faces, dark as cooled pitch. In some places the crust is only a few handspans thick. Warm vapor leaks through hairline cracks, carrying mineral odors—sulfur, wet iron, salt—and feeding pale films that gleam without sunlight.

Nothing here has had time to become spacious.

Vegetation crowds every exposed surface. Trees rise on tangled buttress roots, not especially tall but packed so closely that their crowns interlock. Their trunks are lacquer-dark from condensation. Thick ropes of vine pass from tree to tree until the whole forest behaves less like a collection of plants than one suspended body. Ferns erupt from branch forks. Broad leaves overlap in layers, each shaped to catch runoff from the one above. Cup plants store cloudy water in their centers, and tiny rootless weeds drift over those cups like green dust.

The canopy does not admit daylight so much as dilute it. At noon, the forest floor lies in a deep bottle-green dusk. At night, which seems to begin early and end reluctantly, there is almost no distinction between open eye and closed. Shapes are known by warmth, smell, pressure, and sound. Occasionally a split in the canopy reveals a strip of bright sky, but the gap is quickly crossed by wings, leaves, or rising steam.

The darkness is not quiet.

It is almost impossible to hear any single creature for more than a heartbeat. The forest produces sound in layers: the continuous grind of insects; throat-drumming from the flooded hollows; shrieks exchanged between unseen crowns; leathery wingbeats; claws skittering on basalt; branches snapping under moving weight. Some calls are so low they are felt through the ribs before they are heard. Others are thin enough to make the teeth ache.

At intervals, the entire region erupts.

A flock may lift from the canopy in numbers large enough to shove the leaves downward with the recoil of their departure. Burrowing colonies may surface all at once, covering a clearing in pale, jointed bodies. Tree-dwellers flee ahead of hunting swarms, and the flight of one species provokes another until the disturbance travels for leagues: crowns shaking, vines recoiling, fruit raining onto stone, predators following prey and larger predators following them.

There are no paths. Any temporary opening closes rapidly. Seedlings straighten after passage. Creepers loosen from branches and drop into gaps. Fungi inflate from bruised soil overnight. Even animal trails migrate because the animals themselves seldom travel alone. Herds push routes through the undergrowth, abandon them when parasites accumulate, and cut new routes elsewhere. A place crossed in the morning may be impassable by evening.

The abundance is deceptive, because free water is scarce. Every pocket of moisture is occupied.

Tree hollows contain transparent larvae, flat-backed crabs, drinking moths, rootlets, eggs, and ambush hunters resembling fragments of bark. Steam vents are ringed by soft red moss and heat-loving grazers. When rain fills the channels, fishlike animals emerge from buried mucus cases and crowd the flow before it has finished forming. They feed, mate, and lay their drought-proof eggs in a single feverish cycle. As the water sinks, winged scavengers descend, and the channels become ribbons of clicking shells.

Rain transforms the forest more violently than fire.

It begins with warm drops large enough to slap leaves sideways. Then the canopy becomes a roof under bombardment. Water pours down trunk grooves, floods root chambers, and turns ash-rich soil into black paste. Dry gullies become torrents within minutes. Because the stone is fresh and sharp, the water does not meander; it plunges along fractures, changing direction abruptly, stripping loose soil but scarcely rounding the rock beneath.

The animals respond before the first drop. Burrows seal. Arboreal colonies knot themselves together beneath branches. Amphibious things climb upward from cracks in the ground. Great long-legged browsers stand motionless among the buttress roots, their nostrils closing while rain streams from the fringes of their hides. Afterward, everything feeds.

Nothing decays quietly. Fallen matter is occupied before it settles. A fruit striking the ground vanishes beneath mouths. A carcass seems to ripple, collapse, and disperse. Even shed skins are cut apart and carried away. The soil remains oddly thin despite the forest’s richness; nutrients do not rest in it. They circulate in bodies, roots, spores, and fluids, passing from one hunger to the next.

There are signs of tool use, though no buildings and scarcely any artifacts.

Small dark-furred primates break volcanic glass from fresh outcrops by striking stones together. They use the flakes immediately—to open armored fruit, cut parasitic vines from their limbs, or slice into nests—and then drop them. Certain long-beaked marsh birds select hooked twigs to pull grubs from steaming cracks. In the western thickets, colonies of broad-handed creatures weave living leaves into rain shelters, but these are temporary folds in the vegetation, still rooted and growing, and cannot quite be called structures. Nothing stands apart from the ecology long enough to become architecture.

The nearest thing to a law of the place is that all stability attracts occupation.

A quiet pool clouds with eggs. An exposed ledge acquires basking bodies. A dead branch becomes a bridge, a hunting platform, a nursery, and finally food. Any reliable fruiting tree accumulates such a dense halo of feeders and predators that it seems to possess weather of its own. Even silence, when it occurs, is rapidly filled.

There are stranger patterns.

On windless nights, vast sections of the canopy begin pulsing with dim blue-green light. The glow does not come from one organism. Fungi brighten along the trunks, insects answer from the leaves, and throat sacs flare among the branches until the forest appears to breathe in color. The illumination travels outward in rings, though no obvious center exists. Animals stop calling as each ring passes. For a few seconds, the deafening forest becomes perfectly still.

Then the sound returns all at once.

Elsewhere, basalt vents exhale warm mist in regular intervals. Trees around them have grown their leaves into funnels, directing condensation toward their own roots. Whole communities depend on these breathlike emissions. When a vent closes—something that happens without warning—the grove enters immediate crisis. Roots crack stone in search of another seep. Animals raid the water cups of plants. Predators gather at the remaining damp places. Within days, the lushness thins; within weeks, another vent may open nearby, and the living mass shifts toward it.

Thus the forest migrates without going anywhere.

Its densest regions creep over the plain according to moisture, heat, and the movements of animals. Groves collapse and re-form. Channels appear, fill with life, and seal beneath roots. Young ridges rise through old feeding grounds. Nothing is ancient except the competition itself.

At the margins, where the canopy breaks against fields of newly exposed stone, the landscape becomes briefly legible. Black terraces extend beneath a low, humid sky. Curtains of vine hang from the forest edge. Thousands of flying animals commute between isolated green islands, filling the air like blown ash. Shallow rain pools reflect no stars because they are already covered in bodies.

Behind them, the darkness of the forest waits without stillness—warm, wet in patches, furiously alive, continually making and unmaking its own ground.
