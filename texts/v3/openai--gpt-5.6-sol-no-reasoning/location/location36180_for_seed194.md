The city is hidden inside a seed.

Not a small seed by ordinary measures, though nothing here permits ordinary measure. Its shell lies buried beneath an old mineral plain, wedged in a seam of cool salt and basalt where no rain has fallen and no groundwater remembers passing. From outside it might be mistaken for a black stone: thumb-shaped, blunt at one end, polished by pressures older than the surface above it. Its coat has reached the settled smoothness of things that can scarcely erode further.

Inside is a hollow.

The hollow is enormous to those forms of life that belong to it, yet nearly empty. Its chambers are not packed with tissue as a seed’s chambers should be. They are long, vaulted absences held open by ribs of dry cellulose. A traveler can walk for hours beneath one arch and meet nothing but the next arch, and beyond that another darkness.

There is no water. The city has never known it.

Its life is vegetal, but not green. The plants here conduct no familiar photosynthesis; there is no light to harvest, not even the dimmest mineral glow. Their chemistry is founded on dry exchanges between root salts, stored carbon, sulfurous dust, and the faint electrical stresses produced by the stone outside. What passes for sap is a creeping resin, cold and nearly solid, moving through capillaries over decades. A drop of it would take a season to descend a stem. Exposed to the hollow, it hardens at once into black amber.

Almost everything visible—if visibility were possible—is plant.

The floor is bark worn flat. The walls are compressed pith. Bridges are braided roots stretched over pits whose bottoms cannot be sensed. Towers rise as hollow thorns, and stairways coil around them in the natural spirals of phyllotaxis. Broad dead leaves have been folded into roofs, screens, doors, gutters for dust, and resonant panels. Even the cordage, wedges, hinges, bowls, masks, and hand tools are shaped from horn-hard wood or chipped from phytoliths: pale blades of silica grown inside ancient stems.

There are no animals. There have never been animals here—no insects hidden under the bark, no worms in the substrate, no bats in the vaults, no citizens in any animal sense.

The city itself is the population.

Its districts are colonies of specialized growth. A doorway is a patient organ that draws its two lignified lips apart when it recognizes the chemical trace of an approaching root-tip. A public square is a low communal pad where neighboring trunks press their signaling fibers together. The “streets” are empty gaps deliberately maintained between tissues so that pressure waves can travel cleanly. What appears to be urban planning is also anatomy. What appears to be architecture is also kinship.

Because there is no light, shape is known through sound.

The city is loud.

Every surface participates in a perpetual wooden thunder. Great seed-coat plates contract in the cool and release with cannonlike cracks. Tensioned roots groan across the voids. Dry towers knock against their neighbors with ponderous, irregular blows. Thin leaf membranes buzz in streams of escaping gas, while the deepest structural fibers emit tones too low to be heard as sound; they are felt instead as a tightening in the walls, a pressure in the stem, an instruction passing through the floor.

Nothing produces these sounds quickly. A root may bend for thirty years before slipping from its brace, but when it slips, the whole hollow booms. A bridge may spend a century drawing taut and then sing one immense note for nine days. Buried stresses arrive from the surrounding rock after wandering through the plain for millennia. The city receives them, magnifies them, and answers.

Silence, here, would mean catastrophe.

The oldest quarter lies near the seed’s blunt pole, where the tissues have been compressed into laminae finer than paper and harder than fired clay. It is nearly flat, an ancient district ground down not by wind or water but by uncounted cycles of strain. Avenues that once curved through elaborate groves now persist as shallow grooves in black cellulose. Stumps have become polished disks. Whole neighborhoods survive only as changes in grain.

This is called the Low Record.

The city reads it by sending tremors across the floor. Each buried layer returns a different echo. Thus history is not written in marks but stored in densities: famines as brittle bands, periods of expansion as thick porous wood, civic divisions as scars where two tissues grew against each other and failed to fuse. The oldest readable layer tells of no founding. Beneath it are older layers, too compressed to distinguish, and beneath those the original embryonic fold from which every street and tower eventually branched.

There is little vertical grandeur. The cavity is broad and low, pinched by age. Towers seldom rise far before meeting the seed’s inner shell; where they touch it, their tips flatten and spread into ceiling districts. Some communities therefore grow downward, hollowing not the ancient coat but themselves. Their houses are vacancies inside roots. Their plazas are bubbles in cork. Their roads pass through the pith of supporting columns and emerge miles away into an emptiness no wider than a split seam.

The few free-standing objects seem lonely beyond proportion: one forked post in a plaza, one bowl beside a wall, one ladder climbing into darkness. Vast intervals separate them. This sparseness is deliberate. Open volume is precious because resonance is the city’s means of perception, memory, and law. Clutter distorts the civic tones.

Construction is performed without metal and almost without cutting. Soft young tissues are guided around pegs. Roots are tied under tension, grafted, starved, thickened with mineral powders, or killed at precise ages so they cure into beams. Stone is rare inside the shell, but tiny grains enter through microscopic fractures and are carried by contractile hairs to workshops. There they are sorted by hardness. A single flint splinter can serve a district for generations.

Fire is forbidden. It would consume the city, but this is not the only reason. Flame would be intolerably bright.

Darkness is not merely the absence of illumination. It is an environmental constant, protected as carefully as the hollow itself. No organism contains a luminous reaction. No surface shines. Even the pale silica tools are dulled with sootlike spores, though there has never been smoke. If the seed coat were pierced and daylight entered, the exposed tissues would curl away, seal themselves, and grow a black callus over the wound. The event would travel through the city as a shriek of contracting fibers. Entire districts might alter their growth in response, centuries after the breach had closed.

The cool is steady. There are no days and no seasons, only the slight thermal tides of the overlying plain. These arrive softened and delayed. A hot year outside becomes, deep within, a barely perceptible loosening of resin. A long winter contracts the bridges and sharpens their pitch. The city measures time by these tonal changes.

Its metabolism is correspondingly slow. A civic decision may be expressed as a pattern of pulses sent from grove to grove. Consensus takes decades because every district must grow the receiving structures needed to interpret the proposal. Once agreed, the response is architectural. A wall thickens. An avenue narrows. A new resonator is extruded into a public chamber. Laws are not proclaimed; they become load-bearing.

The central hollow contains the city’s strangest institution: an immense, empty amphitheater formed by the inner curve of two cotyledons. Nothing assembles there. Nothing sits. There are no bodies capable of attendance. Yet the chamber has been carefully maintained since the oldest readable age.

Around its perimeter stand thousands of dry stalks, each cut to a different length. They are simple instruments—organ pipes without wind, struck by roots that tighten and release under geological pressure. Most remain silent for centuries. When the outer world shifts, a few answer. Their notes roll around the bowl and collide, producing patterns that the surrounding tissues absorb.

The city believes—or behaves as though it believes—that these patterns describe the universe beyond the shell.

It knows the overlying salt-field as a pressure. It knows distant fault movement as rhythm. It knows meteor impacts as sudden chords. It may know the turning of the planet through a minute, recurrent deformation, though there is no sign that it conceives of turning, planets, or distance. Its cosmos is an acoustical one: reality consists of stresses, intervals, resonances, and the long delayed arrival of force.

At the amphitheater’s center is a shallow depression containing a single loose object, perhaps the only thing in the city not attached to anything else. It is a dry, lens-shaped flake of the original seed embryo, preserved on the bark floor.

Whenever the lowest note sounds, the flake jumps.

Sometimes it turns over.

A whole era may be named for the face on which it comes to rest.

Beyond the cultivated districts lie the Pale Hollows, spaces where growth withdrew so long ago that the remaining architecture has lost almost all scent and elasticity. Their bridges are thin curves of mineralized fiber. Their walls crumble not into soil—there is no moist rot, no decomposer fauna, no fungal loam—but into clean angular dust. Nothing eats the dead here. Death remains as structure until stress reduces it to particles, and particles remain until living hairs gather them for new construction.

Thus the city consumes its past without decay.

At the far, narrow end of the shell, young tissues still grow. “Young” means they are only tens of thousands of years old. Here the darkness is crossed by fine roots searching the sealed inner coat for chemical differences. They tap, withdraw, branch, and tap again. Their sounds are small compared with the old city’s thunder: dry ticks, whispering scrapes, a faint rainlike patter in a world that has no rain.

One root has found a flaw.

The flaw is narrower than a hair and filled with salt. No air passes through it. No water waits beyond it. Still, the root has begun to build around the place: first a collar, then a chamber, then an avenue oriented toward the wall. Other districts, alerted by subtle changes in tension, are slowly extending resonant fibers in its direction.

Perhaps in another thousand years the flaw will open.

Perhaps the city will emerge—not as a sprout into sunlight, for it has no machinery for light, and no expectation of sky, but as a black, dry web creeping between salt crystals beneath the plain. It might build a second hidden city in the seam outside. It might discover that its seed is only one among millions, each buried separately, each holding a hollow civilization that has listened in darkness since before the desert became a desert.

Or the flaw may close.

The exploratory root may harden into a useless hook. The new avenue may become ceremonial, then ancient, then a shallow line in the Low Record. The city will continue without disappointment. Its resin will move. Its bridges will tighten. Its empty streets will preserve the distances needed for sound.

And in the central hollow, after an interval longer than any structure can remember, the buried stone will press against the seed again.

The stalks will strike.

The dark will fill with thunder.
