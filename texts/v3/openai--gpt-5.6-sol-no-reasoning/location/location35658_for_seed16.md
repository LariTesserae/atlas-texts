The city lies inside a crack in the salt.

From above, there is almost nothing to distinguish it: a low white plain under a hot, colorless sky, its surface crazed into polygons and shallow blisters. No road approaches. No smoke rises. The entrance is a seam narrow enough to step across, shaded by an overhanging plate of mineral. Warm air surges from it in slow breaths.

Below the seam, the crack opens.

It is not vast. A person could cross the inhabited portion in an hour, if its streets were direct, but they are folded through ledges, shafts, chambers, and blind courts. The city occupies the walls of a deep, narrow cavity rather than its floor. Houses have been hollowed into saltstone, sulfurous clay, and old deposits of glassy resin. Bridges of bundled reed-fiber cross the darker gaps. Stairs descend until they vanish into red gloom; ladders rise toward platforms no wider than sleeping mats.

Yet the place feels empty. Its buildings are separated by hollows too broad to use, by vaulted pockets in the rock, by sudden pits breathing heat. A neighborhood may consist of three rooms, a terrace, and a kiln, with fifty paces of vacant cavern on every side. Doorways face one another across darkness. Long ropes connect distant balconies, their knots sliding back and forth like tiny black figures.

There is no water here—not in wells, cisterns, bodies, sap, mist, or memory.

The city’s life is founded on other fluids.

In the lowest chambers, heat wells up through porous stone and softens underground seams of wax, oil, and volatile mineral spirits. These substances seep into ceramic gutters as amber threads. Some are clear and sharp-smelling; others are black, slow, and warm enough to burn skin. The inhabitants divert them through an intricate municipal anatomy of baked-clay pipes, leatherless valves, stone basins, and capillary cords. Nothing is allowed to flow freely. A spill can poison a district, ignite a stairwell, or feed roots into foundations.

The vegetation is dense wherever these channels pass. It does not resemble the green abundance of a wet world. Most growth is ivory, rust, charcoal, or a dark violet that appears black in the dimness.

Pale ribbon-plants pour from cracks in layered fans. Their leaves are dry and flexible, more like cloth or thin horn than foliage. They drink hydrocarbon vapor through felted undersides and store nourishment as wax. Groves of jointed stalks crowd warm courtyards, knocking together whenever the city breathes. On the hottest walls grow blister-vines: networks of translucent sacs containing mobile golden oil. Root-mats creep beneath paving stones, lifting them over decades into rounded ridges. At intervals they put up broad black vanes to catch the convection currents.

The city tends these organisms as orchard, fuel reserve, building material, and ancestor. Stalks become scaffolds and flute-pipes. Ribbon leaves are split into fibers for rope and shade cloth. Hardened root is carved into bowls. Wax-fruit, when roasted in sealed kilns, separates into edible paste, lamp fuel, and a brittle shell used for writing tablets.

No animal pollinates the groves. There are no insects hidden under leaves, no worms in the dust, no birds nesting above the roofs. The plants reproduce by heat, abrasion, and civic disturbance. Certain seed-pods open only when struck repeatedly. Others wait for kiln smoke or the vibration of work songs. Some cast powder into rising air; the city’s shafts and chimneys carry it from garden to garden. Pruning is pollination. Demolition is spring.

The loudness of the place comes first from the rock.

All day, the salt plain expands in the heat. Pressure travels downward and is released through the cavity in concussions: deep knocks, abrupt cracks, and rolling subterranean booms. The city has been here long enough to expect them. Arches are low and thick. Walls lean inward. Roofs are layered from many small slabs so they may shift without collapsing. Bridges hang loose rather than rigid. Door lintels bear generations of wedge marks where masons have corrected the slow deformation of the crack.

Between the booms, there is the continual clatter of the vane-groves. Dry leaves slap walls. Hollow stalks drum against one another. Pipes whistle as hot vapor passes through constrictions. Kiln shutters chatter. Long communal ropes hum over the chasm, tightening in the ascending air and slackening when the breath reverses.

The people add their own noise because quiet is unsafe.

Workers call before crossing a bridge. Pipe-tenders beat coded rhythms on ceramic mains to warn of pressure changes. At each level, a suspended sounding slab repeats the signals upward and downward. Markets are conducted in shouted formulas, partly from custom and partly because the groves obscure faces. At the hottest hour, when the upper seam nearly closes under thermal expansion, every district strikes its walls with wooden mallets. They listen for changes in pitch that betray a new hollow or an approaching fracture.

Thus the city is forever sounding itself.

Its tools are simple but exquisitely adapted: chipped saltglass knives, copper needles hammered thin and repaired until they resemble reddish thorns, stone-headed mallets, clay retorts, fiber sieves, and drills turned by hand. Metal is scarce. Fire is abundant but dangerous, so flames are confined within heavy ovens whose doors face empty rock. Most light comes instead from slow chemical lamps—shallow bowls in which cultivated wax reacts with powdered mineral and gives off a dull red glow.

The result is a city visible only in portions. A stair appears, then disappears. A face glows above a lamp and is gone. Gardens loom like heaps of folded fabric. The highest roofs catch a faint gray line from the surface crack, but below them the illumination is ember-colored and indirect. Shadows are large, soft, and rarely still.

The heat has made the inhabitants’ schedule vertical. During the fiercest part of the day, they descend into outer chambers where the rock absorbs excess warmth. At night, as the plain contracts and cold air drops through the seam, they move upward to sleeping niches among the gardens. Whole households migrate a few dozen meters twice each cycle, carrying mats, tools, lamps, and trays of fermenting wax. Streets change direction accordingly. A stair that is commercial by day becomes domestic at night.

There are rules older than any building.

Nothing may be buried, because roots follow disturbance and pipes follow roots.

No one may make an unannounced hole.

Every open flame must be visible from two occupied thresholds.

A bridge belongs to those who can hear it.

When a wall cracks, the rooms on both sides are abandoned until the crack has “spoken” through one complete heating cycle.

And no channel may be stopped entirely. Even a poisonous flow must be given somewhere to go.

This last law has shaped the settlement more than kings or planners could have. Unwanted seepage is guided into vacant chambers called bitter gardens. There, mineral-tolerant growths bloom into elaborate, inedible reefs: brittle curls, oily membranes, and tall hollow crowns that scream softly when vapor escapes through them. These gardens are beautiful, toxic, and left largely alone. Their roots stabilize the cavern, but their fumes can induce fever and visions. Streets bend around them. Houses look inward rather than toward them. Every district has one dark archway sealed with woven warning cords, behind which its bitter garden continues its slow work.

The city is old enough that repair and erosion are indistinguishable. Steps are hollowed by bare feet, then packed with root fiber, then hollowed again. Pipe leaks become orchards. Fallen rooms become ventilation courts. A collapsed tower may be left where it hangs between ledges, braced with stalkwood and used as a bridge. Nothing remains in its first purpose. Nothing is entirely ruined.

Above, the desert appears empty and still.

Below, in a space hidden between two layers of salt, a small city knocks, whistles, shouts, germinates, expands, settles, and rearranges itself around the rising heat. Its gardens have never known rain. Its streets have never held a hoofprint or wing shadow. Its people possess no word for swimming, but dozens for the movement of oil through stone.

At sunset the plain cools. The seam widens by the breadth of a finger. A blade of dim copper light enters the upper cavity and travels down one polished wall.

For a few minutes, every suspended bridge casts a shadow.

Then the crack exhales, the light withdraws, and the city resumes its red, resounding dark.
