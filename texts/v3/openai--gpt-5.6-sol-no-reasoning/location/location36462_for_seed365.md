The town occupies a shallow bowl near the summit of a young mountain.

It is not a broad place. From the western gate one can see nearly all the way to the eastern wall: a small, steep-sided basin of dark stone, perhaps half a mile across, enclosed by ridges as sharp as broken pottery. Beyond those ridges the mountain falls away into cloud. Nothing of the lower country is visible except on rare clear mornings, when forests and pale rivers appear impossibly far below.

The basin itself seems recently made. Its cliffs have not softened into slopes, and its stones have scarcely begun to round. Black ledges protrude from the soil like blades. Long cracks still open in cold weather, sometimes running through roads and foundations with a dry sound barely louder than tearing cloth. There are no deep gullies and few accumulated screes. Rain has touched the mountain but has not yet had time to finish it.

The settlement was built around these fissures rather than against them.

Its buildings are low, irregular, and widely spaced. Most are assembled from fitted slabs of volcanic rock, secured without mortar by wedges of bone and hardwood. Roofs are woven from reeds, broad leaves, animal hair, and overlapping sheets of bark sealed with resin. A few communal halls stand two or three stories high, leaning inward around courtyards where moisture condenses. Their stone walls are densely furred with moss, though the masonry beneath remains fresh and sharp-edged.

There are roads, drainage channels, boundary stones, cisterns, terraces, stairways, and public shelters, yet the place never feels crowded. Construction occurs in islands. A cluster of houses gathers beneath one overhang; then comes a wide garden, an empty slab of bedrock, or a grove kept deliberately untouched. The town has the organization of a city compressed into the dimensions of a village. Every district has its own hearth, water hollow, refuse pit, tool house, and council stone. No district contains more than a handful of families.

The roads are narrow and dark. They are paved with flat shards laid on edge, so that runoff disappears quickly between them. Because wheeled vehicles are unknown, there is no need for broad avenues. Loads are carried in woven frames, on human backs, or by slow six-legged pack animals whose padded feet grip the slick stone.

Water is scarce in quantity but almost always present in some lesser form.

Cloud presses over the summit most days. It does not often rain hard. Instead, droplets gather continuously on leaves, horns, roofs, ropes, hair, and carved poles. The town harvests this condensation. Nets of spider silk and grass fiber are stretched between forked timbers; they comb moisture from the fog and release it, one bead at a time, into stone basins below. Every roof slopes toward a jar. Every tree has a cup fitted beneath its lowest branch.

After rain, the basin briefly changes. Fine streams wake in the cracks and run silver through the streets. Small pools appear in hollows, clear and cold, then drain away within hours through the porous mountain. Children hurry out with clay vessels before the water vanishes. Plants unfold in the drainage channels. Transparent creatures emerge from holes no wider than a finger, breed, lay eggs, and disappear underground before evening.

There is only one permanent body of water: a pond occupying the deepest part of the bowl. It is no larger than a meeting house and so dark that it reflects nothing. Moss grows thickly around it, but no roots cross its rim. The townspeople draw from it only during drought. When disturbed, its surface contracts from the vessel as though the water dislikes being taken.

For all the thin water and exposed stone, vegetation is lush.

It does not form a continuous forest. Plants rise in dense, separate congregations, leaving bare passages between them. There are groves like green rooms: giant fernlike crowns on pale, jointed trunks; mats of blue-black moss thick enough to swallow an ankle; rope vines strung from cliff to cliff; bushes bearing waxy leaves shaped like cupped hands. Many leaves point upward, not toward the dim sun but toward the fog.

The largest plants are hollow. Wind enters openings near their roots and passes through chambers inside the trunks, though on most days the current is too weak to make more than a breath. Their crowns collect water, warmth, insects, and fallen seeds. Whole communities live in those elevated bowls. When a trunk is cut, frogs and beetles sometimes pour out with the water.

The people cultivate terraces wherever soil has gathered. They grow bitter tubers, moss-grain, red-stemmed greens, and a low vine whose fruit ripens underground. Agriculture is careful and intimate. There are no sweeping fields. Each bed is surrounded by bare stone to keep the roots from wandering, because the plants here are opportunistic and startlingly quick. A neglected garden can close over itself in three days. By the end of a week it may contain species no one remembers planting.

Above everything is the animal life.

At first the town seems quiet enough to be abandoned. Footsteps make little echo. Doors are covered in felted hide. People speak close to one another, rarely raising their voices. Even tools are designed for silence: stone wedges instead of hammers where possible, drills turned by hand, saws made from toothed shell and drawn slowly through wet wood.

Then the eye adjusts.

Animals occupy nearly every surface.

Small gray lizards lie in ordered rows along warm walls. Mothlike insects cling beneath leaves in overlapping layers, their wings patterned to resemble droplets. Pale crabs live in roof thatch and descend at night to gather ash. Thumb-sized deer move through the gardens, stepping delicately between seedlings. Thread-thin snakes knot themselves around rain ropes. Birds hang upside down beneath ledges, too numerous to count, though they seldom call.

The silence is not an absence of life. It is a discipline shared by life.

Predation here is governed by vibration. The basin’s dominant hunters live inside the mountain: immense, soft-bodied things that press themselves through cracks and sense impacts through stone. No one has seen an entire one. Now and then a translucent limb unfolds from a fissure, feeling across a road with hundreds of fine cilia. Anything struggling loudly is taken. A dropped jar can summon attention. So can running, chopping, or a panicked flock.

This has shaped everything.

Hooved animals have soft pads. Birds communicate by changing the color of their throats. Insects signal with scent and light. Territorial beasts push one another in slow, almost ceremonial contests rather than charging. Even prey dies quietly when caught. The town’s people teach children to fall without striking the ground, to wrap tools before carrying them, and never to shout into an open crack.

The custom is older than any surviving building: **noise belongs to the stone**.

Certain sounds are safe. Flowing water conceals lesser disturbances, so hard work is done during rain. On wet days the settlement becomes suddenly industrious. Stone is split, timber cut, roofs dismantled, grain pounded, and disputes aired beneath the cover of runoff. The inhabitants call these brief storms “loud hours.” Weddings are held in them. So are trials and funerals.

During dry fog the town returns to near silence, although it is never still.

Creatures migrate constantly between groves. Vines contract and shift their purchase on the cliffs. Condensation travels through the meshwork of nets. Hunters probe the fissures. Fungus blooms across abandoned food and withdraws when touched by daylight. Pack animals browse the roof gardens, secured by braided tethers. Far overhead, immense aerial forms pass through the cloud, visible only as a temporary darkening and a downward drift of shed parasites.

The light rarely strengthens beyond twilight. Clouds flatten the sun into a gray disk, and the enclosing ridges admit little direct illumination. Plants supply some of what the sky withholds. Pale nodules glow beneath leaves. Spores glimmer green when disturbed. A species of snail is encouraged to graze along doorframes because its trail remains luminous for several hours. At night, the settlement is mapped by these traces: wavering lines around houses, cisterns, stairs, and forbidden cracks.

Fire is scarce and closely guarded. Wood is usually too damp to burn cleanly, and smoke attracts winged scavengers. Communal hearths use dried moss bricks and animal fat inside hooded stone chambers. Most food is fermented, cured, soaked, or cooked on stones heated during the rain. Metal is known only from fallen stars. A few chiefs and stone surgeons possess tiny meteoric blades, worn smooth by generations of sharpening. Everything else is made from obsidian, bone, horn, shell, fiber, or the glassy teeth of local animals.

The strange economy of the town is based less on ownership than on safe passage. Families maintain routes: a dry stair, a bridge of woven roots, a tunnel through a hollow trunk, a sequence of stepping stones that avoids the sensitive ground near fissures. Permission to use such ways is traded for food, tools, medicine, or labor. Maps are not drawn. They are worn as bead patterns around the wrist, each texture indicating a turn, slope, hazard, or place to kneel.

The walls do not keep animals out. That would be impossible. They direct migration.

Openings at their bases funnel small creatures toward gardens where their droppings enrich the soil. Elevated arches guide climbing herds over roofs instead of through sleeping quarters. Smooth-faced towers provide nesting places for birds whose presence discourages biting insects. What appears from afar to be fortification is closer to plumbing for fauna—a civic anatomy of corridors, perches, barriers, and invitations.

No inhabitant believes the town belongs solely to people. Houses are closed to certain beasts, granaries to others, but every construction must surrender some portion of itself. A new dwelling is not considered habitable until another species has accepted it. Builders wait for moss to take on the north wall, for roof crabs to enter the thatch, or for one of the small deer to sleep beneath the eaves. If no creature comes, the place is dismantled. Such buildings are believed to have been noticed by something deeper.

The mountain is still becoming.

Sometimes, after a cold night, a fresh ridge stands where a garden had been. Warm vapor rises from new cracks. Black crystals push through floors. The pond loses several inches of water without explanation, and a distant part of the basin becomes damp. The people do not speak of earthquakes; they say the summit is adjusting its thoughts.

Accordingly, nothing is built to last forever. Walls can be unstacked. Roofs can be carried. Bridges can be cut free and hauled to another fissure. Ancestors are not buried, because the ground cannot be trusted to keep them. Their cleaned bones are incorporated into lintels, ladders, tools, musical instruments, and condensation frames. Thus the dead remain above the unstable earth and continue helping to gather water.

Music exists, despite the law of quiet, but most of it cannot be heard by human ears. Drums are pressed against the chest rather than struck into open air. Flutes produce tones lower than speech, felt in the teeth. Ensembles of insects are housed in woven cages and conducted by adjusting heat, scent, and darkness. On festival nights the streets fill with people standing perfectly still while the buildings tremble faintly around them.

Outside the walls, a path climbs to the northern ridge. It passes through widely separated gardens and a grove of silver-barked trees, then ends at a narrow stone shelf above the cloud. Here the inhabitants leave offerings: shed skins, broken blades, cups of pond water, and small models of houses woven from grass.

The shelf overlooks nothing visible. Cloud rises against it, cold and wet, populated by unseen wings.

Occasionally the fog parts just enough to reveal that the summit bowl is not alone. Other peaks stand nearby, each carrying its own dim pocket of vegetation, its own small arrangement of walls and nets. Some show lights. Some are completely overgrown. One appears upside down in the clouds and has done so for as long as anyone remembers.

Between these mountain towns, enormous animals move through the open air.

They travel slowly, buoyed by sacs along their spines, trailing forests of tendrils. Smaller creatures nest upon them. Water pours intermittently from their bodies, creating the brief rains that keep the summit alive. The town’s scholars believe each cloud-beast follows an ancient circuit from peak to peak. The routes are changing, however. The rains arrive later. Strange parasites fall among them. Young beasts have begun circling mountains where no settlement stands.

So the people watch the fog and maintain their nets. They tend gardens that might be moved tomorrow. They build a city lightly on a mountain that has not finished rising, among animals too numerous to name and predators that listen through the ground.

In the dimness, everything waits and feeds and grows.

Almost nothing is silent.

Almost everything has learned to be quiet.
