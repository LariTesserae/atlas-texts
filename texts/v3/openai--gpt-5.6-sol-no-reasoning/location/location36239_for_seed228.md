Far below the surface lies a chamber called the Bright Sump.

It is not vast by subterranean standards. One can see, through spray and glare, nearly from one end to the other: a basin perhaps half a kilometer across, oval and steep-sided, with a roof rising like the interior of a buried cathedral. Yet the place refuses to feel enclosed. Distance is continually broken and rearranged by water. Curtains descend from unseen cracks. Mist opens pale corridors, then closes them. Reflections imply further chambers where there are only wet walls.

The basin is warm. Its air has the thick, mineral closeness of a bathhouse after rain, though no rain falls here in the ordinary sense. Moisture condenses everywhere—in beads on the ceiling, in shining skins over the stone, in droplets that gather along ridges until whole seams release at once. The walls weep continuously. Water lies just beneath the floor as well, pressing upward through porous rock in trembling lenses. Step in one place and a clear puddle appears several meters away. Stand still too long and the ground around your boots darkens, as though the chamber has begun to drink through you.

Light comes from nine narrow structures distributed around the basin. They resemble pillars only at a distance. Each is a matte, bone-colored frame enclosing a vertical absence: a slit brighter than metal in a furnace, but without corresponding heat. No lamp or filament is visible. The brightness appears to enter from a direction the chamber does not possess.

It is a clean, almost noonlike light—white with a faint turquoise edge. It makes every wet surface flash. The chamber has no true shadows; even beneath overhangs, illumination curls back from the pools and rises through cracks in the floor. The brightest objects are sometimes those most completely hidden. If a hand is held behind a stone, its outline may shine through the stone a moment later, delayed and slightly altered.

The pillars are the only intact constructions. Everything else is a trace.

Along the southern wall are three platforms worn almost flush with the bedrock, their corners softened by centuries of runoff. A low bridge reaches halfway across a channel and ends in a rounded stump. Lines of dark alloy emerge from the floor, travel a few meters, and plunge back beneath it. Here and there stand hoops no higher than a person’s knee, thin enough to look fragile, though falling rock has shattered around them without leaving a mark. Some hoops contain water stretched vertically across their openings. The water does not spill. Pebbles thrown through emerge wet, but sometimes several minutes before they were thrown.

None of this machinery seems abandoned. It is merely dispersed, stripped of the visual insistence of a working installation. Its operations are embedded in the basin’s behavior.

The floor is a settled chaos of old erosion. Warm floods have cut branching channels into pale limestone and black volcanic glass. Their edges are rounded and stable, polished by innumerable surges into forms like melted stairs, ribs, sockets, and shallow bowls. Nothing looks freshly broken, though the water moves violently. Even the debris has found enduring positions: dark cobbles nested in pockets, fans of cream-colored grit locked against ledges, single slabs balanced where eddies have held them for generations.

This is turbulence with habits.

Every few minutes, the basin rearranges its currents. A pool drains upward in a twisting column and strikes the ceiling. One of the wall-falls abruptly bends sideways, crossing fifty meters of open air before entering a narrow crack. Channels reverse. Water bulges from the floor in transparent domes, then collapses into warm rain. The loudness is immense—not one roar, but many layered impacts. There is the bass concussion of subterranean flow, the hard applause of falls on stone, the hiss of atomized spray, and a high ringing produced by the pale frames.

The ringing migrates from pillar to pillar. At times it becomes a chord that can be felt in the teeth. Then the water begins to move according to the sound.

Ripples sharpen into hexagons. Droplets pause in grids. Whole curtains of spray fold like cloth and pass through apertures much smaller than themselves. Pressure waves cross the basin visibly, bending reflections before they arrive. During the strongest cycles, loose stones tremble toward the nearest hoop, arrange themselves by size, and are scattered again as soon as the tone breaks.

The place is dynamic, but not young. Its restlessness is ancient, integrated, and exact. The walls have been shaped by these impossible flows into equilibrium with them. Deep scoops show where sideways waterfalls have struck for ages. Long grooves climb upward. In the roof, inverted deltas mark the courses of streams that run against gravity during bright intervals. Erosion here is not a record of simple descent. It is a map of changing laws.

Vegetation survives only in separated stains and tufts. Most stone is bare, too frequently scoured for roots. In sheltered recesses grow low pads of copper-green moss, each surrounded by a dry halo. Translucent reeds stand in three shallow pools, widely spaced, their hollow stems full of moving points of light. They never rise above the knee. When the chamber’s tone deepens, the reeds lie flat before any wind arrives.

A chalk-white vine clings to the western wall, following a warm mineral seam. It bears no leaves, only small bladders that inflate with mist and collapse with soft clicks. Entire meters of vine may remain inert for months, then flower all at once into colorless cups. These flowers point not toward the luminous pillars, but toward whatever object in the chamber is presently casting the least plausible shadow.

Animals are uncommon enough to seem like incidents.

Small black swimmers inhabit the deeper channels. They have no visible eyes and surface only during reversals, when they hang in falling water as though it were a vertical river. Pale, long-legged crustaceans sometimes cross the wet floor in single file, each maintaining several meters of distance from the next. If approached, they do not flee; they flatten and become indistinguishable from mineral flakes.

The largest creature is rarely seen. Evidence of it appears as broad disturbances under shallow water, too wide for the channel that contains them. Now and then, amid the thunder, there is a breathlike sound from beneath the basin—a slow inhalation followed by bubbles rising from dozens of unrelated pools. The old platforms bear smooth oval abrasions, as if something soft and heavy has rested there repeatedly.

The chamber has rules, though they are local and occasionally negotiable.

Water always seeks a boundary, but not necessarily a lower place. Light accumulates in enclosed volumes. Sound can lend weight to small things. Objects completely submerged remain where they are; objects only half-submerged are carried toward their own reflections. Nothing living is moved backward in time by the hoops, though shed skin, fallen leaves, and blood droplets sometimes are. The nine pillars never change position, but measurements between them do.

There are signs that someone once tended the basin. Shallow handholds have been cut beside the safest routes. A row of ceramic markers, each no larger than a tooth, is embedded along a ledge above the flood line. Most are blank. Three carry fine diagrams of branching channels, but the diagrams alter when wet. A small shelter occupies the chamber’s driest recess: four fused ribs of gray material supporting a roof thin as paper. Inside is a bench, a cracked vessel, and an instrument resembling a tuning fork with too many arms.

The shelter is not wholly ruined. Spray veers around it. Its bench remains warm. Once in a great while, the many-armed instrument answers the pillars with a quiet note, and the entire Bright Sump becomes still.

The silence is more shocking than the noise.

Falls halt in midair. Mist hangs as bright dust. The inverted streams cease climbing. Beneath the sudden calm, one can hear tiny sounds normally buried: moss releasing bubbles, stone contracting, the delicate feet of crustaceans, water moving through miles of pores beyond the walls. The chamber seems then less like a cave than a single organ within something immeasurably larger.

Stillness lasts perhaps seven seconds.

Then all nine pillars flare, the floor flexes, and every suspended mass of water falls in a different direction. The basin erupts into radiance and thunder. Warm spray fills the air. Channels overflow, vanish, and return. Yet afterward, the old cobbles settle into their same pockets, the reeds slowly stand, and the sparse white vine resumes its patient clicking.

The Bright Sump changes furiously without departing from itself.
