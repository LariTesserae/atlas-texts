The high country begins long before the land appears mountainous.

For days the ground rises by increments: saturated plains becoming tilted meadows, meadows becoming terraces of black soil, terraces narrowing beneath forests. Water accompanies every stage. It glazes the grass, hangs in the moss, seeps from banks, and gathers in clear, cold basins where drowned leaves remain visible far below. Even on rainless mornings, droplets form along every twig. The atmosphere seems perpetually close to condensation, as though the whole region were a cloud persuaded, temporarily, to take solid form.

At full elevation, the landscape opens into an immense plateau broken by old ravines. It is high enough to make its own weather and broad enough that the far ridges show the muted blue of separate climates. Storms may be walking over the western reaches while sunlight lies calmly across the east. From the central heights, veils of rain can be seen advancing for hours, darkening forest after forest without ever revealing the horizon.

There is almost no bare ground.

The plateau is densely occupied by life: waist-deep sedges, glossy shrubs, fern fields, moss blankets, low forests twisted by wind, and taller forests wherever the ravines give shelter. Plants root inside plants. Moss carries pale fungi; fungi bear films of green algae; seedlings sprout from the fibrous crowns of living trees. Fallen trunks seldom reach the soil. They are caught in the understory, where they soften into suspended gardens before sinking by degrees into the accumulated dark.

The trees grow close enough that their crowns form continuous surfaces. In some districts this canopy resembles a rough green sea, with wind passing across it in long, visible currents. Leaves turn silver, then dark, then silver again. Flowers are generally small but innumerable: blue stars beneath the shrubs, white bells in wet hollows, red filaments protruding from bark. Their abundance is discovered gradually. From a distance the forest seems sober and cool; within it, every hand’s breadth contains color.

The landforms are ancient and settled. Nothing has the sharp, provisional appearance of a young mountain chain. Ridges are broad-backed. Cliffs are rounded at their rims and deeply stained below. Water has used the same routes for ages, cutting ravines not violently but completely, until each has become a world with its own rivers, fog, trees, and migrations. Exposed stone wears thick crusts of charcoal, amber, and pale green. Even fresh breaks soon begin to weep mineral water, as if the rock itself is saturated.

Erosion continues everywhere, though rarely as catastrophe. Rain removes the mountains grain by grain. Streams carry dark organic silt through tunnels of roots. Frost opens narrow seams; roots enter them; the next thaw loosens a thin plate of stone. Slopes occasionally settle with a muffled movement, lowering whole patches of forest by a few inches. The plateau is not being ruined by these processes. This is its completed behavior, its durable state: rising mist, descending water, stone entering soil, soil climbing again into trunks.

The rivers are numerous but seldom broad. They braid through peatlands, vanish under mats of vegetation, and return at cliff edges as long white falls. Some waterfalls descend so far into wooded chasms that their lower halves dissolve into mist. Others run over shelves in transparent sheets no deeper than a leaf. In the highest basins, shallow lakes occupy weathered hollows. Their surfaces are dark from reflected forest and their margins tremble with floating roots.

Though there are no roads, walls, towers, pipes, tools, or worked surfaces, the region has an industrial quality.

It arises from the organisms.

Across vast sections of the plateau, colonies of iron-shelled insects process the forest’s fall. They move in narrow, disciplined streams through concealed channels in the moss, dismantling dead leaves into precise fragments. Their nests descend into warm mineral seams, and faint heat rises from them on cold days. Larger chambers vent through porous stone, releasing regular breaths of white vapor. Seen across a valley at dawn, thousands of these vents resemble the exhaust of an invisible manufacturing district buried beneath the trees.

The insects are not builders in the familiar sense. They leave no freestanding structures and shape no object for its own sake. Their mechanisms are their bodies, their colonies, and the chemistry of the ground. Individual castes crush fiber, cultivate fungi, concentrate metals, or carry electrical charge along wet networks of root and mycelium. The plateau’s soils are threaded with conductive filaments. When atmospheric pressure drops before a storm, weak currents move through them, coordinating blooms, spore release, migrations, and the opening of vents across hundreds of miles.

At such times, the landscape appears to switch on.

Pale bands illuminate beneath translucent fungi. Certain pools acquire a dim internal sheen. Great stands of fern slowly rotate their fronds toward the charged wind. This is not bright enough to alter the natural daylight, and by noon it is usually invisible. Yet on overcast mornings, entire hillsides may pulse once or twice with subdued green, each pulse traveling outward like a signal through a living circuit.

There are animals everywhere, but they do not make the place loud.

Small grazers with dense gray coats move beneath the sedges, cropping narrow paths that close behind them. Long-legged browsers step through bogs on divided feet, almost without disturbing the water. Tree-dwelling creatures pass from crown to crown and seldom touch the ground. Their weight can be followed by the shifting of leaves overhead, though they themselves remain hidden.

The plateau’s largest animals live along the ravines. They are heavy, slow-bodied creatures plated in overlapping keratin, dark with moss and moisture. They scrape mineral films from the cliffs and travel in old circuits between salt seeps. Their tracks become pools; frogs breed in them; insects lay eggs along their margins; seeds collect and establish miniature thickets. A single footprint may persist as habitat for years.

Predators are lean and patient. Some wait near warm vents, knowing prey will gather there in cold weather. Others hunt by sensing changes in the ground’s electrical field. Chases are uncommon. The density of vegetation favors stillness, short distances, and sudden conclusions.

Birds are abundant but sparing with their voices. Broad-winged species travel the updrafts along escarpments. Tiny nectar-feeders work the understory flowers, their wingbeats softened by damp air. Flocks sometimes cross overhead in the thousands, visible only as a momentary stippling of the clouds. Most calls are single notes: a drop of sound from within the foliage, answered much later from another slope.

Quiet here does not mean absence. It means that sound is absorbed almost at once.

Moss drinks footsteps. Wet leaves do not rattle. Wind spends itself in the densely layered canopy before reaching the ground. Rivers are muted by overhanging roots, and even the tallest waterfalls land in deep beds of vegetation with a low, continuous breath rather than a roar. Close by, there are countless minute noises—the ticking of condensation, mandibles clipping leaf tissue, water entering water, bark contracting in cold—but they do not combine into clamor.

Thunder is the great exception. Storms form above the plateau from moisture released by the plateau itself. The clouds build slowly, tower upon tower, while charged colonies below begin their dim synchronization. Lightning often travels horizontally, following the conductive wet forest for impossible distances. Thunder then rolls through the ravines like something geological awakening. It may take minutes to fade.

Rain falls cold and straight. It fills every leaf cup, renews every seep, and presses the ferns flat. Streams rise quickly but predictably, occupying polished channels prepared over millennia. Animals withdraw into hollow root masses or shelter beneath the plated bodies of larger species. The fungal lights brighten. Venting colonies close their upper pores and redirect their warmth through the root web.

Afterward, the entire plateau exhales.

Mist lifts from millions of surfaces. Water hangs in the air without quite becoming rain. The canopy emerges and disappears in layers, revealing distances too great to hold in one view: the near forest jeweled with droplets; the middle ravines drowned in cloud; the remote uplands standing like islands; beyond them, further weather moving over further green.

There is no sign that anyone has ever crossed this country with the intention of possessing it. Nothing is cleared, aligned, inscribed, fenced, or named upon the land itself. Yet neither is it empty or idle. Material moves continually through innumerable living systems. Water is captured, filtered, lifted, and released. Minerals are mined by roots and concentrated in shells. Heat is generated underground. Charge passes from storm to soil, soil to fungus, fungus to leaf. Waste from one process becomes feedstock for the next with a completeness no designed industry has achieved.

It is a wilderness operating at the scale of an economy, an engine without architecture.

Its laws are old and quiet. Nothing stands alone. Every surface must eventually host another life. Every hollow must receive water. Every fall must become foundation. No process is permitted a final product: antler becomes calcium garden, carcass becomes heat, cliff becomes silt, silt becomes forest, forest lifts water back into the sky.

And over all of it lies the cool, ordinary light of the high plateau—neither mystical nor dim, but clean enough to show how thoroughly the world is occupied.
