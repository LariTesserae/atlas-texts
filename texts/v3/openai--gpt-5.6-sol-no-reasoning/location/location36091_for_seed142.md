The city occupies the summit of a continent.

There are no peaks around it. Whatever mountains once rose here have been worn down to a single immense table of dark mineral, high above the deeper atmosphere. Its horizons are nearly level, though not smooth: low ribs of stone cross the plateau in parallel ranks, and shallow basins lie between them like impressions left by vanished seas. Nothing flows through these channels. Their floors are plated with salts of iron, magnesium, and stranger metals, fused by age into dull scales that do not lift even in the wind.

There is almost no wind.

The air is warm, thin, and chemically dry. It carries no scent of soil, rain, resin, or decay. Inhaled through a filter, it tastes faintly electrical. Exposed metal does not rust in the ordinary way; instead, it acquires over centuries a violet-black film, hard and smooth as fired glaze. Stone keeps the residual heat rising through the plateau, so walls and streets remain warm to the touch despite the absence of visible sun.

The darkness is not night. Night would imply an approaching morning.

A dense stratum of opaque atmosphere lies far overhead, absorbing or scattering nearly all celestial light. The city sees only a weak, sourceless radiance at the horizon, too dim to cast useful shadows. It reveals silhouettes rather than surfaces: factory stacks, gantries, suspended roads, terraces cut into the ancient rock. Detail emerges only near machinery, where hooded lamps make narrow amber pools. Beyond each pool, the dark closes again without gradation.

Seen from above—if such a vantage were possible—the settlement would resemble a sparse circuit laid across many kilometers. Its districts are widely separated, linked by straight rail lines and buried conduits. There are no suburbs in the old sense, no gradual thinning of houses into fields. A block of towers ends, and then there may be five kilometers of empty mineral pavement before the next installation: a refinery, a pressure station, a cluster of inhabited vaults, an array of silent pylons.

Most structures are low because the plateau offers no shelter from stresses moving through the crust. They are broad, many-walled, and anchored deep. Their oldest courses have been carved directly from the summit rock. Newer levels are assembled from ceramic slabs, black alloy, translucent furnace glass, and enormous bricks sintered from local dust. Roofs carry no gutters. Doorways have raised thresholds and layered seals, relics either of poisonous dust or of some vanished concern no one now remembers.

The city has a name, but it is not spoken outdoors. Sound travels too far here.

Its industrial systems continue to operate with extraordinary restraint. Nothing roars unless it must. Turbines turn belowground in gas-filled chambers. Ore travels through sealed pneumatic arteries. Foundries are enclosed in thick shells whose seams glow faintly when a casting begins. Freight trains move on magnetic beds without wheel noise, announced only by a pressure in the ears and a long bar of blackness sliding across the already black plain.

Once every few hours, somewhere beyond sight, a coupling strikes home.

The sound crosses the plateau as a single metallic note. It may take minutes to fade.

The machinery does not appear to be expanding the city. It maintains it. Furnaces remake worn components. Excavators recover buried conduits, replace them, and seal the trenches with material indistinguishable from the surrounding stone. Processing towers separate useful gases from the air, then return nearly everything in the same proportions. Heat is vented into geological sinks. Dust trapped in one district is compressed and deposited in another. The whole urban organism seems devoted to keeping its total condition unchanged.

There are factories whose only product is replacement parts for those same factories.

There are warehouses filled with identical bearings, valve petals, ceramic joints, and spools of uncorroding wire. Inventory marks show that some have stood untouched for thousands of years. A part is removed only when its counterpart fails; another is then fabricated and placed in the emptied recess. Nothing accumulates. Nothing is exhausted. Production is less an economy than a ritual of equilibrium.

The streets reflect this logic. They are not crowded, and many were never intended for walking. Broad service corridors run between windowless walls, their surfaces polished by ages of infrequent traffic. Intersections are marked by rows of dim studs. Elevators descend into inhabited strata, maintenance strata, and still deeper levels for which the controls provide no labels.

No vegetation softens any edge. There is no moss in the expansion joints, no weed in a crack, no lichen on the oldest monuments. Dust gathers according to static charge, forming thin, exact borders around insulators, then remains there for decades. In courtyards, mineral fragments lie where they fell. They have none of the provisional look of rubble. Every object seems to have arrived at its final position.

The plateau itself is older than the city by an interval too large for civic records. Erosion has not made it dramatic. It has done the opposite. Cliffs have withdrawn. Ridges have become low swells. Impact scars have relaxed into circles perceptible only in survey maps. The land is worn almost beyond landscape, reduced to enduring differences in hardness and chemistry.

At the plateau’s rim, many kilometers from the central districts, the ground finally descends. Not in a sheer precipice, but in immense, shallow steps, each hundreds of meters high and broad enough to hold a town. The darkness below is deeper. Warm gases fill the lower country, hiding its floor. At intervals, the tops of ancient towers protrude from that opaque layer. Some may be geological needles. Some carry lights.

No road goes down.

Rare fauna persist in the city, though the word *animal* is only approximately correct. The most commonly seen are small, jointed things the color of furnace soot. They live inside electrical substations and feed on charge imbalances, arranging conductive dust across their backs in delicate branching patterns. They move slowly, sometimes remaining midway across a wall for an entire season. When disturbed, they fold flat and become nearly indistinguishable from a scorch mark.

Larger creatures are reported near the abandoned compression yards. They have hollow skeletons and translucent membranes stretched between six limbs. They do not fly—the air is too thin—but stand over warm vents and let thermal currents hold them partly upright. Their bodies contain almost no liquid. Flexible salts circulate through narrow channels, softening with heat and stiffening when they cool. They make no cries. Their presence is inferred from momentary occlusions of distant lamps.

Nothing hunts them, as far as anyone knows.

Nothing appears to reproduce.

Yet their number never quite reaches zero.

The inhabitants of the city are seldom visible. They move through enclosed passages, in vehicles, or beneath layered protective clothing. Apartments are cut into the warm rock around communal machine rooms. Their interiors are lit weakly and continuously, without day-night cycles. Public chambers contain maps, pressure gauges, shift records, and slabs engraved with laws so old that their grammar has changed.

The first law forbids open flame.

The second forbids the import or manufacture of free water.

The third requires that every removed mass be replaced by an equal mass within the same district.

Other laws concern noise, heat, illumination, and the alteration of horizons. No structure may rise high enough to change the skyline when viewed from designated stones outside the city. No lamp may shine above a certain angle. Excavations must be filled. Names of demolished buildings are transferred to their replacements.

These are not merely customs. The plateau seems to enforce them.

An unattended excavation slowly closes—not by collapse, but by an almost imperceptible upward movement of its floor. Excess heat migrates through the stone toward colder machinery. Towers built beyond the permitted height develop perfect horizontal fractures and settle until they match the old silhouette. Measurements repeated over generations show no net change in the city’s mass, temperature, atmospheric pressure, or outline.

Even accidents are absorbed.

A factory once exploded in the eastern works. Witnesses saw its shell open in silence and white metal scatter across several kilometers. By the time response crews arrived, the fragments had cooled into geometric plates. The crater was exactly compensated by a low rise elsewhere in the district. Reconstruction began, using the fallen material. When completed, the factory differed from its predecessor only in the positions of two interior stairs.

That was nine centuries ago. The same two stairs remain the city’s most famous innovation.

At the center stands an open square, perhaps the only deliberately empty space within the inhabited zones. Its paving is a single expanse of matte black stone. No pipes run beneath it; no rails cross it. Around its boundary, industrial facades rise without doors or signage. In the square’s center is a circular shaft sealed by a disk of pale material.

The disk is neither metal nor rock. It is always slightly warmer than the surrounding pavement. Instruments detect no emissions from it, but anyone standing nearby hears a sound like distant surf.

No one in the city has seen an ocean.

The noise may be a machine beneath the plateau. It may be an acoustic memory embedded in the seal. It may be the movement of matter in some adjacent condition of the world. Whatever its source, it never varies. Recordings made centuries apart match at every measurable interval, down to the smallest fluctuation.

Occasionally, one of the soot-colored creatures crosses the square. It reaches the pale disk, stops, and unfolds its conductive spines. For a moment the branching pattern on its back glows with a dim blue light—the only blue in the entire landscape.

Then it folds itself again and continues into the dark.

Nothing follows.

Nothing has changed.

Across the high plateau, lamps burn inside their hoods. The sealed trains glide between widely separated districts. Deep underfoot, turbines maintain their exact revolutions. Beyond the last structures, the oldest terrain in the world extends toward the rim: warm stone, mineral scales, shallow channels, and sparse black pylons standing beneath a lightless sky.

The city endures there not against decay, but after it—past weather, growth, conquest, and ruin, in the strange calm of a place that has already become everything it will ever be.
