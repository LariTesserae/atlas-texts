The city occupies the hollow inside a bell.

No one living there knows who cast it. Its bronze curves away beyond sight, a warm brown sky mottled with green mineral blooms. The city is built across the bell’s inner lip, where centuries of dust have collected in a crescent no wider than a market street. Beyond that narrow ledge there is almost nothing: a tremendous, radiant emptiness descending toward the unseen clapper.

The place is low in every local sense. Houses crouch. Streets sink into the dust. Doors are cut near the floor, and people move bent-backed beneath awnings woven from pale, leathery leaves. Yet the void beside them is immeasurably deep. Looking across it, one can see districts on the opposite wall as tiny stitches of white masonry, upside down from one another, connected only by ropes that sag through miles of brilliance.

There is no water.

The inhabitants drink neither rain nor wells, and have no words for rivers except in inherited songs. Their gardens are fed by metal. Thick-rooted plants grow directly from the old bronze, dissolving it grain by grain with sour secretions. Their roots spread flat against the curved wall in black, branching fans; their leaves are inflated bladders of translucent ivory that capture warm vapor and sift useful salts from the air. At noon, whole terraces of these plants brighten from within. The city then seems overgrown by paper lanterns.

These are lush gardens by local measure, crowded and extravagant, though each plant is separated from the next by yards of swept emptiness. The spacing is necessary. Root colonies that touch will poison one another, and so every garden keeps its own round court of bare bronze. From a distance the cultivated ledge resembles a necklace: islands of tangled foliage divided by broad, shining gaps.

The most prized growth is bellvine. It lays copper-colored tendrils along masonry and produces hard green pods filled with waxy meal. There are also ash fans, whose fronds grind airborne grit into edible gray powder; ember moss, warm to the touch; and tall whisper-stalks with jointed leaves that turn continuously toward vibration rather than light. Their seeds are flakes thin as breath. They cross the void in storms and may take a century to find a wall.

Animals are uncommon. Small hook-footed grazers live among the root fans, each no larger than a thumb joint. They are blind and nearly weightless, with shells like scraps of tarnished foil. Once or twice in a generation, a sail-beast rises from the depths: an immense, starving shape borne upward by heated air, all rib, membrane, and trailing mouths. The city extinguishes its lamps when one passes. Usually it drifts on toward the crown, feeding on seed dust. Occasionally it catches against the buildings, and for days the streets fill with knives, shouting, and the smell of hot resin.

The city itself has accumulated rather than been planned. Its oldest rooms are excavated into the compacted dust of the ledge, roofed with ribs of fired clay. Above them stand white blocks cut from mineral crust. Above those are platforms of lashed wood, although wood is so precious that a straight beam may have served six houses across twenty generations. New buildings are narrow towers propped against old ones. Bridges cross the courts. Stairs climb façades and end at trapdoors. Alleys run beneath kitchens, shrines, root beds, and public ovens.

Despite this density of construction, it feels abandoned. Most chambers are empty.

The city was built for a population many times larger than the one that remains. Whole neighborhoods are sealed behind clay doors. Courtyards hold only dust circles and a single working plant. Kilns burn in avenues broad enough for vanished processions. A person may walk all morning through connected rooms without meeting anyone, though signs of habitation persist everywhere: a swept threshold, a basket hung beyond the reach of hook-grazers, fresh cord tied around a cracked stair.

Craft is done with stone, ceramic, shell, bone, and the city’s omnipresent bronze. The bronze cannot easily be melted; fires fierce enough to soften it are difficult to sustain without fuel. Instead, workers cultivate etching lichens in patterned clay molds. Given several months, the lichens eat hinges, needles, saw teeth, bowls, and delicate perforated screens directly from the wall. These objects emerge already weathered, green at the edges and engraved with the digestive trails of the colony that made them. Fine tools are sharpened on glassy nodules found in the dust. Heavy loads are moved with ropes, wedges, capstans, and patient crowds.

Everything is old enough to have found its resting shape. Corners have been polished round by hands. Stair treads sag like soft wax. The underside of every arch bears a black crescent where warm dust circulates. Repairs are never disguised. Walls show strata of clay patches, woven braces, mineral mortar, and plaques naming the households that prevented a collapse. Nothing here is regarded as finished. Stability is a kind of slow conversation.

And over all of it is the sound.

The bell never stops ringing.

It does not ring as a struck bell rings, with one blow and a fading tone. It is excited by deep currents in the surrounding gas, or by some mechanism beneath the clapper, or by the turning of the world itself. The bronze sky trembles continuously. The vibration passes through foundations, teeth, cookware, roots, and bone. At its quietest it is a colossal hum that makes speech difficult. At its loudest it becomes a physical pressure. Dust leaps from floors. Leaves flatten. Hair lifts. Mortar sheds from walls in pale sheets.

Conversation is conducted chiefly by touch. People press fingertips to one another’s wrists and tap in a quick, intricate language. Public announcements are hammered into railing networks that carry distinct rhythms throughout a district. Names are not spoken but drummed. Songs are danced barefoot on resonant floors. To lie is said to be harder hand-to-hand, because the pulse betrays hesitation.

The ringing also governs the ecology. Bellvines open their pods only at certain harmonics. Whisper-stalks bow before a loud cycle and rise afterward heavy with metallic dew—not water, but droplets of a low-melting alloy condensed from the air. Citizens gather these beads with ceramic combs and trade them to tool growers. Hook-grazers mate during the deep notes. Seed storms occur when the pitch climbs through a thin, painful register that cracks old plaster and sends white flakes whirling into the void.

Light enters from an opening impossibly far above. It is clean, relentless, and golden-white. The concave bronze gathers it and throws it from wall to wall until shadows become faint and multiple. Even enclosed lanes glow through cracks. People wear broad clay visors painted with soot on the underside, and most windows face sideways rather than upward. Night exists only when the opening is periodically eclipsed by something vast outside. During these intervals the entire city cools, the plants dim, and the bell’s inner sky turns black-green. Far districts appear as constellations of furnace mouths.

Warmth soon returns. The metal heats. Air climbs the walls in violent invisible rivers, carrying seeds, grit, shed scales, and scraps of cloth. Then, at a certain hour, the current reverses. Anything not tied down is drawn toward the drop.

The ledge has been shaped by this breathing over an age. Its dust lies in streamlined dunes between buildings. Streets curve with the airflow. Towers present rounded backs to the upward gust and open balconies to the downward one. Every roof is tethered. Every child learns three knots before learning household signs. The first law of the city is not moral but gravitational:

**Nothing loose.**

The second is:

**Do not answer a rhythm from below.**

Sometimes, within the deafening common tone, another pattern rises through the bronze. It is slow, deliberate, and unlike weather. The oldest buildings have their foundations cut away from the wall so they will not carry it. The sealed neighborhoods are said to have answered once, hammering a greeting into the bell.

No one agrees on what happened afterward. Only that those districts remain intact, brightly illuminated and perfectly empty, their gardens still opening and closing to the endless sound.
