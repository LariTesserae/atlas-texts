The city stands on a warm, low plain of new stone.

It is not ruined, though from a distance it can resemble a ruin: thousands of squat walls, blunt towers, stepped roofs, causeways, pens, cisterns, and kilns crowded together beneath a ceiling of dark cloud. Nothing rises very high. The ground is too soft for monumental weight, and the builders possess neither cranes nor complex joinery. Their city spreads instead—broad, compact, and repetitive—until its outer embankments blur into the steaming flats.

Everything is made from what the plain has only just begun to provide. Gray clay is pressed into bricks and dried beside communal ovens. Porous blocks are cut from shelves of pale mineral crust. Dark volcanic cobbles, still sharp-edged and scarcely weathered, are stacked without mortar. Roofs are shallow domes of reedless daub laid over ribs of bone and timber brought from far beyond the plain. Wood is precious here. A door lintel may be older than the house beneath it, carried from building to building through generations.

There are no plants.

No grass grows between the paving stones. No moss darkens the wells. The canal margins are bare clay, stamped smooth by feet and hooves. Even in neglected corners there are only salt blooms, slick films of mineral color, and beads of condensation trembling under ledges. At first glance this absence makes the city appear unfinished, as if its streets await the arrival of green things. After a while it seems complete in another way: a world made entirely of earth, water, animal bodies, smoke, and human labor.

Water is everywhere without dominating the view. It lies just below the surface, pressing dampness into floors and foundations. Shallow wells fill almost as quickly as they are emptied. Narrow channels run beside the larger streets, fed by warm seepages that issue from seams in the raw bedrock. After the daily rain, every courtyard holds a skin of water. Roof edges drip for hours. Walls weep through their pores. The air condenses on pottery, hide awnings, horns, hair, and the polished handles of tools.

The plain has scarcely learned to drain. There are no old river valleys, no rounded stones, no deep gullies. Water moves through cuts made by people and through shallow natural swales little older than the city. The exposed ground beyond the walls is raw and planar: clay beds, recent lava sheets, chalky shelves, and low blisters of stone pushed up without yet being softened. Cliffs, where they occur, are crisp as broken pottery. Rain has not had time to carve them.

Yet the city itself never seems new. It possesses the settled stillness of a pattern that has always existed.

Its streets follow strict, repeated dimensions. Every lane is narrow enough that two loaded animals must turn sideways to pass. Every seventh lane widens into a market court. Every district has a round cistern, a kiln yard, a slaughter floor, and a covered sleeping hall. The forms recur outward in rings, adapted only slightly to cracks and pools in the ground. Houses are continually patched, but never redesigned. A collapsed wall is rebuilt on the same line with the same number of courses. Channels are dredged to their customary depth. Gates open and close at fixed hours.

The climate does not vary. The warmth never becomes heat and never cools. The cloud ceiling remains at the same low altitude, charcoal-colored but not stormy, luminous enough to erase true night and too dense to permit full day. Rain falls at the same hour in the same steady volume. Wind comes from the same quarter. The seepages never rise or diminish. Shadows remain soft and short.

In this equilibrium, sound has become the city’s weather.

It is deafening.

Herds occupy as much of the settlement as people do. Broad-backed cattle crowd the outer rings, horn tips painted in district colors. Goats fill rooftop pens and climb stairways polished by generations of hooves. Pigs churn the warm mud of drainage yards. Dogs bark from thresholds. Fowl with clipped wings beat against wicker cages, and clouds of small gray birds circle beneath the clouds, nesting not in trees but in holes deliberately left in walls. In the wet flats outside, long-legged animals graze on mats of pale microbial scum and sift tiny shell-creatures from shallow water.

The animals cry almost continuously. Bells of fired clay knock against collars. Hooves strike stone. Wooden cart wheels shriek on axles greased with fat. Pestles pound grain imported from other regions. Mallets compact clay. Stone chisels tap in the quarries. Kiln crews shout over roaring draft holes. Water wheels turn where the channels permit them, their simple paddles slapping with relentless force. Market callers repeat the same short phrases until they lose the quality of speech and become part of the general roar.

Conversation is conducted close to the ear or by gesture. Children learn hand signs before they learn spoken words. Agreements are sealed in view of witnesses because no one can reliably hear an oath in a public court. Watchers on the walls communicate with flags by dim day and pierced lantern pots in the cloud-lit dusk.

Even private rooms hum. Water ticks from ceilings into bowls. Animals breathe behind partition walls. The city’s packed earth transmits pounding from distant yards. A sleeper can identify districts by vibration: the heavy regular stroke of the western clay mills, the rapid stone-on-stone chatter of the shell workshops, the dull communal stamping where bricks are formed.

The fauna are not merely livestock. They are the city’s principal technology.

Cattle turn mills and drag sledges. Dogs pull narrow carts through lanes where larger beasts cannot fit. Birds carry short messages between districts, following feeding trays placed atop gatehouses. Small burrowing animals are kept in ceramic boxes and used to locate changes in groundwater by their agitation. Fish and hard-shelled water creatures are raised in cisterns, feeding on scraps and mineral films. Dung fires the kilns. Bone supplies needles, handles, roof ribs, hooks, hinges, combs, and the toothed edges of harvesting tools used in distant lands.

Metal exists, but only as a rarity: soft copper hammered into thin plates, lead weights, occasional meteoric iron passed down as sacred cutting edges. Most work is performed with chipped glassy stone, ground basalt, shell, bone, cordage, leather, and clay. The city’s sophistication lies not in its tools but in organization. Tens of thousands of simple acts, repeated precisely, sustain a settlement that no individual could design.

Food arrives along the raised roads. Since nothing green grows on the plain, caravans come from beyond the cloud belt carrying dried grain, pressed cakes, fruit paste, fodder, timber, and woven fiber. They return with salt, ceramics, animal products, pigments, and blocks of fresh-cut stone. The roads are low embankments, only a body’s height above the wet ground, but they can be followed for days. At intervals stand identical rest enclosures: circular walls, a well, tethering stones, and a clay horn that can be sounded toward the next station.

Its note is enormous. Other horns answer across the flats. The sound travels under the cloud cover and returns softened but undiminished, so that arrivals are announced long before they can be seen.

Beyond the last wall, the city does not end cleanly. Pens become corrals, corrals become quarry pits, quarry pits become shallow pools. Brick fields extend outward in geometric patches. Lines of tethering posts cross the bare clay. Herds stand knee-deep in warm water, their reflections broken by rain. Birds cover the raw stone in such numbers that from afar they can be mistaken for a dark mineral deposit.

There is little erosion, and so every mark remains. Cart ruts persist until deliberately filled. A dropped jar leaves its fragments sharp for decades. Hoofprints dry into permanent cups at the edge of the flats. The city’s refuse heaps do not slump into soil; they retain each deposited layer—shell, ash, bone, sherd, clay—as legibly as courses in a wall. Archaeology begins here almost immediately. The past lies exposed because the landscape has not yet acquired the habits that would bury or blur it.

Nothing changes, but nothing is silent or empty.

Rain falls. Channels run. Kilns burn. Herds shift in their enclosures. Hands lift and strike, lift and strike. The cloud remains. The warm plain holds its water near the surface and its city close to the ground.

From the outer causeway, looking back through dim vapor, the whole settlement seems less built upon the new earth than pressed into it: a vast, dense impression made by animals and people before the world’s stone has fully hardened.
