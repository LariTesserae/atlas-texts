The place occupies the crown of a mountain, though from within it the mountain is almost impossible to see.

A ring of black stone encloses the summit: not a wall, exactly, but a natural-looking escarpment no more than a few hundred paces across. Its ridges are sharp and undecided, with thin fins of rock standing where older landscapes would have rounded them away. The summit seems newly raised. Whole shelves remain split along bright mineral seams, and in some cracks the stone is warm enough to steam after rain.

It rains often here, but briefly. Clouds snag against the crown, unload in sudden vertical sheets, then lift away into white glare. Water runs over the dark rock in silver threads, gathers in shallow basins, and disappears through holes too narrow to admit a finger. Minutes later it returns elsewhere: beading from vertical faces, swelling beneath mats of moss, or condensing in midair along invisible planes. At certain hours droplets form in perfect horizontal rows, as if resting on glass that cannot be touched.

The heat is constant. Even at this elevation the air has the weight of a lowland summer, dense with moisture and metallic scents. Breath comes easily at first, then seems too warm to satisfy. Bare stone burns under the sun. In shadow, however, water keeps the surfaces cool and slick. A person crossing the crown moves repeatedly between these two temperatures—radiant heat and wet mineral chill—sometimes within a single stride.

Vegetation grows lushly where it grows at all, but it does not cover the summit. There are generous intervals of bare rock and open water. Each patch of life is sharply bounded, like an island laid into the stone.

Low ferns crowd certain fissures, their fronds thick and translucent. Cords of blue-green moss descend from weeping ledges. Three or four small trees stand far apart around the enclosure, each with pale bark and a sparse, umbrella-shaped canopy. Their roots do not enter the ground. They divide into hundreds of hair-fine strands that hover a fraction above it, gathering vapor and whatever rises from the mineral seams. When a rainstorm approaches, the roots lift together and point toward the clouds.

Between these islands lie shallow pools. They are too clear. Their bottoms appear close enough to touch, even where dropped objects take several seconds to arrive. Some pools reflect the sky; others reflect adjacent pools, or show the summit under different weather. In one, rain is always falling upward. In another, the black rock is overgrown by a forest that does not exist here, its foliage moving in a wind the observer cannot feel.

There are only a few structures.

Near the center stands a narrow shelter with no visible entrance. It is roughly the size of a wayside shrine, assembled from six slabs of a gray substance that resembles ceramic until sunlight passes through it. Then faint internal geometries become visible: filaments branching and rebranching too finely for the eye to follow. Moisture avoids the shelter. Rain bends around it, leaving a dry oval on the rock, while runoff divides upstream and rejoins below without touching its base.

At the northern edge, half of an old footbridge projects over a cleft. Its deck is weathered wood, or an exact imitation of wood, complete with lichen and insect holes. It extends only four paces before ending cleanly in open air. Occasionally the missing half appears as a distortion in the rain. Anyone standing at the end can hear footsteps continuing away, diminishing toward a destination beyond the summit.

Scattered nearby are small artifacts: a hoop sunk upright in stone; a row of dull metal studs; three white cups fused to a ledge; a thread-thin tower no taller than a person. None has controls. None appears ruined. They may be instruments, or anchors, or the external organs of a single system buried through the mountain.

The summit’s rules reveal themselves gradually.

Water never flows across the central line from west to east, though the ground slopes that way. Seeds cast over the line fall normally, but their shadows land on the opposite side. Sound behaves differently near the pools: a whisper may cross the whole enclosure, while a shout vanishes at arm’s length. Metal left overnight becomes intricately folded without changing its outer dimensions. Every seventh rainstorm falls through clothing and skin without making them wet, yet fills the stone basins all the same.

The machinery—if it is machinery—works mostly below perception. Fine tremors pass through the rock. Condensation gathers into diagrams, persists for a breath, and drains away. Shadows acquire extra edges at noon. Once or twice a day, the summit becomes fractionally larger: paths lengthen, pools draw apart, and the enclosing ridge retreats by several steps. Then there is a soft pressure in the ears, and everything returns to its former scale. Only flowing water seems to remember the expansion. It arrives late, spilling briefly over dry ground.

Life here has adapted without becoming abundant.

Small silver insects inhabit the moss, but seldom expose themselves. They move in coordinated lines beneath the surface, causing the green to ripple like fur. A dark, long-legged bird visits after storms. It walks the pool margins without calling, inserts its beak into reflections rather than water, and sometimes draws out pale, writhing things that cannot be seen from above. After feeding, it launches over the escarpment and falls out of sight before opening its wings.

There are signs of a larger animal: blunt tracks filled with rainwater, scraped bark high on the pale trees, warm hollows in beds of fern. It is never encountered directly. At dusk, one may notice vegetation settling in a sequence across the summit, as though something invisible has passed through it. The shelter’s internal filaments brighten at those moments.

Most of the time the crown is quiet. Water ticks into water. Steam whispers from cracks. Leaves release accumulated rain with soft, isolated slaps. The wind moves overhead but seldom enters the ring. Distant thunder arrives muted by the cloud layer below, so that storms seem to be occurring beneath the mountain rather than above it.

Yet the place is not still. It is continually sorting itself.

Pools empty and refill. Moss advances across a slab during one humid hour, then retreats when the stone warms. Mineral crystals grow along fractures in fast, glassy fans and dissolve in the next rain. The hovering roots turn toward changing pressure. Invisible surfaces tilt, gathering necklaces of condensation. The central shelter darkens and clears in cycles too slow to watch but too fast to ignore.

Near sunset, heat rises from the summit in wavering columns. The scattered trees cast ordinary shadows, long and lucid. For a short while everything appears comprehensible: a wet volcanic crown, young rock, hardy growth, remnants of some remote expedition. Then the pools begin reflecting stars before any stars are visible overhead.

One by one, the metal studs set into the ground answer with a faint blue point.

The points do not illuminate the landscape. They describe it—plotting the pools, the trees, the sharp escarpment, the little sealed shelter—and then continue beyond its boundaries, marking unseen features in empty air. Some coordinates hang over the precipice. Others appear far below the stone.

The last light leaves the summit slowly. Water keeps moving in the dark, following channels made only moments before, through a place that may be newly born, continuously rebuilt, or held at the precise young edge of its own creation.
