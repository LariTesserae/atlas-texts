The city stands on a high shelf of black mineral, warm beneath an ordinary yellow sun. It is not large. From one rim to the other is perhaps the distance of a slow afternoon’s walk, though the streets fold so often that crossing it feels longer. Beyond its boundaries, the land falls away in fresh cliffs: hard, angular faces of basalt, green glass, and pale salt, scarcely softened by weather. There are no gullies, no rounded stones, no traces of rivers. The plateau seems to have risen recently and all at once, lifting its crowded little world above a vast dry basin.

Nothing here has been built by hands.

The city is grown.

Its towers are the hollow trunks of columnar organisms rooted deep in fissures. They stand shoulder to shoulder, reddish brown and dull gold, each splitting near its crown into vaulted ribs. Where neighboring ribs meet, they fuse, making arches, galleries, enclosed courts, and narrow covered streets. Some trunks are no wider than a person; others contain warm chambers large enough for a gathering. Their walls are layered bark, cork, wax, and mineral scale. They have doors only because certain animals repeatedly pass through the same soft places, and windows where colonies of pale borers have eaten toward the light.

The newest towers remain sharp at every edge. Their buttresses have pushed up slabs of the plateau rather than bending around them. Fresh seams show bright orange or violet where the rock has cracked. Fine black dust lies below, but little has had time to collect. Nothing looks ruined. Even the abandoned chambers appear unfinished rather than old.

There is no water—not hidden in wells, not held in clouds, not running as sap. The life of the shelf uses another fluid: a thin amber solvent distilled from the mineral air. At night it condenses inside sealed tissues, never forming exposed pools or droplets. Plants circulate it slowly through braided vessels. Animals carry it in tough bladders behind their lungs. Spilling it is rare and dangerous; in open air it flashes into a fragrant vapor, leaving crystals like powdered brass.

For this reason the greenery is lush without being soft.

Thick ribbons of foliage hang from the arches, overlapping in dark tiers. Their leaves are dry, warm, and faintly flexible, with surfaces like leather or mica. Some are black enough to mirror the sky. Others are chalk-white and turn their narrow edges toward the sun. Dense cushions of filamentous growth fill cracks between buildings, each thread sheathed in silica. Copper fans open from shaded walls. Low groves of jointed rods crowd the courtyards, knocking almost inaudibly against one another when the heated air shifts.

No part of the city is empty for long. Small creatures occupy nearly every surface, but their abundance does not make the place loud.

Six-legged grazers, no larger than fingernails, move in orderly bands across the leaves, shaving off mineral bloom. Flat animals cling beneath cornices and breathe through rippling seams in their backs. Long-bodied gliders hang between towers on translucent membranes, waiting hours before changing their grip. In deep passages, pale herd-creatures stand pressed flank to flank, their eyeless heads lowered into warm vents. Their movements are so coordinated that an entire herd may slowly reverse direction without a hoof clicking against stone.

The largest residents resemble narrow, upright mantles of folded hide. They travel singly through the boulevards, stepping on many fine limbs concealed beneath them. Every few days one stops at a living wall and remains there, apparently asleep, while the wall grows a chamber around its body. Months later the chamber opens and several smaller mantles emerge. Whether these creatures inhabit the city or are one of the means by which the city reproduces is impossible to decide.

Sound is absorbed everywhere. Cork walls drink footsteps. Hanging foliage breaks the wind. Even falling mineral flakes descend like ash. Standing in one of the central courts, one hears only intimate noises: the dry drawing of a leaf along bark; a creature’s sealed joints flexing; a faint pulse traveling through a tower’s inner vessels. Occasionally, far below the plateau, the young rock contracts with a single deep knock. The city becomes still after such a sound, all its animals pausing at once. Then, one by one, they resume.

The streets follow warmth rather than distance. Broad avenues run above deep faults where heat rises from the plateau’s interior. Cool passages narrow and are gradually closed by growth. Arches lean toward neighboring arches; plazas tighten by a finger’s width each year. The whole settlement is dense because life survives by enclosure. Open space wastes vapor. Shade preserves it. Every organism contributes another membrane, another wall, another folded pocket in which the amber chemistry can continue.

Yet the city never feels dark. Sunlight enters through the high lattice of crowns and falls in clean, familiar shafts. It strikes polished leaves, tawny bark, and exposed facets of young stone. Reflections pass from surface to surface until even the lower streets hold a calm bronze daylight. At noon the air is warm enough to soften waxy growths. Their scent—resin, hot dust, bitter spice—collects beneath the vaults.

Change here is almost too slow to witness. Towers incline over generations. A street closes. Another opens when a root splits the ground. The plateau itself may still be rising, but by increments beneath notice. From the cliff edge one can see concentric fractures across the dry basin, as if other shelves may someday lift and bear cities of their own.

There are rules to the place, though no one has written them.

Nothing living exposes its inner fluid.

Nothing crosses an unshaded court at noon unless pursued.

When the rock knocks, all movement ceases.

No species owns a chamber permanently; walls admit, enclose, and release according to seasons of heat that last many years.

And everything, predator and grazer, tower and creeping filament, yields passage to the slow procession that occurs at sunset.

Then thousands of small, dark animals descend from the highest crowns. They move through the streets in complete silence, each carrying within its translucent abdomen a grain of stored daylight. The procession winds down toward the warm faults, filling the city with a muted constellation. One by one the lights vanish into cracks in the new rock.

By dawn they return empty, and the plateau has risen—perhaps no more than the thickness of a leaf.
