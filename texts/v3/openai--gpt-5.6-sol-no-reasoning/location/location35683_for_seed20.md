The place is a warm sea held high above the world.

It occupies a hollow no larger than a village, though “hollow” is misleading. There is no reliable basin beneath it. The water hangs between several young black pinnacles, filling the intervals from cliff to cliff and rising far above them in a suspended, uneven mass. Its upper surface cannot be seen from within. Its lower surface sometimes can: a trembling dark mirror hundreds of fathoms below, beyond which lies open air and, very far down, the pale confusion of clouds.

Everything here is submerged, but nothing agrees on which way submerged means.

The pinnacles are newly made—dark glass, red porous stone, mineral crusts still soft enough to bruise. Their edges have not had time to become slopes. They thrust through the water as blades and broken teeth, sharp enough that passing currents split upon them with a continuous concussion. Some faces remain warm from their formation. Touching them releases strings of silver bubbles and a smell, somehow perceptible underwater, of iron, salt, and thunderstorms.

There is little life. In sheltered cracks grow tufts of colorless weed, each strand as thin as hair and rooted not downward but toward whichever direction was most stable when it germinated. Most tufts point differently. A few low mats of rust-red organisms cling around the warmest vents. They open into tiny mouths when struck by pressure waves, then seal themselves for hours.

The only animals are occasional translucent swimmers shaped like knots in clear cord. They appear singly, never in schools. Each carries a dim amber pulse in its center. When the water turns violent they pull themselves tight, becoming almost invisible, and allow the sea to throw them where it will. Nothing seems to hunt them. Sometimes one passes into a fold of water and fails to emerge; sometimes two come out.

The noise is absolute.

It is not merely loud but architectural, a pressure in which all other sensations are embedded. The suspended ocean roars along its boundaries, though those boundaries are often nowhere visible. Currents collide overhead with the sustained impact of landslides. Cavities in the stone bellow. Dense trains of bubbles rise, turn sideways, and explode against invisible surfaces. Deep within the water comes a regular detonation like a giant door slammed shut, except no two blows arrive from the same direction. The cliffs answer in long mineral groans.

Silence occurs only in small moving pockets. These clear, round absences drift through the sea like bubbles, but contain water rather than air. Entering one does not stop the surrounding violence; it removes the concept of sound. Stone struck within such a pocket gives no vibration. One’s own movement becomes noiseless and curiously incomplete. The pockets seldom last more than a few breaths before bursting with a crack that can shear soft growth from the rocks.

The water is warm, nearly body-warm in most places, fever-hot around the black vents and suddenly cool near its exposed underside. It is clear for only a few arm-lengths. Beyond that distance, suspended mineral dust makes a dim bronze murk. There is no sun, yet a weak illumination seeps through from several directions, changing without rhythm. Shadows often precede the objects that cast them. At times every edge glows faintly blue, revealing intricate fractures inside the rock. At others the whole place darkens until only bubbles and the amber hearts of the swimmers remain.

Currents do not flow through this sea so much as revise it. A torrent may open in motionless water, run upward for several minutes, then harden into a transparent column around which the rest of the ocean pours. Sheets of water peel away from the main volume and flap like fabric. Vortices travel through solid stone, making the pinnacles twist without breaking. Distances expand under pressure: a crack close enough to touch may require a long swim to reach, while a distant cliff can arrive abruptly against the hand.

The sea is continually making rooms.

Four walls of denser water close around a volume of thinner water. A floor forms from silt that has not yet settled. An arch of bubbles locks into place overhead. For a few moments there is a chamber, dim and almost calm, furnished with shelves of pumice and dangling threads of weed. Then a pressure front passes through. The walls collapse inward, the floor streams up the sides, and the chamber becomes a spinning shaft.

Among the pinnacles are scattered structures, though nothing here possesses or remembers tools.

They resemble small shrines, roofless cells, stairs of three or four steps, lintels resting upon paired uprights. They are built of the same young volcanic stone and are too precise to dismiss at first glance. Yet none bears a cut or fastening. Watch long enough and their origin becomes apparent: the sea assembles them.

A current lifts flat fragments. Pressure holds them together. Mineral foam seals the joints. For an hour, perhaps a day, a black doorway stands on a ledge where no doorway stood before. Colorless weed begins to colonize its sheltered corners. Then the water changes its mind. The seal softens, the stones fly apart, and elsewhere a stair begins constructing itself downward into empty water.

Some formations are less innocent. Narrow corridors appear between parallel slabs and remain longer inside than outside. Passing through one can return a swimmer to the entrance facing the wrong direction. A ring of pillars sometimes rises around a vent, each pillar bent toward the next, while the vent’s bubbles descend in a perfectly straight line. Nothing built here is evidence of builders. Architecture is simply one of the temporary states of geology.

At the center of the suspended sea lies an open space bordered by six uneven spires. It is perhaps the size of a small chapel. No current enters it directly, yet it is the most turbulent point in the whole region. Water there repeatedly forms and destroys shapes too large to be animals and too deliberate to be waves: a coiling trunk, a ribbed dome, an enormous closed hand. They surface from no direction, hold for a heartbeat, and dissolve into foam and gravel.

With each dissolution, the surrounding landscape changes slightly. A pinnacle gains a ledge. A crack seals. A mineral garden appears already grown, then fades as though remembering it never existed. The rare transparent creatures gather at the edge of this space but will not cross into it. Their amber centers blink in rough unison with the deep, door-slamming detonations.

The laws of the place are local and short-lived.

Heat may fall like sediment. Buoyancy attaches itself to color. A stone moving quickly can become liquid while water held motionless develops a cutting edge. Cause and effect frequently pass one another in the currents: a plume of dust emerges, then later the cliff fractures that released it. Bubbles gather before a vent opens. The echo of a collapse arrives first, followed by the collapse, followed by the original sound a second time.

Yet the place is not formless. Beneath its chaos is a severe, shifting order. For several minutes all motion may obey the six central spires. Then the governing shape becomes a spiral, or a pair of intersecting planes, or the pulse of an amber animal drifting through the dark. Whatever pattern takes hold is complete. Water, stone, light, sound, and distance submit to it together.

The greatest boundary is below.

There the warm sea ends against open atmosphere in an undulating underside, a ceiling viewed from the wrong side. Waves run across it upside down. Loose stones fall toward it, slow, and become caught in surface tension, rolling along the boundary like beads of black mercury. Now and then one breaks through. It vanishes into the bright cloud-depth beneath, trailing a long cord of water that stretches after it and snaps with a report louder than thunder.

No one can remain near that underside for long. The sea there begins to forget which side of the skin it occupies. Fins, weeds, bubbles, and bodies acquire reflections that move independently. A swimmer may look down and see not the sky below but another submerged basin, with another swimmer looking up. Sometimes that second place contains older, eroded pinnacles and abundant pale forests. Sometimes it contains only an immense eye-colored darkness.

Above—if above is still the proper word—the ocean thickens. Its water becomes almost syrupy, crowded with pressure and low red heat. Black peaks disappear into that dim mass. The deafening blows originate there, or seem to. After each one, the entire suspended sea shudders, its lower boundary bulging toward the distant world.

The place feels recent. Not young in years, necessarily, but continuously new: a landscape whose creation has not concluded. Every surface is raw. Every arrangement remains provisional. Erosion has scarcely begun because nothing keeps its shape long enough to weather. The sea builds cliffs faster than currents can round them, breaks them, and tries other cliffs.

It is a high, warm, drowned fragment of country with almost no country beneath it—a violent little ocean caught in the act of deciding what an ocean is.
