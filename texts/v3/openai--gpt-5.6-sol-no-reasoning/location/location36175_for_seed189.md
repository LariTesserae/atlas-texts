Beneath the ground lies a room no larger than a chapel and far less stable.

It has no stone walls. What appears at first to be a cavern is the interior of a plant: a warm, hollow organ buried beneath a dry plain, grown rather than excavated. Its shell is layered rootwood, pale rind, amber pith, and membranes thin enough to shine. Every surface is alive. The floor gives faintly under pressure. The walls tighten when touched. Far overhead—perhaps twelve meters, perhaps only two—the ceiling opens and closes in overlapping green-black lobes.

There is almost nothing inside.

That emptiness is the chamber’s most startling property. In a place so wholly made of vegetation, there are no thickets, no mossy clutter, no hanging vines. Plant life has not occupied the landscape; plant life has become the landscape and left its interior vacant. The great hollow resembles a seed from which the seed has vanished. A few taut root-strands cross it at unlikely distances, narrow as cords, carrying clear sap in pulses. Otherwise, the warm volume stands open.

The light has no obvious source. It is ordinary daylight—soft, directional, bright enough for color—yet the place is far below the surface. Shadows fall consistently toward one wall in the morning and reverse in the evening, though there is no sun and no opening. If a hand is raised, its shadow sometimes arrives before the hand. On rare occasions two noons pass through the chamber in quick succession.

The walls grow weather.

Moisture gathers inside their translucent tissues until blisters form, swell, and break. Rain then falls inward from the sides as often as from above: brief, heavy showers of warm droplets, each lasting less than a minute. Water collects in shallow dimples in the floor, silver pockets no wider than bowls. Within hours it is drunk back through hair-fine pores, leaving behind white rings of mineral salt and a smell like cut cucumber over hot soil.

There is never enough water to make the chamber lush in the familiar sense. There are no pools deep enough to wade, no stream. But it is always damp somewhere. Condensation runs along the root-cords in traveling beads. One can watch a bead crawl against gravity, hesitate at a branching, and divide into three perfect smaller copies.

The chamber is young. Its surfaces have not yet learned smoothness. Fresh ribs thrust from the walls in sharp pleats, wine-red at their tips. Splits in the rind are raw and fragrant. Root spurs emerge with pale points hard enough to puncture skin, then soften over the course of a day and fold back into the wall. Nothing here bears the wear of age. Damage closes quickly, but not by restoring what was present: a torn place improvises. It may heal as bark, leaf, translucent flesh, or an eye-shaped knot that follows movement for several hours before sealing shut.

This constant formation makes the cavern loud.

Sap strikes through the vascular channels with the concussion of distant machinery. The walls crack as they expand. Root-cords pluck themselves like immense strings, their notes too low to hear cleanly but strong enough to blur vision. During the inward rains, droplets drum on stretched membranes until the entire hollow sounds like a roof in a storm. There are also sudden reports—green, wet thunderclaps—when the chamber adds a new fold to itself.

Between these events come long intervals of almost perfect visual emptiness, though never silence. A soft chewing seems to continue somewhere beyond the walls.

Fauna are scarce. Once every few days, a white six-legged creature emerges from a pore near the floor. It is no larger than a thumb and has no visible mouth. It crosses the chamber with solemn speed, taps each water pocket, and enters the opposite wall. Whether these are animals, mobile fruits, or messages the plant sends through its own body is unclear. They leave paired tracks that continue for a short distance after the creatures have disappeared.

Occasionally a moth appears. It is broad, dark, and warm to the touch, with branching veins in its wings that match the chamber’s vascular pattern exactly. It does not fly unless no one looks directly at it. Its wingbeats can still be heard, circling high in the hollow, while it remains visibly motionless on the wall.

There are signs of intelligence but no structures and no artifacts in the usual sense. Along one low ridge, thin flakes of hardened rind regularly detach in shapes useful for cutting, scraping, or puncturing. Near them grows a rope-like tendril that can be peeled into fibers. A hand axe could be assembled here without stone or metal, but the place would have grown every part of it. Tools made from the chamber remain alive. A knife of rind slowly sharpens itself if fed moisture; a fiber cord contracts when warmed; a cup folded from membrane tries to return to the wall.

Nothing manufactured remains separate for long. Set a tool down overnight and root hairs begin to claim it. After several days it becomes an organ whose former purpose can only be guessed from its shape. There are no ruins because the living substrate digests the distinction between object and environment.

The rules of distance are unreliable. The hollow looks intimate, its opposite wall close enough to reach in a few strides. Walking toward it may take an hour. A root-cord that seems to span the chamber can be stepped over, yet a beetle-sized creature may require half a day to crawl around it. Sound obeys the visible scale rather than the traveled one: a whisper at the far wall arrives immediately, while a shout nearby may return much later, exhausted and faint.

There are places where the floor opens not into deeper earth but into smaller versions of the same chamber. Looking down through a split reveals another bright hollow complete with inward rain, root-cords, and perhaps a tiny figure standing at its edge. Sometimes the figure looks up. Sometimes it raises an arm before the observer does.

These openings are temporary. Their edges are fresh and sharp, lined with green tissue. By dusk they close like mouths.

Warmth comes in pulses. The average air is pleasant, close to skin temperature, but every few hours a slow wave of heat moves across the chamber. It can be followed by smell: first peppery sap, then ripe fruit, then the mineral odor of rain. The walls flush dark red as it passes. Water pockets tremble. The white walkers, if present, flatten themselves against the floor. Afterward, new growth appears somewhere—one more rib, one more cord, one more impossible recess.

The place may be a bud preparing to open into a world for which the surrounding earth is only a husk. It may be the interior of a root dreaming of sunlight. It may exist beneath many landscapes at once, receiving noon from each of them and rain from none.

Those who remain too long notice plantlike changes in their own proportions. Hair turns toward the unseen light. Fingernails become translucent and show branching green currents beneath. Sleepers wake with the taste of soil and memories of standing motionless under an open sky.

Yet the chamber does not feel hostile. It is merely active at a scale and tempo that make hospitality irrelevant. It shelters because shelter is one of its incidental shapes. It gives water because water must cross it. It offers tools because growth sometimes produces edges.

Eventually, without warning, the entire hollow contracts.

The apparent ceiling descends. The walls draw inward. Root-cords snap taut with deafening notes. Every shallow pool lifts from the floor in a trembling sheet and rises into the membranes. For several breaths the cavern becomes no larger than a clenched room, warm and dark and full of the odor of crushed leaves.

Then it expands again.

The light returns first. After it, the emptiness.

But the chamber is never exactly the same shape twice.
