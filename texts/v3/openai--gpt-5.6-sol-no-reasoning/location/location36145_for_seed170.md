The high country begins above the last breathable dust.

It is not mountainous in any familiar sense. There are no folded ridges, no volcanic cones, no valleys cut by vanished rivers. The land stands high because the whole region has been lifted into a broad mineral shelf: a continent-sized table of pale salts, iron ceramics, and black, blistered glass. Its edge lies beyond sight. The surface rises and falls in long increments, seldom steep enough to notice while crossing, yet enough to leave distant horizons stacked one above another like misaligned sheets.

There is no water here. Not hidden underground, not locked in frost, not remembered in dry channels. The chemistry of the plateau has never required it. Heat passes through the stone with a metallic clarity. At noon, the lighter flats shine like old bone; the dark outcrops soften at their edges and give off wavering skins of refraction. Hollows accumulate not moisture but heavy gases—clear, warm, nearly motionless lakes distinguishable only where the horizon behind them bends.

The terrain is ancient and thoroughly weathered. Wind has worked it for eras, though the atmosphere is too thin and steady to raise much dust. Erosion proceeds grain by grain. Exposed stone has been combed into ribs, fluted into parallel grooves, and rounded into low forms that resemble machinery left beneath a sandblaster for ten million years. Nothing looks newly broken. Even fractures have polished lips. Even impact pits have sagged into shallow, patient bowls.

From a distance the plateau seems empty.

It is not.

The first sign is sound.

A continuous industrial roar inhabits the air: not one engine, but the blended output of innumerable engines too far away to separate. Beneath it runs a regular hammering, slow and immense. There are turbine whines at the upper edge of hearing, electrical crackles without visible lightning, and long descending notes like freight trains braking somewhere below the crust. Because the air is thin, these sounds should not carry. They do. At times they arrive from the wrong direction. A thunderous stamping may seem to issue from the open sky; a shriek first heard ahead may pass through the listener and continue beneath the ground without changing volume.

There are no factories, no towers, no roads, no built things of any kind. The industrial noise comes from the fauna.

Across the flats move the mills.

Each is a colony, or an animal, or an event temporarily persuaded to possess a body. The smallest are the size of carts. The largest occupy several square kilometers. They consist of rotating mineral organs arranged around a hollow center: vanes, drums, toothed rings, pistons, flywheels, and hot translucent bladders full of reactive gas. None of these components have been manufactured. They grow by collecting dust and sorting it crystal by crystal, laying down bearings, insulating plates, conductive whiskers, and interlocking cogs. Their apparent steel is a secretion of iron and carbon. Their ceramic housings are shell. The cables trailing behind them are nerves.

They travel very slowly. A large mill may cross a handspan of ground in a day. Yet the plateau is covered with them so completely that slowness becomes overwhelming. Everywhere on the horizon something turns. Vast herds overlap at different scales: wheel-bodied grazers rasping oxide films from stone; tripod furnaces testing the ground with incandescent feet; flat, many-segmented things that advance like conveyor belts; tall latticed animals whose revolving heads harvest static charge from the sky.

Their abundance is difficult to perceive because of the distances between them. The country remains visually open. A creature may stand alone in ten kilometers of white plain, but there are tens of thousands of such plains, and each has its solitary occupant. When viewed from a high escarpment, the emptiness resolves into migration: scattered black points moving in broad, centuries-long streams.

They do not feed on vegetation. Nothing grows here in the sense of root, leaf, or spore. The mills consume gradients. They graze differences in heat, pressure, charge, magnetism, and probability. Where the sun has warmed one face of a boulder more than another, tiny gear-mites gather along the boundary and click until both sides are equal. Where heavy gas pools in a basin, bellows-bodied herds descend to inhale it, separating its components and exhaling them on opposite ridges. Needle swarms drink voltage from the air. Burrowing rotors follow stresses through the rock and leave relaxed stone behind them.

Thus the land is old not despite its inhabitants, but because of them. They have worn it into equilibrium.

No slope remains steeper than the mills can use. No deposit remains richer than their mouths can refine. No sharp thermal contrast survives for long. The plateau is a landscape endlessly processed, its resources not exhausted but redistributed into a state of exquisite poverty. The fauna manufacture nothing except themselves, and even themselves only provisionally. When one dies, smaller kinds disassemble it. Bearings migrate into jaws. Plates become armor. The final dust is sorted and returned to the plain in bands so fine they appear painted.

At close range, their animal nature becomes unmistakable. Pistons flinch from shadows. Gear trains stutter when alarmed. Exhaust stacks turn toward unfamiliar vibrations. Some species court by synchronizing their internal cycles, two massive bodies gradually bringing every wheel and valve into phase until they produce a single deafening tone. Others fight by stealing momentum: they interlock rims and remain nearly motionless for years, each slowing the other by imperceptible degrees. The victor departs carrying both creatures’ accumulated rotation. The loser cools, surrounded by scavengers.

Above them hangs an ordinary-looking sky.

Its color is a washed, high-altitude blue fading to ivory at the horizon. The sun is neither monstrous nor dim. Shadows are sharp but plausible. This normality makes the failures of distance more troubling.

The horizon is not stable. Features sometimes rise above it before they have approached. A mill seen ten kilometers away may be the rear portion of one standing behind the observer. Tracks converge upon the animals that made them, then continue ahead as though remembering future passage. On certain flats, shadows arrive hours before the bodies casting them. They crawl over the salt, wait, and are eventually filled.

Space becomes least dependable near the great feeding grounds.

These are regions where reality itself has developed a gradient. Nothing marks their boundaries except the density of animals and a deep, rhythmic concussion in the bones. Here vast circular herds gather around distortions too subtle for the eye: places where left and right differ in cost, where elapsed time has a faint flavor, where an object is marginally more likely to exist on one side than the other. The mills feed by equalizing these disparities. Their rotating organs disappear in sections, returning polished or multiplied. Some drive long proboscises into empty air and pump. Nothing visible flows, but their bodies brighten with heat.

Around such gatherings, perspective breaks into industrial geometries. Parallel lines cross. The underside of the ground may be glimpsed between two stones. Distant creatures appear as diagrams of their own internal workings, every chamber visible without the outer shell becoming transparent. Sound repeats before occurring. The plateau sometimes reveals a second version of itself several meters above the first, crowded with the same herds in slightly different positions. For days the two surfaces may coexist, grinding slowly through one another without collision, until a migration aligns them again.

The fauna seem adapted not merely to these faults but to the task of maintaining them. Without the herds, differences might sharpen. A small inconsistency could deepen into an edge between incompatible worlds. The mills graze those edges down, just as they graze heat from stone. Their endless mechanical thunder is the sound of the region remaining singular.

Or mostly singular.

There are predators.

They are rare enough that a traveler might at first mistake the absence around them for true emptiness. A predator has no fixed machinery. It resembles a moving omission—a region within which the common mills cease to have ever existed. Its passage is visible in the sudden smoothness of the ground and audible as a hole traveling through the general roar. Herds detect it decades in advance. They begin turning, one fraction of a degree at a time, until whole migration streams have bent away.

When cornered, the mills do not flee. They assemble.

Thousands lock their outer structures together and form complexes resembling refinery cities: towers of bodies, braided transmission organs, chimneys breathing incandescent gas. These are the only cities on the plateau, and none are constructed. They rise over generations from coordinated fear. Their combined engines produce a note that can split stone far beyond the visible horizon. Sometimes the omission turns aside. Sometimes the city becomes a clean patch of untouched-looking land, and all evidence that anything stood there vanishes except for echoes, which may continue for centuries.

Heat governs the broadest rhythms. During the long day, the plateau expands. Hairline gaps open between mineral plates, and small fauna emerge in overwhelming numbers: chrome scales, screw-limbed crawlers, swarms of hinged foil that flash in the sunlight. They cover the ground without making it look occupied, because each reflects the terrain perfectly until it moves. At dusk they fold themselves into cracks. The large mills stop harvesting thermal differences and begin venting what they stored. Their bodies glow dull red across the plains.

Night is not cold, only less hot. The sky darkens, and the plateau answers with scattered furnace light. From an escarpment, one can see thousands of animals marked by ports, seams, and slow internal fires. Their spacing suggests constellations laid across the earth. Some lights belong to creatures beyond the geometric horizon. Others may belong to no present creature at all.

The noise never ends. It changes shift.

The high whines fade after sunset. Bass engines deepen. Courtship sirens travel between remote basins. Beneath everything, the slow hammer continues: one blow every several minutes, regular enough to become part of silence. Its source has never been located. Some creatures orient toward it. Some migrate away. With each impact, loose grains leap a finger’s width from the ground and hang there slightly too long before falling.

Nothing here welcomes an observer, but little is actively hostile. The greater danger is being interpreted as a gradient. Warmth against cooler air, chemical richness against depleted stone, the organized electrical activity of a nervous system—each is food. Small fauna gather first. They align along shadows and seams. They do not bite. They equalize.

Given time, a foreign body would be cooled, discharged, chemically sorted, and gently dispersed across several kilometers in layers of exceptional purity. The process might take months. It would resemble erosion more than attack.

That is the governing rule of the plateau: difference attracts life, and life abolishes difference.

Yet complete equilibrium is never reached. The sun rises unevenly. The ground strains. The world misremembers its dimensions. Predators make absences; absences create edges; edges feed mills. Across the broad high shelf, under an almost reasonable sky, the innumerable engines continue their patient husbandry.

From afar it remains an empty desert.

Up close, every grain is attended.
