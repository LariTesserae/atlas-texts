The basin is low enough that weather settles into it.

Cloud presses close over a plain of fabricated stone, and the plain shines blackly under a film of recent rain. There is no native ground here. No soil, no bedrock, no buried seam untouched by manufacture. From rim to rim—far beyond what the darkness allows the eye to confirm—the land is an assembly of cast slabs, clinker, vitrified aggregate, ceramic armor, and iron foundations driven through older iron foundations. What resembles geology is only industry made ancient.

The surface has been eroding for so long that its original purposes are illegible.

Roads have broadened into shallow valleys. Walls have declined into ridges no higher than a person’s knee. Towers survive only as circles of corroded bolts around rain-filled depressions. Repeated abrasion has rounded every exposed corner, not softly but conclusively, as if the basin has been filing itself toward a final shape for millions of cycles. The oldest concrete has lost its cement and become beaches of pale manufactured gravel. Sheets of steel have weathered into laminated red-black scales. Glass has been ground to a dim powder that gathers in drains and gives the runoff a faint internal gleam.

Nothing here is ruined in the dramatic sense. Nothing has recently fallen. The place passed through collapse so long ago that collapse itself has become a stable terrain.

Across that terrain stand the surviving works.

They are widely spaced: pump houses like blunt islands, ventilation pylons, low switching stations, enormous horizontal sheds whose roofs merge with the wet horizon. Between them extend kilometers of open platform. Their lamps are few and weak—amber pinpricks behind armored panes, green bars reflected in puddles, occasional blue discharges deep inside fenced compounds. Most illumination comes indirectly, from electrical arcs hidden behind structures or from the clouds turning momentarily gray when some distant furnace opens.

Then the darkness closes again.

The sound does not.

It occupies the basin as completely as air. A low mechanical roar rises through the slabs and enters the body through the feet. Over it move harsher layers: turbine whine, hammering relays, the periodic concussion of pressure gates, chains dragging inside unseen housings, fans with blades the size of buildings chopping the atmosphere into pulses. At irregular intervals, something beneath the plain performs a vast, slow stroke. The whole fabricated landscape answers with a boom too deep to hear cleanly. Water jumps in the puddles. Loose flakes of oxide creep a fraction of a centimeter toward the nearest seam.

There are no voices among these sounds, and no animal calls. No wings beat around the lamps. No rats inhabit the ducts. No pale fish turn in the sumps. The machines generate all motion and nearly all noise, but they do not imitate life. Their activity has the impersonal continuity of weather.

Water is frequent without being abundant. It arrives as drizzle, condensation, seepage, and sudden industrial rain.

Moisture beads on every cold surface. It runs down riveted housings in straight mineral lines and hangs beneath pipes until vibration shakes it loose. The plain is subtly graded, so runoff moves everywhere in broad, shallow sheets, often only a few millimeters deep. In lower tracts these sheets collect into marshlike expanses interrupted by rows of old foundations. Their water is dark, iron-tasting, and warm in places where buried conduits still carry heat. Elsewhere it films over with fragile skins of mineral salts. When a pressure valve opens miles away, the skins shiver and split into polygonal rafts.

Some rain rises from below.

Cooling trenches exhale vapor through grates, and the vapor condenses under the low cloud deck. For hours it accumulates overhead, thickening the dark. Then it returns in large cold drops, falling hardest over the very machinery that made it. Thus the basin possesses a closed and artificial weather cycle: seep, steam, cloud, rain, drain. Little escapes. Even storms appear to have been manufactured and used many times.

The sparse vegetation survives in the margins of this cycle. Black rushes grow from cracks packed with airborne grit. Their stems are hollow, metallic-looking, and rarely taller than the bolts beside them. Colorless moss forms thin crescents beneath leaking joints. In sheltered gutters, wiry mats spread across old insulation, fed by condensation and trace compounds washed from the roofs. There are no meadows, no thickets, no progression toward forest. Each patch is isolated from the next by long distances of bare plate.

These plants do not flower. Nothing would carry pollen if they did.

They reproduce when the machinery permits it. The rushes release dustlike spores in response to subsonic vibration; the moss fragments under heavy rain and travels through the drainage network. A colony may vanish when a pipe cools and reappear years later at an outlet ten kilometers away. Its ecology is mapped not by sunlight or season but by pressure schedules, corrosion rates, and the switching of buried power.

The cold is moderate but penetrating. It comes from evaporation, from wet metal, from the immense volume of unheated structure. Breath clouds in the open tracts. Handrails draw warmth out of anything that touches them. Yet shafts of anomalous heat cross the plain: warm gutters, steaming seams, doors around which the rain dries before landing. These gradients shift according to operations no observer can easily predict.

The machines are not derelict. They are functioning, though function has become difficult to distinguish from habit.

In one sector, gantries travel endlessly between empty loading bays. Their wheels scream on tracks worn almost flat. Each gantry lowers a hook into a rectangular aperture, waits, raises nothing, and proceeds to the next. In another, conveyors carry identical blocks of compacted rust beneath the plain. The blocks emerge wet and intact at a distant station, where crushers reduce them to powder. Pneumatic lines transport the powder back underground. There it is pressed into blocks again.

Elsewhere, pumps labor to move water uphill by a meter, after which it spreads over a spillway and returns through porous pavement to the same reservoir. Furnaces melt salvaged alloys into ingots. Stamping halls punch the ingots into perforated sheets. Corrosion galleries expose the sheets to salt vapor until they crumble. Vacuum systems collect the residue for remelting.

Not every process is circular. Some machines still seem to accomplish a real transfer: heat migrates toward the northern works; clean water disappears beneath sealed districts; electricity arrives in violent surges from beyond the rim. But the network is too old and too interconnected for purpose to remain local. Every useful action may be only one phase of a larger futility. Every apparent waste may be feeding an operation on the other side of the basin.

The architecture assumes maintainers who never arrive.

Walkways remain cleared by sweeping arms. Doors unlock when approached, though there is no one to approach them. Indicator panels present rows of patient symbols. Some have been polished concave by centuries of automatic cleaning. Emergency beacons rotate over empty muster courts. Speakers issue warnings in tones whose intervals have outlived their language. The alarms are almost continuous, but the machinery treats alarm as one more ordinary state.

There are rules here, and they are mechanical rather than moral.

Open water must flow toward a numbered drain, even if the numbers have worn away.

Any standing structure must shed its rust onto the western side.

Heat may cross beneath a road but never above it.

No gate closes until the next gate has begun to open.

Every moving component must announce itself, which may explain the deafening noise.

Nothing organic may accumulate beyond a certain mass.

The last rule is enforced most visibly. At intervals, narrow inspection vehicles emerge from recessed depots and traverse the open plain on many small wheels. They are low, blind-looking machines with combs and sampling needles. They stop at moss patches, remove a measured portion, seal it in metal cartridges, and return underground. They ignore solitary stems. They ignore spores. They permit life only as a thin discontinuous stain.

No animal life remains, perhaps because the sound prevents navigation, perhaps because the water lacks something essential, perhaps because the inspection system once had broader definitions. The absence is absolute enough to become palpable. Every pool invites the expectation of a ripple that never comes. Every overhead cable looks prepared to bear birds and bears none. In the vast sheds, there is not even the dry ticking of insects.

The basin’s strangest feature appears only during a drainage reversal.

Without warning, the pumps alter pitch. The change travels across the region like a front, station by station, until the roar has shifted into a lower key. Water hesitates on the slabs. Runnels stop. Then every puddle begins to drain upward.

Droplets climb corroded walls, gathering as they rise. Curtains of water pull themselves from sumps and ascend toward roof gutters. Marsh sheets contract into silver cords that crawl up pylons and vanish into high pipes. The rising water reveals the shallow topography below: embossed arrows, enormous serial numbers, outlines of dismantled structures, and concentric machining marks hundreds of meters across.

For several minutes the plain can be read as a single component.

Its valleys are channels. Its ridges are braces. The distant sheds are housings fitted to its edge. Even the basin rim has the smooth repeated teeth of an immense gear, nearly erased by age. What seemed to be a constructed landscape is not a landscape covered in machinery, nor even a landscape assembled from machinery. It is the exposed face of one machine, broad enough to possess weather.

The revelation never lasts. The upward rain disappears into the clouds. Pressure gates slam with horizon-wide concussions. Water falls again, hiding the marks beneath reflective darkness. The terrain returns to seeming regional, accidental, almost natural.

And all through the cool artificial night, the ancient works continue refining themselves toward a state they may already have reached: flatter, rounder, emptier, more stable. Material circulates. Water circulates. Heat, rust, and alarms circulate. Sparse life waits in the seams for favorable vibration.

Nothing is born. Nothing quite ends.

The whole basin wears away, and the wearing-away is the work.
