The city stands on a high plateau beneath a lid of hot, dark cloud.

From a distance it looks dense: ranks of towers, elevated conduits, retaining walls, chimneys, reservoirs, and bridges extending across the whole breadth of a shallow mountain basin. Nearer, the impression reverses. The structures are shells separated by enormous intervals. Streets made for processions of machinery cross one another without traffic. Arcades run for kilometers and contain only heat. A single pumping station may occupy a district; a single staircase may climb the outside of a tower broad enough to have its own weather. The city is urban in organization but nearly void in occupation, as if civic planning had been applied not to people but to pressures, liquids, and distances.

Everything is made from dark mineral materials: poured basalt, iron ceramic, smoked glass, slabs of pumice sealed with metal, and old copper alloys gone black rather than green. There is no vegetation. No weeds split the paving, no moss softens the cistern rims. Where something fibrous hangs from a wall, it proves to be a mineral deposit—long whiskers of salt, piezoelectric crystal, or glass drawn hair-thin by repeated heating. The ground itself seems unfinished by life. It has the severe, chemically articulate appearance of a planet before roots.

Water is everywhere, though seldom in abundance. The cloud ceiling condenses on the upper faces of the towers. Droplets gather along cables, rails, and cornices, then fall through hundreds of meters of warm air. They strike the lower plazas with isolated, heavy clicks. Black walls continually weep. Pavements shine beneath films so thin they reveal every abrasion in the stone. In recessed courtyards, shallow pools lie motionless except where gas rises through them in slow hemispheres.

Nothing here is cool. The rain arrives hot. Handrails sweat at almost the temperature of skin, and the cisterns breathe humid air through bronze grilles. Beneath the plateau, geothermal chambers warm the entire city from below. Heat comes up through seams in the paving and through vast latticed vents, carrying the smell of wet iron, sulphur, and fired clay. The upper air is oppressive rather than bright: a steaming altitude, where elevation has thinned the atmosphere without granting relief.

The city was built around a system of collecting, separating, and returning water. Roofs slope inward. Whole neighborhoods form funnels. Streets descend almost imperceptibly toward settling courts where reddish rainwater passes through graded beds of crushed stone. Old channels braid under newer channels; pipes cross abandoned railways; aqueducts disappear into buildings and emerge on the far side transformed into open cascades. Yet the volume of water never seems sufficient to justify the scale. The infrastructure handles a drizzle as though preparing for an ocean.

Perhaps it once did.

Along the northern rim, erosion has opened the city in section. A cliff cuts through foundations, subway vaults, drain galleries, and older masonry buried under later plazas. Water threads down this exposed face, entering one century’s conduit and leaving through another’s doorway. The cliff has weathered into a settled architecture of its own: scalloped basalt, rounded buttresses, rust fans, and smooth channels polished by ages of intermittent flow. Fallen buildings have not become rubble so much as geology. Their floors project from the precipice like sedimentary bands.

The plateau beyond is broad and largely empty. Low domes of volcanic glass rise from shallow mineral flats. Causeways connect the city to remote industrial enclosures, each one several kilometers from the next. At night—or what passes for night under the permanent cloud—small amber lamps wake along these roads in sequence. No vehicle follows them. The illumination travels outward by itself until the horizon briefly describes the full extent of the network, then goes dark in reverse order.

The machinery still works, though not continuously and perhaps not correctly.

Deep pumps start without warning. Their vibration passes through the paving before their sound arrives, a pressure in the feet and teeth. Somewhere inside a sealed factory, belts engage, flywheels turn, and enormous shutters open to admit moist air. Condensers knock softly. Valves exhale. A foundry tower may glow dull red behind wet glass for an hour, then cool into invisibility. These events are distributed so widely that the city remains mostly quiet. Each mechanism seems alone with its task.

Silence here is not the absence of activity but the space between activities. One hears an individual drop strike a distant sheet of metal. The slow contraction of cooling beams. Water moving inside walls. Occasionally a deep metallic report crosses the basin, followed minutes later by a second sound from underground, as though the first had fallen through the city and struck its buried counterpart.

There are few animals. Pale, jointed things sometimes emerge from condensation drains at dusk. They have translucent shells, no visible mouths, and move only from one wet shadow to another. Above the thermal towers, small black fliers circle in the updrafts, but they may be scraps of manufactured membrane released by deteriorating filters. They never call, never land where they can be observed, and occasionally fold themselves into geometric shapes before vanishing into the cloud.

In the lowest reservoirs lives something less easily classified. Fine metallic threads gather wherever hot water and electrical current meet. At first they resemble precipitated copper or iron bacteria, but over years they arrange themselves into branching meshes that bridge pipes, interrupt gauges, and open valves. Workers—if there ever were workers—appear to have accommodated them. Later construction includes narrow porcelain channels leading from reservoirs to control housings, almost as if the city learned to route this mineral growth as part of its circuitry. The meshes withdraw when exposed to strong light. This may explain why illumination is kept dim.

The strangest district lies at the basin’s center. There, a circular plaza several kilometers across has no buildings at all. Its surface is a single plane of dark stone, shallowly concave and always wet. Rain and condensation should drain toward the middle, but droplets behave differently on it. They travel uphill in thin, separate lines, choosing routes that resemble streets. At the rim, each line enters a groove cut into the paving and is conducted into the city.

In heavy weather, the plaza becomes legible. Silver threads sketch blocks, avenues, towers, rail yards, and reservoirs—not the city as it is, but a second plan rotated slightly beneath it. Some outlines correspond to ruins below ground. Others indicate structures that have never been found. Now and then, one of these watery buildings brightens, and somewhere in the surrounding city an engine starts.

The local rules seem to be made of gradients. Water flows toward need rather than depth. Heat accumulates in vacant rooms. Echoes travel downward. Metal remembers repeated pressures and slowly bends toward them. Empty buildings remain stable, while occupied spaces—on the rare occasions anything enters them—begin to corrode rapidly, as if presence itself were chemically aggressive.

For this reason, perhaps, the city has preserved its emptiness.

Across the high basin, the dimness never wholly lifts. Cloud hangs close enough to erase the upper stories, and towers vanish into brown-grey vapor while their foundations gleam with warm rain. The far districts appear only when lightning spreads silently within the cloud: terraces, cranes, cooling stacks, arches over nothing, all extending farther than expected. Then darkness closes again, and the city returns to droplets, heat, black masonry, and solitary machines carrying out ancient municipal decisions.

It does not feel abandoned. Abandonment implies that its intended inhabitants have left.

This place gives the more unsettling impression that the inhabitants were only ever temporary—that the true city is the circulation itself: hot water climbing through stone, currents passing through mineral threads, lamps signaling across empty distances, and buildings serving as the fixed organs of a slow, dispersed intelligence. Its thoughts take the form of weather. Its memory is erosion. Its speech is the opening and closing of valves.

Above it, the cloud condenses.

Below it, the plateau burns.

And between those two inexhaustible conditions, the city continues.
