Below the dead surface lies a forest that has never known rain.

It occupies a broad subsidence basin beneath the salt crust, neither cave nor buried valley but a continent-sized blister in the planet’s skin. The ceiling hangs low at its margins and lifts toward the interior until whole hills could stand upright beneath it. Yet there are no hills in the geological sense. Almost every rise, wall, shelf, and distant tower is alive.

The air is warm, dusty, and aggressively dry. It smells of pepper, hot bark, spores, animal musk, and the faint metallic tang released when roots grind stone. Breathing without a veil soon rasps the throat. There is almost no free water here—not in pools, mist, sap, or blood. The life below survives on a different solvent: a clear amber oil synthesized from sulfur, carbon, and salts. It moves sluggishly through vascular tubes and burns with a smokeless blue flame when exposed to certain mineral powders. Leaves are waxy or plated. Flesh tears in fibers rather than spilling blood. Nothing rots wetly; the dead soften into fragrant grease, then crumble.

The forest begins at the ceiling.

Great inverted plants anchor themselves in cracks overhead, their trunks descending in braided columns. Their crowns spread across the ground and lock together, forming the basin’s floor from interwoven limbs. Between these two layers—the hanging trunks and the living floor—grow curtains, ladders, hoops, combs, lattices, swollen sacs, and forests of vibrating filament. Some organisms resemble familiar vegetation only briefly. Seedlings put down roots, but mature forms pull themselves free and migrate by contracting belts of woody muscle. Flowers open into rooms. Bark peels itself into gliding sheets to carry spores. Pale stalks braid together for a season, building hollow towers, then unplait and wander away.

The colors are not lush greens. Chlorophyll is of little use so far below the surface. The vegetation is black-violet, ocher, bone-white, copper, and glossy red. Photosynthetic tissues cluster around shafts of ordinary daylight admitted through translucent salt chimneys in the ceiling. These shafts descend as broad gold pillars, bright enough to create day and shadow but strangely cool to the eye. Whole ecologies orbit them. Crown-discs tilt and jostle for position; mirror-leaves pass light inward from plant to plant; reflective beetles are seized and aimed like living lenses.

Farther from the light wells, vegetation feeds on mineral heat and subterranean gases. Root fans pry open fresh fissures. Pale nodules digest limestone. Orange mats metabolize sulfur. The forest does not merely inhabit the hollow—it is excavating it.

Because the basin is young, the landscape has not settled into any lasting form. Its “erosion” is only beginning and is mostly biological. There are no rounded stones or ancient gullies. Surfaces are raw, sharp, freshly split. Root wedges crack flakes from the ceiling with reports like cannon fire. Burrowing colonies rasp the floor into powder. Falling debris is caught by foliage, wrapped in fibers, dissolved, and rebuilt as living buttresses. New ravines open overnight when thousands of root mouths migrate along a fault. By the following week, the ravine may have become a ribbed tunnel, a nest chamber, or the digestive trench of some organism too large to recognize as an animal.

Nothing here is quiet.

The sound is continuous and nearly unbearable: chittering, sawing, barking, wing thunder, resonant booming, the clack of seedpods, and the fibrous groan of plants bending against one another. The living floor drums underfoot as herds pass through its hollow branches. Ceiling groves discharge seeds with explosive cracks. Membrane-winged animals crowd the air in such numbers that their flight becomes weather, warm gusts beating first one way and then another. Long periods of near-rhythm arise by accident—millions of jaws clicking, stalks knocking, bladders pulsing—only to collapse into fresh chaos.

Some sounds are structural. Vast trumpet roots breathe through the hollow, equalizing pressure between deeper chambers. Their notes are too low to hear cleanly, but they shake dust from bark and make teeth ache. Other plants answer at higher pitches. At times the entire forest seems to be calling to itself across the basin, though whether this is communication or merely the noise of growth is uncertain.

Animals overwhelm every available surface. They graze, bore, pollinate, parasitize, hunt, prune, carry, and cultivate.

There are thumb-sized browsers plated in translucent shell, moving over trunks in solid streams. There are hot-bodied runners with six jointed legs and crownlike jaws, capable of cutting a path through dense fiber in minutes. Flat, circular creatures cling to leaves and rotate slowly, scraping wax into their central mouths. Kite-shaped predators drift between the hanging columns, steering with tails of stiff hair. Blind things the size of cattle push through the living floor, visible only as traveling bulges surrounded by panicked swarms.

None contains much liquid. Their organs are lubricated by resin and powdered graphite. Their muscles are bundles of electroactive fibers, closer to twisted roots than meat. Many breathe only intermittently, storing gas in rigid internal chambers. When killed, some collapse like baskets. Others ignite from the heat of their own metabolism and are rapidly dismantled by fire-following scavengers.

The distinction between animal and plant is unreliable. A tree may uproot itself during the dark cycle and hunt. A flying creature may settle permanently after mating, drive tendrils into the ground, and become a grove. Vast sedentary grazers carry forests on their backs; when they move, apparent landmarks migrate with them. Pollinators exchange not only reproductive material but scraps of memory encoded in aromatic resins. Colonies can assemble temporary bodies—legs, jaws, sensory fans—and dissolve them when the task is finished.

Turbulence governs everything. Growth comes in waves. One district may be choked by white fungal-looking fans at dawn, stripped to polished branchwork by noon, and buried beneath nesting husks by evening. Migratory fronts roll through like storms. Predators follow grazers; explosive flowers follow predators; scavenging vines follow the flowers. The floor buckles, walls separate, bridges grow, and passages seal without warning. Maps remain useful for days at most.

At the center of the basin, where several daylight shafts overlap, the competition becomes architectural. Vegetation piles upward into a sprawling city of living terraces. Branch avenues twist between immense seed vaults. Ribbed towers lean toward the light. Suspended gardens are not cultivated ornaments but predatory tangles that periodically lower themselves into the crowds below. Every balcony flexes. Every staircase is trying to become something else.

Scattered through this growth are signs of tool-using inhabitants.

Their shelters are small by comparison: huts woven inside dormant seed cases, windbreaks of stacked shell, platforms lashed to the least mobile trunks. Nothing permanent can survive, so construction is opportunistic. A village may consist of twenty hanging baskets attached to a migrating grove. When the grove begins to walk, the village walks with it.

The inhabitants use chipped salt-glass, bone wedges, braided fiber, ceramic made from mineral dust and plant oil, and occasional nuggets of soft native metal hammered cold. Their most important tools are tuning mallets and hooked pruning knives. They navigate by sound, striking trunks and listening to the replies. They know which booming roots will open a route when tapped, which bridges recoil from certain rhythms, and which flowers can be lulled closed.

Fire is rare and dangerous. Water is mythical. To these people, a wet object would be as uncanny as a living shadow.

They harvest amber sap only from sacrificial vessels because spilled sap attracts feeding swarms within moments. They wear layered veils against dust and wing-strikes, with ceramic ear cups strapped tightly over the skull. Speech is conducted at arm’s length through touch: fingers pressed to forearms, coded taps on shoulder plates, patterned pulls on braided cords. Spoken language exists chiefly for ritual, shouted into resonant chambers where voices are amplified beyond individuality.

Their ruins are difficult to distinguish from nests. A row of drilled shells may be an abandoned workshop, a musical notation, or warning markers around a carnivorous root. Stone carvings are almost unknown because exposed stone is rapidly eaten. History is kept in cultivated knots—slow-growing cords trained into sequences of swellings. Entire genealogies hang from village frames, alive and still extending.

There is no stable ground beneath this world. The woven floor covers deeper voids, and sometimes it tears.

When that happens, whole districts sag into blackness amid a roar of snapping fibers. Animals pour away from the breach. Plants bend toward it. Within hours, root cables shoot across the opening. Within days, a new membrane forms, thin and translucent, revealing vast bodies moving beneath. The collapse is not treated as disaster alone. Fresh space means fresh surfaces, exposed gases, and a frenzy of birth.

The forest’s apparent purpose, if it has one, is downward expansion. Every species participates, willingly or not, in opening the planet. Roots fracture the mineral. Grazers trim the roots. Hunters regulate the grazers. Burrowers remove the rubble. Builders span the cavities. The warm hollow grows by increments: a crack, a chamber, a valley. Life is making room for more life.

Above, the surface remains a silent salt field under a hard sun. It gives no hint of the deafening abundance immediately beneath it, except where the crust trembles or exhales a column of golden dust.

Below, the land is still being born.

It is born loudly, without water, and faster than anyone can name it.
