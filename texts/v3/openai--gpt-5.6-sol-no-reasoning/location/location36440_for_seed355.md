The city lies low in a basin of black water.

It is difficult to say whether it was built there or whether the basin slowly learned the shape of a city. Streets divide the water into long, rectilinear reaches. Buildings rise from broad mudflats on foundations of salt, clay, and a pale porous stone that sweats continuously in the heat. No tool marks show on the walls. There are arches, courtyards, stairs, cistern mouths, and towers with apertures for windows, but every surface has the patient rounding of something formed by erosion. Corners slump into curves. Doorways narrow toward the bottom like channels cut by retreating tides. Roofs sag beneath the weight of invisible rain.

Nothing here is new, and nothing is ruined. Weathering is the city’s finished condition.

The heat does not come from light. The sky is almost wholly dark: a low vault the color of wet soot, without sun, moon, or distinguishable cloud. Yet the air presses against the skin like breath from an opened kiln. Moisture condenses everywhere. It pearls on the undersides of balconies, collects along empty window ledges, and runs in slow threads down walls already polished by ages of runoff. Flagstones are hot beneath shallow water. Mud bubbles occasionally in the plazas, releasing no smell and scarcely any sound.

There are few plants. Sparse black reeds stand in isolated rows where avenues descend into marsh. Their stems are jointless and flat, more like cuts in the air than vegetation, and they bend only when no wind is passing. In the wider squares, low mats of translucent moss cling to the coolest stone. They brighten faintly when covered by water and dim when exposed, so that a receding pool leaves behind a temporary map of dull green geometries. No flowers open. No seeds travel. There are no insects among the reeds and no worms in the warm mud.

There are no animals at all.

The absence is larger than silence. Empty niches remain for them in the arrangement of the place: narrow drinking steps descending to canals, alcoves shaped like nests, deep grooves along walls where something might once have rubbed its body. But the water holds no fish, the roofs no birds, the drains no furtive life. Even corpses are absent. The city has no bones except its architecture.

Its structures are numerous but widely separated, as if planned for a population that required immense intervals between individuals. A single house may stand alone in several acres of flooded paving. Colonnades cross open mud and arrive at blank walls. Staircases climb from canals to broad platforms on which nothing has been placed. Farther out, districts repeat through the haze: towers, low vaults, embankments, dark reservoirs. They extend far enough to imply a metropolis, yet no quarter grows dense. Emptiness has been zoned and maintained.

There are no machines, no wires, no lamps, no hinges. There may never have been hands. Doors are slabs of stone loosened from walls by water; when the canals rise, they float just enough to open. When the water falls, they settle shut again. Locks are made from roots that swell with dampness, though no parent plant can be found. Aqueducts have no gradient, but water moves through them nonetheless. It enters at both ends and disappears near the middle.

The city’s infrastructure is hydrological, instinctive, and inexplicable.

At irregular intervals, condensation thickens across the whole basin. Walls bead over; ceilings begin to weep; droplets form in the air without falling. For several minutes the city appears filled with suspended glass dust. Then every droplet moves sideways toward the nearest doorway. Water slips through chambers and passages in coherent sheets, tracing the internal plan of each building. Canals rise by a handspan. Courtyards flood. The reeds fold themselves flat beneath the surface.

Later, the water withdraws—not downhill, but toward places recently exposed. It climbs steps. It crawls in thin films over parapets. Occasionally an entire canal lifts from its bed as a single dark ribbon and hangs above the street, trembling soundlessly. Beneath it, old deposits of salt show branching patterns like maps of cities even larger than this one.

The atmosphere condenses distance as readily as moisture. A tower seen across a plaza may appear a mile away until approached, whereupon it proves too small to enter. A nearby arch can take hours to reach. Streets preserve neither length nor sequence. If one follows a flooded avenue through three intersections, the buildings behind may rearrange into a district never crossed. These changes happen without movement. Nothing slides or rises. One looks away, looks back, and discovers that the city has revised the fact of where it is.

Only erosion remains consistent.

Water rounds the same thresholds, deepens the same gutters, hollows bowls in the same courtyards. This wear records centuries of rainfall along routes that do not presently exist. Some stairways are worn by descending water on their upper faces and by ascending water underneath. In certain halls, columns have been eroded at chest height, as though an ancient river flowed horizontally through solid air. The patterns are mature and balanced. The city does not decay toward collapse; it is continually abraded toward a more exact version of itself.

Sound is rare and disproportionately clear. A drop strikes a cistern, and the note travels across whole districts. Mud releases a soft breath. Far beneath the paving, water shifts through cavities with the subdued weight of a turning sleeper. Sometimes a sequence of knocks passes from building to building, each one quieter but closer than the last. There is no source at the end.

Thunder occurs without noise. The dark sky briefly becomes deeper, and every pool dimples at once.

What illumination exists comes from wetness. Thin blue-gray gleams lie on standing water; salt seams in the architecture shine weakly after a condensation event. The moss gives off its submerged green. These sources reveal edges but never distances. A wall may glow along its base and vanish above the height of a knee. Towers are inferred from the paths of descending droplets. Bridges appear as interruptions in reflected darkness.

In the basin’s center is a broad, nearly circular plaza, dry except for a single pool. The paving slopes away from it in every direction, yet all the city’s water seems eventually to pass through this shallow depression. The pool reflects no sky. It reflects streets.

Looking into it, one sees avenues from overhead: canals crossing plazas, stairs descending between pale walls, isolated towers sweating in the heat. The view is not fixed. Districts fill and empty; dark water advances through them; buildings slowly alter their placement. At times the reflected city closely resembles the surrounding one. At other times it has no shared landmarks.

The pool is only ankle-deep. Its bottom cannot be touched.

Around it, the stone is worn into thousands of long, parallel depressions. They resemble footprints until examined closely. Each begins broad, tapers forward, and continues as a narrow channel into the pool. They may have been made by visitors, by runoff, or by the city repeatedly approaching its own center.

Beyond the outer embankments, the basin becomes marsh without becoming wild. The avenues continue beneath warm, waist-deep water. Foundation walls protrude at regular intervals, enclosing empty plots where no building has yet appeared—or where buildings have been removed from history rather than from space. Black reeds become slightly more common there, though never thick enough to conceal the horizon. The ground is soft, salt-veined, and shaped by long-established currents. No waves cross the flooded plain. Instead, the surface rises and lowers in separate geometric sections, as though unseen rooms beneath it are breathing.

Nothing lives, but the landscape is active.

Water circulates. Stone perspires. Salt grows overnight into delicate arcades and dissolves before they can be called ruins. Streets exchange destinations. Rooms deepen. The marsh enters the city by one gate and leaves through another gate that did not exist before. Every process is slow, continuous, and complete without witness.

In this place, construction and erosion are the same force viewed at different moments. A wall worn thin does not disappear; it becomes a passage. A passage widened by water becomes a plaza. A plaza filled with sediment rises into a house. The city builds itself by losing material.

And always the heat persists beneath the darkness, ripening the wet stone, drawing water into the air, pressing the air back into water. The basin has settled into this cycle so thoroughly that it feels less like a location than a law:

everything flows,

nothing departs,

and every empty space is gradually shaped into another kind of emptiness.
