At the summit of a newly risen volcanic island, where the air is too thin for ordinary breath and the stone still remembers being liquid, there is a crack no wider than a hand.

Inside it is a country.

The opening lies between two blades of black glass. Rain gathers there, not as a stream but as a trembling lens held by surface tension. From above, the fissure appears shallow. One sees wet walls, orange mineral crusts, perhaps a few pale things moving below. But an eye placed close to the opening cannot find the bottom. Distance behaves differently past the first inch. The crack expands inward while remaining narrow outside, descending through heights that cannot be reconciled with the thickness of the mountain.

It is an abyss in miniature: a hot, wet, vertical wilderness folded into a seam of cooling rock.

The walls are fresh. They have not had time to soften into soil, nor to crumble into slopes. They rise as vitreous ridges, hooks, needles, and translucent shelves, their edges sharp enough to divide droplets without breaking them. Basalt the color of bruised iron alternates with bands of green glass and red-black slag. Sulfur blooms in yellow fans, but nothing here is truly floral. There are no roots, no mosses, no lichens, no leaves. Wherever something seems to grow, it proves to be a mineral secretion, a bacterial film too thin to see directly, or an animal holding perfectly still.

Heat wells upward from the sealed magma below. Water enters from rain and condensation, runs down the cooler upper faces, and meets vapor ascending from the depths. Thus the cleft is perpetually wet without possessing a stable river. Its waters occur as films, suspended beads, descending veils, and brief pools cradled in imperfections in the glass. Some surfaces weep continuously. Others become wet only when watched from one angle. A drop may fall for several minutes past stacked ledges, vanish into steam, and later reappear above its point of origin, slightly saltier than before.

The uppermost reaches are merely hot. Deeper in, heat acquires weight. The air presses against the walls in transparent folds. Water simmers in hollows no larger than fingerprints. It does not always boil upward: in some cups the bubbles sink, dragging silver skins of vapor into the stone. At intervals, a pulse from beneath turns every droplet opaque. The entire cleft clouds with condensation, and for a few breaths nothing is visible but pearl-gray mist and the nearest black edge.

Then the heat clears it again.

The place is crowded almost beyond comprehension.

Animals occupy nearly every surface and every interval between surfaces. They replace vegetation not just in number, but in function. Long-legged grazers stand in branching colonies like pale grass. Sessile filter feeders open mineral-colored fans into the steam. Animals shaped like overlapping scales armor the walls and drink condensed water through their feet. Chains of soft transparent bodies hang from ledges, each individual gripping the next, making living nets across the abyss. In calmer pools, red pinprick swimmers form shoals so dense that the water appears to contain a second, granular liquid.

Most are minute, though size is unreliable here. A six-legged creature can walk beneath the arch of a water droplet and seem no larger than dust from outside; followed through the lens of the drop, it becomes a tall, deliberate beast crossing a convex lake. It carries plates of dark silica on its back. Along their edges cling smaller animals, and smaller ones cling to those. Entire food webs travel with it.

No greenery supports them. The base of life is chemical and predatory.

Colorless films inhabit the boundary between hot stone and water, feeding on sulfur, iron, and gradients of temperature. These films have no visible bodies, only an oily distortion and a faint whitening of the rock. Tiny raspers harvest them. Needle-mouthed runners hunt the raspers. Glass-shelled ambushers prey on the runners. Above them drift nearly weightless sacs that consume the heat shed by pursuit itself. Nothing is wasted—not breath, not motion, not the difference between one degree and the next.

The abundance should produce a roar. It does not.

The cleft is very quiet.

Water ticks against glass. Steam gives soft inward sighs. Occasionally there is the dry click of a silica shell closing or the faint tearing sound of many feet changing direction at once. The largest predators make no cry. They pass through the crowds with such delicacy that prey vanish before any alarm can spread. Even falls are silent: objects descending into the interior seem to lose their sound first, then their apparent weight, and only afterward their visibility.

One predator resembles a knot in the air. Its body is difficult to separate from the distortion caused by heat, and perhaps there is no distinction. It grazes the crowded ledges by bending the apparent position of prey until they step into its mouth. Another is a flat, obsidian-colored animal that lives flush against the wall. It does not chase. When droplets pass over it, the reflection inside each droplet shows the animal somewhere else. Small creatures flee the reflection and run directly across the real body, which opens beneath them without moving.

Deaths occur constantly, but the landscape never becomes littered. Scavengers arrive before bodies finish dying. Some dismantle flesh; others consume shape, leaving behind intact matter arranged in no recognizable anatomy. Shell fragments are carried toward the hottest cracks and melted onto the bearers’ own armor. In this way crude tools arise without construction.

A few animals use detached splinters of volcanic glass. Narrow-bodied hunters wedge them between their forelimbs and scrape feeding films from protected grooves. Others tap the rock with chips of basalt, testing for hollows in which soft prey hide. No workshop exists, no nest, no built shelter. Tools are found, carried briefly, lost, and found again. A useful shard may pass through thousands of limbs while remaining indistinguishable from the untouched debris around it.

Some shards appear older than the island.

This is one of the cleft’s quiet impossibilities. The rock itself is young: unweathered, unstable, still cracking as it cools. Yet certain loose flakes are rounded by ages of handling. Certain species bear scars from events that have not happened. In deep reflective pools, hunters can be seen waiting at ledges that have not yet fractured into existence.

The farther inward one looks, the less firmly scale holds.

A ledge across the fissure may seem close enough to touch. Reaching toward it reveals a gulf full of drifting fauna, hot rain, and distant storms. A bead of water on that ledge contains a horizon. Along it move herds of black specks beneath a white vapor sky. If the bead slips, the horizon tilts; the herds climb what becomes a vertical sea and gather at the rim before impact. When it strikes a lower shelf, the world does not necessarily end. Sometimes it divides into several smaller climates.

Extreme altitude also persists inside the mountain’s seam. The abyss has its own thin upper air, its own falling pressure, though it descends rather than rises. Creatures near the deepest visible reaches spread membrane wings to catch currents that should be lifting them, yet they continue downward toward an apparent summit: a brilliant black pinnacle inverted beneath the world. Lightning crawls soundlessly around that point, not through clouds but through the geometry of distance.

Rain falls there from below.

It rises from unseen hot reservoirs as round droplets, slows, and hangs among the ledges. Animals enter these hovering waters and swim without disturbing their surfaces. Some pools migrate in this manner, carrying entire populations upward until they contact cooler rock and collapse into ordinary runoff. During these migrations the cleft becomes denser still. Flying, crawling, swimming, and sessile creatures occupy the same finger-width of space, each oriented to a different gravity.

The walls respond to life without being alive. Where crowds gather, basalt grows warm and slightly flexible. Where a surface is abandoned, it hardens into cutting glass. New fractures therefore follow the movements of animals. Passages open under migrations and seal behind extinctions. The landscape has no architecture, yet it acquires corridors. It has no intention, yet the busiest routes become deep, smooth channels polished by bodies and water.

At the seam’s outer lip, daylight is ordinary. Clouds cross the sun. Wet glass glitters. The colors are clear: rust, sulfur yellow, black, translucent gray, the occasional electric blue of an animal’s internal fluid. There is no supernatural glow. Even the impossible things are plainly lit, casting appropriate shadows when they remember to.

Near dusk, the stone cools rapidly. Condensation thickens and pours inward. Many animals fold themselves flat; others emerge from pores too small to contain them. The living nets draw taut across the fissure, harvesting the evening flood. Predators move among them. Steam subsides. The clicks and droplets become rarer until the silence seems absolute.

Then, deep inside, something immense turns over.

There is no audible rumble. The evidence is visual: every suspended drop elongates in the same direction, pointing toward a depth that was not there moments before. The animals freeze. Reflections close their eyes. Fine cracks spread through the fresh volcanic glass and stop just short of the surface.

By morning the seam is unchanged from outside.

It is still only a wet crack between two blades of stone, high on a barren summit. No plant softens its margins. No path leads to it. Rain trembles at the entrance, and tiny pale creatures shift beneath the water.

But the crack is fractionally wider.

And from somewhere far below—or far within, where heat, height, and smallness become the same direction—a single warm droplet climbs into daylight carrying an animal that has never before been larger than a mountain.
