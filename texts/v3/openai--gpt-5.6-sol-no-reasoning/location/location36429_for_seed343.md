The country begins above the clouds.

Its mountains are not sharp. Whatever teeth they once had were worn away before the present forests took root, before the present animals acquired their shapes. The summits are broad, bowed surfaces of gray stone, rising to tremendous elevations without drama: high plains lifted into thin air, immense backs rounded by an erosion so old that it seems less like damage than completion. Ravines descend from them in long, shallow folds. Cliffs occur only where whole portions of the plateau have quietly fallen away.

From a distance, the heights appear green. Up close, there is almost no ground.

Vegetation has become the terrain’s upper substance. Layer upon layer of roots, runners, leaf-sheaths, fungal cords, and compressed stems lie over the stone, sometimes only ankle-deep, sometimes deeper than a house is tall. Walking upon it is like walking on an old roof: springy in places, solid in others, with concealed hollows capable of swallowing a person. The largest plants do not resemble trees so much as systems of architecture. Their trunks crawl horizontally along the contours for miles, fastening themselves to bare rock with pale gripping pads. From these recumbent trunks rise widely spaced towers of foliage, each one a green-black column crowned with enormous, wind-torn leaves.

Yet the country feels empty.

The plants fill nearly every available surface, but they do so in great continuous gestures. A single colony may cover an entire valley wall. One mat of silver grass can occupy a day’s journey. The immense leaf towers stand far apart, and between them the open uplands extend under a huge sky. There is little undergrowth, little clutter, no tangled profusion at human height. Life here has consolidated itself into a few vast bodies. The effect is of being alone in an inhabited world.

Water is never abundant, though almost everything is damp.

Rain arrives briefly and often sideways: warm, sparse showers driven over the plateau in gray veils. Much of it never reaches the lower layers. Leaves collect the droplets and conduct them toward deep cisterns formed where stems overlap. At dawn, condensation covers every surface. It beads on the waxed plains of leaves, trembles on hooked hairs, then runs through grooves toward hidden reservoirs. These pockets are the true springs of the heights. Some are no larger than bowls. Others lie inside the vegetation as dark ponds, sealed from the sky beneath woven roofs of living tissue.

Permanent rivers are rare. The channels across the stone are ancient and disproportionately broad, relics of a wetter age. For most of the year they hold only threads of tea-colored water winding across beds of smooth cobbles. After rain, they awaken violently. Water pours from the plant mats all at once, turning quiet channels into warm, foaming torrents for an hour or two. Then they diminish, leaving stranded leaves, drowned insects, and long rows of animals drinking from the remaining pools.

Those animals make the silence of the open country impossible.

The first sound at sunrise is a low vibration, felt through the feet before it is heard. It comes from the great leaf towers. Thousands of winged creatures have spent the night folded into their crowns. As the light warms them, they begin to flex, and the separate rustlings join into a single sustained roar. Then the colonies rise.

They are not birds, though from a distance they resemble flocks. Each has four translucent wings and a narrow, furred body, with hind legs that trail like grass stems. Millions pour from one tower, circle in the warm uplift, and stream toward another valley. Their wings strike the air with such density that conversation becomes impossible. Sunlight dims beneath them. Their shed scales fall like dust.

Other voices answer. Broad-shelled grazers bellow across the root plains. Burrowing animals drum against the hollow trunks from within. Troops of long-armed cliff dwellers beat hanging seed cases against stone, warning neighboring troops away from condensation pools. Small creatures make up the gaps: whistles, clicks, rasping choruses, dry rattles from inside flowers. Even the plants contribute. Their tall leaves are perforated by age and wind, and the air passing through them produces deep organ tones that travel from ridge to ridge.

The loudest periods come in the afternoon, when warm air climbs the escarpments. Animals launch themselves into the updrafts by the hundreds of thousands. Predators follow: pale, sheetlike things that barely move their wings, steering instead by altering the curvature of their bodies. They pass through the swarms with open mouths. Beneath them, the grazing herds migrate in dark bands across the green. The root mat flexes under their combined weight, and its buried chambers exhale through vents with booming reports.

From far below, the whole plateau can be heard before it can be seen.

The abundance of fauna is possible because nearly the entire landscape is edible, although not equally and not at all times. Animals graze leaves, tap pressurized stems, gnaw mineral-rich root bark, steal nectar, excavate fungal nodules, or consume the countless other animals doing the same. Death is processed quickly. A carcass attracts dense, rolling carpets of scavengers. By the following morning, little remains but teeth and polished plates, and even these are eventually carried away by plants that coil around them and dissolve them for salts.

There is no rich soil in the usual sense. Nutrients travel in bodies. The animals are the plateau’s moving earth.

The climate is warm despite the altitude. The air is thin but seldom cold, heated by a persistent current rising from the lowlands and by the dark vegetation itself. Nights are mild under cloud and abruptly cool under clear sky. Frost occurs only in exposed hollows, where it forms delicate white basins before sunrise. The native plants tolerate it poorly. Around these frost pockets the vegetation withdraws, leaving bare stone rings hundreds of years old.

Such openings reveal the age of the land.

The stone is smooth to the touch, its grains rounded and sealed beneath skins of ocher and violet mineral growth. Pebbles fit into old depressions as though they have rested there forever. Former waterfalls survive as enormous polished steps descending into valleys that no longer possess enough water to use them. In places, the bedrock has weathered into low domes separated by shallow grooves. Nothing points upward except life. The geology has finished making edges; the vegetation is now the only thing that builds.

Human structures are sparse and difficult to distinguish from nests.

Shelters stand at long intervals near reliable water pockets: low frameworks of bent branches stitched with rawhide and roofed in overlapping live leaves. The leaves are not cut. Builders guide their growth across the frame, tying them with fiber until the shelter closes itself. A dwelling may take years to complete, but once established it repairs its own roof and directs rainwater into a central clay-lined jar.

There are no large settlements. The land will not support stillness in groups, and the noise makes coordination difficult. Families and small bands occupy separate ridges, communicating by mirrored sunlight, smoke, and patterned banners rather than by shouting. Paths are marked with carved seed shells hung from stalks. Each shell has a distinctive shape, readable by touch in fog or darkness.

Metal is known only as an occasional cold fragment found in old streambeds. Most tools are stone, tooth, shell, or cured plant fiber. Knives are chipped from dense green rock exposed along the plateau margins. Long needles come from the mouthparts of flying grazers. Water vessels are grown: young gourds are pressed into molds and allowed to harden in place. Rope is everywhere and of extraordinary quality. Bridges, snares, roofs, sandals, ladders, armor, and memory records are all made by knotting fibers from the crawling trunks.

The largest works are the crossing lines. Where erosion has opened deep gaps in the plateau, people have trained living cables from one side to the other. The first tendrils are carried across by gliding animals baited with fruit. Once attached, they thicken over generations. A mature crossing may be broad enough for a herd, though it sways visibly in the afternoon winds and hums with nested creatures. Crossing one is less like walking on a bridge than entering a horizontal forest suspended over the clouds.

Ruins are present, but modest. Low circles of stone occur on exposed saddles. Rows of postholes survive in the rare bare ground. There are images scratched into polished rock: animals still recognizable, other animals no longer seen, and recurring diagrams of valleys filled with water. No walls stand high enough to cast a permanent shadow. Anything taller was dismantled by weather, roots, or nesting colonies long ago.

The people of the plateau observe one rule with nearly religious strictness: nothing hollow is entered without first making noise.

Hollows belong to something. A fold beneath the plant mat may contain a condensation pond, a sleeping predator, a breeding chamber, or merely a cavity under tension. Striking the surface with a staff reveals the difference. Water answers softly. Empty space booms. An occupied chamber responds in movement.

Another rule governs the leaf towers. During the morning emergence, no one stands beneath a crown. The falling scales can blind, and the animals that fail to fly drop in thick numbers, followed by predators. By noon the ground around each tower is swept almost clean by scavengers, and travelers may pass.

Weather changes the country without transforming it. Fog rises from the lower ravines and spreads across the uplands in long level sheets, leaving the highest green structures floating like islands. Short rains darken the vegetation and fill its channels. Sun follows, warm and ordinary, revealing distances: ridge beyond ridge, plateau beyond plateau, all softened by age and clothed in continuous living fabric.

At dusk, the flyers return.

They arrive against the fading light in converging rivers. The roar grows until the air itself seems granular. Towers bend beneath the accumulating weight. Grazers kneel and press themselves into the root mat. The human shelters close their leaf shutters. Predators continue hunting above the crowns, visible as pale absences among the swarms.

Then darkness comes, though not quiet.

The great plants release stored moisture through their pores. Condensation begins to patter from leaf to leaf, an indoor rain beneath a clear sky. Animals call from the reservoirs and root chambers. The plateau creaks as temperatures fall and its living surface contracts. Far off, an old river channel may still be draining the afternoon shower, its diminished water moving unseen through miles of vegetation.

Beneath all of it lies the rounded stone: extraordinarily high, extraordinarily old, worn almost to peace.

Above it, life has rebuilt every missing edge.
