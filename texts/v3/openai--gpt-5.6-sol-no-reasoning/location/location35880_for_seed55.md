The world is an ocean contained inside a work of masonry.

There is no seabed beneath it in the natural sense. Wherever the descending dark finally meets resistance, it finds fitted stone: broad flags, ramps, retaining walls, collapsed vaults, stairs worn shallow by water, and causeways laid from one dim distance to another. Beneath those are older courses, and beneath those, presumably, more. No native rock has ever been exposed. The entire basin—from its floor to the farthest enclosing walls—is constructed.

Its makers possessed no engines, no fine instruments, no imperishable alloys. They built with wedges, levers, plumb lines, braided rope, wooden cranes, and the labor of generations. Their achievement is not intricate. It is immense.

The water is cold throughout. Near the upper reaches it carries the chill of rain and low cloud; deeper down it settles into a nearly motionless body only a few degrees above freezing. There are no warm vents or violent currents. Temperature changes by fractions, over leagues. A swimmer—if there were swimmers—might descend for hours without noticing any boundary, only a gradual stiffening of the water and a pressure gathering against the body.

No swimmers remain.

There are no fish, crustaceans, worms, corals, or drifting animalcules. Nothing darts from a crack when disturbed. Nothing grazes the growth from the walls. No shells collect in corners. The absence is so complete that the vegetation has spread without teeth, burrows, or pruning.

Most of that vegetation is simple: black-green films, long ribbons of cold-water weed, pale mosses rooted in mortar, and forests of soft branching algae. It covers nearly everything within reach of the faint upper light. Parapets have become rounded hedges. Courtyards are choked with fronds that bow together under the slow passage of water. Whole façades vanish beneath layered mats, some thin as skin, others deep enough to engulf an arm.

In the darker zones, growth changes character without becoming strange. Stems broaden to catch what little light descends. Leaves become almost black, laid flat against sloping masonry. Color is largely theoretical here: green where the upper water briefly clears, brown in the common dusk, charcoal below. Colonies spread by patient contact. One patch reaches another over centuries. Spores settle in silt and wait through long cold seasons for a handspan of illumination.

The nearest thing to weather occurs within the water itself.

At intervals, a dense chill moves down from the flooded rooflands and pours over the edges of terraces. It behaves like a slow invisible fog, bending weed as it descends. Elsewhere, warmer layers drift laterally through arcades hundreds of leagues long. Fine sediment rises from one district and falls upon another. These events are rarely violent. The architecture is so large that currents take months to cross a chamber and years to circle a basin.

Above, if “above” can still be used, rain falls continually into an already drowned country. It strikes the water in a remote, all-encompassing murmur. The surface lies under a low ceiling of cloud and may itself be divided by walls, embankments, roofs, and open cisterns. Some structures emerge there: blunt towers covered in moss, drowned domes breaking the rain like dark islands, and mile-long retaining ridges on which shallow-rooted plants form sodden meadows. But there is no true dry ground. Water stands in every hollow, runs down every stair, occupies every room, and fills the air with suspended cold.

The landscape is low not because it is small but because nearly all relief has been worn away. Once there may have been steep monuments, high gates, and vertical cities. Time and water have reduced them to long gradients. Towers have become mounds. Steps are smooth inclines. Carved figures survive only as shoulders and shallow hollows. Inscriptions persist as parallel grooves that the algae preferentially fill.

Erosion has reached a mature condition. Corners have rounded to the shape the currents permit; unstable spans fell ages ago. What remains is massive, settled, and difficult to distinguish from permanence. Blocks larger than houses rest tightly together, their joints packed with mineral silt and roots. Where walls have collapsed, the rubble has been worn into fitted-looking forms, as though age were continuing the original construction by slower methods.

The materials repeat: dark basalt carried from an unknown source, pale limestone gone gray under water, fired brick in enormous quantities, and dense timbers preserved in the cold, tannin-dark depths. There is simple metalwork too, but little survives intact. Copper stains appear as green fans on stone. Iron has swollen into soft red masses. Lead clamps remain deep in the masonry, dull and almost untouched. The most durable devices are those with few parts: stone hinges, counterweight shafts, sluice gates, pivoting bridges now fixed open or shut.

Nothing functions quickly. Some gates may still move.

A gate the height of a cliff can spend a century settling through the last fraction of its arc. Water passes the narrowing gap with a low vibration that is heard only nearby. A counterweight chamber fills grain by grain with silt. Eventually the balance changes. Somewhere far away, a slab lifts from its sill, and a new current begins—a cold migration through halls that have been still since before recorded memory.

Such events are the great disturbances of this place.

Usually, the sound is small. Rain at the surface. Water slipping through joints. The faint creak of saturated wood. An occasional stone clicking into a more stable position. Vegetation brushes vegetation with a noise like cloth moved in another room. Vast chambers absorb echoes almost before they form. In the deepest districts, quiet becomes pressure: not the absolute absence of sound, but the presence of so much water that every sound seems reluctant to travel.

The made world has many kinds of space. There are shallow precincts where roofless streets lie under a few fathoms of dark water, densely planted and blurred by rain. There are colonnades descending in tiers until their pillars disappear. There are enclosed reservoirs vast enough to develop their own internal currents, joined by doors and canals. There are narrow service passages packed with roots, silt, and collapsed baskets. There are open squares whose paving stones extend beyond sight, interrupted by cistern mouths and low altars worn featureless.

Farther down lie the foundational levels: buttresses, quarry-like cuts that are nevertheless lined with brick, and enormous vaulted undercrofts built directly atop still older roofs. These regions receive no daylight. Vegetation thins there, though it does not disappear. Pale films subsist on dissolved minerals and debris descending from above. Long rootlike cords hang through cracks from illuminated districts, crossing darkness to fasten on lower walls. They are not the organs of trees so much as continuous colonial growths, each strand replacing itself too slowly to observe.

There is no soil except what has accumulated upon construction. Silt gathers in corners, but beneath every bed of it lies a floor. Plants grow in pulverized mortar, soot washed from ancient interiors, rotted rope, waterlogged timber, and dust produced by the wearing of stone against water. Even fertility is an artifact of decay.

The scale defeats any single plan. One region suggests a city, another a dam, another a temple precinct or agricultural terrace, but together they exceed those categories. Canals are as broad as seas. Walls continue past the curvature of visibility. A roof may support forests of algae for hundreds of miles before breaking around a shaft that descends into blackness. The whole structure could be a continent-shaped vessel, or a succession of civilizations that never built on earth because the older masonry always lay beneath them.

The world follows simple rules.

Everything rests on something made.

Everything hollow eventually fills with water.

Anything raised will be rounded, anything inscribed will be softened, and anything moving will slow.

Plants may cover, bind, and preserve, but no mouth will eat them.

Light enters reluctantly and does not reach far.

Change comes not through catastrophe but through accumulation: a film thickening, a joint opening, a basin equalizing with another basin after ten thousand years.

Because there are no animals, there are no paths in the vegetation, no nests, no bones, no sudden alarms. Yet the place does not feel sterile. It feels occupied by growth and architecture in equal measure. Every surface has been made once by hands and made again by water. The drowned terraces are lush, but their lushness is solemn and uninterrupted. Nothing harvests it. Nothing watches it sway.

At rare intervals the clouds thin. A weak gray illumination spreads over the upper water and penetrates perhaps a dozen yards. Then the drowned walls appear: straight lines under rippling rain, green parapets, ranks of pillars, submerged stairs leading downward from one flooded court into the next. For a few minutes the vast construction becomes legible as a fraction of itself.

Then the clouds close.

The gray lines sink back into black-green water. Rain resumes its steady texture over the surface. Far below, a loosened block settles against another with a single muted knock.

The sound travels through the masonry for miles, enters the water, and is gone.
