The land is called nothing, because names cannot remain attached to it.

It occupies a low part of the world—perhaps the lowest part, though no surrounding height can be seen. Its horizon is interrupted not by mountains but by distance itself: miles of warm, dark flats folded into one another, each nearly level, each draining imperceptibly toward the next. No single view explains its size. Weather disappears into it. Thunder arrives from storms weeks away. Tides pass beneath the soil without any visible sea.

Everything here has been eroded beyond the shapes that erosion usually leaves. There are no fresh gullies, sharp stones, or recognizable cliffs. The remaining rock lies in enormous smooth-backed masses, old as cooled worlds, their surfaces polished to the soft curves of bone held too long in water. Between them spreads a mineral plain compacted from ages of mud, shell, ash, salt, and bodies. It is not loose ground. It is the terminal layer: dark, warm, saturated, and pressed until it has almost become stone again.

Water is everywhere, though seldom gathered into open lakes. It films the flats. It trembles in pores. It rises under a footprint that has not yet been made. The rocks sweat continuously, feeding black seams that branch and reunite without flowing downhill. Warm rain falls from low cloud, but often stops a handspan above the surface and hangs there in a dense, quivering layer of beads. At other times the ground rains upward. Droplets lift from cracks, accelerate into the sky, and vanish into the overcast with the hard hiss of thrown sand.

The air is warm enough to feel bodily. Moisture condenses in the lungs before it condenses on skin. Breathing has the intimacy of sharing breath with something immense.

Vegetation is sparse if counted by stems. A few pale reeds stand in widely separated rings. Matlike plants cling to rock depressions, colorless except where bruised into violet. There are low black fans with no visible roots, and cords of red moss growing in ruler-straight lines across the flats. Yet the apparent bareness is misleading. Every available surface is occupied.

The mud is packed with translucent membranes, sleeping spores, mineral-feeding films, blind rootlets, abandoned egg cases, and the slow folds of organisms too broad to recognize as organisms. What looks like a field of bare stone may be one ancient hide. What seems to be shallow water may tighten when shadow crosses it. There is no empty soil, no unclaimed droplet, no gap between one life and another—only layers of dormancy and pressure. Life here has abandoned height and display. It survives as coverage, compression, and incorporation.

Nothing grows upward for long.

Anything that rises more than a few feet draws the wind.

The wind does not blow across the flats in a continuous direction. It strikes downward in columns, bursts sideways from the ground, twists in stationary walls, and sometimes moves as a solid front only inches thick. It strips reeds into fiber and rolls those fibers into dense balls that travel for years. These accumulate in certain basins until the basins appear full of dun-colored boulders. Then rain touches them, and the boulders unfold all at once into forests of thread. For several minutes the plain becomes crowded with swaying filaments hundreds of feet long. Lightning travels through them horizontally. By dawn they have collapsed, fermented, and sunk beneath the mud.

Such creation and ruin are ceaseless. Islands of foam rise from underground water and harden into chalk plateaus before cracking under their own internal tides. Slick mounds inflate along the horizon, assume the shapes of distant hills, and burst, releasing warm fog filled with tiny shells. Channels appear overnight, carrying water in opposite directions along the same bed. Vast sheets of ground peel loose and creep over older ground, swallowing pools, reeds, stones, and one another. The country rearranges itself without becoming young.

Its turbulence is not renewal. It is the motion of something ancient that has forgotten how to stop.

The sound is nearly unbearable.

There is the continuous concussion of air collapsing into subterranean chambers; the bass grinding of stone layers shifting under the saturated crust; the shriek of wind passing through microscopic holes in exposed rock. Rain on the flats does not patter. Each drop provokes a sharp resonant crack, so that a shower becomes a continent-wide sheet of applause. When the suspended rain suddenly falls, the impact is like an avalanche of glass.

Beneath all of this lies a deeper noise, too low to hear cleanly. It shakes water into patterns and makes the teeth ache. Pools wrinkle into elaborate geometries under its influence. Mud rises in little crowns. The rare animals freeze when it strengthens, orienting their bodies along invisible lines.

These animals are mostly solitary and low to the ground. Broad, soft-shelled crawlers graze mineral films from the rocks, leaving polished paths that remain visible for centuries. Long-legged waders cross the flooded seams, though their legs seem to touch the ground at different moments in time: one step dimples the water immediately, the next only after the animal has moved on. Occasionally a flock of small white creatures erupts from the mud. They circle without wings, borne on pressure changes, then plunge back into the earth as if it were deep water.

There are larger beings, but they are known only by displacement. A marsh several miles across will lift slightly at one edge. Pools will spill toward the sky. The low vibration will cease, producing a silence more violent than sound. Then the land will settle somewhere else, leaving a warm depression paved with broken shell.

The dimness never clears. Cloud presses low, illuminated from within by diffuse amber pulses. There is no visible sun. Shadows are weak but disobedient. They collect under stones whose undersides are luminous; they lean against the wind; occasionally they detach and drift over the flats like smoke. During the darkest intervals, the water gives off a dull gray light, revealing buried strata crowded with outlines—leaves, fins, hands that may only resemble hands, whole ecosystems flattened into successive pages.

Distance behaves badly here. A nearby rock may require three days to reach. A line of reeds on the horizon may brush against the ankles. Sound can arrive before its event, and the plain often begins roaring in anticipation of storms that never occur. Travelers—if there could be travelers—would find their own tracks waiting ahead of them, filling slowly with upward rain.

Scattered through the immensity are structures, though it is unclear whether anything built them.

They are small compared with the landscape: low circular walls of fitted stone, narrow doorless shelters, solitary arches worn almost flat. Some stand knee-deep in mud. Others are embedded in the backs of moving rock masses. They contain no tools, inscriptions, hearths, or worked ornament. Their stones fit too closely to admit water, yet water wells continually from their interiors.

Inside each structure, the deafening country becomes momentarily quieter. Not silent—never silent—but reduced enough that separate sounds can be perceived. Drips. Scratching beneath the floor. A pulse from somewhere very far below.

The walls seem less like refuge than listening organs.

No structure remains in the same location. They do not visibly move. The plain folds, floods, slides, or changes its account of distance, and afterward an arch that stood in the east is found half-submerged a hundred miles west. Sometimes two occupy the same patch of ground. Their stones pass through one another without breaking, producing a dense blur that moans in several pitches. Sometimes a structure is absent while its dry interior remains: a patch of air shaped like a room, standing alone in the rain.

There may once have been a coast. Long curved ridges cross the flats, each resembling a wave reduced by millions of years of weather. But they also resemble ribs, dunes, eyelids, and the growth rings of something too large to have lived. Water gathers along their inner edges and crawls uphill when thunder sounds. At irregular intervals, all the ridges flex together.

Then the entire plain becomes a single expression of motion.

Mud puckers into spirals. The hidden waters surge upward. Stone backs roll over in their beds. Reed rings close like mouths. Clouds descend until they drag across the surface, and lightning passes from ground to sky in vast branching roots. The sparse animals vanish. The scattered shelters fill to their ceilings with clear water that does not spill out. For hours, perhaps for years, the landscape creates and erases forms faster than perception can stabilize them: towers of rain, inverted pools, plains suspended above plains, soft hills walking on thousands of temporary legs.

When the convulsion ends, the country lies low and smooth again.

Almost nothing stands. Almost nothing appears alive. Warm water trembles over ancient stone beneath a dim, bruised sky. The horizon returns, immense and nearly level.

But everything is occupied.

Everything is moving.

And beneath the deafening weather, the whole old plain is still settling into its final form.
