The place lies inside a drowned tree.

Not in a hollow exactly, though at first it resembles one: a narrow chamber sealed within the swollen trunk of something ancient and broad-rooted, buried beneath a cold inland sea. Its boundaries are close enough to touch. On every side, black wood rises in soft ridges and splintered shelves. The grain curls around the chamber like fingerprints. Yet no matter how far inward one swims, the walls never quite converge, and no matter how sharply one turns, the chamber continues ahead.

It is a pocket no larger than a room, containing distances that water alone can enter.

Everything is submerged. The water is nearly fresh, faintly mineral, and cool enough to numb exposed skin without freezing it. It has settled into layers. The upper water tastes of tannin and rain; the lower water carries iron, old salt, and the clean stony scent of newly opened ground. Between them hangs a thin inversion where drifting particles stop falling. Silt, spores, fish scales, and pale scraps of bark remain fixed there, packed together into a dim horizontal weather.

There is almost no light. Far above—or perhaps only a handspan overhead—the trunk admits a diluted green-black glow through seams in its wood. These seams do not illuminate objects so much as give darkness texture. They reveal the nearest fern tip, the sheen on a submerged root, a row of air pearls gathered beneath a shelf of bark. Beyond that, forms are understood through pressure and obstruction. The water feels occupied.

Vegetation has filled every available surface.

Moss grows underwater in dense, furred mantles, black at the base and bottle-green where the faint light reaches it. Liverworts overlap like wet scales. Thread-thin pond plants issue from cracks in the wood, although there is no soil within them, and send translucent leaves across the chamber until they knot with neighboring growth. Small ferns unfold from vertical walls. Their fronds are soft and almost colorless, adapted to the darkness; when disturbed, they close one leaflet at a time, slowly enough that the movement may take an hour.

Roots are everywhere. Some belong to the drowned tree and retain its coarse, woody grain. Others are young, white, and searching. They enter through sealed surfaces without making holes. They braid the chamber into narrow passages, bend around empty spaces, and thicken wherever nothing has touched them. Fine root hairs turn every crevice cloudy. There is little open water: one must pass through curtains, nets, and soft thickets, parting them with the body and feeling them close behind.

No current explains their motion. The plants lean together, then gradually apart, in cycles longer than tides.

The floor is low and uneven, covered by a deep layer of newly deposited mud. Beneath it, sharp ridges of pale stone protrude as though they have only recently crystallized. Their edges have not yet been rounded. Some are glassy, some chalk-soft, some fibrous like broken bone. They form miniature gullies and scarps beneath the silt: a young terrain hidden inside an ancient trunk. From time to time a ridge settles with a movement too slight to see. A line opens in the mud; clear water wells out; after several days the crack is closed by moss.

Erosion here has barely begun. Water works patiently at the sharp places, taking from them not sand but translucence. The oldest stone points become clear. Once completely transparent, they cease to resist passage. Roots grow through them. Mud falls through them. A hand would also pass through, though it would emerge cold and smelling of distant rain.

Scattered through the growth are small structures.

They are not tools and show no workmanship. They resemble the remains of building only because nature has repeated a few architectural forms: arches of root, stacked plates of mineral, little chambers roofed with bark. In one recess, six upright sticks support a curled leaf like a pavilion. In another, stones stand in a near-perfect ring around a spring no wider than an eye. Farther in lies a stair of dark wooden ledges. Each step is just large enough for a thumb. It descends into mud and continues below it, where the water becomes too dense to see through.

These arrangements are temporary but extremely slow to vanish. Nothing maintains them. Nothing seems to have constructed them. If disassembled, they return over months—not necessarily in the same location, but always with the same number of pieces.

Fauna is rare. Most movement belongs to plants or to the suspension of the water itself.

Minute white worms inhabit the moss and pull back from warmth. A colorless snail may sometimes be found grazing on a mineral face, leaving behind a trail darker than the surrounding stone. There are tiny crustaceans in the lower water, but they remain still for such long intervals that colonies of algae grow over their shells. When they finally move, each makes a single abrupt leap and then waits again.

Only one larger animal is known here: a narrow fish with no eyes and too many lateral lines. It is seldom encountered directly. More often there is a pressure against the water, a long displacement among the roots, and afterward every leaf in the chamber points the same way. The fish appears unable to turn. It swims along curves that the place provides for it, following corridors that are not present before it arrives. If seen, it is pearl-gray, nearly weightless, and longer than the chamber should permit. Its tail may pass for several minutes after its head has disappeared.

The quiet is dense rather than empty. Water muffles nearly everything. There is the occasional tick of stone contracting in the cold, the soft rupture of an air bead from rotting wood, the faint brushing of one root against another. Sounds do not travel normally. A noise made nearby may arrive from deep below. A noise made twice may return only once. The chamber sometimes preserves a sound for days, releasing it when the surrounding growth has assumed the same shape it held at the moment the sound was made.

Thus a snapping twig may be heard long after the twig has softened into pulp.

The water itself obeys local rules. Buoyancy changes from one root enclosure to the next. In some pockets, fallen matter rises; in others, air sinks in silver droplets and gathers along the floor. There are regions where water lies within water as a distinct substance, bounded by trembling skins. These inner pools are colder and much deeper than their dimensions allow. Plant stems entering them emerge at different ages: a fresh green shoot may go in and come out brown, branched, and laden with spores.

Distance is similarly unreliable. The black wall can be touched almost everywhere, but there are openings between strands of moss that look across submerged forests. Their trees are identical to the tree containing the chamber. No view remains stable when approached. The distant forest contracts into a bead of water; the bead hangs from a root; inside it, if one looks closely, is the same dark chamber and the dim suggestion of someone looking in.

Nothing happens quickly. The place does not surge, hunt, collapse, or bloom before the eye. Its impossibilities are patient.

Mud accumulates upward and sideways. New stone hardens from cold springs. Roots exchange positions over seasons without tangling. Darkness thickens, then thins by an amount perceptible only to moss. Once in a very long while, rain can be heard pounding on the surface of the inland sea, though the drowned trunk lies far beneath it. The sound enters not from above but from every piece of wood at once: a muted, encompassing hiss, as if the chamber has become the interior of a raindrop.

During those rains the air pearls beneath the bark begin to multiply. They detach and descend. Vegetation turns its leaves downward to receive them. The little architectures settle deeper into the mud, and the unseen fish passes through the roots.

Then the rain stops. The last echoes remain trapped among the ferns.

Cold water closes over everything. Growth resumes its nearly motionless crowding. The hidden place goes on making more interior than its walls can hold.
