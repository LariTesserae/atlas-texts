At nearly five thousand meters, the basin is not a hollow so much as a crowded shelf in the sky.

It lies between two massifs of dark volcanic stone, broad enough to contain a watershed, a migration route, and the remnants of an industry, yet hemmed so thoroughly by cliffs, talus fans, mineral terraces, and ranks of freestanding pillars that no part of it feels empty. Distance exists here, but vacancy does not. Every slope has already been occupied by scree; every ledge carries a crust of salt or lichen; every channel is fitted into an older channel. The land has had a long time to arrange itself.

The heat is startling. Sunlight at this altitude arrives with almost no softness in it. By midmorning the black rock is too hot to touch, and the air over the flats trembles. Snow peaks stand on the horizon, clean and implausible, while the basin below them bakes. Shade remains cold, sometimes bitterly so. A person stepping behind a boulder can pass in a few paces from furnace heat into air that smells of frost.

Water is present chiefly by evidence.

There are branching stains on the canyon walls, polished runnels, and mats of dried algae curled like old paint. Storm pools occupy the deep sockets in the stone. Some persist for months beneath shelves of basalt, dark and nearly circular, their surfaces trembling with springtails and the breathing of hidden larvae. Elsewhere, pipes hammered into the mountain sweat continuously. Condensation gathers on their shaded undersides, swells into drops, and falls into little mineral bowls. These become crowded watering places: insects, mice, small bronze lizards, birds with red throats, all arriving according to an order that is frequently disputed.

Once or twice each week in the warm season, clouds climb over the western ridge. Rain may not reach the ground. It hangs beneath the clouds in gray curtains and is taken apart by the hot air. But when a storm does break through, the entire basin changes at once. Dust darkens. Stone releases the smell of iron and old smoke. Dry channels become ropes of brown water, moving stones that have waited years to be moved. The pools fill, overflow, and connect briefly into a shining network. Within hours the network is gone again, but not without consequence: fresh silt is laid against retaining walls, seeds open, insects rise, and the basin’s animals redistribute themselves around the renewed pockets.

Vegetation survives less as continuous cover than as a set of precise claims. Thorn cushions press flat to the ground. Wiregrass grows inside cracks too narrow for grazing mouths. Pale shrubs gather below condensation lines, each with roots extending far beyond its visible crown. On the warmer cliffs, fleshy plants occupy seams in the rock and turn their leaves edge-on at noon. During the weeks after rain, tiny orange and violet flowers appear over the flats. They are so numerous that from a distance the basin seems richly vegetated, though one can walk among them without brushing a single stem.

The fauna is far more abundant than the sparse plant life appears able to support. Much of it is migratory, subterranean, or dependent on the basin’s vertical layers. Herds of narrow-bodied ungulates cross the upper terraces at dawn, placing their feet on paths worn smooth over generations. Rock rabbits live in the talus. Vultures and long-winged hawks circle in the thermals, while dense colonies of swifts nest in holes in the pillars. Beetles emerge at night to harvest condensation. Pale moths travel upslope in such numbers that they collect like ash around the few electric lamps.

Predation is constant but rarely dramatic. A shadow passes over the flats; everything small stops moving. A fox-colored cat steps from one boulder to another, and the birds reorganize themselves without a cry. At twilight the basin’s silence becomes visibly busy: wings flicker, antennae rise, burrows open, and the edges of pools acquire concentric ripples.

The industrial works are scattered across this ecology rather than gathered into a town. They seem at first like ruins, but many are still functioning.

A compressor house clings to the northern wall, its corrugated shell bleached almost white. Two kilometers away, a row of extraction wells descends through the mineral terraces. Cableways span the deepest gullies, their towers isolated on narrow spurs. An ore-crushing station stands alone near the center of the basin, surrounded by no settlement, only graded aprons and heaps of dark aggregate. Narrow pipelines follow contours across the cliffs, vanishing into tunnels and reappearing much farther on. Most structures are small compared with the land, but they are connected underground and by radio, parts of a single distributed machine.

The system extracts heat and dissolved metals from deep brines. Its visible activity is intermittent. Valves open without warning. Pumps engage somewhere behind the ridge. A cable bucket travels slowly across a gap, apparently empty. At several vents, clear vapor issues into the hard blue air and condenses at once, creating localized weather: warm fog in a ravine, droplets on black stone, a fringe of moss where otherwise nothing green grows.

These wet industrial pockets are among the basin’s richest habitats. Birds nest in the latticework. Lizards warm themselves on insulated conduits. Mineral-loving bacteria stain drainage sumps orange, green, and oily purple. The machinery was built to resist the living world, but the living world has learned its schedules. Herds arrive when purge-water is released. Moths hatch during maintenance blackouts. Hawks wait on cable towers when the compressor’s vibration drives rodents from their burrows.

There are people, though they are seldom seen. Each installation has a shelter of stone or sheet metal, usually half buried against the wind. Operators travel the service road in low, broad-wheeled vehicles, raising plumes that remain suspended for hours. At night, three or four sodium lamps define the inhabited basin: an amber point at the wells, another at the crusher, one at a switching station on the eastern pass. Their separation makes the landscape seem larger.

Human marks from earlier cycles lie beneath the current works. Low walls cross the slopes for reasons no longer obvious. Cairns have been rebuilt so often that ceramic insulators and machine bearings appear among their stones. In a cliff recess above the main channel, an ancient stair climbs to a dry cistern. Later workers cut a conduit through it. Still later, swifts filled the conduit with nests. Nothing is entirely abandoned; it merely acquires another use.

For all this activity, the basin is mostly quiet. Thin air does not carry sound generously. The compressor’s pulse can sometimes be felt through the soles before it is heard. Cable wheels produce a remote metallic complaint, then stop. Gravel ticks down sun-heated slopes. Wings make brief paper sounds at the pools. The loudest natural event may be a storm, but even thunder is altered here—flat, abrupt, swallowed by the surrounding rock.

The basin has its own atmospheric rules. Heat climbs the walls each day and pulls wind upward through the gullies. Near sunset the flow reverses, pouring cold air from the snowfields into the baked low places. Fog forms where that descending cold crosses the vents. It collects in trenches and behind retaining walls rather than spreading evenly. One may look down from a ridge upon dozens of separate white pools of cloud, each occupying a different compartment of the land.

This thermal reversal also makes the pillars sing, though only faintly. The stone contains narrow mineral seams that expand during the day and contract after sunset. As shadow crosses the basin, clicks pass from one pillar to the next. Not an echo, exactly, and not random: a slow sequence governed by the angle of the sun and the differing thicknesses of rock. Workers use the clicks to judge the hour when radios fail. Animals respond as well. The first evening click brings beetles from beneath the gravel; the fifth sends the swifts back to the cliffs.

Erosion here is neither violent nor finished. The terrain has settled into durable forms: aprons at the stable angle of repose, gullies armored with heavy stones, terraces bound by mineral crust. Wind and flood maintain those forms more often than they destroy them. A pillar sheds a slab; the slab becomes shelter. A channel jumps its bank; within a season the new course is lined with tracks and seedlings. The industrial works, too, are being incorporated into this equilibrium. Pipes catch sediment. Foundations divide runoff. Waste heaps weather into cliffs with their own ledges and nesting sites.

From the highest pass, the whole region can be read at once: black ribs of mountain, copper flats, pale roads, brilliant pockets of water, the fine geometry of pipelines, and innumerable moving animals reduced by distance to dots. It is an open view of an intensely occupied place. Rock, heat, machinery, weather, and life are fitted together with almost no margin.

Then the sun lowers behind the western massif. The glaring basin becomes blue in less than a minute. Heat lifts from the stones in visible shivers; cold begins pouring down from above. A valve opens somewhere out of sight. Vapor blooms in a ravine. The pillars click in succession, and across the terraces thousands of small creatures begin to move.
