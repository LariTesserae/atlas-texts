The plateau is alive all the way down.

There is no soil here, no bedrock in the ordinary sense, and almost no water. What appears from a distance to be a high, pale continent is a single aggregation of plants: root compressed over root, old vascular tissue mineralized beneath younger growth, layer after layer descending farther than anyone could excavate. Its upper surface lies beneath a white, enormous sky. Along the horizon, it curves perceptibly away.

The living ground is cool and rigid underfoot. It resembles cork where it is young, bone where it is old, and weathered iron where centuries of airborne salts have entered its pores. Great tracts are almost featureless—silver-green plains separated by tens or hundreds of kilometers of open brightness. The vegetation is total, but not lush. Life fills every available substrate by becoming the substrate; individual forms stand far apart because each is connected below to everything else.

There are no rivers. No dew gathers at dawn. Clouds pass overhead without rain, composed largely of fine organic crystals and volatile oils lifted from the plateau itself. The plants do not drink. Their chemistry is based on cold sap-solvents that remain fluid in narrow capillaries: clear terpenes, ammonia-bearing resins, liquid hydrocarbons distilled and re-distilled through living membranes. Water, when trace amounts arrive in meteoric dust, is trapped immediately, dismantled, and rationed molecule by molecule.

The high country is intensely bright. A thin atmosphere and a glaring sun cast shadows as dark and precise as cuts. Leaves avoid exposing broad surfaces to it. Most are needles, mirrors, transparent vanes, or slender black filaments held edge-on to the light. At noon the plains seem sparsely furnished: a few distant crowns, a field of low fibers, miles of bare-looking ground. But the apparent barrenness is deceptive. Every surface is photosynthetic. Even the bleached ridges underfoot contain slow green cells several meters deep, feeding on light conveyed through glassy strands.

Then the wind begins.

It is almost always beginning somewhere.

Across so vast and elevated a living surface, differences in heat, electrical charge, and gas exchange generate weather on a continental scale. The plateau breathes unevenly. One province opens its pores while another seals them; one releases a billion tons of warm vaporized resin while another consumes it. Pressure fronts race across the plain. The sky darkens not with water cloud but with pollen, shredded cuticle, spores, hollow seeds, and pale flakes sloughed from the ground.

The sound is immediate and overwhelming.

Miles of needle foliage hiss together, a hard, continuous noise like furnaces under pressure. Tower-plants bend and strike their hollow trunks against neighboring spars, producing booms that travel through the substrate. Dry seed chambers detonate in chains. Root tissues, contracting with the cold front, give deep reports from below. Along old escarpments the wind finds rows of natural apertures and turns whole cliff faces into organs: bass notes too low to hear cleanly, felt instead in the chest and teeth, beneath shrieking overtones.

There is no shelter from the noise because the land itself conducts it. Even in a still hollow, distant storms arrive through the soles as vibration. Small creatures communicate by stamping coded rhythms into the vascular floor. Others have abandoned hearing and navigate by pressure, electric fields, or the taste of airborne compounds. To possess delicate ears here is to possess them only briefly.

The plateau’s relief was not carved by water. It has been pruned by wind, fatigue, fireless oxidation, and growth.

Old mountain ranges rise where buried root systems folded against one another over geological time. Their slopes are bands of lignin, silica, amber, and blackened cellulose, tilted like sedimentary rock. Ravines mark dead veins: transport channels once broad enough to carry torrents of resin, now empty and polished by centuries of abrasive pollen. Tall mesas are the stumps of ancient reproductive towers. Their summits may be hundreds of square kilometers across, fringed with new growth that slowly eats downward into the remains.

Everything has the settled look of great age, though almost nothing keeps its shape for long.

The margins of ridges are rounded and dark with weathering. Fallen trunks have been stripped to smooth arches. Exposed tissues protect themselves beneath metallic skins produced from dust and atmospheric ions. Yet on any given day an entire plain may buckle upward as gas accumulates beneath it, split into radiant seams, and release forests of threadlike organs. By evening those forests may have collapsed, fertilized, and become a new layer of ground.

Creation and destruction occur at industrial speed.

There are no factories, no engines, and no built structures, but the processes of the plateau have the scale and rhythm of heavy manufacturing. In the north, kilometer-long belts of leaf tissue travel continuously over buried rollers of swollen root, presenting fresh cells to the sun and carrying spent ones into digestive trenches. Elsewhere, groves of piston-plants pump atmosphere through underground chambers. Their jointed trunks rise and fall in synchronization, hammering day and night. Catalytic reefs crack airborne hydrocarbons into usable compounds; wax refineries sweat translucent fuel into cisterns formed from their own flesh; electrical forests raise conductive spines into charged weather and flash with blue corona.

None of this is designed. Or, rather, design and evolution are indistinguishable at this scale. No hand assembled the mechanisms. The mechanisms reproduce, compete, mutate, and are grazed upon.

From far away, the refinery groves resemble scattered cities. Up close, they are more unsettlingly organic: valves opening like stomata, tubes healing around obstructions, pressure bladders budding small replicas of themselves. Their smoke is viable. Their waste wakes and crawls away.

Fauna moves through this immensity in bursts.

The most visible are the sail herds. Each animal is little more than a dark knot of muscle beneath a broad mineral membrane. Thousands launch when a pressure front arrives, skimming for days above the plains and feeding on airborne spores. Their shadows pass like torn clouds. When the wind drops, they fold their sails into narrow legs and stand widely separated, pointing into the sun.

Beneath them run pale six-limbed grazers that shave the plateau’s photosynthetic skin. They must keep moving because the ground heals rapidly behind them, sealing their footprints with clear sap. Packs of smaller animals follow to drink—not water, which most native bodies cannot tolerate in quantity, but the fresh solvent that wells from each bite.

Some animals are inseparable from the plant machinery. Glass-bodied mites live inside the transparent light guides. Burrowing drums inhabit hollow roots and beat against the walls to redirect the flow of sap. Vast, slow filter-beasts anchor themselves over breathing vents, spreading ribbed mouths into the gale. During quiet periods they appear to be low hills. When the plateau exhales, they inflate to astonishing height and sing with the escaping gases.

Predation is common but difficult to distinguish from pollination, pruning, parasitism, and repair. A hunter may tear open a walking grazer, distribute its spores, consume its flesh, and then seal the wound in the ground. Some animals are born as seeds. Some plants detach after a century and become migratory. Classification fails repeatedly.

The sparse appearance of the country persists even at its most active. Its distances are too great to be crowded. A storm of ten million animals can cross beneath a sky so broad that it seems no more than a smudge. A pumping grove audible from two hundred kilometers away may remain below the horizon for days. Solitary reproductive towers rise at intervals like pins driven into the curve of the world, each surrounded by an exclusion plain where its roots have poisoned all rival growth.

These towers produce the loudest events.

They spend decades accumulating charge and volatile tissue. Their outer scales peel back one by one, exposing a central stalk that climbs into the high atmosphere. Winds spin its vanes until the entire structure hums. When the electrical gradient is sufficient, the tower discharges upward—not a lightning strike descending from the sky, but a branching river of light thrown from the land. The report crosses the plateau in multiple waves. The tower then bursts, launching sealed botanical capsules beyond the horizon.

For several minutes afterward, every conductive filament on the plain glows violet. Animals flatten themselves. Root pumps stop. The deafening background falls away.

Silence here is not peace. It is recoil.

Then the ground shudders, the pumps resume out of sequence, and sound floods back in layers: first the subterranean pounding, then the hiss of foliage, then the escalating shriek of the wind. Capsules burn overhead. Distant growth fronts lift green-black walls against the sun. A range that stood for ten thousand years loosens along an ancient seam and leans, almost imperceptibly, toward collapse.

The governing rule of this place is that nothing inert remains exposed for long. Anything that falls is incorporated. Dust is caught by hairs. Salt becomes armor. Bone becomes scaffolding. Gas becomes flesh. Even open space is harvested by sails, kites, scents, and electric fields.

Yet emptiness is never conquered. It is necessary to the system. The immense gaps between groves give the winds room to accelerate. The bare-looking plains serve as collectors of light. The remoteness of each great organism prevents the entire plateau from being consumed in one convulsion.

Thus the living continent holds itself between fullness and vacancy: vegetation everywhere, landmarks rare; ancient forms, incessant upheaval; cool air, violent energy; a world without water that behaves, at times, like an ocean.

Its forests do not sway beneath storms.

They manufacture them.
