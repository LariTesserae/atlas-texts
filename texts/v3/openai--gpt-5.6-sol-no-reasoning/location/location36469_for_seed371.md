The place is a narrow cleft high in a young mountain range, scarcely wider than a cathedral and enclosed on three sides by black, newly fractured cliffs. The rock has not yet softened into slopes. It stands in plates and splinters, its edges pale where recent breaks expose the interior. Long seams of pyrite and wet mica catch what little light descends from above, flashing briefly and then disappearing into the dark.

There is no path into the cleft. The floor is a confusion of angular blocks, shallow fissures, and roots braided so densely that they sometimes seem more reliable than the ground. The elevation makes the air thin and cold, but the cleft is sheltered enough to hold moisture. Clouds strike the ridge, tear, and spill through the upper notches as mist. Rain rarely falls for long. Instead, water appears everywhere in small quantities: condensation on leaves, beads along stone ribs, dark moss saturated beneath the surface, temporary runnels threading between rocks. In hollows no larger than bowls, clear pools tremble continuously.

Vegetation fills almost every space not occupied by fresh stone. It is lush without being warm or expansive. Compact alpine shrubs rise in crowded tiers, their leaves dark, leathery, and nearly black in the low light. Pale grasses grow long and lax, combed flat by downdrafts and springing upright in the intervals. Ferns inhabit the cliff cracks, each frond cold with droplets. Thick cushions of moss bulge over ledges and fractured boulders, vivid green where the mica lends them a dim metallic glow.

The plants are young in their arrangement but mature in form. They have colonized this cleft with startling speed. Landslides continually strip one wall bare while the opposite wall greens over. Roots probe fractures opened only months before. Mats of vegetation detach in storms and descend intact, settling across lower rubble like pieces of turf laid by no hand. Nothing here resembles a stable forest. It is more like a vertical thicket forever being assembled and dismantled.

No animals live in it. There are no insects under the leaves, no birds passing through the slit of sky, no burrows, nests, droppings, webs, or gnawed stems. Flowers open anyway—small white cups and clusters of blue bells—but no pollinators visit. Seeds are cast to the wind or shaken downslope. The absence is not uncanny so much as absolute, one of the ordinary conditions of this severe altitude. Plant life and mineral life seem sufficient.

The darkness comes partly from the depth of the cleft and partly from the weather. Even at midday, the upper opening is only a crooked gray ribbon. The dense growth below receives light as reflection: dull silver from wet rock, a submerged green beneath leaves, occasional blue flashes from quartz faces. At night the space becomes complete blackness. There is no bioluminescence and no distant artificial glow. Only lightning, when storms cross the ridge, shows the whole chamber at once—cliffs, roots, airborne water, and thousands of tossing leaves—then removes it again.

Though nothing has been built here, the cleft sounds industrial.

Wind enters through several narrow apertures at different heights. Each passage gives it a separate pitch. One produces a deep, reciprocating throb like a distant piston. Another screams steadily through a crack no wider than a hand. Loose stone plates knock against the cliff with the irregular clang of sheet metal. Water descends hidden shafts and pounds hollow chambers below, giving off a heavy, continuous concussion. When rain increases, the runnels converge through channels in the rubble and begin to pulse, surge, and choke, as if pumps beneath the mountain were starting and stopping.

The loudest sound comes from the rock itself. This range is still lifting. Frost enters the new fractures; pressure shifts through unweathered strata. At intervals there is a report like quarry blasting, followed by a quick fusillade of falling fragments. Larger collapses begin with a low vibration felt through roots and teeth. Then a section of wall releases, pulverizing shrubs beneath it and sending cold dust through the vegetation. Nothing mechanical exists here, yet the forces have the rhythm, violence, and scale of machinery: compression, rupture, transport, sorting. The mountain is its own factory, producing talus, soil, and fresh surfaces without rest.

The cleft changes by the hour. Gusts reverse direction abruptly. Mist condenses into rain, rain turns to sleet, and sleet rattles down through the foliage like handfuls of bearings. A temporary stream swells across the floor, shifts a bank of stones, and disappears into a new crack. Moss folds over the abandoned channel. A root mass, exposed by erosion, catches windblown debris until a small dam forms; minutes later the dam bursts and scatters its contents into a lower pool.

Despite all this motion, erosion has barely begun to round anything. Water polishes narrow tracks but leaves the surrounding rock knife-edged. Fallen blocks remain geometric. Soil exists only in pockets trapped among roots, dense and black and shallow enough that a hand might reach bedrock almost anywhere. The cliffs retain the raw architecture of their formation: vertical joints, overlapping slabs, interrupted mineral bands. Their damage is fresh rather than ancient.

The whole place is bounded and intimate. From almost any point, if visibility permits, one can make out the walls that contain it. Yet its density makes it difficult to comprehend. Leaves conceal fissures; fissures contain water; water undermines stones; stones shelter moss; moss holds soil; soil anchors roots that pry the cliff apart. Every surface is occupied or in the process of becoming available. There are no open meadows, no broad views, and almost no quiet.

Its governing rule is simple: nothing remains exposed for long, and nothing covered is secure. Fresh rock is greened over. Vegetation is buried. Water opens passages and then abandons them. Wind packs the chamber with cloud and empties it again. The cleft is lush not because it is gentle, but because disturbance creates innumerable small places for life to take hold. It is barren of animals, untouched by construction, and yet relentlessly busy—a dark, cold workshop in which a young mountain is being broken into the first ingredients of a living landscape.
