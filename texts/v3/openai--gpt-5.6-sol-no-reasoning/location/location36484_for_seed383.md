Below the surface lies a city built in the bed of an ancient inland sea.

The cavern is so broad that its farther walls are usually mistaken for weather. They appear as pale bands beyond the inhabited districts, blurred by suspended mineral dust and the immense distance. Above, the ceiling is not a rooflike vault but a second landscape: shelves, hollows, descending promontories, and long limestone plains polished by vanished water. It hangs low in some quarters and retreats beyond sight in others. Entire neighborhoods fit beneath a single downward bulge of stone.

Everything has been eroded past drama. Stalagmites have become rounded stumps. Channels once cut by violent floods are now shallow, sinuous depressions. The bedrock has the smooth, resigned character of something touched by water for ages after it ceased to resist. Even fractures have softened. Their edges crumble into pale powder under the hand.

Across this worn basin stands the city.

It is urban less through height than extent. Thousands of low structures are distributed over the cavern floor: circular courts, stepped platforms, retaining walls, kilns, cisterns, pens, workshops, and broad roads bordered with waist-high stones. Yet the construction remains sparse. Between districts lie long intervals of bare rock, abandoned channels, dry plazas, and fields of rubble arranged into inexplicable grids. A traveler can leave one crowded ward, walk for half an hour through silence, and arrive at another cluster of dwellings glowing on the horizon.

There is no timber. No moss softens a wall, no grass works into the paving. The city is made entirely from what the cavern yields: chalky blocks, slabs of dark shale, knapped glass, bone-colored ceramic, twisted cord spun from fungal skins, and metals used so sparingly that a copper hook may pass through six generations. Doors are stone panels turning on polished sockets. Roofs are shallow corbelled domes or membranes stitched from translucent animal gut. Scaffolds consist of stacked masonry and ropes suspended from natural anchors in the ceiling.

The people who made it possess ingenious hands but few machines. Stone hammers ring softly in cloth-lined workshops. Obsidian blades are pressure-flaked beneath bowls to catch every valuable splinter. Heavy loads travel on sledges over wetted lanes, hauled by teams of pale animals. Water gates are lifted by counterweights of rubble. Elevators are baskets, ropes, and patience. Where something must move quickly, hundreds of hands move it.

The brightness is the city’s great contradiction.

White light pours from the ceiling in broad, sourceless sheets. It travels through veins of crystalline salt that branch through the overburden and gather daylight—or something sufficiently like daylight—from fractures far above. The veins brighten and dim unpredictably. At their strongest, every stone casts a sharp blue shadow and the cavern becomes painfully clear from end to end. At other times only isolated patches shine, dividing the city into luminous islands and cool gray distances.

Mirrors of mica are mounted on walls and tilted by hand. Whole guilds spend their days steering reflected light into homes, cisterns, slaughter courts, and tunnels. Their adjustments make the illumination seem restless. Bright rectangles crawl over facades. A road may blaze for ten minutes, then vanish into dusk as an operator three districts away changes the angle of a plate.

The air is cool and faintly saline. Moisture comes intermittently. Condensation first gathers on the ceiling as a silver haze, then forms beads, then rain. Because the cavern roof is uneven, these rains are intensely local. One courtyard may receive a hard, vertical shower while the next remains powdered with dust. Water races briefly along the ancient channels, filling rock pockets and communal cisterns. A few hours later most surfaces are dry again, though dampness remains in cracks and beneath paving slabs.

There are no plants.

Life here begins elsewhere in the chain. Thin microbial films bloom invisibly on damp mineral faces, feeding on salts, sulfur, and the periodic brilliance overhead. Fungi grow in sealed clay rooms, not as forests of caps but as dense gray loaves beneath the floors. They are cultivated on waste, bone meal, shed skin, and imported mineral slurry. Most citizens seldom see them. To expose a growth chamber unnecessarily is considered both wasteful and obscene.

Animals, however, are everywhere.

They are mostly small, pale, and economical: six-legged grazers that rasp microbial residue from wet stone; translucent burrowers nesting in warm kiln walls; flat-bodied scavengers slipping under doors; mothlike things whose wings produce no audible flutter. Long-backed herd animals, blind and soft-hoofed, are driven through the avenues to feed at recently wetted basins. They lick the stone for hours, harvesting films too thin for human eyes. Their flesh is bitter, their milk almost clear.

Above the markets hang colonies of bladder-bodied fliers. They steer with ribbons of skin and descend whenever blood, fungus, or fresh condensation is exposed. In dry districts, tiny shell creatures accumulate in the road hollows like spilled beads. After rain they awaken simultaneously and migrate toward the cisterns. People sweep channels for them, sorting edible varieties from those used for dye, glue, poison, or divination.

Despite this abundance, the city is quiet.

Nothing here has evolved to call over wind or foliage. Animals communicate by vibration, scent, flashes of skin, and the tapping of limbs against rock. Wheels are rare. Hammers are muffled to prevent fractures. People speak closely and seldom shout, for sound travels unpredictably beneath the ceiling. A single raised voice can return hours later from some remote gallery, thinned and altered, interrupting a meal in a district that never heard the original cry.

The usual sounds are intimate: the scrape of a sledge runner, dripping water, rope fibers tightening, bare feet crossing powder, the dry mouth-clicks used to guide herd animals. During local rain, a patch of city suddenly fills with pattering, then quiets just as abruptly.

And yet the place is never still.

The cavern is ancient, but its equilibrium is turbulent. The crystalline veins overhead swell and contract with the changing light. Hairline fissures open. Salt plates detach from the ceiling and descend soundlessly until the final instant, exploding across plazas in white shards. Condensation shifts weight through the rock. Old channels reverse direction after minor settling. A cistern that served a neighborhood for centuries may drain through a new crack overnight; elsewhere, a dry cellar begins filling from below.

Districts are therefore temporary even when their walls are old. Streets are rerouted weekly. Houses are dismantled ahead of anticipated fractures and rebuilt on safer stone. Markets follow water. Pens follow the markets. Light crews drag mirrors across the basin. Entire populations migrate from one quarter to another, carrying doors, roof membranes, hearthstones, ancestral jars, and numbered blocks of masonry.

The city has no fixed center. Civic authority belongs to whichever plaza currently possesses three things: reliable light, potable water, and unfractured ground. Councils rise and dissolve with the cistern levels. Maps are made on loose clay tiles so obsolete sections can be broken off. Addresses describe conditions rather than coordinates: *behind the warm fault, under the second brightness, west of the bitter pool*. Children learn routes as sequences of textures felt through the soles.

The oldest structures are not buildings but absences. Broad, flattened zones interrupt the city where previous ceilings fell and were cleared away grain by grain. Some have become ceremonial spaces. Others are avoided because the stone beneath them gives off a slow vibration.

At the basin’s lowest point lies a shallow river that does not continuously exist. For months its channel is only a smooth road winding through the districts. Traders pitch stalls there, animals sleep in its potholes, and processions use it as the easiest path across the cavern. Then, without rain, black water arrives from unseen country deeper underground. It comes swiftly but almost without sound, shouldering aside stalls and carrying pale fauna in dense, writhing mats.

Everyone knows the river’s signals: a coldness around the ankles, shell creatures climbing walls, the sudden dimming of mica mirrors. At those signs the road-city folds itself away. Cloth is rolled. Masonry plugs are pulled from drainage cuts. Herds are driven uphill. Within an hour the commercial avenue becomes a braided torrent full of ceramic buoys and fishing barriers.

For several days the whole city rearranges around it. Ferries appear. Water markets gather on ledges. Children search newly flooded cracks for soft-shelled animals. Then the flow dwindles into disconnected pools and oily ribbons. Fishlike things seal themselves inside mud chambers. The stalls return. Dust covers the waterline.

Far beyond the inhabited zones, under the brightest portion of the ceiling, stand rows of structures too eroded to identify. They may be foundations, tombs, dams, or formations mistaken for masonry. Their stone is the same stone as the basin floor, rounded until construction and geology are indistinguishable. Animals avoid them during dry periods but crowd there when condensation begins.

No one excavates the place. The citizens say it has already been excavated by time, down through every possible city, and that what remains is not ruin but the final arrangement.

Even so, after each ceiling-fall, unfamiliar walls are found beneath the debris.

They are always clean.

They are sometimes warm.

And their doors, though made without hinges or metal, are occasionally open.
