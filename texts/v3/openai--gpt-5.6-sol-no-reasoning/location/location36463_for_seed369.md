The place lies in a notch high on the warm side of a mountain, a shallow basin no larger than a small grove. Its boundaries are clear without being abrupt: dark ribs of stone rise on three sides, while the fourth falls away into bright distance. Beyond that lip, lower hills are visible through vapor, their forests reduced to soft green folds. Clouds travel beneath the basin as often as above it.

The basin floor is uneven and deeply weathered. Broad slabs of charcoal-gray rock have been rounded into low backs, bowls, and saddles, their surfaces pocked with old rain-hollows. There are no fresh breaks. Every edge has softened; every fallen stone appears to have finished falling long ago. Rust, pale lichen, and thin mineral skins mark the slow commerce between water and rock.

Water is everywhere, though nowhere abundant enough to form a lake. It shines in the stone bowls, threads beneath mats of roots, and seeps from seams halfway up the enclosing walls. The largest spring issues as a clear sheet no thicker than a leaf, spreading over black rock before gathering into a shallow runnel. Rain remains here for days. Even in dry weather, condensation beads on shaded faces and drops steadily from fern tips.

Because the basin is warm, this constant wetness produces a lush but curiously open growth. Vegetation gathers in islands rather than forming a continuous thicket. Ferns crowd the spring heads. Broad-leaved plants stand waist-high in the pockets of deep soil, their leaves waxy and scarred by falling water. Small trees grow far apart, each twisted by exposure, their crowns broad and low as if sheltering invisible occupants. Between them lie expanses of bare stone and amber sedge. The emptiness is not sterile; it seems deliberately preserved around each living cluster.

Moss grows extravagantly where it grows at all. It upholsters certain boulders from base to crown, leaving neighboring stones almost naked. On the basin walls, long filaments of gray-green lichen hang in wet tassels. After rain, tiny red cups open among them. A day later they close and become nearly impossible to find.

There are no structures and no enduring marks of construction. Yet some of the stones suggest use. Near the lower edge of the basin, a shelf of hard volcanic rock is scattered with flakes sharp enough to cut skin. They may be natural splinters produced by heat and rain, though several fit comfortably in the hand, and a few bear repeated chips along one edge. Nothing indicates who shaped them, if anyone did. No path leads to the shelf. No hearth, posthole, wall, or buried shard accompanies it. The place permits the thought of early hands but refuses proof.

Animals are seldom seen. A small bronze-colored lizard sometimes warms itself on the exposed slabs. High overhead, a solitary dark bird circles without calling. There are no herds, no persistent insects, no churned mud around the pools. Occasionally a three-toed impression appears in the soft margin of a spring and remains until the next rain smooths it away. The print is too large for the lizard and too narrow for the bird, but its maker never seems to cross the open ground while anyone watches.

For so small and sparsely inhabited a place, the basin is loud. Water amplifies itself against the hollow rock. Each drop landing in a rain-cup gives a distinct, resonant note; dozens together create a disorderly metallic music. Wind enters through the downhill opening and passes across cracks in the stone, drawing out low tones like breath over bottle mouths. The largest fissure produces a continuous hum at midday, when the sun has warmed the outer rock. Near dusk it falls silent.

Thunder is common but distant. It moves through the mountains below, rising into the basin as a rolling pressure rather than a sharp report. Leaves shiver before the sound arrives. During heavy rain the separate drips and whistles merge into a broad roar, though the water itself never becomes violent. Nothing floods quickly here. The basin receives, holds, seeps, and releases.

Its strangest quality appears in the rain-hollows. Each reflects the sky at a slightly different hour. One may hold the white light of noon while the neighboring cup shows the rose-gray of evening. A third reflects stars even under daylight, faintly but unmistakably. The effect changes too slowly to observe directly. Over several days the hours seem to migrate from hollow to hollow, passing around the basin in an irregular circle.

The plants respond to these displaced lights. Ferns beside the nocturnal pools remain folded through the day. Flowers near a dawn-lit basin open whenever that water brightens, regardless of the actual sun. The rare lizard avoids all reflected darkness, stepping around it with care. Nothing else appears troubled.

There is no sense that the phenomenon is new or unstable. The stone cups have held their separate skies through ages of erosion. Roots bend around them. Moss stops at their rims. Water evaporates from them reluctantly, and when one finally dries, its borrowed hour persists as a patch of light on the bare rock until rain returns.

Change here is patient. Trees lean a little farther downhill each century. Springs exchange channels as roots thicken and decay. Mineral stains lengthen by the width of a finger over a lifetime. The basin has reached no final form, but its transformations are so gradual that they resemble customs rather than events.

At the open edge, all the water threads gather into a narrow seep and vanish beneath a lip of stone. Far below, perhaps, they become a stream. From within the basin there is only the sound of their departure: a deep, hidden pouring that never grows louder and never quite stops.
