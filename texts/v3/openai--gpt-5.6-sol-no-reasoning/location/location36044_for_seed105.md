The country lies beneath an ocean that has no surface.

There is water above, water below, and water extending outward through a chain of drowned highlands. Yet nothing here suggests an ordinary seabed. The terrain rises in long ridges and steep-sided valleys, preserving the shape of a mountain region rather than a continental shelf. Black summits stand thousands of fathoms above deeper plains but remain submerged under an immeasurable ceiling. No sunlight has ever reached them. Height is felt only through pressure, slope, and the direction in which loose things fall.

The water is bitterly cold. It moves in broad, slow layers that sometimes shear against one another, bending vegetation eastward on one ridge and westward on the next. Where these invisible currents meet, they twist into vertical rivers. Silt, pale leaves, and splinters of stone climb through the dark as though carried up a chimney. Whole forests bow, straighten, and bow again.

Most of the mountains are bare. Their surfaces have been worked smooth by an age of immersion: rounded ledges, fluted walls, deep runnels cut by mineral-heavy currents. Exposed rock is dark green, blue-black, or the dull gray of quenched ash. Along fault lines, chalk-white deposits have gathered in terraces like frozen foam. The landscape appears settled into these forms, neither young nor ruined, every cliff and basin worn until it belongs precisely where it is.

Between the ridges lie wide valleys filled with black water. The emptiness of them is considerable. One may travel for hours over rippled sediment and encounter only a single boulder, a grove the size of a room, or an upright post whose purpose has been forgotten. Distance is difficult to judge. In the absence of a horizon, features appear abruptly at the limit of whatever light is carried, then vanish just as completely behind.

Vegetation grows wherever the stone offers a seam.

It is lush but discontinuous: dense islands separated by great tracts of open ground. Forests of ribbonweed occupy sheltered slopes, their fronds broad as sails and almost colorless. Their roots enter hairline cracks and slowly pry the mountains apart. On higher spurs grow rigid branching forms resembling leafless trees, though their wood is translucent and flexible. Each branch carries cups that open toward changes in pressure rather than toward light. When a strong current passes, thousands of these cups turn at once.

Low mats cover some valley floors. They are velvety, dark, and warm by comparison with the surrounding water. From them rise hollow stalks containing dim green fluid. The fluid circulates in pulses from root to tip, but it gives little illumination—only enough to reveal that the plants are moving when the water is still. There are no grazing marks, no burrows, no eggs attached beneath the leaves. Nothing bites, nests, swims, crawls, or feeds.

There has never been animal life here.

This absence is not quiet.

The mountains sound continuously. Water is forced through tunnels and fissures, producing low horns that can be heard across whole valleys. Stone plates grind deep underground. Stands of cup-trees clap and knock together in the current, while hollow reeds emit piercing, sustained tones. At intervals, trapped gases escape from the sediment in violent columns. The rising bubbles expand, burst against overhangs, and make the dark tremble like distant thunder.

Some sounds have regularity. On the northern ridges, a sequence of three heavy blows passes through the rock every few minutes. Farther downslope, something answers with five. These are not signals. They result from chambers filling and emptying under pressure, though the precision of the exchanges makes that explanation hard to keep in mind.

The scattered structures are easiest to find by listening.

They stand on saddles between peaks, in shallow caverns, and beside the mouths of the sounding tunnels. Most are built from fitted stone without mortar. They are modest things: circular shelters, waist-high walls, stairs descending into sediment, clusters of pillars tied together with braided plant fiber. No settlement contains more than a few buildings, and many structures are separated from the next by miles of open water.

Their construction is simple and painstaking. Stones were struck into shape with harder stones. Holes were bored by sand and patient rotation. Transparent branches were split into pegs; ribbonweed was twisted into rope. The makers possessed no engines, no sealed vessels, and little metal. Copper survives here and there as green stains around a rivet or the outline of a blade long since dissolved.

Doors were unnecessary. Roofs are pierced to let currents through. Some shelters contain stone platforms bordered by grooves, as though occupants once rested there and required anchors to keep from drifting away. Others contain hand-sized bowls carved directly into the floor. The bowls continually fill with pale mineral sediment, then empty themselves when the pressure changes. Rows of notches on nearby walls may record these cycles.

There are no bones.

There are no images of bodies, either. The old carvings depict currents as nested lines, mountains seen in profile, branching plants, and geometric cavities. One recurring mark resembles a circle with its center removed. It appears over entrances, on tools, and on solitary boundary stones far out in the valleys.

The most intact structure occupies the crest of a high ridge. It consists of seven rings of standing slabs, each ring lower than the one within it. The current flows uphill through the complex. Leaves and grit are drawn along the slope, pass between the stones, and rise through an opening at the center. There they disappear—not into a vent, but into a region of water darker than the surrounding dark.

This is one of the place’s rules: darkness is not uniform.

Some darkness merely conceals. Some has depth of its own. A carried lamp, whether chemical or made from glowing plant sap, can illuminate rock and vegetation, but its light bends away from certain hollows. These patches migrate slowly. They collect beneath shelves, inside buildings, and in the lee of pillars, behaving almost like sediment. A sufficiently strong current can tear one loose. It then travels as a black sheet across the landscape, extinguishing the faint light of every stalk it touches. The plants recover after it passes, though they tend afterward to grow in a different direction.

The water also remembers motion.

A disturbance may recur long after its cause is gone. Push through a grove and, days later, the fronds may part again along the same path. Strike a wall and the impact can return at irregular intervals, softer each time, over the course of years. Around the oldest ruins, the accumulated repetition has become hazardous. Ropes pull themselves taut in remembrance of vanished loads. Stone doors shudder with the echo of being opened. Clouds of silt repeatedly rise in the outline of absent feet.

Perhaps the builders are not entirely absent. Perhaps what remains of them is distributed through these delayed motions: no organisms, no ghosts, only gestures retained by the medium in which they lived.

Above the mountain chain, the water grows colder and clearer. The summits support sparse gardens of glassy shrubs. Their roots spread across stone in precise spirals, gathering frostlike crystals from the current. From these heights, during rare blooms of mineral light, several valleys become visible at once: ridges receding in blue-black tiers, pale forests in protected basins, white vents, isolated roofs, and enormous curtains of vegetation streaming from cliffs.

Still farther above lies not air but a denser, heavier ocean suspended overhead.

Its lower boundary can sometimes be seen as a wavering plane across the darkness. Against expectation, the denser water does not descend. It presses downward without mixing, a second sea resting upon the first. Lightning—or something with the shape of lightning—travels inside it, illuminating vast branching shadows. Each flash arrives silently. The sound follows much later as a crushing boom that rolls through the valleys, dislodges stones, and sets every hollow plant singing.

These upper storms drive the country’s active seasons. Currents strengthen. Warm mineral water erupts from the faults. Forest mats spread rapidly over fresh sediment, then split apart as the ground heaves. Curtains of ribbonweed tear loose and migrate across valleys like drifting fields. They snag on ruins, bury stairways, and root wherever they settle. Entire groves may be overturned, their translucent root systems exposed to the dark.

Nothing consumes the fallen vegetation. It does not rot in the usual way. Instead it slowly becomes clear, then brittle, then stone. Much of the pale terracing on the mountains is petrified growth laid down layer by layer. The landscape erodes and grows at once: currents carve the cliffs while forests rebuild them in mineral form.

In the deepest basin, all sound eventually converges. Horn calls from the tunnels, subterranean grinding, reed shrieks, thunder from the upper ocean, and the remembered blows of old tools travel down through water and rock. They overlap there until no source can be separated from another. The basin floor vibrates continually.

At its center stands a small roofless house.

It is the simplest construction in the drowned range: four fitted walls, one stone platform, and a doorway facing the downward slope. Thick vegetation surrounds it but does not enter. Recurrent currents sweep its floor clean. Above the platform hangs a loop of braided fiber, impossibly preserved, swaying in a motion that does not match the water.

Every few hours, the loop is drawn downward.

The platform gives a soft knock.

Across the valley, then along the neighboring ridge, ancient chambers answer one by one.
