The high country is entirely built, though no hand has ever worked here.

From a distance it resembles a chain of green plateaus suspended above the cloud line: broad, uneven tables linked by saddles, causeways, and long ascending ramps. Their margins are sharp and new. Walls descend in sheer pleats, glossy after rain, while narrow towers rise from the flats like unfinished scaffolds. There is no exposed stone. There is not even soil in the usual sense. Every surface—from the load-bearing depths to the thinnest parapet—is living vegetable matter.

The landscape constructs itself.

Great trunks lie horizontally beneath the plateaus, fitted together in lattices wider than rivers. They are not fallen trees but structural organs, grown straight across empty air and locked at their crossings by swollen wooden joints. Above them spread floors of densely braided roots, several hundred layers deep. Bark slabs overlap like roof tiles. Hollow stems make drainage galleries, buttresses, stairs, retaining walls, and vaulted reservoirs. Fine roots pack the seams. Moss seals them against evaporation. Where a section bears too much weight, pale ribs push up from below and harden in the sun.

Yet for all this construction, the country feels open and sparsely occupied. Its architecture is immense, but its vertical elements stand far apart. A single column-tree may anchor an entire green square kilometer. One can look across long expanses of low, wet weave to distant pylons, each carrying a loose crown of sails. Between them there are broad courts empty of anything taller than ankle-high fronds. The emptiness appears deliberate, as if the plateau were a city planned for inhabitants that never arrived.

There are no animals. No insects move under leaves. No birds cross the hot sky. Nothing grazes, pollinates, hunts, burrows, or watches.

The plants have assumed all those roles themselves.

Vines travel. They lift their leading ends, feel across gaps, and lower themselves elsewhere. Bladder-flowers pulse pollen into ducts shared with distant groves. Seed pods crawl by repeatedly swelling on one side and shrinking on the other. Carnivory exists, but without prey: pitcher organs digest obsolete leaves, failed bridges, and pieces of the structure cut away during repair. Waste is rare. Dead tissue is softened, liquefied, and drawn back into the living floor.

Water is everywhere near the surface. Rain falls often, hard and warm, but even between storms the atmosphere sweats. Condensation pearls on waxy balustrades and runs along veins designed as gutters. Floors give slightly underfoot, releasing clear water through pores. The plateau edges weep continuously into the clouds below.

The largest reservoirs are open chambers sunk into the plazas. Their rims are smooth, green, and steep. Hot rainwater fills them to the lip, stained amber by sap, then empties without warning through muscular drains. The released water can be heard passing far beneath the surface: a massive internal rushing, like rivers in the walls of a house.

Heat persists despite the elevation. The light is ordinary daylight—white sun, slate storm shade, occasional gold toward evening—but the air has the saturated warmth of a greenhouse. Mist does not cool the skin. Rain strikes warm. Sap collecting in shallow channels can become almost too hot to touch, heated by fermentation in the lower layers. Now and then a floor vent opens and exhales a humid vegetal breath smelling of cut grass, resin, pepper, and deep stagnant water.

The country is young. Its edges have not softened. New-grown walls retain ridges left by their expansion, and towers end in pointed buds rather than weathered crowns. Causeways meet plazas at awkward angles. Some stairways climb into blank walls because the chamber they were meant to enter has not yet been grown. Elsewhere doors appear first, freestanding frames of lignified vine, while rooms slowly gather around them.

This youth makes the place dangerous, not through intention but through continual revision.

Nothing remains architecturally settled. A long gallery may contract in the afternoon and become a tunnel. A courtyard may crease down its center, rise like a folding leaf, and shed its reservoir in two opposing cataracts. Bridges thicken under rain, grow top-heavy, and split into branching alternatives. Towers shed whole skins of bark that sail across the open courts and root wherever they land. A wall that was load-bearing in the morning can turn translucent by noon as its substance is withdrawn to reinforce some distant ridge.

The changes are not gradual enough to ignore. They come in waves.

Before one begins, the smaller leaves rotate to expose their pale undersides. Water trembles in every cup. Then a deep strain passes through the plateau. Joints crack like felled timber. Root cables snap taut in the hollows. Floors bulge, seams unzip, and fresh structures surge upward, wet and brilliantly colored. Their growth is audible: a chorus of splitting fibers, creaking cellulose, popping bladders, and broad leaves slapping open.

The sound travels for leagues.

Thunder contributes, but the landscape is louder than the weather. Rain drums on acres of hollow foliage. Waterfalls roar from the margins. Vents whistle. Cane towers knock against one another across wide intervals, producing notes so low they can be felt through the floor. When storms cross the plateau, the crowns of the distant columns unfurl into vast ribbed fans. Wind catches them, and the entire high country bellows.

Afterward comes a brief, uncanny stillness—not silence, because water continues everywhere, ticking, dripping, pouring—but a pause in construction. New surfaces shine raw beneath the normal gray light. Sap beads from clean fractures. Root stitches begin drawing broken edges together.

The storms do not merely batter the architecture. They appear to instruct it.

Rain striking one district causes walls to rise in another. Lightning, when it touches a tower, runs harmlessly through wet veins and triggers an eruption of white tendrils along the horizon. Wind pressure is remembered in the grain. Each gale leaves the structures better braced against the direction it came from, though less prepared for all others. Thus the plateau becomes increasingly elaborate and increasingly unstable: a record of recent weather written as rooms, arches, reservoirs, and buttresses.

No central plan is visible. The place is coordinated by liquids and tension. Changes in water level pass as messages through connected stems. A cut or collapse alters pressure, and whole valleys respond. What looks from above like a designed metropolis is closer to a single distributed organism dreaming in architecture.

Its rules are simple but produce strange results.

Nothing may remain empty for long unless emptiness serves a structural purpose. Nothing may die without becoming material. Water must descend, but the living floors continually lift it again through capillaries, so rivers circulate vertically as much as they travel downhill. Shade causes ceilings. Repeated footsteps—if footsteps existed—would cause roads. Persistent sound thickens nearby walls. A cavity that resonates strongly enough is preserved; a silent one is gradually filled.

This gives the country an eerie acoustical geography. There are amphitheaters grown around the roar of drains, cloisters surrounding the drip of condensation, and miles of tubular corridors devoted to carrying thunder from one plateau to the next. Certain arches repeat the boom of distant collapses long after the original sound has died. The plants respond by reinforcing those echoes as if they were current events. Sometimes an old thunderclap, trapped and returned by living chambers, causes a tower to brace against a storm that passed years before.

Sparse groves occupy the broad courts. Each is less a collection of plants than a complex building with botanical habits. One resembles a many-story fountain: stacked bowls of leaves overflow into one another, feeding a dark cistern at its base. Another is a cage of red roots enclosing an empty shaft through which hot mist rises. Farther on, a low dome opens at dawn into radial streets and closes at dusk into a sealed, rain-filled bud.

Between these structures lie long distances of woven green pavement. Tiny transparent leaves cover it, all aligned. They sink when clouds gather, allowing rain into the tissue below; afterward they rise together and cast millions of pinprick reflections. Walking there would feel like crossing the back of some immeasurably large resting creature, except that the creature never truly rests.

At the limits of the plateaus, the made world remains unfinished. Root bridges thrust outward over cloud-filled space and end in searching fans. Drops collect at their tips, fall, and vanish into brightness below. Some bridges find neighboring heights and fuse with them. Others become too long, bow under their own wet mass, and tear away with a sound like a mountain splitting. Their fragments descend only briefly. Seeded along every edge, they unfold in the warm air into gliding sheets, drifting until they catch on a lower tower and begin another district.

So the landscape expands by collapse as much as by growth.

Toward evening, storms often move beneath the plateaus. Lightning illuminates the clouds from within, outlining the hanging roots and downward-reaching foundations. For a moment one sees that the apparent mountaintops are only upper surfaces. Below them extends an inverted forest of columns, reservoirs, curtains, and slowly lengthening probes. Rain pours both downward from the plateau and upward in wind-torn veils from the cloud sea. The whole structure seems suspended between two wet atmospheres.

There are no lamps when darkness comes. Pale tissues briefly retain the day’s light, and fermenting pools give off a weak green haze, but most of the country disappears. Its immense processes continue audibly: the creak of contracting spans, the concussion of water gates, the hiss of warm vapor, the wet rip of a new chamber opening.

By morning, the skyline has changed.

A tower stands where there was an open court. A familiar causeway has branched into seven narrow paths. One reservoir has lifted itself bodily onto root legs and begun a slow migration toward the eastern edge. In the distance, a whole fresh plateau unfolds from a compact knot, surfaces flattening and locking together while waterfalls pour from every seam.

Under plain daylight, with no witness and no builder, the green city resumes making itself—vast, hot, rain-polished, and perpetually new.
