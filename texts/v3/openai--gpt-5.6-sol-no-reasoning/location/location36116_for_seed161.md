The town occupies a cleft near the summit of an immense mesa, though from within it the surrounding height is difficult to grasp. Its boundaries are close: two walls of old red stone, a narrow opening toward the sky, and at either end a steep stair cut into the rock. Yet beyond those stairs the land falls away for several thousand feet. Clouds travel below the town. On clear days, distant plains appear as pale, level bands through the lower gates.

The cleft is warm, dim, and crowded.

Its stone walls lean inward without quite meeting. Centuries of wind have rounded every exposed edge, polished the upper ledges, and worn the mesa into its final durable shapes. Nothing here looks newly broken. The cliffs are old enough to have lost their rubble; all loose material departed long ago, cast grain by grain into the country below. What remains is hard ironstone, dense and dark, streaked with ochre and black mineral varnish. Handholds have become hollows. Steps have sagged into shallow bowls beneath generations of bare feet.

There is no spring. No stream enters the cleft, and rain may fail for years. The town lives by dryness rather than merely enduring it.

Its plants do not contain much liquid. They are tough, fibrous things fed by dust, sunlight, and the faint moisture of breath. Gray cable-vines cling to the shaded cliffs, driving hair-thin roots into seams in the rock. Their leaves are small overlapping scales with waxy undersides. Higher up, where the cleft receives a brief bar of afternoon sun, fan-crowned shrubs grow from clay jars and masonry pockets. They are cultivated for pith, cordage, bitter flour, and cool shade. Pillar succulents stand in fenced courtyards, but their sap is caustic and never drunk. When cut and heated, it yields lamp oil and a rubbery sealing pitch.

Much of the apparent greenery is lichen: broad olive, rust, and charcoal crusts spread over roofs and cliff faces. The people scrape it for dye and medicine but harvest by strict rotation. A stripped wall may require thirty years to recover. Each patch therefore belongs not to a household but to a generation.

Buildings fill nearly every usable surface. They are made of fitted stone, sun-cured clay, woven fiber, hide, and old timber carried up from the distant lowlands. The lower houses are half excavated into the cliff and connected by passages barely wide enough for one person. Above them, rooms stand on stone brackets and poles, their flat roofs serving as lanes for the next tier. Ladders rise through hatchways. Bridges of braided vine cross overhead. Storerooms, shrines, workshops, nesting shelves, sleeping alcoves, and animal pens occupy separate layers until the distinction between town and cliff becomes uncertain.

No structure is high in itself, but the settlement climbs vertically. A person may enter a dwelling through its roof, descend three levels in darkness, cross a public corridor, and emerge onto a balcony hundreds of feet above the plains.

The highest masonry is ancient and nearly featureless. Its builders cut blocks so closely that no blade fits between them. Later generations have fastened lighter structures to these foundations: clay ovens, screened lofts, awnings, cages, and platforms roofed with woven mats. Repairs are conspicuous and continual. Pale fresh plaster lies over smoke-blackened walls. Pegs, lashings, wedges, and patches reveal the life of the town more clearly than monuments do.

Metal exists, but sparingly. A family may possess one bronze knife or a copper awl. Most tools are chipped stone, horn, fired clay, and polished bone. The hardest work is done with lever, wedge, rope, patience, and many hands. Doors turn on stone pins. Grain is ground in saddle querns. Freight moves up the mesa in hide slings along fixed ropes, hauled by teams chanting in the upper gatehouse.

Those hauling songs contribute to the town’s constant noise.

Although the place changes slowly, it is seldom quiet. Sound gathers between the cliffs and cannot escape. Pigeons thunder from nesting holes at dawn. Small cliff antelope stamp and snort in pens. Dogs bark from roofs at shapes far below. Fiber looms knock steadily in shaded chambers. Pestles strike mortars. Stonecutters tap rather than hammer, listening for hidden fractures. Somewhere, almost always, a pulley complains under load.

Voices carry with startling clarity. An argument on the seventh tier reaches the market floor enlarged by echoes. Children use coded whistles across the cleft. Traders announce themselves from the lower stair long before they appear. At midday, when work briefly subsides, insects fill the interval: dry-winged cicadas, burrowing crickets, and black bees nesting in tubes built into warm walls.

Fauna thrives because the town concentrates food, shelter, and shade in an otherwise severe landscape. Lizards crowd the sunlit parapets. Bats sleep behind reed screens and emerge at dusk in such numbers that the narrow sky seems to clot and move. Pale moths feed on flowering cable-vines. Seed mice live beneath granary floors, pursued by lean cats and small brown snakes. Beetles are gathered from dung pits and roasted. The cliff antelope provide milk, hair, manure, and horn, though never much meat; an animal is too valuable to kill except at the end of its long working life.

Outside the settlement, birds own the height. Vultures rest on the mesa rim. Hawks drift almost level with the upper courtyards, watching the roofs. People suspend mirrored shells and feathered cords above infant rooms to discourage them.

Warmth remains in the rock after sunset, so the nights are mild at first and only gradually cool. Daylight itself enters reluctantly. For most hours the town receives a muted red glow reflected from one cliff onto the other. Direct sun crosses the floor for less than an hour, and people mark seasons by the path of that bright stripe. Workshops shift with it. Animals turn their bodies toward it. Mats of seeds and sliced cactus pith are hurried into it to dry.

Fire is tightly governed. Every flame must stand in a clay basin, and open lamps are forbidden above the middle tiers, where roofs are fibrous and close together. Cooking takes place in communal ovens sunk into bare rock. At night, most illumination comes from shallow niches containing slow-burning wicks. From across the cleft these lights resemble dull amber eyes, stacked one above another.

Water, when it appears at all, is treated as an unpredictable mineral deposit. A rare storm may send a brief sheet down the mesa, but the town has no open cisterns; evaporation would take nearly everything. Instead, gutters lead into sealed underground jars, their mouths plugged with wax and clay. Such stores are reserved for infants, illness, and ritual. Daily life depends on moisture obtained from food, animal milk, succulent pulp processed to remove toxins, and dew combs set high on the rim during the coolest weeks. People wash with warmed sand, ash, scrapers, and aromatic smoke. Vessels are cleaned by abrasion. Floors are swept constantly, though dust always returns.

The oldest law of the cleft is that nothing loose may be thrown over the edge.

Below the town lie paths, grazing shelves, and the lower stair, and even a potsherd dropped from the summit can kill. Refuse is therefore sorted with care. Bone becomes tools, dung becomes fuel and soil, broken pottery becomes aggregate, fiber becomes pulp, and ash is packed into wall cavities. The town grows partly from the accumulated remains of its own use. Its floors rise imperceptibly over generations. Doorways must occasionally be recut upward.

Time is visible less in ruins than in accommodation. A corridor bends around a boulder no one remembers falling. A shrine has been polished blank by hands. A public stair preserves two parallel sets of depressions: narrow ancient footprints near the wall and broader modern ones toward the drop. Families inherit the duty of replacing particular ropes, feeding particular animals, tending particular lichen fields, or listening for cracks in particular sections of cliff.

There are no sudden seasons. Flowering moves slowly from the warm lower gate to the upper ledges. Animal births follow months later. Dust darkens the lichens; then years of wind clean them again. Houses are added one chamber at a time and abandoned just as gradually. A sealed room may remain inside the town for centuries, known only as a thickness in a wall.

The mesa itself appears finished. Wind still passes over it, hot and tireless, but has little left to take. Its summit is bare pavement; its flanks are smooth escarpments banded by vanished ages. The cleft survives because it is one of the few imperfections erosion could not widen. Sheltered inside that flaw, the settlement has become dense, noisy, and alive—a small vertical world fastened to an enormous height.

From the uppermost platform, reached by a worn ladder and a final crawl between stones, the town disappears beneath one’s feet. Only smoke threads, pigeon wings, and the irregular drumming of work rise out of the crack. Beyond it stretches a vast dry country under a bleached sky.

Then the wind shifts. Warm air climbs from the plains, carrying dust and the faint smell of stone. Every suspended bridge tightens. Every awning strains in the same direction. Thousands of leaves turn their pale undersides at once.

For a moment the whole cleft brightens, not with water or flame, but with the soft silver of a living place conserving everything it has.
