The whole region lies under warm water, though “under” is not reliable here.

At first it resembles a drowned industrial lowland: a broad basin of refineries, drainage canals, worker settlements, and cultivated floodplain submerged beneath a dark inland sea. The bottom is never far away. Chimneys rise through the murk like black reeds. Roof ridges, gantries, reservoir rims, and the upper forks of electrical pylons appear and vanish among forests of vegetation. There is no clear surface overhead—only a trembling brightness, dim as tarnished metal, sometimes ten meters above and sometimes apparently beneath the mud.

The water is warm enough to feel inhabited, but nothing animal lives in it.

No fish turn between the buildings. No insects skate the intermittent surfaces. There are no shell beds, larvae, worms, or pale scavengers in the sediment. Even the smallest drifting bodies are vegetable: spores, seed fluff, shredded membrane, pollen clots, green filaments. The absence is so complete that every quick movement looks, for an instant, like life—and then resolves into a leaf caught in machinery.

Vegetation has taken the basin with extraordinary force. It grows in dense tiers from every material, but does not soften the place. Young roots split fresh concrete along sharp, clean fractures. Glossy vines cable themselves between pylons. Broad translucent leaves overlap in the water like panes of green glass, each one pulsing slowly as pressure waves pass through it. From flooded rail beds rise thickets of jointed stems, copper-colored at the base and nearly black at their tips. Mats of fernlike growth hang upside down from the false surface. Pale root curtains descend from empty air and stop abruptly, as if inserted into the world along invisible seams.

This is new growth over a new ruin. Nothing has had time to round off.

The broken slabs retain keen corners. Excavator bites are still legible in embankments. Landslips expose bands of orange clay whose edges have not yet dissolved. Where roads have buckled, their asphalt stands in jagged fins, each painted line still bright. Yet the basin feels ancient because the plants are already enormous and because the machinery has no human scale in operation. It seems less abandoned than promoted into some larger, impersonal phase of manufacture.

The noise is continuous and crushing.

Deep below the sediment, turbines turn. Their vibration travels through water, steel, roots, and bone as one sustained mechanical weather. Pump stations hammer without pause, although most are wholly flooded. Somewhere beyond sight, a chain of stamping presses strikes in irregular sequence: nine impacts, silence, three impacts, a long metallic tearing, then nine again. Sirens rise and flatten into pure pressure. Loose roofs drum. Pipes bellow. Cavitation blooms around unseen rotors with the sound of mountains being ground to slurry.

Because sound moves so powerfully through the basin, distance collapses. A valve opening five kilometers away seems to occur inside the listener’s teeth. The creak of a nearby stem may arrive from every direction. There are moments when the machinery synchronizes by chance and the entire flooded plain becomes a single deafening chord. Leaves fold inward. Silt leaps from the bottom. Concrete dust erupts from submerged walls. Then the chord breaks apart, and thousands of separate engines resume their quarrel.

No one tends them.

Small structures remain scattered among the growth: inspection huts, relay cabinets, concrete kiosks, steel stair towers, a few windowless dormitories. Most are built on low pads and are consequently almost lost in the weeds and water. Lamps burn inside some of them. Behind wired glass, yellow indicators blink with a deliberation that suggests messages, though the patterns never repeat. Cable bundles leave these buildings and pass into the mud. When uncovered, the cables are warm.

The larger industrial works are less stable. They are built and unbuilt by the basin itself.

A refinery may stand on the eastern horizon for an hour, crowded with columns and flare stacks, all pouring black water upward. Later the same place is an open field of submerged grass. A conveyor emerges from a hillside that was not there yesterday, runs across the flooded valley, and feeds armfuls of living vines into a crusher. The crusher compacts them into wet green blocks. These travel onward, disappear into a tank, and reappear overhead as clouds of leaves. Sometimes an entire assembly line passes slowly through the landscape without moving: first its loading end manifests beside you, then successive portions become real farther west, while the portions behind cease to have existed. Bolts, grease marks, and caution labels are all exquisitely precise during their brief tenure.

The dominant process is not decay but revision.

Water columns descend sideways. Rain falls in sealed rooms. Pools form vertical walls that can be entered like doorways, though what lies through them is usually another part of the same basin at a different stage of construction. Buoyancy changes without warning. A person may begin sinking toward the mud, pass a hovering stand of bamboo, and continue “downward” into the side of a warehouse. Inside, the water hangs in rectangular volumes between dry aisles. Forklifts rest on walls. Moss grows toward several competing ceilings.

There are many surfaces, none sovereign.

Some are silver membranes stretched among treetops. Some are ragged and foam-edged, rolling vertically between buildings. One appears as a perfectly flat black disk half buried in a field. Leaves that touch it emerge from another disk kilometers away. At times the whole region turns over—not violently, but conceptually. Mud becomes sky-colored; chimneys hang downward; root systems spread above like storm clouds. The machinery continues operating through each inversion, its oil never separating from the water, its flames burning as dark, cold hollows.

The low ground is divided into districts by fresh levees and canals. These features are too sharply made to be old, but no excavators are visible at work. Channels open in minutes as the soil separates along ruler-straight lines. Warm water rushes in, bearing mats of vegetation and fragments of architecture. Elsewhere, trenches close, pressing together so neatly that vines crossing them are fused stem to stem. New islands thrust up only a meter or two: raw clay platforms crowded almost at once by reeds, pipes, switchgear, and flowering creepers. Their flowers are large and colorless. They open in response to factory sirens rather than light.

There is reproduction here, but no pollination. Seedpods develop within hours, split, and release screws, glass beads, or coils of fine copper along with seeds. Certain shrubs fruit with ceramic insulators. A low succulent accumulates charge and discharges blue arcs through neighboring plants, creating temporary electrical groves. Black mangrove-like trees root in cooling ponds and draw metal salts into their trunks; when broken, they show concentric rings of wood, aluminum, wood, and braided wire.

The flora behaves less like an invading jungle than like an alternative industry.

It sorts sediment. It raises scaffolds. It filters oils into fragrant resins. Vines enter intake pipes and emerge from exhaust manifolds as woven mats. Leaf clusters rotate on their stems, driving small generators. Great bladder plants inflate beneath collapsed buildings and lift them, slowly, into new positions. There is no sign of intention, but there are standards. Repeated forms appear throughout the basin: the same seven-branched root joint, the same hexagonal leaf spacing, the same precise bore through concrete. When growth deviates, nearby machines begin to howl, and the deviant patch is usually crushed, drowned, or folded out of space.

The water itself may be the factory’s principal material.

It varies in density from corridor to corridor. In one canal it is almost vaporous; in the next it supports towers. Some volumes are granular, pouring like warm glass beads. Others stretch when disturbed and snap back with a thunderclap. Pipes pump water from full reservoirs into empty ones without changing either level. Vast quantities are compressed into trembling cubes stacked in yards between creepers. Occasionally one cube ruptures. Its contents do not flood outward but bloom: branching sheets of liquid propagate through soil, walls, leaves, and existing water, making every boundary briefly visible.

During such events, the landscape reveals how crowded it truly is. Hidden canals shine inside roads. Buried pipe networks flare beneath the mud. Roots occupy walls, boilers, tanks, and air alike. Structures overlap without displacing one another. A stand of broadleaf plants may contain, in its volume, three pump houses and the lower half of a transmission tower, each solid from its own angle. The basin is not packed merely with matter but with incompatible versions of itself.

The turbulence never clears anything away. It adds.

Storms develop below the water. Dark spirals of silt twist along the bottom, dragging sheds and vegetation into slowly turning columns. Electrical discharges crawl horizontally through the murk, illuminating acres of leaf and steel in stark green flashes. Pressure fronts bend chimneys, flatten forests, and send unsecured machines tumbling end over end. Minutes later the plants rise unbroken. The chimneys may return taller, or multiplied, or made of compacted roots.

There are intervals of relative calm, but silence never comes. In the calm, one can see across the basin: dim ridges barely above the flood, drowned neighborhoods, stacks and pylons fading into bronze-green distance. The openness is deceptive. Sight travels, movement does not. A destination can remain the same apparent distance away for days. Roads coil beneath travelers. Canal junctions exchange positions. Landmarks drift without disturbing the mud around them.

Then the production sirens begin.

Every plant turns the same face toward an unseen center. Pumps accelerate. Floodgates open onto water already present. The false surfaces overhead sag. Buildings flicker through successive designs—brick, steel, root, concrete, membrane—while maintaining the same footprint. Across the regional plain, lines of pale light ignite inside submerged pipes. They converge somewhere beyond the refinery districts, beneath the lowest part of the basin.

Whatever lies there is never exposed for long. Sometimes it appears to be a turbine of impossible diameter, its blades turning through soil and water without moving either. Sometimes it is a forest arranged in concentric circles, every trunk vibrating like a driven piston. Sometimes it is simply a depth: a downward opening in a place where down has otherwise failed, filled with roaring darkness and warm rain.

Everything in the basin seems to feed it, or to be expelled by it.

The young land, the drowned works, the lush metallic vegetation, even the laws that decide which way water falls—none feel permanent. They are trial products moving through an immense submerged plant. The place is manufacturing its own geography in real time, rejecting whole valleys, recycling horizons, pressing fresh islands from mud and memory.

And because there are no animals, there is nothing here that flees.

The leaves endure the thunder. The towers endure until revised. The water receives every collapse and immediately begins constructing another.
