The land lies low enough that the horizon is mostly water and accumulated weather. It is not a sea, though from a distance it resembles one: a dim metallic breadth extending beneath a ceiling of cloud, divided into innumerable shallow basins by ridges worn nearly flat. Water fills every depression. It films the stone, gathers in seams, hangs beneath overhangs, and wells soundlessly from pores too fine to see. Nothing here appears dry. Even the air has a surface tension.

There is no vegetation. No moss softens the rock, no reeds stand at the margins, no buried roots interrupt the slabs. The plain has been exposed for so long that erosion has passed beyond ruin into refinement. Peaks, walls, and loose debris have all been reduced to low, interlocking forms: black shelves, lead-colored terraces, shallow cups of blue ice, and rounded blocks fitted together with the intimacy of old teeth. Sediment occupies every gap. Ice occupies the sediment. Water occupies hairline spaces in the ice.

Yet the country feels overcrowded.

Across the plain, in densities difficult to comprehend, living things gather around the pools. They are not large. Individually, most could be held in two hands; many are smaller than a fingernail. They possess no recognizable symmetry. Some resemble hinged flakes of slate, opening and closing along bright internal seams. Others are clear bladders with rings of dark cilia turning inside them, as though their mouths have been swallowed and continue feeding from within. Thick shoals of needle-bodied animals thread beneath the surface film without breaking it. Pale discs cling to wet rock, migrating so slowly that only the clean trails behind them reveal motion.

They are fauna only by behavior. They pursue, graze, mate, flee, molt, swarm, and die. No flora supports them. The first layer of the food web feeds on differences: gradients of pressure, cold, salinity, electrical charge, and perhaps time. Around every place where meltwater touches ancient brine, tiny glassy animals arrange themselves in concentric ranks. They drink the boundary. When the two waters finish mixing, the ranks collapse, and larger creatures descend upon them.

This happens everywhere at once.

The cold is profound but not absolute. Water remains liquid under thin skins of ice, warmed locally by friction, mineral reactions, and the immense metabolism of the swarms. In some pools, cold rain strikes and freezes from below upward. In others, ice melts without gaining heat. Fog condenses in horizontal sheets a few centimeters above the ground, each sheet carrying droplets that move in strict parallel, as if sliding along invisible panes.

The light has no evident source. It is a low gray illumination permeating cloud, water, and stone unevenly. Shadows sometimes appear before the bodies that should cast them. Far out on the flats, bands of cobalt radiance pass beneath the rock like searchlights under thin cloth. At their approach, every animal becomes still. Their shells close. Their cilia halt. Even falling rain hesitates in the air.

Then the band passes, and the plain erupts again.

The loudness is continuous. There is the ordinary clatter of hail and shell, the suction of water pulled through millions of feeding apertures, the crack of expanding ice. Beneath this is a deeper industrial tumult: pistons laboring underground, rotors spinning without housings, long conveyor rhythms transmitted through bedrock. No machine can be found. There are no buildings, ducts, wires, roads, slag heaps, or manufactured objects. The landscape is untouched, and nevertheless it operates at the scale of a factory district.

One ridge emits a hammering pulse every eleven seconds. Another gives off a turbine whine that rises for several hours, shears abruptly into three separate notes, then begins again. Across the central basins, jets of compressed vapor blast from natural fissures in coordinated sequences. The vents open and shut like valves, though their edges are smooth with geological age. Water is drawn into one set of cracks and expelled kilometers away as freezing mist laden with powdered metal.

Nothing appears to control this system. Nothing appears to have built it.

The machinery may simply be what matter does here when it becomes sufficiently old.

The plain’s packed quality extends below its surface. Stone lies on stone without voids, but each stone contains chambers larger than the stone itself. Water entering a pinhole may be heard falling for days. Animals vanish into these apertures and later emerge from a neighboring pool, encrusted with minerals that do not exist in the surrounding country. Occasionally one returns older than its species. Occasionally it returns before it entered.

Predation is violent and brief. At intervals, the shallow water bulges into a moving mound. A creature like a mass of braided mercury passes under the basin floor—not beneath the water, but beneath the idea of depth. Shoals scatter. Hinged flakes clap shut in chain reactions, making a roar like thousands of dropped tools. The predator surfaces through whichever reflection is darkest, turning the reflected sky aside as if it were a membrane. It feeds without visibly consuming anything. Its prey remain present but lose motion, color, and history. Soon afterward, their bodies become indistinguishable from eroded stone.

Thus the terrain may consist partly of the dead. Perhaps entirely.

Rain comes often, though not always downward. Storms travel close to the ground, dense black columns dragging curtains of droplets sideways across the terraces. Some basins receive rain that rises from them and vanishes into cloud. Other showers fall only on animals, leaving the surrounding rock untouched. Thunder rolls below the horizon, transmitted through water before air, and the fauna orient themselves toward it in enormous rippling fields.

There are migrations. In the dim interval that serves as evening, millions of creatures leave the pools and move overland. Their bodies tile the flats so completely that the ground appears to flow. They follow no fixed channel, but their aggregate passage forms broad branching patterns resembling river deltas, vascular systems, and electrical diagrams. The pattern changes depending on where it is observed from. Seen at ground level, it leads toward warm seepage in the east. Seen from a ridge, it describes a vast rotary engine. Reflected in water, it spells an instruction in an alphabet no observer has yet learned—and perhaps cannot learn without being altered by it.

The oldest regions are the flattest. There, erosion has completed its work so perfectly that distance becomes unreliable. A person could walk toward a nearby pool for hours while watching it remain precisely nearby. Sound crosses these flats instantly, then arrives again much later by ordinary means. Rain rings spread through puddles before the drops hit. Predators chase prey into yesterday. Birth occurs in the wake of death rather than before it.

Despite all this, the place is not chaotic. Its impossibilities have habits.

Every process repeats, though never in the same order. The subterranean pounding regulates the opening of shells. The migrations influence the rain. The rain changes the salinity gradients on which the smallest animals feed. Their feeding alters electrical currents in the rock. Those currents illuminate the deep blue bands. The bands silence the plain, and in the silence something beneath the world advances one increment.

At the northern edge, if direction retains meaning, the basins merge into a broad black mere crowded from shore to shore with floating organisms. Their transparent bodies lock together into a single restless pavement. Waves pass through them without moving water. Under this living surface, immense circular shadows rotate. The factory noise is strongest here, a layered concussion of presses, bearings, furnaces, and assembly lines. Sometimes the mere exhales warm vapor smelling of iron and deep salt. Sometimes it produces new species in identical batches, thousands at once, and distributes them outward on currents that flow uphill.

No chimney marks the place. No hand has shaped it. There are no ruins because there was never a building age. Industry here precedes invention. Production precedes purpose.

Beyond the mere the fog thickens until it becomes load-bearing. Rain pools on its upper surface. Animals crawl into it and leave tracks that remain suspended after they have passed. Within the fog, silhouettes repeat at different scales: a small feeding animal, a basin shaped like that animal, the entire horizon shaped like the basin. Each contains the next. The farther one looks, the more crowded the landscape becomes, until open distance itself seems packed with copies, migrations, weather fronts, and unseen mechanisms.

And under everything—the water, the fitted stones, the abundant blind lives, the cold clouds and their reversed rain—there continues the tireless mechanical rhythm.

It does not sound as though the world is breaking.

It sounds as though the world is being manufactured, continuously, from its own worn-out remains.
