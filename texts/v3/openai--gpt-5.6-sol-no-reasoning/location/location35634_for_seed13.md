The city stands high inside a basin of new black stone.

The surrounding peaks are sharp enough to look recently broken from a larger mass. Their ridges have not yet softened into soil or scree; they rise in long, clean planes of charcoal basalt, pale granite, and greenish glass. Cracks run through them with the precision of joints in masonry. Nothing falls from these heights. No frost loosens the rock, no rain rounds it, and no river carries it away. Each ledge, chip, and splinter keeps the exact posture in which it first appeared.

Between the peaks opens a broad upland floor, several valleys wide but clearly bounded. It is not crowded. Outlying buildings sit far apart, with orchards, grazing lawns, exposed stone, and long damp meadows between them. Farther inward, those separate constructions gather into districts and terraces until they become a city: not immense, but complete, with walls, streets, reservoirs, workshops, shrines, animal courts, stairways, and several tall towers. From any height its plan is easy to read. The place has the orderliness of something made all at once.

Its builders possess little that is mechanically elaborate. The city is urban through coordination rather than advanced machinery. Every block was split with wedges, every beam trimmed with adzes, every drain lined by hand. Stone arches span the larger roads, while roofs are made from overlapping slate, turf, bark, or tightly woven reeds. Copper appears at door pivots and along ceremonial roofs, dulled to a permanent blue-green. Most tools are stone, bone, hardwood, or simple bronze. There are no engines, no turning mills, no smoke-belching furnaces. Water is lifted in clay jars. Goods are borne on backs, sledges, and the broad, patient shoulders of animals.

Yet the city does not seem unfinished or poor. Its low technology has produced a richness of surfaces. Thresholds are polished by feet but never wear further. Wooden columns carry dense carvings of leaves, eyes, paws, and mountain profiles. Walls are fitted so closely that moss grows only in lines chosen for it. Streets widen into plazas paved with hexagonal stones, then narrow into steep lanes where dwellings share retaining walls. Bridges connect terraces above ravines too fresh-edged to have been cut by ordinary water.

Moisture inhabits the basin without dominating it.

Rain is always present over one quarter of the western valley: a fine vertical curtain under a mild gray cloud. The curtain neither advances nor weakens. Beyond it, the air is dry and lucid. Along the boundary, individual drops seem to become themselves, falling into fern beds and shallow stone channels. Elsewhere condensation beads on north-facing walls, gathers beneath overhangs, and lies as silver on broad leaves. Springs occupy dark pockets at the bases of cliffs. They fill pools but never overflow. Narrow streams connect a few of these pools to the city, sliding over black rock without deepening their beds.

There is no true weather. The dampness is arranged.

The principal river begins beneath a split mountain east of the walls and passes through the basin in a succession of clear, level reaches. Its water moves—or gives every appearance of moving—but a floating leaf remains beside the same stair for as long as anyone watches. Ripples hold their curved shapes around bridge piers. White threads of water hang beneath sluice gates, bright in daylight, making almost no sound. At the city’s southern margin, the river enters a fresh fissure and is gone.

Gardens occupy the wetter terraces. They are lush in color but uncrowded: clumps of tall ferns, blue-leaved shrubs, low conifers, moss cushions, and small trees bearing waxy red fruit. Plants seldom overlap. Each has open ground around it, as if the entire ecology observes an old courtesy of distance. The meadow grasses are knee-high and jeweled with moisture. Pale flowers remain permanently open. Orchards hold both blossom and fruit on the same branches, neither ripening nor falling.

Nothing sprouts. Nothing withers.

The same law holds among the animals, though life is abundant. Slate-colored goats stand along impossible ledges. Long-legged deer graze in the northern fields, each mouth lowered to the grass without ever taking a visible bite. Small striped cats occupy walls warmed by daylight. Green-black birds fill the roof ridges at generous intervals, their feathers sleek, their heads turned toward streets below. Moths rest under every bridge. Fish hold station in the river’s clear chambers, fins flexing around bodies that do not advance.

At first the city seems motionless. Then one notices the smaller motions: a goat’s ear turning; a bird’s throat swelling; the slow closing and opening of a cat’s eyes; insects shifting their feet on stone. These acts repeat, but they produce no consequence. No creature becomes hungry or old. Predators watch prey with intense, ancient concentration, yet never spring. Eggs remain warm beneath birds. The young remain young. Wounds, where they exist, neither heal nor worsen.

The people are subject to the same equilibrium.

They inhabit the streets in modest numbers, leaving broad spaces between gatherings. A potter shapes one vessel at a courtyard wheel, wet clay rising endlessly under steady fingers. Quarry workers hold wedges against a half-split block in the eastern yard. In the market, woven baskets contain lentils, herbs, salt, eggs, mushrooms, and red orchard fruit. No stall empties. No loaf grows stale. A copper worker raises a hammer and brings it down with a muted note; the sheet beneath it preserves the same shallow dent.

Whether these people are trapped, blessed, or simply native to such a condition is not evident. Their faces are calm, but not vacant. They can speak. They can choose where to walk and whom to visit. They remember conversations, tell stories, make promises, and invent songs. Yet material change resists them. A wall can be planned but not completed. A bowl can be carried but not broken. Food can be tasted but never consumed. Doors open and close, although neither state lasts unless someone remains to hold it. The world accepts gestures and rejects outcomes.

For this reason, society is organized less around production than around attention.

Citizens take turns standing beside the rain. They memorize the unchanging distribution of drops and give names to the brighter ones. Families adopt particular animals, not to feed them but to witness their minute repetitions. Surveyors walk the city each day, measuring cracks that have never widened. Gardeners comb permanent dew from leaves into cups, although the droplets return as soon as the leaf is released. Architects design impossible additions on wax tablets, each line erasable but the finished plan somehow never transferable to stone.

The highest tower is an observatory built of fitted white rock. Its broad stairs ascend above the roofs to an open platform. From there the whole basin is visible: black young mountains, wet green terraces, spacious districts, the fixed river, the distant rain, and beyond the western pass a suggestion of further uplands. The sun occupies a comfortable place in the southern sky. It casts ordinary shadows, neither long nor short. Its light has warmth enough to distinguish sheltered walls from open air, but not enough to alter anything. Clouds are few and sharply edged. They do not travel.

The quiet is not absolute. Water whispers without progressing. Wings make brief papery sounds. Sandals touch stone. Somewhere a hand drum gives a single low beat at regular intervals, although no one can determine whether the interval is a breath, an hour, or a generation. Voices carry far in the cool thin air and seem startling whenever they begin. Echoes return exactly once.

There is no wind.

Banners therefore hang straight down from towers, showing woven symbols: a split peak, an antlered animal, a vessel beneath three drops. Chimneys preserve pale ropes of smoke above them, more like columns of dyed wool than emissions. The smell of the city is correspondingly layered and constant—damp basalt, cedar shavings, cold ash, crushed fern, animal hide, clay, and bread held at the moment it becomes fragrant.

Toward the outer basin stand older-looking places, though none can truly be older. There are roofless precincts, roads that end against untouched cliffs, foundations laid out for districts never begun, and gates facing slopes no traveler can cross. On a high northern saddle, upright stones form a circle around a shallow pool. The pool reflects stars despite the daylight. Each star trembles in place on the water while the real sky remains blue.

This is the basin’s principal strangeness beyond its refusal to change: it contains signs of histories that cannot have occurred. Murals show the city under snow, though snow has never fallen. Songs describe floods, fires, migrations, dynasties, and children growing tall. Fossil shells appear in the new mountain glass. Grave terraces overlook the river, their stones inscribed with lifespans, although no one living can remember a death.

The inhabitants do not agree on what these signs mean. Some say the basin is the preserved center of a world whose time moves elsewhere. Some believe all possible pasts accumulated here when the mountains rose. Others maintain that change is occurring, but so evenly in every substance, creature, and mind that no local observer can detect it.

A traveler might initially experience the place as peaceful. Nothing threatens collapse. No storm approaches; no cliff crumbles; no beast hunts; no cold deepens toward night. The roads are safe, the water clear, the people hospitable. One can sit beneath a fruit tree beside a motionless river and hear birds spaced across the roofs.

Only gradually does the peace acquire weight.

There will be no evening. The fruit’s scent will never grow stronger. A sentence can be finished, but the silence after it feels identical to the silence before. On the mountains, every sharp edge waits in perfect youth. In the western valley, rain continues to fall into ground that never becomes wetter, and all across the open city, living things attend patiently to a world that permits presence, pattern, and thought—but no arrival, no departure, and no becoming.
