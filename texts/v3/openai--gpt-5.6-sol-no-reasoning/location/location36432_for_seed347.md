The city lies below the ground without being enclosed by it.

Above, a young limestone plain gathers cold rain. The water descends through innumerable cracks, widening some, sealing others with pale mineral deposits, until it reaches an immense collapse basin beneath the surface. Here the roof has fallen away in long strips. Daylight enters through these openings as through streets between high buildings, gray at dawn, colorless at noon, briefly silver when the weather clears. Seen from the rim, the basin is easy to mistake for a deep, forested valley. Only from below does its buried nature become apparent: the overhanging margins, the roots hanging out of ceilings, and the shelves of intact earth suspended high overhead.

The settlement covering the basin floor has no builders.

It is made by water, plants, and generations of animals acting without design, yet it has the density and organization of a city. Towers rise wherever springs deposit lime around reeds, roots, and fallen trunks. Some are narrow and fluted; others have merged into blocks with terraces, courtyards, blind passages, and dripping arcades. Fresh deposits are cream-white, almost soft, and carry the precise impressions of moss stems and leaf veins. Older walls are dark with algae or clothed in saxifrage, liverwort, fern, and low cold-growing shrubs.

There is almost no bare ground. Every surface belongs to something.

On the basin floor, black saturated soil lies beneath a close mesh of roots. Pools occupy the spaces between mineral towers. Their edges are crowded with sedges, horsetails, dwarf willows, and broad water plants whose leaves lie flat beneath the rain. Moss rises over fallen wood until logs become green embankments. Fungal shelves fill crevices like stairways. Alder thickets crowd the slower channels, leaning inward until opposite banks interlace. Even the open water is densely inhabited: strands of weed below, insect larvae in the silt, snails on the stones, films of minute life at the surface.

There are roads, though none were laid. Herds of small hoofed animals have pressed narrow routes through the vegetation, always choosing the same dry ridges between pools. Generations of passage have hollowed these paths to dark lanes roofed by stems. Broader avenues are made by seasonal floods. When the underground rivers rise, they flatten grasses, carry off loose litter, and leave long winding corridors of washed gravel. Fish follow these routes while they are submerged. Mammals use them after the water withdraws.

The cold is persistent rather than severe. Breath shows in shaded quarters even in summer. Water emerging from the rock has the same chill throughout the year, and mist forms wherever it meets the marginally warmer air of the basin. Condensation beads on leaves and hangs beneath overhangs. Nothing fully dries. Fallen feathers remain dark; bark stays swollen; stone keeps a permanent wet sheen.

The landscape is young. A series of recent collapses opened this level to daylight, and its edges have not yet softened into old slopes. Broken limestone stands at abrupt angles. New sinkholes show raw walls striped with soil, chalk, and tangled roots. Splintered ledges project over pools. Some mineral towers are still hollow frameworks, delicate around the plants that shaped them. Others end in sharp rims where last winter’s ice broke away the upper crust.

Yet life has occupied the new ground almost at once.

Mosses arrive in months. Willow seed catches in wet fractures. Ferns unfold under shelves that did not exist the previous year. Birds nest in fresh cavities before the loose stone has settled. At the foot of each new fall of rock, springs rearrange themselves, and a district of the city begins: first a seep and a green stain, then a fringe of sedge, then lime accumulating around stems, then walls, islands, channels, and inhabited hollows.

Fauna is abundant but seldom conspicuous. The quiet makes every slight movement distinct.

Small deer browse in the willow quarters, moving carefully through water up to their knees. Their paths are marked by clipped shoots and paired prints full of clear rain. Otters use the lower canals and leave fish scales on flat stones beneath the banks. Pale trout hold motionless in spring mouths. Frogs gather in the reed courts, though in the cold they call only briefly, their voices low and widely spaced. Water beetles draw circles on sheltered pools. Salamanders occupy the saturated foundations, slipping under bark and into porous mineral walls.

Birds are everywhere above eye level: wrens in root tangles, thrushes among the alders, dippers beside fast water, small owls in the darker stone galleries. They rarely form loud flocks. Calls are softened by vegetation, mist, and the absorbent walls of moss. A wingbeat close by may sound louder than a bird singing across the basin.

Large predators pass through without announcing themselves. A lynx may watch from a terrace crowded with fern. Foxes follow the raised animal roads. In the deepest marsh, where the vegetation closes over old pools, broad tracks sometimes appear and end at the water. The city has no empty outskirts in which an animal is truly absent; it has only layers of concealment.

Its silence is not a lack of sound but a lack of distance. Noise does not travel far. There is the small ticking of droplets, water whispering under mats of roots, sedges brushing one another, the occasional settling of young stone. Rain striking the exposed pools is loudest, but even that becomes local, divided among thousands of leaves and ledges. Floodwater can roar in the deeper conduits, yet the sound emerges from openings as a subdued vibration, as if the ground were breathing.

Water governs the place without ever taking one final course. Channels divide around growing mineral barriers, reunite in pools, vanish beneath peat, and return at the foot of distant walls. During prolonged rain, the lowest neighborhoods disappear. Fish move among drowned shrubs. Otters swim through archways that deer used the week before. Seeds and fragments of moss are carried onto fresh ledges. When the level falls, mud films every stem, and the basin resumes its terrestrial shape with its boundaries subtly altered.

In winter, water continues beneath a skin of ice. Clear icicles descend from the buried roof margins; some reach growing stalagmites below and form temporary bars across passages. Snow falls through the long openings but seldom lies evenly. It catches on branches and terraces, outlining the city’s height while the warmest springs remain open and dark. Animals concentrate there. Green plants persist in the vapor, making small lush chambers inside the cold season.

The upper world is visible but feels remote. Roots descend from it. Rain comes from it. Occasionally soil spills through a crack with seeds, beetles, and stones. Sun crosses the largest openings for a few hours and travels over the terraces like a slow searchlight. Most of the basin receives ordinary reflected day: sufficient for leaves, insufficient for glare. There are no luminous mysteries, no impossible colors. The strangeness is only that ordinary processes—dripping water, root growth, trampling feet, nesting, decay—have accumulated so densely that they resemble civic order.

Every niche is occupied, and every occupant helps produce another niche.

Roots crack the new stone. Cracked stone guides water. Water lays down walls. Walls shelter moss. Moss catches silt. Silt supports willow. Willow feeds deer and shades fish. Beavers, where the channels deepen enough for them, block a run and flood an alder quarter; the drowned trees become perches, then rot, then foundations for mineral ridges. Nothing oversees this, and nothing remains stable for long.

From a high ledge the basin appears calm: a cold green expanse packed between pale cliffs, patterned by pools and low canopies, extending beyond sight beneath the broken plain. Up close it is ceaselessly under construction. A wall thickens one drop at a time. A bank collapses softly into a channel. A hoof opens a spring. A seed roots in the print. Water changes direction.

By the following rain, there is already a new street.
