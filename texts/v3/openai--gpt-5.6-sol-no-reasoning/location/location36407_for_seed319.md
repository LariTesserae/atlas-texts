The city stands on a high shelf of limestone, warm and almost bare, overlooking a basin whose far rim is usually blurred by moisture. From a distance it appears densely built: ranks of square towers, kiln stacks, cistern domes, viaducts, retaining walls. Up close, its emptiness becomes apparent. Buildings are separated by broad courts of mineral dust and black paving. Warehouses stretch for blocks with only one lit doorway. Rail lines divide and reunite across vacant terraces. There is enough construction for a metropolis, but not enough movement, vegetation, or visible life to fill one.

Water is everywhere without gathering into abundance. It beads on iron, breathes from grilles, darkens masonry, and draws pale trails down walls. The limestone beneath the city is saturated, and the plateau sweats continuously through its fractures. Every parapet has a wet underside. Every tunnel smells of clay and warm metal. After noon, condensation forms on the shaded faces of buildings and runs into gutters cut so deeply by centuries of flow that some resemble narrow ravines.

There are no true rivers on the shelf. Water travels through the city in thin sheets, hidden pressure lines, weeping seams, and sudden vertical drops. It vanishes into stone throats and reappears several districts lower, mineral-heavy and nearly hot. Along the escarpment, it escapes from thousands of apertures. The whole outer wall glistens. Curtains of droplets descend into the basin, too dispersed to look like waterfalls, catching ordinary daylight in brief silver motions before becoming haze.

The sound is enormous.

It does not come from frenzy. Nothing here moves quickly enough for that. The noise is continuous, architectural, almost geological: pumps driving groundwater uphill; compressors feeding the foundries; ventilation turbines turning in buried chambers; ore mills revolving behind walls ten meters thick. Long trains crawl across elevated rails with wheel-flanges screaming around curves. Pressure valves discharge at intervals, each release like a ship’s horn blown inside the streets. Beneath everything is a bass vibration from the plateau itself, conducted through stone foundations and iron pipes.

Conversation is conducted close to the ear, by hand signal, or through speaking tubes set into the walls. Public signs use shutters, lamps, and slowly rotating arms. Bells are useless. Sirens are reserved for events so grave that entire banks of machinery must be silenced to make them audible.

The city has been here long enough for its industry to have become landform. Slag heaps are rounded hills with drainage channels and survey markers. Disused cooling towers have weathered into hollow cliffs, their upper rims softened by mineral deposits. Brickwork has lost its corners. Iron rails are polished, rusted, replaced, and buried in successive generations until streets contain metallic strata. In some places the original plateau lies twenty meters below the present ground, covered by foundations, ash, broken ceramic conduit, and poured stone.

Erosion is neither ruin nor disaster here. It is part of civic maintenance. Water is expected to eat the city. Buildings are designed with sacrificial faces: thick outer courses meant to dissolve, flake, or become furred with calcium. When a wall grows thin, another is built inside it. Thus the oldest quarters consist of nested rooms, each smaller than the one before, with damp abandoned corridors running around their inhabited cores. Doorways accumulate behind doorways. Stairs end against newer retaining walls. The city contracts inward while expanding at its margins.

The streets follow ancient channels in the rock. They slope gently toward immense grated shafts, though rainfall itself is infrequent. Most wetness rises from below or condenses out of the basin air. Storms arrive only a few times a year, but when they do, the shelf disappears under warm gray water. Courtyards become shallow reservoirs. Stone steps turn into cascades. Tramlines hum beneath the flood, and citizens cross on raised walkways with railings worn smooth by generations of wet hands.

Afterward, the city shines for days. Steam lifts from roofs. Dust becomes red paste in the low places, then dries into curled scales. The noise of drainage joins the machines: rushing underfoot, hammering in vertical pipes, whispering through walls.

Vegetation survives in sparse, exact locations. Wiregrass grows around leaking hydrants. Dark reeds stand in settling ponds behind the refineries, widely spaced and stiff with metallic salts. A few figlike trees occupy cracks in the western retaining walls, their roots entering cisterns no one can reach. Their leaves are small, gray, and always wet. The city protects these trees not from affection but because their roots reveal structural weaknesses years before the masonry shifts.

Across the plateau beyond the outer districts, the ground is mostly exposed stone. Rain and industrial vapor have carved it into shallow basins, ribs, and long parallel grooves. Here and there stand isolated installations: pumping houses, transformer yards, capped wells, semaphore towers. Roads run between them in ruler-straight lines, interrupted where older sinkholes have opened. Nothing is abandoned casually. Even distant ruins remain connected by pipes that tremble with pressure.

Animals are seldom seen. Pale insects collect on warm switchgear. Small, nearly featherless birds nest inside exhaust baffles, emerging only during maintenance shutdowns. In the drainage tunnels live transparent eels or eel-shaped things, blind and slow, feeding on bacterial films around the hot-water outlets. Their presence is inferred more often than witnessed: a wake in a black cistern, a soft body striking a grate far below.

There are rumors of larger creatures in the submerged caverns under the oldest ward. The pumping authority classifies the recurring blockages as mineral events.

The basin below the city is broader than it first appears. Its floor is a patchwork of shallow wetlands, salt flats, evaporation pans, and old industrial districts half buried in pale sediment. Causeways cross it toward neighboring shelf towns. On clear mornings, their stacks can be seen releasing vertical columns of vapor. By afternoon, heat bends the view, and the settlements seem to hang detached from the ground.

Very little changes from hour to hour. Gantries rotate slowly. Freight platforms descend with such patience that their motion is noticed only by comparing them to windows. Reservoir levels rise over weeks and fall over months. Whole districts are opened or sealed according to pressure cycles measured in decades.

The city keeps time by corrosion.

Public records note when a bronze threshold becomes illegible, when a bridge loses the last of its original rivets, when mineral growth closes a conduit. Certain walls serve as calendars: each wet season leaves a dark band, each dry interval a pale one. Officials scrape narrow windows through the deposits and read the history beneath. Some municipal arguments concern stains laid down before any living family arrived.

Ordinary daylight falls on all of this without drama. The sky is usually bright but colorless, softened by basin haze. Shadows exist, though their edges are dampened by airborne moisture. At sunset the western faces of the factories turn ocher, then brown. For several minutes every wet pipe and glazed brick reflects fire. Then the plateau cools slightly, condensation thickens, and the city begins to drip more loudly.

Night does not quiet it. Lamps glow behind wire cages. Signal arms continue their measured turning. Foundries pulse dull orange through high vents. Empty avenues vibrate beneath machinery hidden several stories down.

The place’s deepest strangeness is not that it is loud, wet, or built beyond apparent need. It is that the city and the plateau have reached an agreement. The machines keep the saturated rock from collapsing. The rock supplies the heat and pressure that drive the machines. Water dissolves the foundations, but also carries away their waste and deposits new stone inside their cracks. No one can say whether the settlement occupies the landscape or whether it has become one of the plateau’s organs.

From the basin, the city resembles an eroded mountain with lit windows.

From within, the mountain can be heard working.
