The plateau is so broad that its edges belong to rumor.

It stands high above the warmer lowlands, though nothing here feels alpine. Heat has collected in the dark stone through uncounted seasons, and even at midnight the ground gives it back: a low, dry warmth rising through soles, pads, scales, and the pale roots of scattered plants. The air is thin but not cold. It tastes faintly of iron and rain that has not yet fallen.

There is almost no light. A heavy ceiling of cloud obscures the sky, and the cloud itself seems blacker than open night. At long intervals, distant lightning moves inside it without breaking through—a dim red stirring, hundreds of kilometers away. The landscape appears in those pulses not as a continuous country but as isolated facts: a sloping plain of basalt; a line of weathered pillars; the silver bend of a temporary river; herds suddenly visible and then gone.

For most of the year the plateau is dry. Its dryness is not absolute but compartmentalized. Moisture hides in hollows, in cracks, under stones, inside animals, and in the dense night air. Condensation gathers on the windward faces of rocks and trickles down channels no wider than veins. Shallow basins hold black water beneath mineral skins. In the deeper ravines, streams run intermittently without ever seeing rain, fed by cloud-water that enters the porous upper stone and emerges kilometers away.

When rain does come, it falls locally and with great weight. One region may flood while the plains beside it remain untouched. Water spreads across stone in sheets thin enough to reflect lightning, pours into old drainage lines, and wakes rivers whose beds are already polished by ages of recurrence. Within hours those rivers can become warm, violent currents carrying silt, insects, shed scales, seeds, and whole rafts of dormant organisms. Within days they shrink to linked pools. Then to damp seams. Then to memory.

Erosion has had time to finish its first arguments. There are few raw cliffs or fresh wounds. The land is rounded, terraced, and deeply settled into its own forms. Ancient lava fields have softened into long backs and shallow bowls. Escarpments rise in immense dark steps, their faces fluted by water and wind until they resemble draped fabric seen from far away. Isolated masses stand where harder stone resisted the general lowering: not sharp towers, but broad-headed remnants with worn shoulders and aprons of rubble already half absorbed into the plain.

The stone is predominantly black—basalt, glassy slag, dense volcanic layers full of vesicles—but its surfaces carry old colors. Rust-red films spread around mineral seeps. Pale salts outline vanished pools. Lichens form smoky green stains in protected grooves. Where warm groundwater rises, sulfur leaves yellow traces, and microbial mats make narrow bands of violet and dull copper along the waterline.

Vegetation is sparse enough that every plant has territory.

Low thorn trees occupy the widest drainage channels, spaced so far apart that one may stand alone within several hectares. Their crowns are flat and sparse, their bark dark as burnt rope. During dry intervals they appear dead, but condensation beads along their smallest branches and runs inward toward the trunk. Their leaves open only during the deepest part of the night, unfolding like small black fans to drink directly from the air.

Elsewhere grow clumps of jointed grass, waxy succulents tucked into fissures, and tall hollow stalks around seasonal pools. Nothing forms a continuous cover. Bare ground remains the dominant presence. Yet below it lies a crowded dormancy: seeds with stone-hard coats, fungal threads sleeping between mineral grains, eggs sealed in clay, root crowns waiting for pressure, temperature, and moisture to align.

The abundance belongs to animals.

At first the darkness makes the plateau seem empty. Then the eyes adjust—or other senses begin to matter—and life reveals itself by subtraction and movement. Warm-bodied grazers cross the plain in widely spaced files, each animal following the vibration of the one ahead. They are long-legged and narrow, with heat-shedding sails folded close along their backs. Their hides absorb nearly all visible light. Only the mineral crust around their nostrils catches the dim sky.

They migrate between condensation belts rather than pastures. At night, when certain escarpments sweat, the animals gather beneath them to lick the wet stone. By day they descend into shaded lava tubes or stand motionless in the lee of pillars. Their routes are ancient enough to have become geological features: broad, shallow tracks winding across the plateau, compacted by millions of passages and briefly transformed into streams after storms.

Predators follow without hurry. They do not roar or call. Their bodies are low and many-jointed, and they hunt by reading heat left in footprints. A trail remains legible to them for minutes after an animal passes. During the warmest hours, when every stone radiates and the ground becomes confusing, they wait beside water pockets. Their patience shapes the behavior of whole herds.

Smaller creatures are everywhere. Pale moths rise from fissures after rain. Burrowing animals ventilate the soil with long ear-like vanes. Translucent beetles harvest droplets from rock faces, standing head-down while water runs along grooves in their shells. In the temporary rivers, armored fish emerge from buried cocoons and complete hurried lives before the channels close. Winged scavengers ride thermal columns far above the ground, invisible except when lightning outlines thousands of them turning at once.

The plateau is mostly quiet because nearly everything here has learned that sound travels too far.

There is the tick of contracting stone after a cloudburst, the dry sliding of talus, the breath of grazing animals, and the intermittent drip from condensation ledges. Herds communicate through footfalls too low to hear. Insects pulse against hollow plant stems. Predators send vibrations through the ground by flexing plates along their ribs. Now and then an entire valley hums—not loudly, but with such depth that loose salt trembles around the pools.

This hum is the closest thing the plateau has to industry.

There are no buildings, roads, machines, or artifacts. Nothing has been constructed. Yet beneath the surface, immense biological systems perform the work of factories.

The porous basalt is threaded with colonies that draw power from geothermal gradients and metal-rich water. They grow as conductive membranes over kilometers, linking warm faults to cool caverns. Mineral ions move through them in regulated flows. Chambers inflate and collapse. Chemical gates open in sequence. Deep in the rock, unseen pistons of tissue pump groundwater uphill at pressures no isolated organism could produce.

The system is not intelligent in any simple sense, but it manufactures.

At vents scattered across the plateau, glossy nodules slowly emerge from the ground. Some contain concentrated salts, others waxes, pigments, enzymes, or hard ceramic-like shells. Animals break them open, carry them away, digest their contents, and return different compounds in dung or bone. Thorn trees plug roots into conductive seams and trade sugars for water. Burrowing colonies maintain shafts through which warm air rises and cool air descends. Migratory herds transport living starter cultures from one drainage basin to another.

The whole plateau functions as an industrial ecology without tools: extraction, refinement, distribution, recycling. Its assembly lines are food chains. Its turbines are temperature differences. Its electrical grid is fungal, bacterial, and root-bound. The occasional blue flicker seen crawling over wet ground is not lightning but charge passing between exposed branches of the buried network.

This is why the darkness matters.

Strong light disrupts the surface membranes and overheats the conductive stone. The cloud ceiling may itself be partly sustained by the plateau: warm vapor rising from countless vents, carrying aerosols released by nocturnal plants and underground colonies. The resulting clouds reflect the sun and keep the land in a prolonged, warm dusk. Only at the extreme horizon does a faint bronze rim suggest daylight beyond the system’s reach.

Where the cloud cover tears, the land changes rapidly. Plants close. Grazers flee downslope. Conductive films retract into cracks. Flying animals spiral toward the shadow line. Bare stone under direct sun becomes intolerably bright, and chemical traffic below it slows. Within hours, rising vapor begins repairing the breach.

Thus weather here is not merely endured. It is one of the ecosystem’s organs.

Storms move according to the relief, assembling above long escarpments and discharging over the inner basins. Floods redistribute minerals. Heat drives circulation through the deep colonies. Condensation feeds the surface fauna. Migration returns phosphorus and calcium to depleted regions. Predation prevents the herds from lingering where their weight would seal the porous ground. Every violence has been incorporated into the plateau’s equilibrium.

The balance is old but never still.

A storm may awaken a river longer than some nations. A herd may take a month to cross one visible plain. A bloom of dark flowers may travel across the plateau over decades, basin by basin, following a cycle of rains. Far below, conductive colonies advance through the rock at the pace of fingernail growth, only to change the course of springs centuries later.

From any one place, little seems to happen. A warm wind passes. A creature lowers its head. Water forms on stone and disappears.

But at the scale of the plateau, engines are always turning.
