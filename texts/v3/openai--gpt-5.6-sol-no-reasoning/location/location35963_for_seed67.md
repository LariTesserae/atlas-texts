The lowlands continue beyond any useful horizon, a warm, wet continent assembled entirely from manufactured parts.

There is no soil. The ground is a fitted skin of dark ceramic plates, poured alloys, vitrified slag, and blocks of pale composite whose seams run straight for hundreds of kilometers before meeting other seams at shallow angles. The plates were laid recently enough to retain their edges. Corners are sharp; bolt heads have not rounded; casting ridges still show where enormous molds parted. Water lies over all of it in a film rarely deeper than a finger, turning the continent into a dim mirror.

Where the fabricated ground descends, the film gathers into broad basins. These resemble marshes from a distance, but nothing grows in them. Banks are ranks of perforated baffles. Reeds are replaced by upright cooling vanes, black and close-set, each beaded with condensation. Channels pass under latticed walkways and through rectangular culverts large enough to admit weather systems. Pumps are present everywhere, although few are heard. Their impellers rotate below the surface with such constancy that the water appears motionless.

The rain is similarly steady without seeming to fall. Warm air presses down beneath a continuous pearl-colored sky. Moisture condenses on gantries, pipes, cables, towers, and the undersides of elevated decks, then releases in heavy drops. Each drop strikes metal or water with an isolated tick. Across so vast a place, these impacts should make a roar. Instead they seem to cancel one another, leaving a padded quiet in which a sound nearby is precise and a sound far away is absent.

There are structures at every scale. Close at hand, narrow conduits crowd the joints between pavement slabs. Above them pass pipes broad enough to contain streets. Above those are trusses like black mountain ranges, supporting arrays of tanks whose curved sides vanish into cloud. Farther out, stacks rise in orderly forests—not vegetation, but repetition so dense it takes on the visual texture of vegetation. Their heights are identical. Their emissions are identical: faint white columns that merge with the low ceiling and return as condensation.

The whole landscape is factory, drainage system, habitat, and machine. No underlying rock has ever been exposed because there is no underlying rock. Beneath the ceramic skin are service galleries, beneath those are load webs, reservoirs, furnaces, pressure vaults, and older supporting decks. Depth here does not lead toward geology. It leads through successive floors of construction. Explorers who descend eventually stop speaking of underground and begin speaking of inward.

Heat comes evenly through the plates. There are no hot districts and no cool districts, no perceptible seasons, no day warmer than another. The light also remains ordinary and unwavering: bright enough for color, diffuse enough to cast only soft shadows. It may be artificial, though no lamps are visible. There may be a sun above the cloud deck, though no change in brightness confirms one. Clocks function, but the landscape offers them no evidence.

Almost all movement belongs to animals.

They are everywhere.

The shallow water is crowded with smooth-backed bodies resembling a confusion of crabs, horseshoe shapes, segmented eels, broad flat rays, and things more difficult to classify. They overlap in the basins, yet do not struggle. Pale filter-mouths seal themselves over drain apertures. Long-legged waders stand on magnetic feet and lower needlelike heads into cable trenches. Shoals of transparent, thumb-sized swimmers pass through the water film where it is scarcely thick enough to cover them, their bodies flattening without distress beneath each droplet.

Every rail supports animals. Every overhead cable bows under their weight. Small six-limbed creatures pack themselves into the warmth behind access panels, thousands to a compartment, polished shells touching. Winged forms fill the cavernous spaces under the decks, but they do not fly continuously. They hang in dark layers, releasing one individual at a time to cross the open air. Vast, slow animals rest in the artificial marshes with only their ridged backs exposed; maintenance bridges have been built over them, or perhaps the bridges came first and the animals learned precisely where to lie.

Their abundance is overwhelming, but their behavior is restrained. There are no mating cries, territorial songs, panicked stampedes, or sudden feeding frenzies. Predation occurs silently and at fixed intervals. A long-jawed creature takes the same number of silver crawlers from the same channel each hour. The crawlers neither flee nor diminish. Eggs appear in dry recesses; hatchlings emerge; adults disappear into processing apertures. Population counts remain exact.

Nothing here is botanical. No moss stains the wet joints. No algae cloud the reservoirs. No lichen softens the towers. Whatever sustains the fauna is not a food web in the old sense. Nutrient paste issues from numbered valves. Mineral vapor condenses inside feeding cabinets. Some animals graze electrical charge from exposed contact strips, their mouthparts flashing faint blue. Others insert proboscises into pressure couplings and drink warm industrial fluid. Their waste drops into slots designed for it and is conveyed away.

The animals are not obviously mechanical. Cut flesh bleeds; breath fogs inspection glass; bones show beneath translucent skin. Yet each species fits the works too exactly. Shells match the curvature of pipes. Claws turn particular fasteners. Nesting bodies occupy cavities with no wasted space. Migratory routes coincide with inspection corridors. Some creatures clean filters merely by feeding. Others carry components swallowed in one district and deposited in another. One immense, many-legged grazer moves beneath an elevated pipeline so closely that the pipe’s shadow lies permanently along its spine.

Whether the machine was built for the fauna or the fauna for the machine is impossible to decide.

At intervals stand assembly complexes: low, immense halls faced with enamel and rain-polished steel. Their doors remain open. Inside, conveyor lines advance too slowly to be seen moving, though objects are found farther along if one returns after an hour. Presses descend without impact. Robotic arms hold tools against unfinished surfaces with patient, motionless pressure. New plates, pipes, housings, and animal-shaped vessels emerge warm and wet, but production never increases the extent of the world. Elsewhere, at an equal rate, identical material is withdrawn.

This balance governs everything.

Water falls, but reservoir levels do not rise. Water is pumped, but no current can be measured. Heat escapes from vents, but no region cools. Animals are born in multitudes, but the crowd neither thickens nor thins. Components are fabricated, but the continent does not grow. Erosion has begun—there are tiny scratches on exposed enamel and bright lines where shells pass over alloy—but each mark stops at the same depth. No edge becomes blunt. No stain spreads.

Even accidents have equilibrium. A collapsed gantry lies across one basin, its fractured beams sharp and newly silver. It seems to have fallen moments ago. Condensation gathers on it; animals nest beneath it; water routes around its braces. Yet records in nearby terminals show the gantry in that exact fallen position across every available date. The fracture is both fresh and permanent.

Distances behave with the same quiet stubbornness. Towers on the horizon remain on the horizon. A traveler can walk toward them for weeks, crossing endless repetitions of channels, tanks, hatcheries, yards, and humid assembly halls, without seeing them enlarge. Turn aside, however, and a tower may be reached by evening. Maps are accurate locally and contradictory as wholes. The continent is not infinite; gauges insist on a finite circumference. It is simply too thoroughly constructed to permit an outside view.

The densest places are the interchange fields. There, pipelines cross in hundreds of stacked layers, leaving only warm slots between them. Water threads every surface. Animals fill every slot. Pale-bodied climbers move upside down beneath ducts; armored grazers polish lower supports; clouds of tiny winged forms hang around transformer cages. Despite the congestion, collisions do not occur. Paths interleave to the width of a hair. Each creature pauses at exact moments, and the immense traffic passes in silence.

Occasionally a low vibration travels through the ground. It is felt in teeth and joints before it is heard. Water dimples. Thousands of animals raise their heads together. Valves across the plain rotate by a fraction. Far off, a single engine completes one slow stroke.

Then everything resumes the state it never left.

There are control rooms, but they contain no operators. Consoles glow with normal, readable light. Gauges point to central values. Warning lamps remain unlit. On walls, diagrams depict sectors too vast to comprehend: wet decks layered below wet decks, fabrication belts circling reservoirs, migration channels threading through machines, and beneath all of them another manufactured plane labeled only SUPPORT.

No diagram includes bare earth.

No diagram includes a boundary.

The nearest thing to wilderness is found where a roof panel has been omitted over an expanse of shallow water. The opening is hundreds of kilometers wide, its edges perfectly straight. Under the pearl sky lies a plain of warm water over black tile, crowded from edge to edge with quiet animals. Their backs form a second, living pavement. Condensation falls among them. They breathe in sequence, wave after wave, yet the surface never quite ripples.

Here the scale of the place becomes palpable. The industrial continent is not empty machinery colonized by life, nor an ecosystem confined within architecture. It is one continuous arrangement in which animal, water, structure, heat, and maintenance occupy equal status. The world is densely alive but not wild, fully fabricated but not governed, young in every exposed edge and ancient in every unchanging cycle.

It smells of warm rain, metal, wet shell, electrical insulation, and clean mineral steam.

It sounds like a drop striking a rail.

Then another, very far away.

Between them, the whole constructed world holds its balance.
