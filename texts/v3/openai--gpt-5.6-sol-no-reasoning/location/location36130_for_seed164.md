The city stands on the upper surface of a mountain range that has not yet learned to look old.

Its peaks are black, angular, and immense. Ridges rise in clean fractures, their faces still bearing the planar sheen of whatever upheaval lifted them. There are few scree slopes, few rounded boulders, no deep river-cut valleys. Erosion has made only preliminary marks: a pale streak beneath each seep, a shallow groove under every spillway, a softening of exposed glassy edges. From a distance the range resembles a continent-sized piece of machinery broken open along crystalline seams.

It is high enough to carry weather of its own. Cloud rests against the plateau rather than passing over it. Condensation beads continuously on walls, rails, pylons, leaves, and exposed stone. Water lies close beneath the dark ground, trapped in porous layers warmed just enough by buried infrastructure to remain liquid. It appears everywhere in modest quantities: in long marshes behind retaining walls, in trembling skins over basalt, in pipes that perspire, and in silver threads descending cliff faces. Nothing floods. Nothing dries. The water level never seems to rise or fall.

The cold is steady and inhabitable, a clean mineral cold without seasons. Every surface has the same chilled patience. Frost gathers only at certain margins—around the mouths of ventilation shafts, beneath bridge decks, along the outermost leaves of the high gardens—and there it remains, neither thickening nor melting.

Across this terrain stretches the settlement.

Calling it a city is accurate only in function. In form, it is a network of industrial islands separated by astonishing distances. One district may occupy an entire summit: foundries, housing blocks, reservoirs, and electrical yards crowded together beneath a ceiling of cloud. The next is a cluster of towers barely visible three hundred kilometers away. Between them lies almost nothing but raw stone, wet tundra, and the straight black lines of transit viaducts.

The emptiness is not abandonment. It appears planned.

Roads are rare, but rails cross the highlands on enormous concrete piers. Pipe galleries run beside them, carrying water, steam, compressed gases, and substances identified only by colors. Power lines hang from steel frames tall enough to disappear into the cloud layer. At intervals the lines converge upon switching cities: geometric fields of ceramic columns, copper bars, transformers, and luminous control houses. Their grids cover broad valleys, yet only a few structures stand within them. Space is treated as an engineering material, as necessary as steel.

Everything was built recently, or else time has failed to weather it. Bolts retain sharp ridges. Concrete keeps the faint imprint of its shuttering boards. Excavation cuts are crisp and undecorated by fallen debris. Rail beds look freshly tamped. Vast machine halls, clad in dull green metal, have no dents, rust blooms, repairs, or additions. Even the oldest-looking neighborhoods possess the severe incompleteness of models made full size.

The architecture favors low, heavy volumes near the exposed ridges and fantastically tall, narrow structures in sheltered basins. Residential quarters cling beneath overhangs or occupy cavities drilled into the mountain. Their windows glow with ordinary amber and white light. Staircases are heated. Entrances are protected by curtains of downward-blown air. Covered walkways lead from dormitory blocks to refectories, laundries, clinics, and assembly halls.

No one uses them.

There are no animals anywhere. No birds wheel in the cloud. No insects gather around lamps. No rodents live in the warm utility tunnels, and no fish disturb the marshes. The absence becomes most apparent in small places: meal trays stacked beside serving counters, doors opening at scheduled intervals, pedestrian signals changing for vacant crossings. Some chairs have been drawn back from tables. Beds are made. Tool lockers stand open before silent workstations.

Vegetation, however, is abundant.

It occupies the city without conquering it. Mosses form deep green pads on reservoir rims and shaded roofs. Tough grasses grow in the shallow, wet soils between rail embankments, each blade dark and waxy against the cold. Groves of jointed, translucent shrubs fill drainage basins. Their hollow stems collect condensed water and release it drop by drop from black tips. In the larger marshes grow low forests of broad, slate-colored leaves that overlap like scales. Their undersides are warm enough to steam faintly.

The plants do not sway.

This remains true even in the wind. Flexible stems bend to fixed angles but do not oscillate. Drops fall from leaves at exact intervals. Moss margins do not advance. No bud opens and no leaf decays. The landscape is lush but not growing, green but not seasonal—a completed ecology held at its point of maximum equilibrium.

The factories are different only because they are loud.

Their sound crosses distances that should diminish it. Turbines roar beneath mountains. Ore crushers deliver slow, continentally deep impacts. Cooling towers exhale with the continuous pressure of storms. Rail wheels shriek around curves on tracks where no train is visible. At the horizon, extraction engines beat in measured sequences: nine blows, a pause of forty seconds, then nine again.

Nothing speeds up. Nothing falters.

Inside the machine halls, assembly lines operate under bright, ordinary lamps. Robot arms lift components from one belt and place them on another. Presses descend. Welding heads produce sprays of white sparks that appear identical each time. Gantries move ingots between furnaces. Yet the material never accumulates at the end of the process. If watched closely, a manufactured piece—a pump casing, perhaps—passes through several operations and then enters a wall through a narrow hatch. Elsewhere, far upstream, an indistinguishable raw billet emerges.

The factories produce their own inputs.

The mines exhibit the same closure. Excavators cut black blocks from fresh faces and load them into unmanned haulage systems. Behind them, beyond the reach of direct observation, the mountain is always whole. The marshes feed treatment works; the treated water is injected into deep pipes; condensation then restores the marshes at precisely the same rate. Power stations consume fuel refined in the western basins. The exhaust precipitates there as a waxy mineral, which the refineries classify as fuel.

It is less an economy than a diagram of one.

The noise may be the principal product. It fills the emptiness, establishing the size of things that cannot be seen. A compressor heard across a hundred kilometers gives shape to the intervening plateau. The subsonic tremor of a buried mill reveals the depth of the mountain. Electrical harmonics travel through rails and wet stone, making bridge decks hum beneath the hand. In the cloud, sound becomes directional and architectural: unseen walls of vibration, corridors of resonance, chambers large enough to contain weather.

There are quieter places, but none are silent. In abandoned-looking plazas, one hears the minute ticking of thermal relays. On the marsh edge, drops strike leaves in strict polyrhythms. Deep inside residential caverns, ventilation grilles carry the remote thunder of factories. Even a sealed room contains a thin note, as though the entire plateau were the housing of a single energized instrument.

The sky is neither dramatic nor dim. Daylight filters normally through a high pearl overcast. Shadows are soft but present. At intervals the cloud opens onto a blue of startling conventionality. The sun crosses it at an ordinary rate, though clocks disagree about whether it moves at all. Night arrives, but night is not understood as change here. It is another permanent condition laid over the first. The lamps activate; the machinery continues; the same droplets fall from the same leaves.

Seen from the highest ridge, the full strangeness of the place begins to emerge.

The range extends farther than atmospheric clarity should allow. Identical watersheds repeat toward the curved horizon, each containing its sparse city, its marshes, its towers, and its separated industrial precincts. Yet the repetitions are not exact copies. One basin specializes in ceramics, another in bearings, another in the manufacture of immense empty tanks. One summit supports only a hospital complex with thousands of lit rooms. Another carries a rail terminus whose tracks end at the cliff edge, aligned toward a terminus on a distant peak.

Between these presences are voids so large that they seem deliberately protected. Nothing is built there. No trail crosses them. Plants thin to isolated mats around seeps; then even those vanish, leaving black young rock under shallow, cold water. Survey markers stand at thousand-kilometer intervals, all stamped with the same elevation.

Instruments confirm something implausible: despite the cliffs, peaks, basins, and abysses, every surveyed point occupies exactly the same gravitational height. Water placed anywhere settles without flowing. Rails require no uphill power. A stone dropped from a tower falls, but the tower’s summit and foundation are registered as level. The mountains are not variations in elevation. They are variations in the shape of levelness itself.

This may explain why the city is so spread out. Distance matters here; altitude does not. The plateau is a folded plane whose folds possess cliffs without ascent and valleys without descent. Transit maps depict the truth more faithfully than sight does: a vast, flat network, geometrically simple, industrially complete.

No construction crew appears. No maintenance vehicle comes down the rails. Nothing breaks. Fresh rock remains fresh; water remains wet; vegetation remains lush; the city remains ready.

The place gives the impression not of having survived its inhabitants, but of having been designed without any. Its dwellings, clinics, dining rooms, and pedestrian bridges may be components in the industrial cycle just as furnaces and pipes are. The empty beds absorb heat. The traffic lights consume power. The plazas collect condensation. The sealed elevators rise and descend, balancing the electrical grid. Human purpose has been reproduced structurally, stripped of humans, and stabilized.

Far below—or perhaps only very far away—the nine extraction blows sound again.

They pass through the black stone, through the marsh water and steel viaducts, through every unoccupied room. Drops loosen simultaneously from a billion fixed leaves. Signal lamps change. Factory doors open onto white interiors.

Then everything continues exactly as it was.
