The highlands begin without foothills.

A plain simply lifts out of the distance: a broad mineral shelf suspended beneath a warm, colorless sky, its rim too far away to resolve. The ascent is not a slope but a succession of immense, shallow steps. Each step is hundreds of meters wide and scarcely higher than a person, so that from nearby the land seems level, while from afar it rises like an unfinished staircase.

Nothing here has been worn smooth. There has never been rain, never a river, never frost working patiently in a crack. The ground retains the blunt geometry of its formation. Ridges end abruptly. Basins are clean-sided and empty. Splits in the bedrock are narrow, straight, and without rubble, as though they opened only moments ago and then lost the ability to widen.

The stone is pale umber with long bands of nickel-gray and green-black glass. In places it has welled upward in rounded masses and stopped before hardening completely. These domes look soft from a distance, like cooling wax, though their surfaces are tougher than ceramic and warm to the touch. Elsewhere, thin mineral plates overlap like scales. They do not shift underfoot. Nothing shifts.

There is no water—not hidden, not frozen, not carried in the air. The atmosphere is dry beyond any earthly desert. Breath, if released here, would seem an extravagant wetness. The sky holds no clouds. There are no bleached channels, no shorelines, no salt left behind by vanished seas. The landscape has no memory of liquid.

Yet it is green.

The vegetation—or the nearest equivalent—appears in widely separated expanses, each one lush within its own borders and surrounded by kilometers of emptiness. From the plateau these growths resemble dark islands. Up close, they are dense assemblages of mineral life: blades, fans, fluted columns, and soft-looking curtains growing directly from seams in the rock.

Their chemistry is not aqueous. Silicones and warm hydrocarbon oils circulate through sealed tissues. Their leaves are laminated films of carbon, vanadium, and translucent resin. Some stand waist-high in thick, glossy beds; others rise as slender black-green towers with crowns of coppery vanes. Nothing wilts. Nothing exudes sap. Cut surfaces, if such injury could occur, would close by polymerizing into a dark seam.

The groves have an industrial regularity, though no intelligence designed them. Rows repeat because crystalline faults repeat. Hexagonal crowns turn at identical angles toward the sun. Beneath the ground, rootlike conductors connect neighboring stands into vast electrochemical arrays. Sunlight falls on the leaf films and becomes charge; charge passes into catalytic chambers in the stone; gases are drawn from the atmosphere, separated, recombined, and released.

Thus the plateau performs the work of a factory without possessing a single building.

In daylight the groves manufacture themselves. They crack atmospheric compounds, plate their stems with metal, and lay down fresh membranes one molecule at a time. Deep under the terraces, porous rock serves as reaction vessel, storage tank, furnace, and cable conduit. The black bands in the cliffs are not merely ore but busbars grown along natural strata. Pale nodules embedded in the plain are ceramic insulators. Great buried chambers maintain steady warmth through slow exothermic cycles.

The scale of the process is continental, but its visible machinery is sparse. A solitary cluster of silver reeds may govern the chemistry of an entire basin. One low amber mat, no broader than a village, may be the exposed exchange surface of a network stretching for hundreds of kilometers underground. Between these presences lie open reaches of immaculate stone.

There are no roads, pylons, ruins, tools, or dwellings. Nothing has been assembled. The industrial character emerges from metabolism alone: extraction, refining, transmission, deposition. It has smokestacks in the form of hollow stalks, turbines in the form of spiral membranes, foundries housed inside basalt vesicles. Its products are not stacked or shipped. They become the next layer of the organism.

No animals inhabit this system. There are no insects among the blades, no birds above them, no burrowers under the crust. Flowers, where they occur, are not invitations. They are radiant dishes for thermal regulation, opening only to expose reflective interiors. Fruitlike bulbs contain catalysts rather than seeds. Pollination has no place here; continuity travels through the conductive bedrock as changes in voltage and crystal orientation.

The absence of animals makes the lushness uncanny. Nothing grazes it, hides in it, brushes past it, or watches from within. There are no tracks. No leaf has a bite taken from it. No stem bears a scar from impact. The groves are full but unoccupied, abundant without competition.

Sound is nearly absent. With no flowing water and little wind, the plateau offers nothing continuous to hear. In the growths there may be an occasional minute tick as a conductive filament changes phase, followed hours later by a second tick somewhere far away. Standing over a fissure, one might detect a low electrical hum, so faint that it could be mistaken for the pulse inside one’s own ear. The tall vanes do not rustle. Their edges are too rigid, and the air too still.

Even the industrial reactions proceed without clamor. There are no pistons, impacts, or spinning blades. Pressure is balanced everywhere. Gases move by molecular attraction through membranes. Heat passes down fixed gradients that seem never to strengthen or fade.

The light is ordinary in intensity but severe in clarity. At noon, colors appear exact: green-black foliage, ocher terraces, blue-white sky, occasional flashes of native metal. Shadows have clean boundaries. Distance remains visible for a long way, though a fine mineral haze eventually merges the highest steps with the horizon. At morning and evening, the metallic groves catch the low sun one plane at a time, so illumination moves across them in ordered ranks without any part physically moving.

Warmth lies evenly over everything. There are no sheltered cold pockets, no furnace-hot flats. The soil—if the thin layer of catalytic grains around the groves can be called soil—maintains nearly the same temperature as the exposed rock. Day and night alter the surface only slightly. The plateau’s tremendous thermal mass absorbs variation and returns it so slowly that weather never quite begins.

Time here is difficult to read.

The formations appear embryonic: terraces unweathered, fissures sharp, domes arrested in the gesture of rising. Yet they do not develop. The landscape is forever just formed. Mineral extrusions advance only as quickly as older material sublimes back into the air. Groves manufacture new leaf film at precisely the rate that ultraviolet light unthreads the old. Gases released in one basin are consumed in another. Every process has its counterprocess.

Nothing accumulates. Nothing decays.

From a high escarpment, one can see several districts of this equilibrium. To the east stand the dark vane forests, geometrically dense and utterly still. Southward lies a copper plain dotted with widely spaced photosynthetic towers. Westward, the plateau breaks into shallow, newborn chasms whose interiors shine with green glass. Farther north are circular depressions filled not with water or sediment but with transparent gas, subtly different from the surrounding atmosphere and held in place by temperature and molecular weight.

Beyond all of them the steps continue upward.

There may be no summit. Each apparent crest reveals another broad level, another empty interval, another isolated manufactory of living mineral. The world seems to be constructing itself, but construction is its permanent condition rather than a path toward completion. It has all the processes of an industrial age and none of its history: no inventor, no workforce, no consumption, no waste.

Only the high warm stone, the widely scattered green machinery, and the soft, almost inaudible work of remaining exactly as it is.
