The city occupies a mountain range rather than a valley.

It spreads across hundreds of high plateaus, ridges, and enclosed basins, so vast that no single summit overlooks more than a fraction of it. From a distance, the inhabited heights resemble a second geology laid upon the first: terraces stepping along cliffs, walls extending from crag to crag, neighborhoods packed into shelves of pale stone. Mountains rise within the city, and entire districts disappear behind them. Weather may be clear in one quarter while snow is falling three days’ walk away.

The range itself is young. Its peaks are narrow and abrupt, its faces scarcely softened. There are splintered ridgelines, steep scree slopes, fresh faults, and ravines whose walls still show the angular seams of uplift. Frost loosens stone faster than the sparse rain can round it. Rockfalls leave bright scars on darker cliffs. In the upper reaches, boulders balance where they fell centuries ago, their edges still sharp enough to cut leather.

Everything is cold. Even under a clear noon sky, shaded stone keeps the night’s frost. The air is thin, dry in the lungs, and carries little scent. Sunlight is ordinary in color but severe in effect, illuminating every ledge and masonry joint while giving almost no warmth. At dusk, cold descends quickly through the streets. Water skins stiffen; wet steps glaze over; people retreat behind hide curtains and thick wooden doors.

Water is never plentiful, but neither is the range wholly arid. Moisture comes in episodes. Short rains strike the windward peaks and vanish into fractures. Snow gathers in sheltered clefts, then melts by degrees through spring and summer. At dawn, condensation beads on cold roofs and cliff faces. Every settlement is shaped around gathering these small gifts.

Gutters cut into stone lead from roof to roof. Cistern mouths occupy courtyards like dark wells. Channels no wider than a hand run beneath paving slabs, carrying meltwater toward communal reservoirs. Some basins contain shallow lakes, steel-gray and intensely cold, fringed not by reeds but by gravel, moss, and low salt-stained walls. Seasonal streams wake for a few weeks, roar brown through the ravines, and disappear. Their empty beds remain important roads for much of the year.

The city’s density comes partly from necessity. Habitable ground is scarce, and sheltered ground scarcer. Houses crowd wherever a slope eases: under overhangs, behind windbreaks, inside old quarry faces, along the protected side of fortification walls. They are built from fitted local stone, mud mortar, timber pins, woven brush, wool felt, and packed earth. Metal exists but is costly. A bronze hinge may serve generations; iron blades are repaired until they are narrow as reeds. Most lifting is done by people and animals with ropes, wooden windlasses, sledges, and levers.

Buildings are rarely tall in themselves, but they stand upon one another. A lower house becomes the foundation and street of the next. Roofs serve as yards, paths, marketplaces, and threshing floors. Stairways pass through tunnels and emerge onto plazas that are actually the roofs of buried districts. In the oldest quarters, sunlight reaches only at midday. The lanes there are close, dry, and almost silent, their walls polished by shoulders, panniers, horns, and generations of hands.

Beyond the major settlements, habitation continues in thinner lines. Watch stations occupy pinnacles. Herding shelters dot the high meadows. Quarry camps cling to fresh rock faces. Rope bridges cross narrow chasms; where crossings are broader, roads descend in long switchbacks to stone causeways. Tall cairns mark routes across exposed plateaus, each one built large enough to remain visible in blowing snow.

There is no single center. Each basin believes itself a city, and each is joined to others by roads, marriages, water agreements, pilgrimage paths, and seasonal markets. Taken together they form an urban world hundreds of leagues across. Its borders are uncertain because settlement dwindles gradually into pasture, hunting ground, bare ridge, and ice.

Vegetation is sparse but carefully noticed. On sheltered slopes grow tough bunchgrasses, cushion plants, creeping juniper, bitter herbs, and thorny shrubs no higher than a person’s knee. Groves are rare and protected. Stunted birch or pine may survive in deep ravines where water lingers, their trunks twisted by wind. Villagers build low walls around patches of soil and carry manure uphill in baskets. Barley, small beans, turnips, and hardy greens grow in terraces where aspect and drainage permit. A field may be no larger than a room, yet have its own gate, inherited name, and water entitlement.

The apparent barrenness supports immense animal life.

Most of it moves.

Herds of shaggy goats and short-legged sheep pass through streets each morning, so dense that the paving vanishes beneath backs and horns. Pack animals—small mountain cattle, donkeys, and thick-coated hybrids—carry salt, charcoal, grain, stone, hides, and jars of water between districts. On outlying plateaus, wild grazers migrate in broad, deliberate bands, following the retreat of snow. Their paths score the slopes more clearly than roads.

Cliff faces hold colonies of birds. Swifts and choughs pour from cracks at sunrise, while vultures wheel almost without flapping in the thin uplifts. Ground birds nest among terrace stones. Pigeons occupy towers and abandoned rooms; their droppings are collected for fields. Along seasonal streams live frogs that remain buried through the cold months, emerging during brief floods in such numbers that the water seems to pulse. Marmots, hares, foxes, and small cats inhabit rubble outside the walls. Beetles and pale moths crowd the short flowering season.

Animals are present even when unseen. Horn scratches mark doorposts. Feathers collect against walls. Dung dries in carefully stacked cakes for fuel. Tracks cross morning frost before human footsteps do. Storerooms are defended against mice with stone lips and hanging baskets, though no defense is perfect. The city has been built by human labor, but it is inhabited by many species at once.

Despite this abundance, the range is quiet.

Thin air does not carry sound generously, and stone streets absorb little but return noises in brief, clean echoes. Wind is common, yet often moves high overhead while courtyards remain still. Hooves knock softly on earth-packed lanes. Wooden axles complain. Water drips into cisterns with an importance out of proportion to its volume. From distant slopes comes the occasional clatter of falling scree, prolonged and fading.

People seldom shout. Voices are conserved like breath. News travels through runners, marked staffs, knotted cords, painted boards, and the regular movement of traders. Bells are rare because metal is valuable; towers instead use suspended stone slabs, hollow logs, drums of hide, or long horns whose low notes can cross a basin in calm weather. Signals are simple: a reservoir opened, a pass closed, a fire seen, a herd returning, a death.

Change comes slowly except when it does not.

Most days repeat old patterns: frost lifting from sunward walls, animals leaving by the upper gates, market awnings opening, meltwater measured, shadows passing across terraces, smoke thickening toward evening. Houses accrete room by room. Roads migrate as retaining walls fail. A district may spend forty years extending a reservoir and consider this ordinary civic work.

The mountains, however, are not entirely settled beneath them. Small earthquakes are frequent enough that buildings use dry joints, flexible timber ties, and walls thicker at the base. After a tremor, citizens walk the channels first, checking for cracks and diverted water. Landslides can erase a road or expose a new spring. When that happens, the city adjusts rather than retreats. Stone is cleared, shrines moved, walls rebuilt along the altered contour. A ruined quarter becomes a quarry for its successor.

The society’s rules are correspondingly practical. Water cannot be privately withheld during freeze or drought. No wall may shade an established winter field without compensation. Anyone who finds a damaged bridge must leave a visible warning, even if they cannot repair it. Roof paths remain public passageways, though the rooms below are private. Dung, ash, bone, wool scraps, and broken masonry all have assigned uses; waste is less a category than a failure of attention.

Fire is watched closely. Timber is too scarce and neighborhoods too compressed for carelessness. Hearths are sunk into stone, embers covered at night, and every court keeps baskets of sand. Smoke is routed through long interior flues to preserve heat. Above the dense quarters rise communal chimneys, so that in winter whole hillsides seem to breathe from a few dark vents.

At the highest elevations, the built world thins at last. Roads narrow to trails, terraces yield to frost-shattered rock, and only cairns, shepherd huts, and small sanctuaries remain. From there, the inhabited range extends in layers to every horizon: pale walls, black cliff bands, patches of snow, threadlike roads, distant smoke, and reservoirs catching daylight like fragments of dull metal.

It does not look prosperous in an easy way. Nothing is lush; nothing is effortless. Yet the place is crowded with continuities. Every usable ledge has been recognized. Every trickle has a destination. Animals occupy the spaces between human intentions, and people live within the ongoing formation of the mountains.

The city is vast not because it has conquered the range, but because, over many slow generations, it has learned how to remain almost everywhere the range permits.
