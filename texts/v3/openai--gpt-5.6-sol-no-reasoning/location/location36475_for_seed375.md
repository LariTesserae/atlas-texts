The city occupies a shallow basin no wider than a small town, though *occupies* is misleading. Almost all of it is vacancy.

Its streets are narrow black intervals between walls of pale mineral. Its plazas are bare polygons. Towers rise only a little above the surrounding plain, blunt and windowless, separated by distances that feel ceremonial. There are colonnades with no roofs, stairs climbing five or six steps into open air, courtyards enclosing nothing. From the basin rim the place resembles an urban plan pressed into salt: wards, avenues, foundations, clustered dwellings, even something like a central forum. Yet there are no carved blocks, no mortar seams, no tool marks. Every structure is continuous with the ground.

The city grew.

It is still growing.

The plain around it is made of cold, flour-fine mineral under a dim sky. No stream has crossed it; no rain has rounded a corner or cut a gutter. Water is not merely absent but chemically unwelcome. Exposed surfaces repel even frost. The ground contains sulfurous salts, metallic dust, and long-chain crystals that assemble in darkness. Newly emerged ridges are knife-edged. Walls retain delicate fins along their crests, thin as folded paper, because there has been nothing to wear them away except thermal stress and the occasional passage of dry gas.

The basin is low and sheltered. Above its rim, a faint ground wind moves across the flats, but on the streets below the air is almost perfectly still. It smells of stone struck against stone: chalk, iron, ozone, a remote bitterness like burnt seeds.

Light comes indirectly. The sky holds a perpetual violet-gray twilight, brighter near one horizon but without any visible sun. Pale materials do not shine in it; they seem to preserve the little illumination they receive. Dark walls absorb depth until doorways and solid surfaces become difficult to distinguish. At certain hours, greenish radiance creeps upward from cracks in the ground, revealing the city from below. Then every curb casts a tall shadow against the sky.

Nothing here has been built by hand. There are no tools and perhaps never have been. The urban order is the consequence of a colonial organism whose living portion is mostly underground.

Far beneath the streets, dry membranes thread through porous rock. They metabolize without liquid, using migrating ions and the electrical difference between warm stone below and cold air above. Their growth is slow in substance but restless in arrangement. They extrude mineral around themselves, abandoning hardened chambers as their active edges move on. What looks like masonry is a shell, what looks like a sewer is a vein, and what looks like a district is one season’s branching body plan.

The walls are formed where buried filaments converge. Towers appear above reproductive knots. Open courts mark depleted patches from which usable elements have been stripped. The avenues follow lines of mechanical tension through the crust. Their straightness is not geometric intention but the natural fracture of a material growing under compression.

There is sparse vegetation, if vegetation is the word for it.

In protected angles stand clusters of narrow gray blades. They are rigid, leafless, and rooted directly into wall faces rather than soil. Each blade is hollow and divided internally into thousands of dry chambers. At dusk they open microscopic pores and admit traces of reactive gas. By morning those pores have sealed with new mineral, adding another transparent layer to the blade. Old specimens resemble bundles of smoky glass needles. They grow so slowly that a finger-width may represent decades, yet their reproductive phase is abrupt: an entire cluster can splinter at once, scattering slivers that slide across the plazas on electrostatic charge.

Elsewhere, low black rosettes cling to the bases of towers. They orient not toward light but toward stress. Hours before a wall splits, the rosettes turn their scales to face the coming fracture. In this way the city seems to shiver in anticipation, though nothing visibly moves.

Fauna are rarer.

There are small, many-jointed creatures inside the hollow walls, pale as ash and no longer than a fingernail. They feed by scraping fresh crystal from growing surfaces. Since there is almost no free moisture in their bodies, they move in stiff increments: six steps, a pause, six more. Their organs are waxes, salts, and fine conductive threads. They emerge only when the subsurface glow is strongest. Seen at a distance, a procession of them resembles iron filings arranging themselves around an invisible magnet.

A larger animal sometimes crosses the central court. It is tall enough to reach the low lintels and narrow enough to pass through slits that look much too small. Its skeleton is external, a loose cage of black rods joined by flexible white tissue. It has no obvious head. It grazes on the gray blades by standing motionless beside them while the rods of its body vibrate. Afterward the plants remain in place but have become empty husks.

Only one such grazer is usually present in the city. There may be more beyond the basin, separated by kilometers of sterile flats, or it may be the same individual returning along routes older than the present streets.

Despite all this activity, the place is nearly silent.

There is no running water, no leaf-rustle, no wingbeat. The little wall-creatures make no audible footfall. The wind passes over the city rather than through it. Even collapse is strangely quiet because the structures are light and porous: a facade may lean, loosen, and settle into powder with only a faint inward sigh.

Sound occurs chiefly within the stone.

Press an ear to a wall—if there were an ear here to listen—and one might detect a remote sequence of ticks. They pass from district to district, sometimes regular, sometimes gathering into rapid cascades. These are fractures traveling through the organism’s mineral shell. During active growth, the ticks outline a wave crossing the basin: first the eastern colonnades, then the central towers, then the low western cells. The city expands by less than a grain’s width, but everywhere at once.

On colder intervals, it contracts. Door-shaped openings narrow. Streets widen. Thin bridges between buildings pull taut and snap. By the next dim cycle, pale material has welled from the broken ends, branching in search of connection. Thus the plan continually edits itself. A route open at twilight may terminate at dawn in a smooth wall. A sealed room may split and become a lane. Towers bud chambers near their bases, then hollow from within and stand as empty skins.

The ground also moves.

Gas accumulates in shallow cavities under the plazas until broad pavement plates rise several centimeters, remain suspended, and then tilt. Cracks race outward in clean black lines. From them emerges a brief warmth and the green subterranean light. The gray blade-plants turn translucent. The wall-creatures gather at the edges. Black rosettes unfold. For an hour the city becomes intensely alive without becoming louder.

Then the gas drains away into the porous atmosphere. The plates descend, seldom into their former positions. New ridges remain around them. Small courtyards become ramps; ramps become enclosed pits. Because erosion is weak, every event persists. The city is crowded not with objects but with fresh consequences.

Nothing is old in the softened sense. There are no worn thresholds, no rounded statues, no dust-polished steps. Structures may have stood for centuries, but their edges remain sharp, and new skins repeatedly cover old surfaces. Where a tower has failed, its fragments lie angular and clean, as if broken moments ago. Time accumulates here without producing age.

There is a rule to the place: emptiness is necessary for growth.

Whenever structures thicken too densely, the buried organism withdraws minerals from their foundations. Whole blocks become brittle, collapse inward, and are gradually dissolved from beneath—not by liquid, but by ionic migration. The resulting void becomes a plaza. Around its perimeter, new walls rise. The city preserves a precise proportion between body and interval, presence and absence. It cannot fill itself without starving.

A second rule is that no chamber remains entirely closed. Each has some slit, pore, chimney, or hairline passage connecting it to the outside atmosphere. Rooms that become sealed are cut open from within. Their walls craze in branching patterns until one panel falls away. The city appears to insist on exposure, though whether this serves respiration, reproduction, or some stranger sensitivity is impossible to tell.

Its boundaries are distinct. At the basin rim the avenues stop. The last walls become low ribs, then isolated fins, then nothing. Beyond lies the dim salt-field, almost perfectly level and almost perfectly empty. Here and there, far away, a solitary vertical mark suggests another city-organism beginning—or a shed reproductive spire waiting for the ground beneath it to wake.

From the rim, the basin looks abandoned.

From within, it feels unfinished.

New corners sharpen in the dark. Buried membranes exchange charge. Empty rooms open and close their narrow mouths. The cage-bodied grazer crosses an avenue and disappears behind a wall too low to conceal it, yet does not emerge on the other side. Beneath the central court, pressure gathers again.

No voice speaks. No machine turns. Nothing flows.

Still, throughout the cold mineral city, everything is in progress.
