The city lies across the summit of a continent that has almost finished eroding.

From far above, it resembles an immense gray-green table, raised into the cold middle air: thousands of kilometers of old stone worn nearly level, its former mountains reduced to broad undulations and low ribs polished by ages beyond counting. Only at the margins does the land confess its height. There, the plateau ends in scarps so vast that clouds develop halfway down them, and rivers leaving the upland turn to white thread, then vapor, long before reaching any country below.

On the summit there is little standing water. Rain comes intermittently—fine, cold, and nearly vertical—or condenses overnight on stone and leaf. The land drinks it quickly. Water gathers beneath mats of vegetation, in jointed rock, and in a far-reaching system of hand-cut gutters. It appears again in scattered places: black tarns without ripples, shallow channels crossing plazas, wells whose surfaces tremble although there is no wind.

The city is everywhere and nowhere dense.

Its districts may be separated by fifty kilometers of moor, yet all belong to the same arrangement. Roads of fitted stone run arrow-straight to the horizon, splitting around hills too ancient to justify leveling. Low walls mark squares larger than ordinary towns. Roofless halls occupy isolated ridges. Stairways descend into the ground and rise elsewhere after days of walking. There are reservoirs, terraced gardens, causeways, drainage courts, towers no taller than trees, and whole neighborhoods built as concentric rings around empty ground.

No metal glints here. The city was made with basalt mauls, wedges of hard greenstone, fire, water, fiber rope, and an unreasonable abundance of patience. Its builders learned stone so intimately that masonry sometimes seems less constructed than persuaded. Blocks interlock without mortar. Doors turn on polished stone pivots. Water gates are raised by timber capstans. Roofs are woven from alpine reeds and the pale stems of plants that grow only in the wind-shadow of walls.

Because the plateau is vast enough to shape its own weather, each distant quarter has a climate and calendar of its own. Mist provinces drift slowly across the north. In the west, dry cold reigns under a blue sky for months at a time. Along the central depressions, rain walks in narrow bands, leaving one street drenched and the next powdered with frost. Storms do not cross the city so much as live within it, migrating between habitual squares.

Vegetation is abundant but widely spaced, as though the living world has inherited the sparse grammar of the architecture.

There are no forests in the ordinary sense. Instead, enormous cushion-plants occupy acres of shallow soil, their surfaces bright with tiny waxen leaves. Black-rooted shrubs grow in isolated circles around warm fissures. Lichen coats the oldest avenues in cream, orange, and blue-gray diagrams. In drainage gardens, tall translucent reeds fill briefly with collected rain and bow under its weight. Groves of low white trees stand miles apart, each trunk braided from many stems, each leaf dark and fleshy enough to keep water through a season of frozen wind.

Nothing grazes them. Nothing nests among them. There are no insects beneath the leaves, no worms in the wet soil, no fish in the reservoirs, no birds turning over the cliffs. Flowers open without pollinators. Seeds crawl by their own slow tensions into cracks in the paving. Fallen matter is dismantled by films of fungus and patient colonies of green-black mold. The ecology moves, but it does so without animals: by swelling, splitting, sporing, rooting, fermenting, freezing, and thawing.

This absence gives the city its quiet. Wind passes over the cushion fields with a low clothlike hiss. Reeds knock together in cistern gardens. Water ticks from carved mouths after rain. Far below the plateau edge, thunder is sometimes heard in clouds invisible from the summit. Otherwise there are long intervals in which even a dropped pebble seems to travel farther than it should.

Yet the place is not still.

Every dawn, condensation pearls on millions of tilted slabs. Channels awaken. Counterweighted sluices descend by themselves as wooden components absorb water and grow heavier. Courtyards flood finger-deep; planted basins drink; cisterns fill beneath carved floors. At noon the sun loosens frost from walls in sudden glittering sheets. Toward evening, the cold returns across the paving as a visible darkening, and moisture begins again to gather.

In the southern wards, warm air rises from fissures and keeps gardens unfrozen. In the north, the ground heaves each winter, lifting old foundations and lowering them again a fraction out of place. Moss steadily invades houses. Root pressure closes some passages while opening others. Without beasts, all urgency belongs to weather, plants, stone, and the city’s hydraulic machinery.

There are people, though rarely enough to preserve the impression of abandonment.

They live in widely separated households along the old water routes. A settlement of three hundred may occupy structures intended for thirty thousand. Smoke rises from one roof in a whole district. Figures in felted cloaks cross enormous plazas alone, carrying baskets, chipped knives, pottery jars, and polished-stone tools. Their bridges and reservoirs are maintained collectively, but they build little that is new. Most labor is interpretive: clearing one channel, blocking another, deciding which tilted wall may safely be trusted for another generation.

They have no beasts of burden and no memory of animals. Their language contains no distinction between “alive” and “self-moving,” because plants, waterworks, weather, and certain stones all move by themselves, while people are sometimes known not to.

The rules of distance are unreliable here.

A road viewed from a tower may appear to terminate at a hill ten kilometers away, yet a traveler following it can walk for nine days without reaching the hill. Another avenue, apparently lost beyond the western horizon, enters a courtyard directly behind its starting point. These distortions are not constant. They migrate with weather. Fog lengthens enclosed streets. Frost shortens staircases. Rain makes certain doorways deeper than the buildings containing them.

The inhabitants mark safe measurements with cords dyed red. Where a cord disagrees with itself—where its two ends meet despite being stretched straight—the place is bounded by standing stones and left uncultivated.

Time has similar watersheds. In some quarters, lichen grows between one footstep and the next. Elsewhere a flame can remain bent above a lamp for days without consuming oil. There are wells into which a spoken word may be dropped; its echo returns years later in the voice of someone not yet born. The people treat these as civic facts, no more supernatural than ice. Their calendars are local. Their laws specify not only where an act occurred but under which sky, at what pressure, and on which side of the most recent rain.

The oldest buildings are the least ruined. Erosion has worked on them for so long that it has removed every vulnerable form. Corners have become curves, carvings shallow stains, towers rounded mounds containing chambers. The ancient masonry has settled into shapes that wind and frost can no longer meaningfully change. Newer walls collapse. Old ones endure because they have already lost everything that could be taken.

Some have become indistinguishable from terrain. Roads widen into exposed bedrock. Houses continue beneath fields as rooms lined with roots. A ridge may reveal, in low sun, that its regular terraces are a buried amphitheater hundreds of kilometers long. The plateau itself might be construction. No one agrees. Excavation is discouraged, partly because tools are precious and partly because the deeper layers do not consistently lie in the past.

At the center of the city is a basin called the Still Country. It is hundreds of kilometers across and only a few meters lower than the surrounding land. Moisture drains toward it, but no lake forms. Instead, water gathers in isolated patches: a shining strip beside one wall, a circular pool on a hill, droplets suspended chest-high over a field of moss. After storms, these pockets change position.

Here reality is weakest.

Shadows occasionally remain after their owners have moved on. Parallel walls meet at a distance but not when approached. A person may see the back of their own cloak far ahead on an empty road. During cold clear weather, the sky is reflected in dry paving stones, complete with clouds not present overhead. Gardeners sometimes find that the underside of a leaf faces upward on both sides. Cut stones bleed condensation for months. Sound travels around absent corners.

There are rules, but they are discovered rather than explained.

Never cross running water while its reflection is still.

Do not enter a room whose frost is on the inside unless someone has already left it that morning.

Count stairs by touch, not sight.

If a road becomes narrower toward the horizon, turn back before it is thin enough to be a line.

When two rains fall at once, shelter beneath living wood, never worked stone.

These practices keep most journeys ordinary.

At the basin’s center stands no palace, temple, or monument. There is only a square of bare pale rock so large that its far edge drops beneath the curvature of the plateau. Nothing grows there. Rain falls upon it but does not wet it. Footsteps make no sound.

Across the square, at irregular intervals, buildings become visible—vast low silhouettes under perfectly normal daylight. They have walls and entrances but no perspective; distance does not reduce them. Sometimes they look newly built, their stone sharp and dark. Sometimes they appear so eroded that they are only discolorations in the air. People do not approach them. Not because approach is forbidden, but because no recorded traveler has managed to become nearer.

The city has adapted itself around this impossibility. Water channels bend away from the square. Roads stop at its boundary. Moss grows in a thick green margin along its edge, fed by all the moisture the pale rock refuses. Settlements form a sparse ring beyond it, each watching a different set of horizons.

On certain evenings, after a narrow rain and before the frost has returned, every reservoir on the plateau reflects those unreachable buildings. Even cups of water show them. Dewdrops contain their doorways. The inhabitants cover household vessels with woven lids and wait.

Nothing emerges.

Nothing calls.

The whole elevated continent remains almost silent: old stone, scattered habitation, broad cold gardens, and weather moving through the broken dimensions of the streets. Water continues its patient circuits. Roots widen cracks by fractions. Lichens rewrite walls. Somewhere, a wooden gate grows heavy with rain and lowers itself into a channel. Far away, another gate rises.

And over distances too great to understand as one place, the city quietly irrigates itself.
