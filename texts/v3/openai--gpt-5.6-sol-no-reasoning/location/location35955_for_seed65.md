At an elevation where most mountains narrow into summits, the land unexpectedly opens.

It is not a plateau in the ordinary sense, but a broad congregation of stone basins suspended among higher ridges. The basins lie one beyond another for many miles, divided by low saddles and long shelves of exposed rock. Around them rise pale, eroded pinnacles whose upper edges are almost always lost in brightness. There are no trees to lend scale. A distant cliff may be a short walk away or half a day’s journey; without vegetation or buildings, distance is measured by the slow disclosure of cracks, ledges, and bands of mineral color.

The stone is old and settled. Nothing looks freshly broken. Even the steepest faces have been worn into flutings and shallow folds, their edges rounded without becoming gentle. Gray limestone lies over dark volcanic beds, and in places the two have eroded together into striped terraces resembling immense, weathered steps. Scree is scarce. Loose fragments have already found their resting places in hollows, fitted so firmly among one another that they seem mortared there. The landscape has the composure of something whose collapsing ended ages ago.

Yet it is deafening.

Wind crosses the basins continuously, compressed by the surrounding passes and accelerated over the stone. It does not come in gusts. It is a permanent body of moving air, a broad, brilliant current that has held nearly the same direction and force beyond any living memory. Along the western scarps it produces a low, sustained thunder. Narrow fissures whistle at distinct pitches. Cavities in the limestone answer with deeper notes, and whole cliffs vibrate almost below hearing, felt first through the soles and ribs.

The result is not noise as interruption but noise as climate. There is no silence to be broken. A falling pebble makes no audible sound. Wings vanish into the roar. Even close speech is reduced to mouth movements and pressure against the ear. The animals here communicate by posture, color, touch, and vibration through the rock.

The light is nearly as overwhelming. Above the highest cloud belts, the air is thin, clean, and intensely blue. Sunlight strikes the pale stone and rebounds from every surface. Shadows are hard-edged and blue-black, though the wind fills them with reflected glare. Travelers—if there were travelers—would instinctively narrow their eyes and keep them lowered. Snow is largely absent, despite the cool air. Where it falls, the wind scours it from open ground and compacts it into narrow seams on sheltered faces.

Water survives only by refusing exposure.

Toward noon, when the sun warms one side of the rock while the other remains cold, condensation gathers inside deep fissures. Droplets form in darkness, creep down mineral walls, and collect in smooth bowls below the surface. There are hundreds of these hidden reservoirs, but most hold no more than a few mouthfuls. Their presence is betrayed by dark stains, a fringe of moss, or the clustered tracks of animals converging on what appears to be an ordinary crack.

In the lowest basins lie several shallow tarns, separated by miles of dry stone. They are remarkably still. The same winds that overwhelm the plateau pass over them without touching their surfaces, for each tarn occupies a circular depression beneath an overhanging lip. Seen from above, they resemble polished disks of black metal inset into white rock. Brief rain visits the heights at long intervals—not as storms that approach and depart, but as condensation spilling suddenly from a standing cap of cloud around the outer peaks. The rain lasts minutes. Much of it evaporates or runs into cracks before it can gather into streams.

No river crosses the open ground. The river is beneath it.

Far below the basins, water moves through a fixed labyrinth of caves. At certain thin places its pressure can be felt as a minute trembling underfoot. It emerges nowhere on the high plateau. The only evidence is an occasional cold breath rising from a shaft, carrying the smell of wet stone into the dry brightness.

Vegetation holds to strict boundaries. Low mats of black-green lichen cover the lee sides of boulders. Silver cushion plants occupy cracks where dust has accumulated, each mound dense enough to resist the wind and only a handspan high despite great age. A wiry red sedge rings the hidden pools, its blades lying flat against the rock in one direction. There are also small translucent plants beneath overhangs, pale as insect skins. They live on condensation and reflected light, and retract into the stone during the driest intervals.

From a distance the plateau seems barren. Up close, almost every protected seam is inhabited.

The abundance belongs chiefly to animals.

Great numbers of small, long-legged grazers move across the terraces. They are slate-colored, narrow-bodied, and low to the ground, with split pads instead of hooves. They feed on lichen by scraping it from stone with pale front teeth. Herds may contain thousands, yet they spread so widely that the land still appears empty. There can be half a mile between individuals, each animal standing alone in the glare, all aligned into the wind. Their coordination is exact. When one lowers its head, the motion passes through the herd in a gradual wave, visible across an entire basin.

They do not call. A ridge of flexible quills runs from each animal’s shoulders to its tail. Changes in angle and color transmit alarm, direction, courtship, and recognition. At closer range they stamp coded rhythms into the rock. The vibration travels farther and more reliably than sound.

Above them circle broad-winged hunters. These rarely flap. They anchor themselves in the stationary torrent of air and remain almost fixed over particular ledges, casting shadows that barely move over the course of a day. Their feathers are white underneath and nearly transparent at the tips, so their wings appear to fray into the sky. They hunt not by sight alone but by reading disruptions in the wind: the wake of a running animal, the turbulence around a raised head, the warm thread of breath leaving a hiding place.

The cliffs contain still more life. Colonies of small gliding reptiles cling inside the resonant fissures. Beetles crowd the condensation caves. Pale crustaceans inhabit the deeper pools, although no open route connects those pools to any sea. On sunny shelves, dark insects stand with their bodies tilted to avoid overheating, while bright red mites drift in the air on strands fine enough to be carried indefinitely by the unwavering wind.

Despite this activity, little visibly changes.

The herds follow the same routes worn shallow into the stone. The hunters hold the same aerial stations. Water gathers in the same bowls and withdraws through the same hairline channels. Plants grow so slowly that a cushion may retain the shape of a missing stone for centuries. Carcasses do not accumulate. Scavengers reduce them quickly, then lichen and wind erase what remains. Birth and death occur in profusion, but at the scale of the plateau they cancel each other perfectly.

There is no succession here, no advance of forest, no deepening soil, no wandering river. The environment has arrived at a severe equilibrium. Each creature depends on a pressure, route, shelter, or interval that does not vary. The wind is constant enough to serve as geography. Animals navigate by its direction as reliably as by the position of a mountain. The cloud cap renews the same hidden pools. Erosion continues, but so evenly and slowly that the forms appear permanent, as though weathering has ceased to be an event and become a property of the stone.

No structures stand anywhere. No walls, cairns, roads, bridges, stakes, or cut steps interrupt the natural surfaces. Still, there are hints that intelligence exists among the plateau’s animals.

Near certain water cracks, flat stones have been shifted—not stacked or shaped, merely turned to expose the damp undersides where edible growth collects. Small primates with heavy fur and dark, bare hands use flakes of naturally shattered glassy rock to pry shell-creatures from the condensation bowls. They carry these flakes for a time, test their edges, and abandon them when dulled. Nothing is kept long enough to become an artifact in the landscape. There is no settlement because shelter cannot be built against the wind better than it has already been carved by water. There is no fire because fuel is nearly absent and flame cannot hold in the moving air.

Their craft is temporary: a stone selected, used, and released; a bone employed to probe a fissure; braided sedge tied around a bleeding limb, then left to decay. Knowledge resides in gesture and memory rather than objects. Families teach one another where the invisible water lies by pressing palms to particular vibrating surfaces. They read the rock through touch. Their maps are rhythms tapped wrist to wrist.

At the center of the plateau is the largest basin, a shallow expanse perhaps ten miles across. Nothing grows in its middle. The floor is a single sheet of pale stone etched with parallel grooves, all running east. The wind passes over it with such force that the air becomes faintly visible: not dusty, but warped, bending the brilliant horizon into trembling bands.

Animals avoid crossing the central floor except during migration. When they do, they travel in dispersed lines, bodies flattened, claws finding the ancient grooves. Thousands may be present, but the spacing between them preserves the impression of solitude. Overhead, the fixed-wing hunters remain poised. Along the rim, primates crouch in niches with their hands pressed to the ground, feeling the passage of the herd as a distant, intricate drumming beneath the immeasurable roar.

Nothing arrives. Nothing departs. The sun crosses, but every day illuminates the same contours. The tarns retain the same level. The wind never veers. Even clouds seem pinned to the outer summits, endlessly forming and dissolving in place.

The plateau is full of lives and almost empty of things. It is bright enough to erase color, loud enough to erase individual sound, and old enough to have worn change itself into equilibrium. Here, at the top of the world, motion is constant—and the landscape never moves.
