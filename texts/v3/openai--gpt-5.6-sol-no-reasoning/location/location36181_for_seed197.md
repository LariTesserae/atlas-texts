The city lies in a shallow basin no larger than a village green, warm and nearly level, with the suggestion of an old river beneath it. No stone marks its boundary. There are no bricks, no cut timbers, no tools or traces of tools. It is nevertheless unmistakably urban.

Streets divide it. Towers rise over intersections. Galleries pass above plazas, and deep apertures lead into chambers arranged in repeating districts. Everything is plant.

The streets are long strips of pale, tightly braided root, smooth from generations of traffic. Their raised edges turn runoff toward cistern flowers: enormous ground-level blooms whose waxen cups retain rain for days after a shower. Buildings stand on either side, though “stand” is only partly accurate. They brace, lean, tighten, and periodically rearrange themselves. Their walls are the hollow ribs of broad-leaved plants fused edge to edge, green on the sunward side and lavender-gray within. Their roofs are layered canopies that close during rain, funneling water down fibrous columns, then open afterward to release trapped light and clouds of animals.

There is very little solid mass. This is the first contradiction the city presents. Vegetation fills nearly every surface, yet most of its volume is empty.

The towers are thin cages of stems surrounding shafts of warm air. Houses are membranes stretched between living arches. Bridges are single taut vines. Beneath the plazas lie root vaults large enough to enter, but their walls are no thicker than bundled fingers. Even the soil has been drawn upward into living tissue. There are only scattered crumbs of earth below, dark deposits caught in root knots, and shallow pans of old silt at the basin’s lowest points. The city has consumed its own ground and made architecture of it. Between one living surface and the next are clear hollows: lanes, courts, chambers, vertical wells of sun.

One may look across an entire district and see almost nothing obstructing the air.

This openness makes the sound impossible.

It is deafening from dawn to dusk and only differently deafening at night. Millions of small animals inhabit the walls. Cicadas pulse inside hollow columns until each tower becomes a resonator. Flat-backed frogs clap the undersides of leaves. Beetles drum on stretched bark. Birds with translucent throat sacs trade descending shrieks from bridge to bridge. Colonies of winged mites move as single sheets, and when they turn, their wings produce a dry roar like surf drawn over gravel.

The city itself contributes. Pods burst with percussive cracks. Tension vines contract and twang across courtyards. Seed chambers rattle in the warm drafts. Broad roof leaves slap against one another whenever rain approaches, while underground, roots grind slowly through old root sheaths with a noise like massive doors opening.

No quiet space exists. Sound passes through every membrane and travels along every connected stem. Press an ear—or a hand—to any wall and the whole city can be felt: feeding, warning, mating, splitting, drying, drinking. The loudness has substance. Loose spores arrange themselves in rippling patterns beneath it. Water in the cistern flowers dimples even when the air appears still.

Moisture comes intermittently. Most hours are warm and merely damp: condensation pearling on shaded surfaces, vapor breathing from stomata, thin threads of water crawling along leaf veins. Rain arrives suddenly from low, compact clouds. For several minutes the city becomes an instrument of tremendous force. Roofs invert. Gutters unroll. Trumpet leaves rotate toward the sky. Every drop is caught, redirected, divided, and stored in transparent bladders embedded throughout the walls.

Then the clouds pass. The sun returns with ordinary brightness, neither dim nor glaring, and the water begins to migrate.

It does not always move downward.

Droplets climb the sides of towers in orderly files. Pools drain upward into hanging gardens. Sometimes a cistern flower releases its contents as a trembling sphere that rises through a plaza and breaks against the underside of the canopy, raining again from within. This is accepted by the animals, if not understood. Flocks gather below such interior weather. Snails extend their bodies from the walls to drink passing beads. Thread-thin fish swim briefly through suspended water, then seal themselves inside wet seed cases until the next release.

The basin may once have been a bend in a river. Its outer rim is softly scalloped, and old channels curve under the root streets. Erosion has long since reached an agreement with growth. Nothing here looks raw. Breaks are rounded over; dead supports become nurseries; collapsed chambers settle into ponds and are bridged by seedlings adapted precisely to their span. The oldest structures are not the tallest but the most hollow. Time has removed their substance while preserving their outlines.

In the western quarter, an entire avenue consists of absences shaped like former trees. New vines grow around these empty silhouettes without entering them. Birds perch on curves where branches once were, supported by nothing visible. Rain strikes invisible crowns and spills down invisible trunks. Mosses coat the air there in green sleeves. The avenue is considered old, though there is no one present who considers it so.

There are no inhabitants in the ordinary civic sense. No species governs, plans, or builds. Yet the city maintains districts, routes, thresholds, and recurrent public gatherings.

At midday, herds of six-legged browsers move through the main rootways, trimming the walls to a uniform height. Their feeding keeps passages open. Small black birds follow, pressing stray stems into gaps with their beaks, not from intention but in pursuit of larvae that live where walls are damaged. The larvae secrete adhesive sap; the sap repairs the building. Thus traffic, predation, feeding, and construction are the same process seen at different scales.

In the central plaza stands the widest emptiness in the basin: a clear circular volume enclosed by a low ring of flowering buttresses. Animals avoid crossing its ground, though they crowd its edge. Every few hours, the space fills with the shadow of a vast tree that does not exist above it. The shadow turns in a wind unfelt by the surrounding city. Its leaves overlap the real streets, darkening them in precise, moving patterns. Wherever the shadow’s fruit falls, the living pavement opens.

Things emerge.

Sometimes they are ordinary—blind mice, beetles, pale shoots, warm gusts carrying rain smell. Sometimes a flock flies upward from the opening and continues into the soil rather than the sky. Sometimes a mature tower rises root-first, hanging for a moment over the plaza before passing out through the unseen crown of the shadow tree. Once each wet season, the opening produces hundreds of red frogs, all calling at the same pitch. Their combined note alters distance. While it lasts, the city’s far edge appears within arm’s reach, though the walk to it remains unchanged.

Other instabilities are smaller and continual.

A lane may fork into three routes in the morning but have only one history by evening. Animals returning from the vanished branches arrive with pollen from flowers that never grew. Some chambers are larger within because their walls have mistaken resonance for space: a loud enough call can open an alcove; silence closes it. Predators exploit this. Long-bodied cats crouch outside seed halls and roar doorways into being. Prey answer with countertones that turn those doorways into vertical drops or chambers full of thorn smoke.

Nothing here is certain whether it is matter, behavior, or memory.

The ecology has learned not to distinguish them.

Near sunset, the whole basin changes rhythm. Heat stored in the pale root roads rises. Tower flowers unfold high above the streets, each holding a cup of evening condensation. Bats stream from wall cavities in numbers disproportionate to the structures that contain them. They coil above the city in black columns, then dive through windows and emerge from unrelated windows elsewhere. Predatory vines follow their motion, lashing through open air; moths scatter; the transparent fish wake in their seed cases and beat their fins against the pods, adding a glassy chime beneath the greater roar.

This is when the city grows most active. Streets narrow or widen according to the passage of herds. Bridges sag to collect insects and snap upright when lizards run across them. Whole facades flower, are pollinated, and shed their petals in minutes. The fallen petals do not cover the ground. They pass through it and appear on the roofs, where nocturnal grazers consume them.

Darkness never makes the place quiet. Frogs take over from cicadas. Roots boom below the basin in slow sequences. Somewhere at all times, rain can be heard striking leaves, even under a clear sky. The sound may belong to tomorrow’s weather, arriving early through the city’s connected tissues.

At night, pale fungi illuminate certain intersections, but only weakly. Moonlight and starlight remain the principal illumination, caught and multiplied by wet leaf skins. The hollow architecture becomes a lace of silver edges around black volumes. Eyes appear at every height. Some blink. Some are flowers. Some are openings into rooms where a different season is occurring.

The city has rules, though they are ecological rather than decreed.

Nothing dead may remain merely dead; it must become passage, vessel, soil, or song.

Water may rest, but it need not fall.

A structure persists as long as some creature behaves as though it is there.

Every empty space belongs to whatever sound can fill it.

No route is allowed to lead only to one place.

And at the basin’s edge, where the streets fray into reeds and low warm marsh, nothing may leave in the form in which it arrived.

Butterflies crossing outward become yellow leaves. Browsers become knots moving beneath the mud. Rain becomes a smell. A call becomes a flock. From beyond the rim, things enter in similar transformations: wind steps into the first avenue as pale insects; sunlight crawls in as a golden, many-legged animal and dissolves across the roofs.

Seen from outside, the basin looks almost vacant. A few thin spires. A trembling canopy. Reeds around a shallow depression. There is no sign that an urban world occupies the hollows between those stems, or that its population numbers in millions, or that its streets are being revised with every feeding mouth and falling drop.

Only the noise gives it away.

It rises from the little green basin in a continuous, overwhelming column—courtship and weather, hunger and architecture, alarm and germination—all indistinguishable, all amplified by the city’s immense hollowness, as though the earth had opened a mouth there and remembered how to be alive.
