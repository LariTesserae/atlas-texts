The basin lies beneath a continental ice sheet, though it does not feel enclosed. Its ceiling is too high and too dark to establish itself as a roof. Where the lamps fail, one might imagine open night above—but every sound returns, multiplied, from ice and stone.

Cold fills the place completely. It settles into steel, stiffens cable insulation, draws the moisture from breath, and lays clear skins over every exposed surface. The temperature rarely varies by more than a degree. There is no wind in the ordinary sense, only broad movements of refrigerated air caused by machinery and the slow breathing of shafts that lead upward.

Across the basin grows a forest of pallid subterranean plants.

They are not luxuriant. Their abundance is architectural rather than crowded: immense root columns stand hundreds of metres apart, rising from black sediment into darkness. Between them, runners as thick as railway embankments cross the floor in long, gentle ridges. Sheets of fibrous tissue cling to the walls, pleated and translucent, fed by seepage through fractures in the rock. Low mats cover nearly everything else. They resemble moss from a distance, but underfoot resolve into millions of close-packed, colourless leaves with waxy margins and a faint mineral sheen.

The plants have little use for visible light. Their energy comes from bacterial films around their roots, from dissolved compounds carried down through the ice, and from heat leaking out of the industrial works. Where sodium lamps burn, a weak olive pigment sometimes appears. Green exists here only as a stain around infrastructure.

Water is present in pockets rather than expanses. Condensation gathers on the high ice ceiling and falls at irregular intervals in heavy, solitary drops. Narrow streams emerge from warm machinery, run a few hundred metres through channels in the vegetation, then vanish beneath root mats or freeze into blue ribs. Shallow pools lie in depressions, dark and perfectly still under crusts of clear ice. The largest of these is crossed by a pipe bridge and is called a reservoir, though its water level rises and falls according to seasons no one below can directly observe.

The terrain is young. The basin opened only recently in geological terms, cut by meltwater beneath the glacier and then widened by heat from the first installations. Its scarps are sharp. Splintered rock projects from the walls without the rounding of weather. New fissures open with clean, pale faces. Even the plants, for all their scale, seem provisional: quick colonists occupying a space that could close again.

Human structures are scattered widely through this living country. There is a pumping house beside the reservoir, a transformer station on a shelf of basalt, three ventilation towers, and a chain of relay sheds marking an old survey line. Most are small, blunt buildings with insulated metal walls. Their roofs carry ice on the cold side and dense curtains of roots on the warm side. Doors are surrounded by heated frames to keep them from sealing shut.

Rails cross the basin in two long arcs. Only one remains active.

The dominant fact of the place is sound.

At the eastern end, beyond several kilometres of root plain, industrial boring machines press continuously into the bedrock. Their cutter heads are hidden underground, but their force enters everything. The noise is not a discrete roar so much as a permanent physical condition: a deep, deafening vibration that thickens in the ribs and makes pools tremble under their ice. On top of it comes the high whine of turbines, the rhythmic hammer of pressure pumps, the detonation of fractured stone, and the thunder of ventilation shutters cycling somewhere overhead.

Conversation outdoors is impossible. Workers communicate by lamps, hand signs, and pulses sent through wrist units. Warning systems use colour and vibration rather than sirens, because another siren would disappear into the general violence. Inside the service buildings, walls are lined with layered cork, rubber, felt, and compressed plant fibre. Even there, metal cups creep across tables.

The vegetation accommodates the noise. Root columns thicken along lines of vibration, building concentric layers of tough cellulose around their cores. Delicate reproductive structures appear only in the acoustic shadows behind rock shoulders and machinery housings. When the largest drills stop for maintenance, the forest responds to the sudden quiet: sealed pods split open across the basin, releasing dry spores into the still air. This happens slowly, over hours, as if silence were a form of dawn.

Animal life is scarce. Small white crustaceans inhabit the warmer drainage channels. Blind insects graze bacterial skin from the underside of leaves. Once or twice a season, technicians report seeing long-bodied fish in the reservoir, though no one agrees how they arrived. There are no birds, no large predators, no swarms. Most movement belongs to conveyor belts and suspended loads.

The plants themselves move too slowly to watch. Over months, a runner may climb a rail. Over years, a root column may lean toward a generator hall. Maintenance crews cut corridors through the mats, and pale sap freezes on their saws like candle wax. Cleared spaces do not erupt immediately into growth; they close by degrees. A painted mark on the floor may remain visible for a winter, become veiled the next, and disappear entirely after five or six.

There are rules for living below. Never touch a root column while blasting is underway: internal fractures can eject splinters hard enough to pierce protective clothing. Never cross a dark rail without checking the vibration beacon. Never drink meltwater downstream of a copper pipe. If the floor plants fold their leaves, leave the area; they sense pressure changes before instruments do. If the machinery stops without scheduled warning, move toward a reinforced station, because either the grid has failed or the ice above has shifted.

Nothing happens quickly except accidents.

Otherwise the basin changes with patience. Heat spreads outward from a new cable trench and wakes a band of roots over several months. A pool freezes from the edges inward. Mineral deposits slowly whiten a pipe. The ceiling descends by fractions. Engineers extend the works; vegetation absorbs their abandoned routes. Each exists partly by means of the other. The machines supply warmth, carbon, and exposed stone. The forest stabilizes loose sediment, filters drainage, and insulates the foundations. Neither side intended this partnership.

From the western overlook, the place extends farther than the lamps can reveal. Isolated work sites shine through the dark like low settlements. Between them stand the vast, pale trunks, sparsely spaced but innumerable, each emerging from a continuous floor of leaves. Condensation falls through the beams. Trains move as strings of amber rectangles. Beyond, the boring complex throws a diffuse red glow against the underside of the ice.

The whole buried valley shudders around it: dim, frozen, almost empty of animals, and filled nearly to its limits with patient vegetal life.
