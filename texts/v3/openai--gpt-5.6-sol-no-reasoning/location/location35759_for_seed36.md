Below the ordinary ground lies a country of plants.

It is not a cavern in the usual sense. There are no bare walls, no mineral vault, no long history of water carving stone into rooms. The hollow is young—or else perpetually held at the instant of becoming young. Its floor has only just gathered beneath it: pale clay still smooth from formation, soft ridges of mineral paste, blunt shelves not yet cracked or weathered. Nothing here bears the signatures of erosion. There are no channels, screes, worn passages, or fallen blocks. Every surface looks pressed, extruded, swollen into place.

Yet almost none of that geology can be seen. Vegetation has occupied it too completely.

The plants make the floor, the columns, the ceiling, and the distances between. Broad root mats overlap like rafts, firm enough to walk upon and warm underfoot. From them rise clustered trunks with thin, translucent bark. Some join overhead in arching ribs; others flatten into partitions or spread into immense horizontal leaves that divide the hollow into tiers. What first appears to be a forest gradually reveals itself as a single continuous anatomy. Branches graft into neighboring branches. Roots cross and fuse. Curtains of vine descend into beds of moss and pass through them without any clear boundary between species or individual.

There may be many plants here, or only one plant expressing a whole ecology.

The landscape extends for miles beneath the surface, opening into chambers the size of valleys and narrowing between them into crowded green throats. No viewpoint commands the whole. Distance is measured by repetition: another rank of white-green pillars, another suspended meadow, another glowing canopy beyond. The density is immense, but not oppressive. Growth has left passages through itself—aisles, courts, bowls, terraces, and warm clearings—as though the organism had included emptiness in its design.

Light is everywhere.

It does not enter through openings. There are no visible shafts to the upper world, and no sun can be found by following the brightness. The leaves themselves shine. Their veins hold a steady yellow-white radiance, while their flesh diffuses it into green, gold, and a faint aquatic blue. Some vast leaves turn their luminous undersides downward, making ceilings brighter than noon. Small beadlike buds stud the roots and cast sharp pinpricks across the clay. There is almost no shadow except deep inside the densest knots of stems, and even there a diluted glow passes through living tissue.

The heat is constant: close, tropical, and bodily. It has none of the violence of desert heat, because there is no direct sun and almost no exposed rock. Instead it surrounds everything like breath. Bark is warm to the touch. Sap runs just beneath translucent membranes, moving so slowly that it appears motionless. The air smells of crushed cucumber, resin, damp fiber, and the faint sweetness of flowers that never quite open.

Water is present, but not abundant enough to govern the place. It gathers in pockets.

Condensation pearls along the luminous ribs overhead, each droplet held in place without falling. Shallow basins occur where the root floor folds inward, filled with clear, hot water and lined by fine red filaments. Between chambers lie occasional streams, though “stream” is almost too active a word: they are seamless ribbons resting in beds of white roots, without ripple, current, or visible source. Their surfaces are perfectly level. Stems enter on one bank and emerge from the other, continuing through the water as if through glass.

Elsewhere moisture exists only inside the plants. Cut nothing—there are no tools here, and little that could wield one—but the swollen vines suggest reservoirs under pressure. Cup-shaped leaves hold a single ration of rain, although rain never seems to fall. Every quantity is fixed. No pool dries. No droplet grows.

Nothing visibly changes.

The place is lush without growing. Flowers remain forever at the edge of blooming. Coiled fronds never unfurl, though their tension can be felt. Pale root tips press into soft clay without advancing a fraction. Seeds hang beneath leaves in transparent pods, perfectly formed and permanently ungerminated. The whole subterranean forest occupies an impossible equilibrium: fertility arrested without decay, abundance without season.

This stillness extends even to accidents. A branch bowed under its own weight does not sag further. A leaf torn long ago—or perhaps born torn—keeps a bright green edge around the wound. Fallen petals do not brown. They lie on the root paths like scraps of colored wax, neither rotting nor being absorbed.

Sound is correspondingly sparse. There is a low interior hum where the largest trunks meet, too steady to count as a rhythm. Condensation sometimes seems about to drip, but seldom does. The water gives no murmur. Leaves do not rustle because the air does not move. A traveler hears their own breath and the soft compression of root felt underfoot; when they stop, the silence closes without echo.

Animals are rare enough to feel like errors.

In the luminous pools live a few transparent, finless things no longer than a finger. They remain suspended at fixed depths, their hearts showing as dark red commas. High in the vinework, pale insects cling beneath leaves, one insect to a chamber, wings folded and unworn. Occasionally a narrow-bodied creature resembling a lizard may be seen wrapped around a glowing stem. Its eyes are open. It does not blink. Days later it will be in precisely the same posture, yet it is warm and living.

There are faint signs that something once attempted to inhabit the forest—or that the forest once imagined structures of its own.

Low walls appear in scattered clearings, assembled from lengths of hollow stem woven together before they fused. A few small shelters stand beneath leaf canopies. Their doorways are rounded, their rooms empty, their surfaces swallowed by grafts until architecture and growth are indistinguishable. There are bridges over motionless streams, but they consist of roots braided in patterns too regular to be accidental. One broad court contains rows of seatlike bulbs facing a smooth wall of living bark. Nothing has been carved upon it. Nothing suggests metal, fired clay, rope, or tool marks.

Perhaps these are ruins. Perhaps they are organs whose purpose only resembles habitation.

The largest chambers contain stranger formations. Thick trunks rise in incomplete rings around patches of naked clay, as if a grove were being assembled but had stopped at its first instant. Root arches emerge halfway from the ground, smooth and raw, their undersides still filled with unformed mineral paste. Here the boundary between geology and biology disappears completely. Stone buds from wood. Leaves harden toward limestone but never finish. Clay extrudes rootlike fingers, while roots broaden into terraces with the blunt profiles of newly raised land.

This is a world before weather has had permission to begin.

Nothing has been rounded by passage, because there has been no passage. Nothing has been cut by water, because water has not yet chosen a downhill. The terrain is not ancient bedrock exposed by excavation; it is terrain in draft. The vegetation seems older than the ground supporting it, as though life arrived first and persuaded matter to condense around its roots.

In some regions the canopy hangs so low that one must crawl between hot, glowing leaves. In others it opens into radiant underworld savannas, miles wide, where pillar-plants stand amid waist-high cushions of chlorophyll. Farther on are vertical forests descending into broad depressions. Their branches point downward, their roots spread across the ceiling, and their luminous flowers face a floor they will never reach. There is no sense of a deeper abyss. Every descent ends in another level of living architecture, another stable layer held beneath the last.

Orientation becomes uncertain, though never chaotic. The brightness has no direction. The heat does not increase with depth. Pools lie level on sloping surfaces. Roots grow upward, sideways, and down with equal conviction. What functions as ground is simply whichever living mat accepts weight.

The forest has rules, but they are not the rules of development.

A thing may be alive without changing. Water may be present without circulating. Heat may persist without a source. Brightness may fill a closed world without casting time across it. Structures may exist without builders. Raw land may remain raw forever, continuously new but never newer.

The only disturbance is the visitor.

Footsteps leave temporary dimples in the root mats. Breath clouds the glossy leaves. A hand placed against a shining trunk interrupts the light and makes a small, startling shadow. For a moment, the underground country contains sequence: approach, touch, withdrawal.

Then the roots lift. The leaf clears. The warmth closes evenly around the absence.

The place returns to the bright instant in which it has always just begun.
