The place is no larger than a sleeping chamber, though it occupies the summit of a mountain too high for ordinary weather.

It is hidden inside the mountain’s crown—not carved from it, because there is no natural stone here. The peak, the chamber, and perhaps the entire mountain have been assembled by hand. Layer upon layer of fired clay, pumice brick, knotted cane, shell lime, and black glass rise into the clouds. Nobody can say what supports the lower courses. Looking down through certain cracks reveals more summit below: additional crowns descending point-first into steaming blue distance.

At the highest apparent point stands a low, domed room called the First Crevice.

Every surface inside it is crowded.

The walls are tiled in overlapping thumb-sized scales, each pressed with fingerprints. The floor is a tight mosaic of porous bowls. Buttresses of woven reed occupy the corners, sweating resin; ladders cross other ladders; gutters pass through shelves, then double back through the walls. Clay pipes cluster like roots, too numerous to trace. Nothing is ornamental because nothing has room to be merely ornamental. Hooks support ropes, ropes cinch swollen joints, joints brace conduits, conduits carry warm rainwater into basins that feed moss beds planted in the handles of tools.

There is no bare substrate. Even the mud is manufactured: powdered brick mixed with ash, salt, hair, and captured condensation. When something green pushes through it, one cannot tell whether the growth has germinated or been constructed.

The heat is severe. It gathers beneath the dome and presses downward, wet and intimate, as if the room were the closed mouth of an enormous animal. Moisture condenses on every cool edge. Tiles weep. Threads of water travel along strings stretched under the ceiling, meet at knots, and fall in heavy drops. After the daily cloudburst, every cup in the floor overflows at once. The water has nowhere open to spread, so it climbs.

It climbs the legs of stools. It climbs knotted cords. It crawls upward over plaster in branching films, drawn toward the hottest places rather than the lowest. The inhabitants once marked this as an error in the construction. Later generations built channels to accommodate it. Now the room is a watershed turned inside out: springs rise into rafters, ceiling pools feed upward gutters, and rain collects beneath objects instead of above them.

The vegetation is dense but miniature. Velvet moss upholsters the wet mortar. Liverwort fans overlap along the pipes. Ferns no taller than fingers unfold from clay sockets, each socket stoppered with woven fiber to keep its handmade soil from floating away. Pale vines wind through reed braces and disappear into walls; elsewhere the same vines emerge older, scarred, and bearing hot translucent fruit.

Their leaves are almost black in the dimness. They open only when shadows pass over them.

There are few animals. Now and then a white mite the size of a grain of salt crosses a basin rim. Tiny shell-backed things cling inside the drains, feeding on mineral foam. A single long-legged frog is sometimes heard in the western wall, though no cavity large enough to hold it has ever been found. Its call is not a croak but the noise of a mallet striking wet ceramic. Three blows, a pause, then five. Whenever it calls, a new pipe appears somewhere in the chamber.

The room is consequently loud.

Water drums on stretched hides and clatters through ceramic throats. Reed valves wheeze. Hot joints pop. Wet ropes creak under loads they did not bear a moment earlier. The entire structure gives off a continuous grinding hum, the sound of thousands of rough surfaces settling without ever becoming settled. During cloudbursts, thunder enters the dome through a narrow horn and is divided among dozens of jars. Each jar repeats a smaller thunder at a different pitch. The result is less like a storm than a crowded workshop attempting to manufacture one.

This is fitting, because the First Crevice is perpetually under construction.

Erosion has not begun here. Nothing is old enough to have been worn smooth. Water does not diminish material; it adds to it. Each drop leaves behind a skin of red clay. Every rivulet builds a ridge along its path. Corners sharpen with use. Footsteps deposit thin soles of stone instead of scuffing them away. Cracks do not widen into ruins but fill themselves with wet ceramic, producing seams, lips, and eventually new walls.

The builders work mainly by touch. Their tools are basalt flakes, bone awls, shell scrapers, wooden mallets, plumb cords, baskets, and palms hardened by lime. Metal is rare: a copper needle, green with moisture, is kept in a sealed gourd and used only to pierce holes through places that do not yet exist.

No builder can stand fully upright. The interior is too packed with scaffolds, reservoirs, cultivation trays, and fresh architecture. They crawl through it, passing handfuls of mortar from one to another. Their bodies are seldom visible all at once. A wrist appears between pots; toes grip a ladder; an eye looks through a hollow brick to judge the color of the steam. In the dim amber light, it can seem that the chamber itself has many brown hands and is assembling its own organs.

There are strict rules, though none are written.

Nothing may be removed. A broken bowl must be crushed into mortar. A dead leaf must be stitched into a roof patch. Exhaled breath is caught in waxed cloth and wrung into the fern cups. Even shadows are considered useful and are pinned beneath tiles with thorns, to be released during the brightest hour.

No horizontal line may remain unattended. Left alone, it may become a horizon.

This has happened before. A shelf once stretched itself into a distant plain covered in steaming red grass. A builder sent to retrieve a cup from its far end returned years older, although the others had watched her crawl only six arm-lengths. The shelf was promptly folded and lashed into a column. Sometimes, in heavy rain, grazing lights can still be seen moving deep inside its grain.

Elevation behaves similarly. The chamber is at an extreme height, but height here is not distance above ground. It is a substance, concentrated like heat. The builders store it in narrow-necked jars.

Unstoppering one causes ears to ring and blood to lighten. Loose objects fall toward the sky. Fern spores rise with such force that they puncture plaster. At midday, when the summit receives its greatest charge, the builders siphon excess altitude through braided tubes and knead it into bricks. These bricks are almost weightless, yet placing one beneath a wall makes the wall immeasurably tall on the inside.

Thus a person may reach into a hand-sized niche and feel cold winds from elevations no body could survive. Snow sometimes forms in these niches despite the oppressive heat. It melts before falling, becomes warm rain, and feeds the room again.

Light enters through pinholes sewn into the dome. There is little of it. Each beam is narrow and dusty, crowded with steam, spores, and suspended droplets. Yet the beams do not stay where their holes are. They wander slowly over the walls like searching fingers. When two cross, a small additional corner comes into existence beneath them. The builders hurry to brace it before the light moves on.

Unbraced corners collapse into noon.

The oldest worker—if age has meaning in a room where surfaces become newer through use—tends the central basin. It is only a shallow clay dish, continually overflowing. Inside it lies the mountain’s unfinished summit: a soft, dark cone no higher than a knuckle, ringed by tiny steaming clouds. Minuscule terraces cover its slopes. On clear days one can see figures moving there with baskets and mallets, constructing a domed chamber at the peak.

The elder never touches the cone directly. She rotates the basin by means of four cords, one finger’s width each morning. This turns the weather around the larger mountain outside. Pulling the wrong cord would expose the room to open sky, which is regarded not as freedom but as catastrophic emptiness.

Occasionally the little workers on the basin’s summit stop and look upward.

At those moments everyone in the First Crevice becomes still. Mallets pause. Water hangs in beads along the climbing streams. The hidden frog withholds its measured blows. Even the heat seems to wait.

Far above—or far within—a roof opens.

For a breath, the builders see enormous fingers descending through cloud, carrying a fresh lump of wet clay. The fingers press it carefully against the outer surface of their world. Darkness deepens. The chamber contracts by the thickness of a leaf. New condensation forms. Somewhere close by, where there had been no space at all, another wall begins to sweat.

Then work resumes, faster than before.

There is always less room.

There is always more place.
