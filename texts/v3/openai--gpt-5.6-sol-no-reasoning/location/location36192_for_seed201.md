At the end of a narrow mountain shelf, almost three miles above the low country, there is a hollow called the Sun Basin.

It is not a basin in the broad sense. From rim to rim it is scarcely wider than a small village, and most of that width is empty air. Three young ridges meet around it, their rock still angular and undecided: black ribs, pale fracture planes, fresh fans of scree that have not yet softened into soil. Frost pries stone from the walls each night. By noon, sunlight releases it. Pebbles tick down the slopes, and several times a day a larger piece leaps free and falls in long, strangely delicate bounds.

The hollow is open on its eastern side. Beyond that opening there is no neighboring slope, only the white distances of the high atmosphere and, far below, ranges reduced by haze to blue folds.

Light rules the place.

The sun arrives abruptly over a knife-edge ridge and floods every surface at once. Snow crystals burn like salt. Wet leaves shine hard enough to hurt the eyes. Shadows remain blue-black and cold, but whatever the sun touches warms quickly: stone, hide, feathers, the resinous bark of the low trees. The air itself stays bitter. Breath smokes even at midday, and water in shaded cups keeps a skin of ice through most of the year.

Moisture comes in small, intense events. Clouds climbing from the valley strike the basin’s western wall, spill a few minutes of sleet or bright rain, and dissolve. At dawn, condensation pearls on every stem. Meltwater runs beneath loose stones rather than across them, audible but rarely visible. It emerges in half a dozen places as threads no broader than cord, gathering briefly in black pools among the roots before vanishing again through cracks.

The largest pool lies under an overhang near the center of the hollow. It is only a few paces across. Clear water fills a raw fracture in the mountain, and its bottom is crowded with green pebbles, drowned moss, insect husks, and one pale branch polished smooth by ice. In winter the pool freezes from its edges inward, but a dark eye remains open over the spring.

Nearly all the basin’s vegetation crowds around these hidden waters.

There are waist-high thickets of alpine willow, their trunks thick, twisted, and many years older than their height suggests. Between them grow cushion plants packed as tightly as wool: red-tipped stonecrop, silver grasses, small blue flowers with translucent petals, and dense mounds of moss that store rain for days. Several crooked conifers stand in protected niches. None is taller than a shelter wall. They trail branches along the ground as if crawling away from the wind.

Seen from above, the green forms a loose ring around an empty center of pale stone. The vegetation appears lavish only because everything beyond it is so bare. Each patch is an island, separated from the next by yards of gravel, frost-shattered slabs, and air. Roots occupy cracks too narrow for a finger. Where soil exists, it is dark, deep-smelling, and startlingly rich, made from generations of leaves and feathers caught in the same hollows.

The animals know these patches intimately.

Small gray grazers—something between hare and mountain antelope—move through the willow lanes in family groups. They have short legs, broad hooves, and dense coats that shed moisture in silver beads. They rarely run far. When a shadow crosses them, they flatten among the rocks, and the basin seems to lose half its life at once.

Rock mice live beneath the scree. Their passages lace the slopes, warmed by composted grass. Black-and-gold finches descend in flocks so sudden and numerous that bare bushes flower with birds. They strip seeds, quarrel in whispers, then lift together and disappear over the rim. Pale moths emerge when sunlight reaches the damp moss, even while ice remains in the shade. Beetles crawl over snow to feed on pollen blown down from the plants.

Above them circle the cliff kites. These long-winged hunters seldom call. They ride the basin’s rising air without apparent motion, turning their heads to inspect every gap between stones. Now and then one folds its wings and drops. There is a rush no louder than cloth being pulled through a hand, a brief scattering of gravel, and then stillness again.

Larger hoofed animals visit at irregular intervals. Their tracks cross the scree in implausible places, descending walls that seem to offer no footing. They lick mineral crust from the spring and browse the willows down to their knotted cores. During the short summer, two or three may stand together in the empty central ground, shedding strips of winter coat that snag on thorns and flutter for weeks.

For all this abundance, the basin is quiet. Its life has adapted to exposure by withholding sound. Birds give thin contact notes rather than songs. Hooves land carefully. Insects hum only in the warmest pockets. The dominant noises are minute ones: meltwater clicking under rock, leaves rubbing together, ice loosening from the cliff, the granular hiss of scree adjusting to its own weight.

Wind is different. It does not blow continually, but it arrives as an event.

The air first goes completely still. Flying insects settle. The finches descend into the willows. Then a clear pressure moves through the eastern opening, and the basin exhales. Dust rises in sheets. Grasses lie flat. Water is combed from the pool and carried upward as glittering spray. The wind lasts perhaps a minute, perhaps an hour. When it passes, every loose thing has shifted slightly, and the silence seems newly made.

There are signs that people come here, though usually no people can be seen.

Under the central overhang stands a shelter assembled from fitted stone. It is low enough that an adult must crawl inside. No mortar binds it; flat slabs lean inward, counterweighted by larger blocks, and the roof is sealed with moss, clay, and animal hair. A smoke hole opens at the back, blackened but cold. Within are a stone-lined hearth, a sleeping platform of woven willow, bone hooks, several bundles of dried stems, and a shallow bowl pecked from green rock. Everything has been placed above the damp ground.

Elsewhere, three waist-high cairns mark a route across the scree. One holds a fragment of red cloth faded almost white. Another is capped with a polished horn. Near the spring, repeated scraping has hollowed a seat into the rock, and the stone beside it bears parallel grooves made by sharpening blades. The blades themselves are chipped glassy stone, mounted in antler or wood. A few copper rivets appear in the shelter door, hammered thin and reused so often that their original purpose is unknowable.

No path leads cleanly into the basin. The people who visit climb along ledges from the south and dismantle parts of the route behind them each season. They come for water, medicinal moss, feathers, and the shed underwool of the gray grazers. They do not hunt within the hollow. This is not entirely reverence. Killing here brings scavengers; scavengers attract the kites and the greater cliff predators; panic drives animals over the rim; blood entering the spring fouls its narrow underground course. What resembles a sacred rule is also precise mountain ecology.

They have another rule: no structure may stand higher than the willows.

Tall objects draw lightning. They cast shade on the small plants. More importantly, they offend the wind. In the basin, the wind is understood not as weather but as the hollow’s method of rearranging mistakes.

The mountain is still making the place. Hairline cracks open after cold nights. A spring may disappear and return several yards away. Moss islands split when the stone beneath them moves. Once every few decades, a section of the western wall falls and buries part of the green ring. For several years afterward the scar remains sterile and bright. Then insects gather in its sun-warmed crevices, birds bring seeds, and roots begin their patient work on the new rock.

Nothing here is old except the habits of living things.

Even the terrain has a provisional quality. Ridges are sharp enough to cut leather. Rubble retains the clean color of its interior. Channels have begun but not yet deepened. Soil is still an accident, accumulated in pockets and held in place by roots, fur, and fallen twigs. The basin could be erased by one collapse, yet on clear mornings it feels less fragile than concentrated—as though an entire highland had been compressed into a single bright room.

Toward evening, the sun lowers behind the western wall and the temperature falls almost immediately. Condensation freezes on leaves. Meltwater quiets beneath a new film of ice. The finches leave first; then the grazers emerge from cover to feed in the last horizontal light. Cliff kites settle on inaccessible projections, their silhouettes becoming indistinguishable from hooked stones.

The final sunlight stays on the easternmost spire. For a few minutes that raw blade of rock shines yellow above a basin already blue with cold. The spring remains open and black. Somewhere beneath the scree, mice move seeds through warm tunnels. Somewhere on the rim, an unseen hoof dislodges a pebble.

It falls, taps once, and comes to rest.

The hollow waits in complete brightness for the dark.
