At the top of a cool green mountain stands a city with almost no inhabitants.

It occupies a shallow bowl no more than a mile across, enclosed by low ridges of pale, newly exposed stone. The ridges have not yet acquired the worn anatomy of mountains. They possess no gullies, scree slopes, or deep-cut streams. Their faces are blunt and softly jointed, as if lifted from beneath the soil only recently. In places, slabs remain tilted against one another, separated by narrow black seams. Water wells from those seams without having carved channels. It beads, gathers, and spills in thin, immaculate curtains.

The city was built around these weeping rocks.

Its buildings are numerous enough to fill a town, but they are widely spaced. Each stands alone in its own court of moss and rain-dark paving, with thirty or forty paces of open ground before the next structure begins. There are plazas broad enough for markets and stairways wide enough for processions, yet most lead only into silence. From the surrounding ridges, the settlement resembles a diagram laid out at large scale: squares, circles, straight avenues, cisterns, terraces, and roofless enclosures arranged with deliberate care, but very few moving figures.

There is no true center. Instead, the city has several empty hearts.

One is a circular reservoir whose stone rim has been raised course by course as the water rises. Another is an amphitheater planted thickly with ferns. A third is a paved court occupied only by a single unworked boulder, glossy with condensation. Paths radiate from all three, overlap, and sometimes meet at blind walls. The plan seems to anticipate districts that have not yet been constructed—or inhabitants who have not yet arrived.

Everything is made by hand. The walls are fitted from split stone and packed earth, without mortar. Timber frames are lashed with bark cord, pegged at the joints, and roofed in layered shakes. Gutters are hollowed logs. Water gates are operated by wooden levers weighted with baskets of rock. Bronze is known but scarce: it appears in a few green-stained hinges, saw teeth, sounding plates, and ceremonial nails. Most daily work is done with stone mauls, horn wedges, fire-hardened poles, and blades of dark volcanic glass brought up from lower country.

The construction is extensive but not advanced. It has been achieved through patience rather than force.

Many walls remain half height. Foundation trenches, lined with moss, run beside streets and then stop. Stacks of shaped blocks wait under woven covers silvered by rain. Because growth is slow here and stone is difficult to split, a house may take three generations to complete. Builders carve their names on unfinished courses, and descendants add theirs above. Completion is not considered a necessary virtue. The city’s most honored structures are still becoming.

The mountain itself seems to share this custom.

Its rock is raw and largely uneroded, pale gray with faint green and blue veins. Soil lies over it in pockets rather than continuous fields. Where water gathers, vegetation rises with astonishing richness: shoulder-high ferns, waxy broadleaf shrubs, dwarf conifers, moss cushions, and dense ropes of flowering creeper. The plants crowd every damp hollow but leave the smooth rock expanses almost bare. Thus the bowl alternates between lush green chambers and great, empty planes of stone.

The air is cool even at midday. Clouds often move just above the enclosing ridges, trailing loose veils through the upper streets. Condensation forms on walls that have not been rained on. Roof edges drip for hours beneath a clear sky. Water is everywhere nearby but seldom abundant in one place: a skin on the paving, a trembling line under a threshold, a shallow cistern, a seep emerging beneath a fern root. There are no torrents, and no river leaves the bowl. Overflow descends through fissures in the mountain, feeding springs far below.

Rainwater is governed carefully. Every roof has a voice in a system of gutters, basins, runnels, settling pools, and underground jars. Certain lanes are deliberately concave, becoming temporary streams after rain. Doorways are raised. Gardens occupy sunken courtyards where dampness persists through dry weeks. The inhabitants say water should be persuaded to pause but never imprisoned. A sealed cistern is taboo. Every reservoir must have an open mouth through which mist, insects, fallen leaves, and moonlight can enter.

The loudness of the place is startling.

It does not come from crowds. Wind enters holes bored through walls and produces low flute tones. Drips fall into ceramic vessels tuned to different depths. Roof gutters knock against support posts. Water wheels turn slowly in hidden chambers, lifting smooth wooden hammers that strike suspended stone plates. Cloth vanes snap over the plazas. Hundreds of little sounds are amplified by empty courts and hard, wet walls until the city seems densely inhabited by labor.

During rain the whole settlement becomes an instrument.

Drops drum on shingles and stretched hides. Overflow spills down staircases. Stone chimes answer from distant terraces. Gutters roar for minutes at a time, then diminish to a gurgling speech. The circular reservoir produces a continuous bass note as water enters through a pipe below the surface. In the fern amphitheater, every frond hisses. Voices, when they occur, are nearly lost.

The people therefore communicate at a distance with flags, mirrors, smoke, and gestures. Spoken conversation is conducted in recessed alcoves lined with moss and wool. Public decisions are made in a chamber beneath the city where the only sound is the slow dropping of water through the ceiling. A speaker must finish before the next drop falls into the central bowl. Some intervals last half a minute, others nearly two. Politics here has developed a measured, fragmentary grammar.

The inhabitants are few—perhaps two hundred in a settlement built for thousands. They live in small groups separated by unused neighborhoods. One district may contain a potter, three children, and an elderly keeper of drains; another, beyond six vacant squares, houses a clan of timber workers. At night their hearths appear as isolated amber points across the bowl.

No one agrees on why the city is so large.

Some believe its builders expected people to climb from the valleys once the lower forests became unlivable. Others say the empty houses are reserved for generations not yet born. The oldest tradition holds that a city should be constructed before its citizens exist, just as a nest precedes the egg. Accordingly, each family spends part of every year making rooms for strangers. They raise walls, cut thresholds, plant enclosed gardens, and leave behind bundles of dry kindling. No locks are made.

Ecology takes advantage of the vacancy. Moss carpets interior floors. Ferns unfurl from hearths. Small alpine trees root in cracks between paving stones, though their roots have not yet had time to buckle the masonry. Pale mushrooms form rings in shaded assembly halls. A creeping plant with blue-black leaves colonizes walls nearest the seeps; when heavy drops strike it, the leaves fold, exposing white undersides and briefly transforming whole facades.

Animals are uncommon. The height and enclosure discourage most large creatures, and the young terrain offers few established burrows or migration routes. Tiny gray frogs live in the open cisterns. Long-winged swifts arrive for several weeks each year, circling above the bowl and nesting under roof beams. A solitary white-coated grazer sometimes appears on the northern ridge, watches the city, and disappears before anyone reaches it. Tracks are treated as events. Children place rings of pebbles around them, and no one crosses the rings until rain removes the prints.

There are no rats, no domestic herds, and almost no insects apart from black midges above the pools and heavy moths that emerge on misty evenings. The absence of ordinary animal noise makes the mechanical and watery clamor seem still greater.

Time behaves gently here. Very little happens quickly except weather.

Buildings rise by inches. Reservoir levels shift across decades. Trees expand their crowns from one generation to the next. A wet wall acquires moss, then ferns, then a shrub; eventually the builders decide whether it is still architecture or has become a garden. No strict distinction is maintained. A completed roof may be dismantled if it interrupts a seep. A courtyard may be declared a wetland and removed from the city plan. Conversely, a stand of trees may be enclosed by walls and given a civic name without being cut.

The most unusual feature is discovered only by prolonged observation: new stone appears.

Not dramatically, and never under a witness’s gaze. A stair left unused through winter may have an extra shallow step by spring. The bottom of a cistern slowly rises, even though no sediment can be found. Pale knobs emerge from bare plazas, first soft enough to scratch with a bone tool, then hardening over years. Walls built directly on bedrock sometimes become fused to it, their lowest courses swallowed by fresh mineral.

The mountain is not merely resisting erosion. It is still forming.

The people shape their streets around these growths. They do not quarry the soft new stone, believing it has not finished deciding what it is. Instead they place small woven screens around it and wait. Some outcrops broaden into benches; others become pillars or seal doorways. One has risen through the floor of a council chamber as a smooth waist-high table. Another is slowly dividing a major avenue. Surveyors mark each change with charcoal lines and dated copper pins, creating dense records of motion too gradual to perceive.

This gives the city its peculiar sense of anticipation. The unfinished buildings are not ruins, though they may look like ruins. The empty plazas are not abandoned, though weeds occupy them. The mountain is young, the settlement is premature, and even the streams have not yet learned where to run.

On clear afternoons, ordinary daylight fills the bowl without spectacle. Wet roofs brighten. Ferns cast crisp shadows. The pale ridges stand against the sky, unscarred and almost featureless. From somewhere across the vacant city comes the knocking of a water hammer, then the boom of a stone plate, then another answering from farther off.

Between those sounds, the place waits—not for restoration, but for arrival.
