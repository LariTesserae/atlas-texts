The landscape is hidden inside the mountain.

Not in a cavern, exactly. There is no open chamber, no darkness broad enough for an echo to cross. It occupies the hairline interval between two masses of basalt: a steep, sealed fracture scarcely wider than a hand, ascending through the young volcanic ridge for several hundred meters. The walls press so close that almost every point in the cleft is occupied—by stone, gas, heat, or the bodies of animals.

The basalt is new. It has not yet weathered into soil or been rounded by rain. Its surfaces are black, angular, and faintly lustrous, with scalloped skins where separate pulses of magma cooled against one another. Here and there the rock remains soft enough, over geological time, to creep. Pale seams of sulfur and iron salts mark the hottest fissures. Needles of crystal thrust into the gap, growing from both sides until their tips meet and mesh.

There is no water. None seeps from above; none condenses on the walls. The gases are too hot and dry. Moisture entering in an animal’s breath is stripped away almost at once, caught in mineral films or carried upward as an invisible trace. Life here runs on fat, salts, inhaled gases, and the steady chemical imbalance between the deep rock and the thin high-altitude air.

The fracture lies near the summit, though no creature inside it ever sees the mountain. Outside, the peak stands above a desert plateau under a bleached sky. Inside, elevation reveals itself only through pressure: the air is thin, quick, and difficult. Every breath must be worked for.

Almost no light enters. A dim red suggestion rises from hotter depths, too weak to illuminate so much as stain the crystal edges. Far overhead, daylight penetrates a few centimeters into the crack as a gray blade and dies. The animals navigate by touch, vibration, heat, and odor. Eyes, where present, are pinpricks useful only for distinguishing darkness from the slightly lesser darkness of an opening.

Yet the place is crowded beyond expectation.

Thousands of small, pale creatures pack the cleft in vertical colonies. They resemble insects because the demands of stone favor jointed legs, gripping hooks, narrow bodies, and hard skins, though they belong to no familiar lineage. Their shells are mineralized with silica and iron. Newly molted individuals are ash-white; older ones darken to rust, then black, becoming nearly indistinguishable from the wall.

They cling in overlapping layers. The smallest inhabit pores in the basalt. Larger grazers rasp translucent films from warm mineral faces—not vegetation, but colonies of simple chemotrophic cells that grow without light, feeding on sulfur, carbon compounds, and metal ions exhaled from the rock. These films are so thin that the walls appear bare. Only under magnification would the fracture reveal its living glaze.

Predators move through the packed colony with terrible patience. Long, flattened hunters insinuate themselves between bodies, advancing one segment at a time over many hours. They have no need to chase. Prey is everywhere, pressed flank to flank. A predator merely anchors its rear hooks, opens its jaws, and waits for the slow pressure of the colony to deliver something edible.

There are winged forms as well, though they cannot truly fly in the narrow gap. Their wings have become vibrating panels. They brace themselves across the fissure and beat those panels against the hot air, driving ventilation through the colony. These living fans are responsible for much of the noise.

The fracture roars.

It is not the changing roar of wind or surf, but a continuous industrial bellow: gas forced through constrictions, shells rattling on stone, countless wing-panels thrumming at slightly different frequencies. Deep below, the mountain contributes a heavy subsonic pulse. Mineral plates expand in the heat, bind against one another, then release with sharp metallic cracks. Every few minutes a pocket of volcanic gas squeezes upward through the crowded passage, producing a rising shriek like steam in a pressure valve.

Nothing has been built here. There are no engines, ducts, cables, furnaces, or manufactured parts. Nevertheless, the fracture behaves like a factory.

Heat enters from below. Thin air enters from above. Living fans regulate the draft. Grazers harvest chemical films; predators harvest grazers; scavengers dismantle shells and return their minerals to the walls. Dense clusters of animals sort themselves by temperature as precisely as components on an assembly line. Near the lower reaches, heat-tolerant colonies oxidize sulfur. Higher up, cooler-bodied forms process their waste gases. At narrow thresholds, armored animals act as valves, shifting by fractions of a millimeter to redirect flow.

The system produces nothing that leaves the mountain except heat, altered gas, and an occasional husk blown from the summit crack. Its industry has no architect and no intended product. It is simply what abundance becomes when forced into a space with almost no room.

Change is slow despite the noise. A crystal may grow for a century before closing one passage. The colonies respond over generations, widening some routes by scraping at softer deposits and abandoning others to suffocation. The basalt itself is still settling. Stress migrates through it grain by grain. New microfractures appear as pale lines, then warm, then fill with animals.

Erosion has hardly begun. The mountain is not being worn down so much as completing its first adjustments after birth. Edges remain keen. Fallen particles have not accumulated into dust because every grain is seized, swallowed, cemented into a shell, or fused back to the hot wall. There are no old surfaces. Even where the rock has stood for thousands of years, it possesses the raw look of something cast yesterday.

At intervals lasting decades, the fissure tightens. Colonies compress. The rattling rises in pitch as shells grind together. For months there may be no perceptible movement, only mounting pressure. Then some internal contact gives way, and the entire population shifts upward by the width of a fingernail. To the creatures, this is migration, disaster, and geological age compressed into one event. To the mountain, it is nothing more than settling.

At the narrowest point—an inconspicuous seam beneath a shelf of black glass—the landscape becomes smaller still. Two warm rock faces approach within a few millimeters. The inhabitants there are almost flat, layered one upon another like dark scales. Their synchronized breathing pulls gas through the seam with a deep mechanical hum. Heat reddens the iron in their shells, though the glow is too faint for sight.

Here the whole place can be touched at once: one wall against the back, the other against the limbs, neighbors above and below, the mountain’s pressure passing through every body. There is no horizon. No empty ground. No weather except temperature and flow.

Only the packed, lightless ascent of stone, breath, and living machinery—loud as a mill, slow as a continent, concealed inside a crack that from the outside appears to be nothing at all.
