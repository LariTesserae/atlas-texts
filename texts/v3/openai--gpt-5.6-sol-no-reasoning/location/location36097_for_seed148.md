At first it seems to be a small, abandoned city in a hollow.

The hollow is low and shallow, no more than a few streets across, enclosed by smooth banks of black loam. There are no cliffs and scarcely any exposed stones. The earth appears too new for that: uncut, unweathered, still holding the blunt shapes of whatever pressed it upward. Its slopes have the soft, provisional look of clay lifted from a mold. After rain, little gullies begin to form, but roots close them before they can deepen. Erosion is less a history here than a negotiation just getting underway.

Almost nothing stands in the open. The avenues are broad, the rooms cavernous, the squares empty. Buildings occur at generous intervals, separated by darkness and low expanses of leaf. Yet vegetation occupies nearly every available surface. Emptiness here does not mean barrenness. It means a city whose substance has been spread thin around immense hollows: trunks curved into vaults, woven root floors, walls of living pith surrounding vacant chambers. The air itself remains largely unobstructed, but every boundary is alive.

The city may have been planted rather than built.

Its towers resemble pollarded trees enlarged to civic proportions. Their outer bark is plated with iron scales and ceramic insulators, while their interiors are hollow, ribbed by pale wood and furnished with ladders grown as regular sequences of knots. Long bridges sag between them: cables wrapped in vine, vine wrapped in copper, copper disappearing into woody callus. In places the distinction between infrastructure and plant anatomy has become useless. Sap runs beside coolant. Flowering tendrils coil through transformer housings. The central pumping station opens and closes its vents like stomata.

No lamp illuminates more than a few steps. Most of the light comes from weak industrial signals: a red diode beneath moss, the blue shudder of an overloaded relay, a furnace seen through several curtains of root. The upper canopy admits almost nothing from the sky. By day, the city is charcoal green. At night, it is not noticeably darker.

Moisture arrives irregularly. There is no lake, only black puddles cupped in leaves and a narrow river that passes under the eastern wall, surfaces briefly in a cistern square, then dives into roots again. Rain tends to be brief and fine. It ticks on iron bark, gathers on hairs and cables, and continues dripping for days after the clouds have gone. Condensation sweats from cold pipes. Animals drink from bolt heads, trumpet-shaped flowers, and the shallow impressions left by vanished machinery.

The air is cool enough that breath sometimes shows. It smells of wet cellulose, ozone, fungal milk, and old machine oil. Heat persists only in isolated organs of the city: beneath a boiler-house grown into a stump, around buried dynamos, inside swollen fermentation tanks where fallen leaves are converted into gas. Small creatures cluster at these warm sites in living carpets.

There are thousands of animals, though one may stand for a long time without seeing any.

They inhabit thickness rather than open space. Beetles circulate in the walls. Pale mice travel through bundled conduits. Moths sleep under switch plates, opening their wings whenever the current changes. Thin black frogs live in the telephone mouths and give a single soft note before rain. Colonies of glass-bodied mites graze electrical corrosion from copper lines. High overhead, unseen birds move through the canopy, their wingbeats displaced from them by several seconds. Now and then a flock crosses an empty square, but its shadows arrive first, running across the ground before anything has taken flight.

Larger fauna use the avenues. Root-antlered deer step from doorway to doorway, pruning the city as they feed. Low, many-jointed predators follow them, placing their feet with mechanical care. These hunters are covered in fur except where numbered plates interrupt the body. Whether they were manufactured, domesticated, or merely named by a vanished industry cannot be determined. They rarely attack anything in sight. Instead, they stalk the future positions of prey, crouching beside places where a deer will later appear.

The city is busy without being loud.

Sap pumps throb below hearing. Belts turn deep in sealed workshops. Leaves touch one another with a dry, intimate hiss. Occasionally a distant engine starts, operates for exactly forty-seven seconds, and stops. Nothing obvious changes. The loudest common sound is water falling from one leaf basin into another. Even predation is nearly silent; cries are absorbed by the vegetation and later released elsewhere as birdsong, sometimes weeks afterward.

Near the middle of the hollow stands the civic greenhouse, although “stands” is not always true of it. It is a long glass structure with a brick foundation, narrow chimneys, and roof trusses overgrown by white creepers. On some visits it lies north to south; on others, east to west. Its doors remain fixed in place when the building turns, so that they may open onto walls, soil, or the interior of some room that has not yet grown.

Within are production beds arranged in severe municipal rows. They cultivate hinges, cable fruit, translucent membranes, and small green pistons. Workers are absent, but the machinery continues husbandry. Pruning arms descend from rails. Seed sorters click once every few hours. A rusted gantry carries baskets from one empty end of the greenhouse to the other. The baskets return full of warm feathers.

This is where the landscape’s rules begin to loosen visibly.

Roots do not always lead to the plants they support. A vine cut on the western wall may bleed sap from a pipe across the square. Distances vary with fertility: fertile rooms are larger inside, while dead rooms contract until their opposite walls touch. Every seed contains a small, dark interior space. Most are microscopic, but old seeds may hold corridors, stairwells, even weather. Animals sometimes enter them. Their descendants emerge from newly germinated trees bearing adaptations to climates that have never existed outside.

Growth is the city’s principal form of construction. Plans are encoded in seasonal sequences. One species lays foundations, another raises columns, another produces windows as sheets of hardened resin. Pollinators carry not only pollen but zoning instructions. If the wrong moth visits a flower, a staircase may grow sideways or an apartment may bud from a bridge. Such mistakes are not removed. The city incorporates them, producing districts that can only be entered by crawling through blossoms or waiting for gravity to turn.

Gravity does turn, though locally and without warning. Dew may slide up a stem. A hanging nest may drop toward a wall and remain there. In one alley, leaves fall horizontally into a drain set chest-high in the masonry. The drain leads to a very young river, no wider than a hand, which is still deciding which direction is downhill. It carries grains of raw mineral, soft and uncolored, as though stone here has not finished becoming stone.

The ground shares this incompleteness. Beneath a skin of humus lies pale substrate with the consistency of wax. Roots score it with the first channels. Worms knead layers into existence. Pebbles begin as soft nodules around fungal threads and harden only when uncovered. Fossils sometimes form around living animals and dissolve when the animals move. There are places where footprints precede the feet that will make them.

The hollow itself may be under construction by the life it contains. Each root draws the basin a fraction deeper. Each burrowing creature pushes up the enclosing banks. Rain offers erosion, but the plants intercept it, distributing its force through leaves, gutters, vessels, and mouths. Thus the place remains new. It cannot acquire ruins because decay feeds directly into growth, and growth revises the structure before age can settle.

There are suggestions that people once lived here, or will.

Small platforms wait at intersections. Handrails are polished. Rows of coats hang inside a station, damp at the shoulders. Ticket machines accept discs sliced from certain seed pods and issue destinations such as **UNDER THE NEXT RAIN**, **THIRD ROOT**, and **YESTERDAY’S SOUTH QUARTER**. The rail line consists of two narrow beds of moss. At uncertain intervals, the moss flattens in a wave and a wind passes along it, carrying the smell of passengers.

One should not follow that wind too far. The borders of the hollow are close in ordinary space but inconsistent in memory. Anyone walking outward soon reaches the smooth earthen bank. Anyone looking back from the bank sees a much larger city than the one crossed: miles of dark canopy, smokestacks breathing pollen, innumerable windows glowing faintly beneath leaves. Turning around restores the small basin. Looking back enlarges it again.

The animals understand this better than visitors. They never look across an open place before entering it. They listen, taste the condensation, and watch the plants lean. When every leaf points inward, they hide. When the leaves point upward, rain is coming. When the leaves point toward something that is not there, the city is about to produce a new district.

It happens quietly.

The soil rises in a low rectangular swell. Roots stitch its edges. Beetles arrive carrying screws and spores. A scaffold of stems unfolds, followed by bark, brick, insulation, chambers, ducts, and nests. In less than a night, another factory or tenement has grown around a central emptiness. By morning it is already inhabited by moths and mice. The engines begin their muted work, manufacturing an unnamed product from water, darkness, and animal heat.

Then the streets make room for it.

Not by shifting. Not exactly.

The city simply becomes large enough that it has always been there.
