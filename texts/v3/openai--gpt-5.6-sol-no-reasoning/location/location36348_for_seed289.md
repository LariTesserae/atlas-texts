Beneath a slab of pale stone lies a chamber no wider than a sleeping body.

It is not a cave in the usual sense. No stream hollowed it, no fracture opened it, no roots pried its ceiling upward. The chamber seems to have been caught just before such processes began: a raw interval inside the rock, an embryonic hollow whose walls remain knuckled and unfinished. Blunt mineral buds protrude everywhere. Ribs of limestone press inward. The floor rises in crowded nodules until there is scarcely room for a hand between it and the low roof.

And yet the place is bright.

The light has no visible source. It resides in the stone itself: a cool, white brilliance filling the chamber evenly, leaving almost no shadow. Every calcite grain shines without glittering. Damp surfaces carry flat silver highlights. Translucent shells, insect wings, wet eyes, and quartz splinters are illuminated from within, so that the packed interior resembles a cross-section through some luminous organ.

The chamber is full—far fuller than its dimensions should allow.

Pale reeds grow sideways from hairline seams. Their stems are jointed and sparse-leaved, but they occur in such numbers that together they make a rigid thicket. Between them are cushions of colorless moss, patches of blue-black liverwort, and small waxy fungi shaped like folded ears. None seems to compete for light. There is light everywhere, even under stones and inside the throats of flowers.

The flowers are rare but peculiar. Each consists of a single clear petal cupped around a bead of water. The bead never spills. It does not evaporate or swell, though the cool air is heavy with moisture. Elsewhere, condensation lies in perfect ranks along the ceiling: thousands of droplets, all identical, all suspended at the same degree of fullness. On the floor are shallow pockets of dark water fitted exactly among the mineral bulges, without inlet or outlet. Their surfaces remain smooth even when touched.

There is no flowing water here. There is only dampness held in place.

Life occupies every remaining interval. Tiny white crabs brace themselves in cracks too narrow to admit their bodies. Long-legged mites stand over pools. Beetles with glassy wing cases wedge themselves among the reeds, while flat snails tile the walls in overlapping spirals. Threadworms knot the moss. Minnowlike creatures crowd the water pockets so densely that the pools seem filled with braided muscle rather than liquid.

Larger animals cannot fit, but their forms recur in miniature. There are thumb-sized, featherless things with the posture of herons. There are velvet-backed grazers no bigger than seeds, browsing the fungi in solemn rows. Blind amphibians lie folded into mineral cups, each occupying its depression as precisely as an organ seated in a socket. Many mouths open. Many translucent throats bulge. Nothing is eating, fleeing, mating, sleeping, or dying.

Nothing changes.

The chamber is loud nonetheless.

A continuous chorus fills it: chirring, piping, scraping, wet clicking, the drumming of rigid wings. From the pools comes a dense croak like stones grinding underwater. Reeds vibrate against one another. Shells rasp against the wall. It should be unbearable at such close range, but the sound has no beginning and does not intensify. It is a single fixed loudness, as steady as the brightness. Listen for one call and it seems repeated; listen longer and it becomes clear that repetition would require time. The call is simply there, permanently sounded.

Even echoes do not travel. They occupy the air in nested layers. Near the entrance one hears a thin metallic ringing; a finger’s breadth deeper, the same ringing becomes a hollow boom. Every point in the chamber possesses its allotted portion of the noise.

Among this natural congestion are a few signs that resemble construction. Three small arches of fitted pebbles stand beneath the ceiling. A stair of six shallow steps descends into one of the pools, though the chamber has no being large enough to use it. Along one wall, pieces of shell form a border of alternating black and white. In the farthest recess sits a roofless enclosure made from upright flakes of mica.

These may be ruins, nests, or formations that merely resemble intention. No tool marks appear on them. The pebbles are not mortared; they simply meet with impossible exactness. Moss does not overgrow them, nor does it recede. One clear flower stands in the mica enclosure, holding its permanent drop.

The rock itself looks newly born. Its ridges are soft in shape but hard to the touch, as though the idea of erosion has appeared without erosion yet occurring. Tiny channels begin on the ceiling and end after a few millimeters. Incipient gullies crease the floor, prepared for water that never runs. A delicate stone bridge crosses one such dry channel. Beneath it, a grain of sand is poised where a current might someday carry it.

Someday has no meaning here.

Nothing grows farther. Nothing wears down. No drop falls. No animal completes a step. Still, the chamber is not frozen. Wings hum. Throats call. Antennae tremble. Moisture gleams. It exists in a kind of equilibrium more complete than stillness: all its motions have become properties of place.

To enter, one must lift the pale slab and put an eye close to the seam. The brilliance immediately fills the pupil. Cool damp air touches the face, smelling of limestone, fungal wax, wet shells, and the mineral sharpness before rain. There is no room to crawl inside. Even a probing finger would displace dozens of creatures, crush reeds, break arches, and enter water that has never been disturbed.

But the finger cannot cross the opening.

At the threshold, pressure meets pressure. The chamber is already entirely occupied—not merely crowded, but complete. Air, sound, light, water, bodies, and stone fit together without vacancy. There is nowhere for an intruding thing to be.

So the slab may be lowered again.

Below it, the bright hidden country continues exactly as it has always continued: clamorous, damp, cool, and unfinished, containing every instant of its existence at once.
