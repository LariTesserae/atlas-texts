The valley is entirely manufactured, though from any distance it resembles a mountain range overgrown by forest.

Its slopes are immense stepped shells of brick, ceramic, riveted iron, and pale poured stone, rising almost vertically from a floor lost in foliage. Buttresses stand where ridges would stand. Drainage towers occupy the summits. Long rows of workshops, dwellings, cisterns, conservatories, and abandoned production halls form the apparent geology, one generation of construction laid over another until streets have become ravines and roofs have become terraces. There is no underlying earth. Wherever masonry splits, it reveals more masonry beneath: arches packed with rubble brick, old tiled corridors, corroded beams, conduits wrapped in fiber, foundations resting on earlier foundations whose original purpose has been forgotten.

The highest inhabited galleries are so far above the lower wards that their weather differs. Bright cloud gathers against their outer faces. Rain may fall on the upper orchards while the valley floor remains warm and merely damp, receiving the storm hours later as trickles from ten thousand spouts.

Water is never plentiful in one place. It is distributed.

Condensation pearls on the cold pipes running through warm rooms. Rain collects in blue-glazed gutters and descends through chains, gargoyles, settling drums, and narrow channels cut into stairways. Cistern courts appear unexpectedly among the crowded structures: rectangular pools black with depth, their surfaces covered in duckweed and brilliant insects. Elsewhere, whole neighborhoods depend on moisture wrung from the morning clouds by ranks of wire screens. Every screen sheds into a shared trough; every trough has locks and inspection plates; every leak supports a small jungle.

The main river is an engineered one, slow and elevated, passing halfway up the eastern wall in an open aqueduct broad enough for barges. It does not follow the valley. It crosses it on piers.

Below, many lesser streams exist only intermittently. They run after rain through old tram cuts and worn service alleys, then vanish into gratings. Their channels are permanently marked by moss, polished brick, and pale mineral fans. Children can tell how long ago it rained by touching these stains.

Warmth rises from the works. Deep inside the constructed mountains, engines continue to turn: pumping stations, ventilation mills, lifting drums, dynamos, furnaces kept at low fire. The technology is massive rather than clever. Shafts, belts, pistons, chain elevators, copper busbars, flywheels taller than houses. Machines are repaired indefinitely, their parts recast in local foundries and fitted by hand. Nothing operates quickly. A freight platform may take half a day to climb from the basin to the cloud galleries, its counterweight descending through a separate district with a solemn rattle. Gates open by degrees. Reservoir levels change over months. Some turbines have been turning at nearly the same speed for centuries.

The continual industrial sound gives the valley its weather-like voice. It is loud, but seldom sharp: a layered pressure of engines, fans, water wheels, animal calls, hammering yards, distant whistles, and the hollow clank of chains moving through walls. Near the lower pumping halls, speech becomes a close-range art of gestures and lip reading. Higher up, the noise spreads and softens until individual machines can no longer be distinguished. At night, when smaller workshops shut, the great mechanisms remain. Their pulses pass through floors and bed frames. Residents know the health of the city by vibration.

Vegetation has occupied everything not constantly scraped, heated, or walked upon.

Trees grow from deep masonry planters built into roofs and bridge piers. Their roots travel through utility chases and split grout without ever reaching soil in a natural sense. The soil itself is manufactured: generations of compost, furnace ash, shredded fiber, bird droppings, crushed plaster, kitchen refuse, and sediment dredged from tanks. It accumulates in corners and behind parapets, sometimes to the depth of several stories. Entire groves stand on forgotten roofs now buried beneath dark humus.

The lower valley is lushest. Warm air, leakage, and shade support curtains of fern, elephant-ear, cane, pepper vine, and moss. Orchard terraces climb the sunny western shells: citrus, figs, mulberries, and dense little trees trained flat against radiant walls. On overhead pipes grow epiphytes whose roots drink condensation. High bridges trail creepers down into open space, some nearly reaching the next bridge below. Gardens overlap workshops; chicken runs occupy old ventilation balconies; nurseries fill disused loading sheds with green light.

Nothing here is truly wild in origin, but much has become wild in behavior.

The fauna arrived as livestock, cargo, pest control, companionship, or accident. Swifts nest by the tens of thousands in the upper brickwork, circling the towers in dark revolving bands. Bright lizards bask on hot ceramic roofs. Frogs breed in cooling pans. Goats move along maintenance ledges with small bells tied close to their throats so the sound does not carry into machinery. Monkeys—descendants of animals once trained to retrieve dropped cables—live in the vine districts and steal polished fittings. White moths gather around arc lamps; bats gather around the moths; small copper-colored snakes gather where the bats roost.

In the cistern wards, fish are part of the water system. Some eat larvae, some feed people, and some are used as indicators: when the striped carp rise repeatedly to the surface, a ventilation crew is sent below. Beetles pollinate the enclosed orchards. Pigeons carry tags between districts when electrical lines fail. Every animal seems to have acquired both an ecological role and a municipal one, though the distinction has blurred.

The landscape is extremely high but not expansive without interruption. Views open suddenly and are then closed again. A stair emerges from a fern-dark tunnel onto a viaduct where the whole valley can be seen: stacked green façades, silver roofs, smoking towers, aqueducts crossing empty air, and distant lifts creeping up vertical rails. Halfway across, the path enters another building and the world contracts to wet tile, humming pipes, and shoulder-wide passages.

There are no untouched horizons. Even the sky is framed by cranes, bridges, chimney crowns, and suspended gardens. Yet the place does not feel enclosed. Its depth supplies the openness that a plain would elsewhere. One looks outward and sees distance; one looks downward and sees more of it.

Erosion has reached a mature equilibrium. Rain rounds the stair treads. Cable grooves have been worn into stone lips. Iron oxidizes, expands, breaks surrounding brick, and is replaced by plates bolted over the damage. Mineral deposits thicken around outlets into cream-colored terraces resembling limestone. Roots pry apart walls; masons return and build arches around the roots. The city does not defeat weathering. It incorporates weathering as a construction phase.

Some abandoned surfaces have become so softened by moss and deposits that they look almost geological. Several of the oldest towers are encased in carbonate deposited by leaking water, their windows reduced to irregular caves. Elsewhere, thousands of feet have hollowed a shallow pass through what was once a station concourse. New routes tend to follow these old wear patterns. The city’s circulation has become a kind of watershed.

Its rules are practical and old.

No water may fall more than three levels without being caught.

No root thicker than a wrist may be cut without a structural survey.

A bell sounding continuously means fire; a bell sounding at intervals means a lift is moving through an occupied shaft.

Upper districts must release water before dawn, when the lower pipes are coolest.

No one sleeps beneath an untested counterweight.

Animals wearing blue tags belong to the water authority and must not be eaten.

When a wall begins to sweat, it is marked in chalk and watched for seven days before anyone opens it.

That last rule is treated with particular seriousness. Behind a sweating wall there may be a minor leak, a hot conduit, a forgotten cistern, or an old sealed garden generating its own humid atmosphere. The city contains many interior climates that have persisted unnoticed for decades. Workers breaking through sometimes find rooms densely filled with pale roots, warm rain, insects, and blind fish living in floor channels. Such discoveries are unusual, but not astonishing. They are surveyed, assigned a number, and usually connected to the nearest drainage district.

The light is strong. Sun reflects from pale ceramic, water surfaces, greenhouse glass, and tin roofs until the upper valley seems almost bleached. Leaves become intensely green against it. In the lower levels the same brightness arrives indirectly, scattered down shafts by mirrors and white tiles. Old factories have had their floors pierced so daylight can descend through six or seven stories. Dust, mist, and steam reveal the beams.

At noon the whole structure shines. At evening, light lingers on the highest towers while the basin turns blue-green and lamps begin to burn in galleries one tier at a time. Arc lights flare in foundry courts. Domestic windows glow behind vines. Signal lamps travel slowly with the freight elevators, rising like patient stars along the cliffs.

From the lowest cistern bridge, the place appears almost motionless despite its noise. Water darkens a wall grain by grain. A lift crosses a vast face at walking speed. Birds wheel, settle, and wheel again. Smoke leans gradually with the evening air. Plants thicken around old iron. Deep inside the made mountains, engines maintain pressures and levels established by engineers whose names survive only on corroded plaques.

Everything has been built, but not everything is controlled.

The city has become its own terrain, its own watershed, its own soil and climate. It no longer sits in a landscape. It is the landscape.
