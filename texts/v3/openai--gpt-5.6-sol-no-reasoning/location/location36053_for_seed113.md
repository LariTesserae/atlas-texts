The town lies beneath the ground, though not in a cave.

Its boundary is a nearly perfect oval, three hundred paces long and perhaps half as wide, pressed into warm black stone like a thumbprint in wax. Above it hangs a ceiling without pillars. The ceiling is thin enough, in places, to transmit the pressure of weather: storms pass overhead as bruised fluctuations in the rock, and daylight filters through mineral seams in a faint gray-green wash. No tunnel connects the town to the surface. There are doors in its walls, but they open only onto more wall.

The place seems newly made.

Its streets are sharp-edged channels of pale ceramic, unworn and almost too clean, descending by shallow steps toward a central basin. Towers stand along them at generous intervals—slender, windowless structures with skins like wet bone. Between one tower and the next is more emptiness than town planners would ordinarily permit: vacant plazas, roofless courts, blank terraces, and large squares containing nothing but drainage slots. The city is dense in intention rather than matter. Conduits run everywhere beneath the pavement, and invisible systems occupy the open air, but very little can be seen.

Warm water is always arriving.

It condenses on the underside of the stone ceiling in trembling fields of droplets. It threads down hairline fractures, gathers on projecting crystal blades, then falls in heavy, widely spaced impacts. Every drop rings when it strikes the streets. The ceramic channels amplify the sound, sending it around corners and under bridges until a modest dripping becomes the clamor of machinery. After storms above, the whole town resounds: gutters roar, cistern mouths bellow, and water sheets down the walls in translucent veils.

The rock has not had time to soften. Ledges remain keen enough to cut cloth. Fresh faults expose surfaces glittering with metallic grains, unstained by age. Some cracks are still widening. They advance with intermittent reports like distant cannon, leaving angular slivers on the pavement and releasing breaths of hotter air from deeper layers.

Yet green things have already taken hold.

They grow chiefly at the margins, where the city’s geometry meets the raw excavation. Moss lies thick over the weeping walls, bright and soft in the dimness. Fernlike plants unfurl from drainage seams, but their fronds are broad, translucent, and almost colorless except for threads of emerald running through them. Low mats of bladdered leaves surround the cisterns. When water levels rise, the bladders inflate and lift the plants into floating gardens; when the basins drain, they collapse with long sighs against the stone.

There are no vines crossing the open streets and no roots breaking the foundations. The vegetation appears to respect boundaries it cannot see. It crowds around water, follows cracks, and colonizes bare rock, but stops precisely at certain invisible planes. Along some walls, moss ends in a perfectly straight vertical line. Ferns lean away from empty doorways. Fallen spores drift into the central square and abruptly turn aside, carried by currents that do not disturb mist or flame.

Nothing animal lives here.

No insects move among the leaves. There are no worms in the warm sediment, no fish in the cisterns, no bats beneath the ledges. Flowers—if the pale radial structures among the moss are flowers—open and close without pollinators. Rot proceeds without visible agents. A fallen frond becomes transparent over several days, then separates into water and a lace of mineral residue. The absence of animals makes the town feel less abandoned than withheld, as if one entire category of life has not yet been admitted.

The buildings continue to operate.

Doors dilate when water approaches, admitting floods into chambers that should not have space behind their walls. Stairways fold themselves flat beneath rising pools. Bridges lift by fractions of an inch to shed accumulated moisture. At irregular intervals, the bone-white towers emit pulses too low to hear directly. The water reveals them: every puddle dimples at once, every hanging drop becomes briefly polygonal, and the floating gardens arrange themselves into concentric arcs.

Then the town changes.

Not dramatically. A curb slides sideways. A plaza acquires a narrow new seam. One tower is found a little farther from its neighbor than before. The movements leave no rubble and no abrasion. The settlement is apparently enlarging its interior while its outer boundary remains fixed. Survey marks placed across the streets disagree from hour to hour, though each measurement is internally exact.

The deepest basin occupies the center. It is circular, black, and usually full to its rim. Rain arriving from the ceiling eventually finds its way here, yet the basin never overflows. Its surface gives back no reflection. Droplets vanish into it without ripples, but their impacts are heard much later from the walls: hard, bell-like knocks traveling outward through stone.

Around the basin stand nine empty shelters, each large enough for one person. Their proportions are unmistakably deliberate—seats, handholds, shallow alcoves at head height—though whoever built them did not account for any familiar body. A seated human would find the floor too low, the ceiling too close, and the handholds positioned behind the shoulders. Thin writing covers the interior surfaces. It is not engraved or illuminated. It becomes visible only under condensation, appearing as dry strokes while the surrounding wall darkens with water.

The strokes revise themselves.

Most changes happen during the loudest runoff, when close observation is difficult. A cluster lengthens; a line closes into a loop; whole passages migrate from one shelter to another. The writing may be instructions to the city, or records generated by it, or simply another drainage system operating on a scale too fine to see. If touched, the marks feel warmer than the wall. If copied, the copy is accurate until the next pulse from the towers, after which the original has changed and the copy has not.

Dimness here is not merely the absence of light. The town seems engineered to preserve it. Luminous fungi fail to brighten beyond a weak jade haze. Lamps cast short, dense halos and illuminate no distant surfaces. In certain courts, light bends downward and pools along the paving like vapor. It can be stirred by passing feet, though there are no feet but those of a visitor.

At what might be evening, the ceiling cools. Condensation thickens, leaves contract, and the streets begin their nightly rearrangement. Channels turn on hidden pivots to face the central basin. Reservoirs empty in sequence from the outer wall inward. The noise becomes immense within the small enclosure: cataracts, resonant pipes, snapping stone, the bass vibration of the towers. Through it all, the vegetation moves in measured waves, folding toward the water and opening again behind it.

By morning—or by the local condition most resembling morning—the town is fractionally deeper.

A new step appears at the base of every stair. Fresh black rock shows above the rooflines. The oval boundary has descended without excavating anything, leaving the overhead surface no nearer and the chambers below no more crowded. The settlement is not sinking through the earth. It is making “below” beneath itself.

This may be its only true industry.

Water arrives from a world above that cannot enter. The towers measure it, the plants slow it, the streets divide it, and the central basin removes it from ordinary space. In return, the town manufactures depth: a quiet, cumulative downwardness, added one thin layer at a time.

No residents maintain the process. No fauna test its edges. The shelters remain vacant, the plazas clear. Everything waits in warm mist beneath the ringing ceiling, active without haste, lush at the margins and hollow at the heart.

And whenever the rain is strongest, something knocks from farther down.
