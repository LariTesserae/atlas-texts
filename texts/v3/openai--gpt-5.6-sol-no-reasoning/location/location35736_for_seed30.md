The place occupies a shallow saddle near the summit of a warm basalt upland. It is not large. From any point within it, the enclosing rim can be seen through the steam: a dark, irregular wall perhaps half a kilometer away, low enough that clouds continually spill over it. Nothing beyond the rim is visible for long.

The saddle has only recently begun to exist.

Its floor is a sheet of black mineral material, smooth but not polished, still carrying the swollen contours of something poured and arrested before it could settle. There are no gullies yet, no softened corners, no accumulated soil. Rain lies where it falls in silver films. Water has not had time to teach the ground its usual vocabulary of channels and banks. Instead, it crawls over the surface as a single shallow skin, dividing around protrusions and joining again without leaving a mark.

In places the rock weeps upward.

Clear beads gather from within the basalt, swell against gravity, and lift a finger’s breadth into the air before bursting into cool vapor. Elsewhere, warm water wells through seams so fine they cannot be seen. It spreads beneath the surface film in wavering lenses, making the reflected sky shudder. The air is saturated, though rain is intermittent. Moisture condenses continually on stone, skin, shell, and metal. Every object carries droplets along its underside.

The warmth is deep rather than bright. The sky remains dim, roofed in charcoal cloud, but the ground radiates the heat of recent assembly. Bare feet would find it almost comfortable at first, then uncomfortably alive: alternating bands of warmth and sudden cold, as if buried circulatory systems passed directly below. Steam lies close to the floor. It does not rise in columns. It pours sideways, following invisible gradients, sometimes flowing uphill.

Vegetation has gained only a tentative hold. Pale jointed reeds stand far apart, never in clusters of more than seven or eight. They have no leaves. Their translucent stalks fill with condensed water until they bow, discharge it through pores in their tips, and slowly straighten again. On shaded faces of stone grows a thin maroon felt, neither moss nor fungus, which retracts when touched and leaves the rock perfectly dry beneath it. There are also a few low fans of black membrane stretched between mineral knobs. During rain they open to catch droplets; afterward they fold into narrow blades and become almost invisible.

The emptiness between these plants is considerable. Each living patch seems isolated, yet roots—or whatever passes for roots here—may be communicating under the rock. When one reed is disturbed, distant reeds bend a moment later, not away from the disturbance but toward the same point in the sky.

Animal life is everywhere, though rarely still enough to count.

Small six-legged bodies skim the water film on broad translucent feet, making a dry ticking noise. Thumb-sized amphibious creatures live inside bubbles they carry on their backs. Their skin is a dull mirror, reflecting not the present surroundings but the saddle as it appeared several minutes before. When dozens cross the floor, fragments of earlier weather move with them: a patch of sunlight already gone, rain that has not fallen for half an hour, the silhouette of a bird no longer overhead.

The birds are long-winged and nearly featherless. They circle low in the cloud and call continuously, producing harsh metallic chords that overlap until the whole basin seems to ring. They do not flap. At intervals one folds itself into a narrow dart and plunges into the shallow surface water. Although that water is scarcely deep enough to cover a foot, the bird vanishes completely. It may reappear from another pool many meters away, usually carrying a ribbonlike animal in its beak.

Those ribbons are the saddle’s most visible prey and perhaps its principal engineers. They travel beneath the water skin in flashing schools, each no thicker than a thread. Collectively they can lift a sheet of water several centimeters above the ground, transporting it as a trembling, mobile blister. Predators strike these blisters from above. The ribbons scatter; the captured water collapses with a slap audible across the entire enclosure.

Thus the place is loud despite its sparse appearance. Birds clang and keen in the steam. Water blisters burst. Reeds discharge with hollow knocks. Beneath all this is a continuous subsonic vibration felt in the teeth and breastbone. Occasionally the basalt answers that vibration with a sharp crack, though no fracture remains afterward.

Scattered around the saddle are nine structures.

They resemble small shelters from a distance: low shells, each large enough for two or three people, placed with no obvious relation to one another. Up close they are difficult to understand. Their walls consist of upright planes so thin that droplets pass through them while wind does not. Some planes are opaque from one side and open onto impossible depth from the other. One contains a view of the saddle under a clear night sky, although the actual sky has not cleared in living memory. Another shows only dense amber fog. A third appears filled with black water, held vertically without a vessel.

There are no doors. Entry depends on temperature. When a person’s skin becomes exactly as warm as the surrounding stone, one wall loses its resistance and can be crossed. Inside, the structures are larger than their exteriors but not vastly so: the expansion is intimate and unsettling, like a room making space for one more breath.

No builders remain. There are marks, however. Along several walls run shallow lines like measurements made by a tool with three points. Narrow stakes stand beside the warmest springs, topped by rings that rotate without bearings or visible force. They turn toward events rather than directions. A ring may point at a bird’s dive, a burst reed, the formation of a cloud, or a footfall made yesterday. Their movements produce a rising whine, one of the landscape’s loudest sounds.

The structures are not ruins. They are unfinished in a more fundamental sense. Their material is still deciding which physical laws to obey.

At dusk—which here is only a further lowering of gray—the walls shed particles too small to see. These move through water and stone, arranging molecules, closing some pores and opening others. The saddle is being fabricated continuously from within. Its basalt is not volcanic, despite its appearance. Layer by layer, an unseen system precipitates rock beneath the existing floor, raising the whole enclosure by fractions of a millimeter each day. The reeds, pools, and animals rise with it. Nothing is buried.

Erosion has therefore barely begun and may never gain the advantage. Rain attempts to score channels, but the marks seal overnight. Loose grains are gathered into the surface as if recalled. A chipped edge remains sharp for an hour, then grows outward to its previous contour. Footprints fade not through weathering but through deliberate correction.

Yet the corrections are imperfect.

One corner of the saddle has started to sag. Water repeatedly gathers there, and the fabricating system has not compensated. A marsh no larger than a house has formed: ankle-deep, warm, full of red bubbles and soft gray sediment—the only true sediment in the place. Vegetation crowds slightly closer around it. The mirror-backed amphibians arrive in hundreds to spawn. Ribbon schools knot together below the surface. Birds gather along the rim. Larger animals, otherwise hidden, come out of the steam.

These resemble lean quadrupeds assembled from wet ceramic plates. Water circulates openly through channels in their flanks. They drink without lowering their heads; the marsh climbs their legs in threads and enters them through valves at the knee. They prey on the bubble-bearing amphibians, cracking the bubbles first with a precise tap so the creatures cannot retreat inside them. Their calls are enormous: low, resonant blasts that make every shallow pool stipple at once.

When several appear, the saddle becomes a compressed theater of pursuit. Small creatures scatter across the shining floor. Birds drop through pools and emerge elsewhere. Reeds bend toward invisible signals. Rings on the stakes spin until their whine becomes painful. Rain arrives abruptly, warm and heavy, drumming on rock and membrane. Steam thickens. The structures flicker between opacity and depth.

Then, as quickly, the activity disperses. The ceramic hunters return to cracks too narrow to contain them. The birds ascend into cloud. Water drains from the marsh—not downslope, but vertically, slipping through unbroken stone. For a few minutes the basin is almost still.

In that stillness, one can notice the newest feature: a line of pale material pushing up through the black floor near the center. It is only a few centimeters long. It could be the first mineral vein, the foundation of another structure, or a root belonging to a plant not yet present. Each night it extends by the width of a fingernail. The fabrication rings all turn toward it, even when nothing visible is happening.

The saddle’s governing rule seems to be that nothing may wear away until it has finished becoming. But no part of the place knows what “finished” means. Water rehearses erosion without achieving it. Animals enact ancient food webs across a surface born yesterday. Buildings remember skies that may belong to the future. The ground rises, the marsh deepens, and every corrective act produces some small new error.

Above, cloud presses against the rim. Below, machinery without components continues its inaudible work.

The whole warm, dim enclosure feels less like a completed landscape than a landscape under negotiation—small enough to cross in minutes, old enough in its inhabitants to contain terror and habit, and geologically younger than the rain currently falling upon it.
