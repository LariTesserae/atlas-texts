The city occupies the upper wall of a continent.

From the lowlands—if the ocher plains fourteen kilometers below can be called lowlands—it resembles a dark mineral seam crossing the face of an escarpment. Only at closer range does the seam resolve into districts: terraces cut into stone, furnaces sunk in old caverns, rail lines pinned to vertical faces, and habitation blocks suspended beneath overhangs where they remain sheltered from falling grit. The inhabited band runs for thousands of kilometers. It is narrow compared with the plateau, yet long enough to have climates, dialects, and industrial ages of its own.

There is no sea and no rain. The planet has no open water at all. Its sky carries a dry, heavy atmosphere of nitrogen, sulfur compounds, and inert gases, warm enough that exposed metal is always pleasant to the touch by day. In the deepest chasms the pressure rises until the horizon trembles. There, pale vapors pool like invisible lakes, detectable only by refraction and by the behavior of animals moving through them. Water exists chiefly as an impurity locked in old ceramics, imported biological tissue, and sealed municipal stores. It is too valuable to participate in weather.

Weather happens without it.

At intervals, temperature differences between plateau and abyss set the atmosphere into motion. Slow tides of dense gas climb the cliffs over several days, carrying flour-fine mineral dust. The dust does not form clouds so much as vast translucent sheets. Sunlight remains ordinary in color, but distance becomes coppery; towers ten kilometers away appear engraved on brass. Static crawls along cables and parapets. Machinery acquires faint halos. Then the tide passes, and the air becomes clear enough to see the opposing wall of a canyon eighty kilometers distant.

The land is old and thoroughly worked by forces that no longer seem violent. The escarpment has been pared into colossal steps: basalt benches, salt scarps, columns of black glass softened at their edges by ten million years of abrasive air. Some cliffs are fluted from summit to base. Others have broad hollows where softer strata were patiently removed, leaving roofs large enough to shelter entire boroughs. Scree descends from them in frozen-looking fans, though stones continue to move—one click, one settling slide, perhaps only once in a season.

Nothing grows. There is no green, no soil, no pollen, and no smell of leaf decay. Unbuilt ground is stone upon stone: rust-red iron oxides, violet shale, ivory evaporites laid down by chemistry rather than vanished oceans, and a porous gray mineral that exhales trapped gases when warmed. Under noon light the plateau is nearly colorless. Toward evening its strata separate into bands of amber, plum, charcoal, and dull silver.

The city follows these bands.

Older quarters were excavated directly into stable rock. Their facades are arrays of dark openings, regular at first glance but distorted by centuries of repairs. Newer districts stand on skeletal pylons planted in the cliff, their streets stacked vertically and joined by freight lifts. There are plazas with no ground, only steel grids through which one sees the abyss. Rail yards cling to slopes steeper than roofs. Counterweighted trains descend empty and return burdened with compressed ores, salts, and living cargo from the pressure basins.

Industry is everywhere, but rarely loud. The machines are large and slow because speed is wasteful and vibration dangerous. Crushers turn once every few minutes. Kilns breathe in day-long cycles. Conveyor belts cross valleys on cables so long that their supports vanish in haze. Pumps circulate molten salts and liquefied gases through insulated arteries beneath the streets. Electrical substations stand in open courts, surrounded by glassy fins that discharge excess current into the mineral air with a dim violet ticking.

The factories do not roar. They murmur through the stone.

Most of the time the dominant sound is ventilation: a low inward breath at one tunnel mouth, an outward breath at another. Far below, a freight car may strike a rail joint, and the note arrives seconds later, thin and unexpectedly pure. Warning bells are discouraged because echoes make their direction unreliable. Signals are visual—shutters, colored lamps, rotating mirrored vanes—or tactile, sent as deliberate pulses through handrails and floors.

The abundance of animals is not obvious until one learns what counts as an animal here.

They are dry-bodied, many-jointed things whose chemistry runs on hot salts, hydrocarbons, and electrochemical gradients rather than liquid water. Most carry their active fluids in sealed bladders under layered mineral shells. Their flesh, when visible, resembles resin, wax, or translucent rubber. They do not graze. There is nothing to graze. They feed on the planet’s differences: heat against shade, charged dust against grounded rock, reactive gas issuing from a fissure, metal recently exposed by fracture.

Copper mites gather in orange drifts around electrical insulators, each no larger than a fingernail. They spread vanes to absorb static, then fold themselves and crawl into cracks to discharge. Whole colonies migrate along transmission lines, compelling maintenance crews to fit the pylons with sacrificial branches.

Cliff kites are broad, nearly weightless organisms with rigid ceramic ribs. They spend years aloft in the updrafts, steering without wings by opening and closing pores along their edges. From below they look like chips of darkness sliding across the sun. They descend only to lay chains of faceted eggs under warm bridges.

In the pressure depths live larger forms. Six-legged bellows beasts walk the canyon floors, inhaling dense gas into muscular chambers and straining out sulfur-bearing compounds. Their calls are below hearing, but windows flex when a herd passes. Narrow-bodied predators follow them at a distance, reading those pressure waves through fan-shaped feet.

The city itself supports more fauna than the wilderness. Warm pipes, waste voltage, chemical leaks, and regular shadows create an artificial ecology richer than the bare plateau. Ribbon animals nest inside elevator shafts. Pale hinge-backed scavengers patrol slag heaps. At night, glass gnats orbit lamps—not for light, but for the electric fields around them. Citizens tolerate some species, cultivate others, and classify the rest as industrial contamination.

There are no gardens. Public spaces have arrangements of stone selected for thermal behavior: black slabs that remain warm after sunset, porous columns that sing faintly when gas tides pass, mirrored gravel that discourages mites. In wealthier terraces, sculptors maintain fields of balanced shards. Wind turns the upper pieces by fractions of a degree each year, so the composition changes across generations.

Because the atmosphere is dry and the processes slow, abandoned things persist. A closed factory may retain its painted safety lines for centuries. Derelict cranes stand with their hooks hanging over depths whose bottoms cannot be seen. Tracks continue beyond inhabited districts into empty mining towns, where doors remain open and dust has accumulated in sharp-edged dunes. Yet erosion is never absent. It rounds bolts, hollows glass, polishes exposed bones, and thins inscriptions from the outside inward. The world does not bury its history; it abrades it until only the deepest cuts remain.

Time is measured partly by this wearing-away. Contracts may last “until the seventh railing is smooth.” Boundaries are defined by the retreat of a salt face. Certain elevators are inspected not annually but after a known cliff shadow has migrated the width of one tower. The people trust gradual change more than clocks.

Their chief rule is that nothing may fall without permission.

On a vertical continent, a dropped object can travel through several jurisdictions, damage cables, start slides, or descend into pressure zones where its materials become chemically unstable. Every exterior tool is tethered. Slag is lowered, never tipped. Buildings have inward-sloping sills. Children learn to hook themselves to street lines before they learn the district maps. Even funerary remains are dismantled and returned through controlled industrial cycles; casting anything into the abyss is considered both dangerous and unbearably extravagant.

A second rule is older: no sealed water is opened under the sky. Whether this began as hygiene, conservation, or superstition is disputed. The rare ceremonies that use water take place in ceramic chambers behind multiple doors. A few drops are placed on heated stone, and those present watch vapor rise. To them it is one of the strangest sights imaginable: matter vanishing upward, briefly making the invisible air visible.

Beyond the last inhabited terrace, the high plateau extends farther than maps agree. It is a level expanse only in the planetary sense. Up close it buckles into low ridges, impact basins, and labyrinths of eroded crystal. Survey towers stand hundreds of kilometers apart. Between them there may be nothing constructed except a buried power line and occasional black marker stones.

Yet the plateau is not empty. During the warm season, herds of thin-legged charge feeders cross it, spaced so widely that one animal may be visible from the next only as a glint. Cliff kites cast moving shadows over the flats. Under the crust, slow colonies of reactive membranes spread along mineral boundaries at the rate of a handspan per century. Whether these count as animals, machines, or merely self-continuing geology remains unresolved.

At sunset, the escarpment releases the heat it has stored all day. The city’s windows brighten in scattered bands, never dense enough to overwhelm the rock. Furnace mouths glow deeper red. Freight lamps begin their patient descent. Far out over the abyss, a gas tide bends the lower lights into wavering columns.

Then the place becomes quiet enough that one can hear grit moving.

Not a storm, not even a steady wind: individual grains touching steel, crossing stone, slipping over an edge. Each begins a fall that may last minutes. Some are caught on lower terraces. Some strike cables and leap away. Some continue into the warm, pressurized dark, toward chemical depths where the city’s lights cannot reach and where enormous creatures move so slowly that, from above, they are mistaken for terrain.
