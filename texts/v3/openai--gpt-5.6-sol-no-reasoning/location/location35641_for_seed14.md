Far below the reach of weather, an ancient sea lies over a plain worn almost level.

The water is black, not from pollution or strangeness, but from depth. What little daylight enters the ocean has already been scattered and consumed far above. Here, illumination comes rarely: a weak blue shimmer from descending organisms, a remote flicker of plankton disturbed in the upper layers, or the cold, narrow beam of some hunting animal. Most of the country exists without being seen.

The seabed extends for many leagues as a pavement of clay, silt, and exposed stone. It is not empty. Almost every part of it is occupied by matter brought down from elsewhere: banks of pale shell fragments, mats of mineral grit, compacted drifts of dead plankton, the softened remains of wood, scales, teeth, bones, and volcanic dust. These have accumulated grain by grain through an age so long that individual events have vanished into strata. The plain has been buried, compressed, uncovered, and buried again. Its ridges are low and blunt. Its stones have lost their corners. Channels that may once have been ravines now differ from the surrounding bottom only by a slight darkening of sediment.

This is an old landscape, but not a still one.

Across the level floor, enormous bottom currents move without visible surface. They travel as dense rivers within the sea—cold water laden with suspended sediment, flowing beneath clearer water because of its weight. When one passes, the quiet plain abruptly disappears into itself. Silt rises in walls. Shell beds roll forward. Pebbles tick and knock together. Soft deposits liquefy and surge across the bottom, pouring into every hollow. A bank assembled over centuries may be stripped away in an hour, exposing older clay beneath: blue-gray, tightly pressed, scored by the traces of creatures dead for millions of years.

The turbulence is broad rather than loud. There is no crashing surf here. Motion announces itself through pressure, through the faint grinding of stone, and through a vibration too low to be heard as a distinct note. The water leans hard in one direction, then slackens, then turns. A cloud of sediment expands over miles. Within it, every grain is moving, yet the world seems silent.

Nothing rises very high. Occasional outcrops stand above the plain like the backs of buried animals, but even these have been planed down by abrasive currents. Their surfaces are densely engraved with shallow grooves. Some were cut by moving gravel, others by older flows whose direction no longer matches the present sea. Where stone is soft, it has been scalloped into overlapping basins. Where it is hard, it wears a thin dark skin of manganese and iron, deposited so slowly that a thickness no greater than a fingernail represents ages.

Sparse life clings to these hard places. Low films of bacteria spread across the rock, colorless in the dark. Small sponges grow as gray crusts no thicker than old lichen. Pale anemones anchor in cracks and hold out translucent crowns, bending together when the current strengthens. There is no true vegetation. No leaves turn toward a sun. The nearest equivalent is the occasional colony of branching organisms that catches falling nourishment from the water. They resemble brittle shrubs, but they grow at the pace of stone and may stand lifeless-looking for generations.

Animals are few enough that their arrival changes the character of the place. A white amphipod crosses the sediment in quick starts, leaving a dotted trail that the next current erases. A long-legged crab picks among shell fragments. Worms remain buried, known only by paired holes and small coils of processed mud. Sometimes a fish passes just above the bottom, dark-backed and soft-bodied, conserving every movement. It does not patrol so much as drift with intent. Its eyes are small or absent; its mouth is broad because food is too rare to refuse.

Larger visitors are rarer still. A shark may descend along the edge of a current, sensed first as a shift in pressure. It crosses the plain without haste and vanishes beyond the sediment cloud. Afterward, the smaller creatures slowly emerge again.

The density of this country is not the density of living abundance. It is geological occupancy. Water fills every cavity. Fine matter occupies the water. Older matter lies compressed beneath the newer. There is almost no bare surface and no clean boundary between sea and ground. The lowest water contains so much suspended clay that it behaves like a second atmosphere, and the uppermost sediment holds so much water that it behaves like a heavy fluid. Animals swim through mud and burrow through sea; the distinction is one of degree.

In places, the seabed fails.

A slope too slight to notice begins to move. First the topmost silt wrinkles. Then an entire layer slides free, carrying stones, shells, bacterial films, and burrowing animals with it. The collapse gathers mass as it travels. It becomes a submarine avalanche, low and wide, sweeping across the plain and packing every object into its front. Behind it remains a smooth scar of older earth. Ahead of it, the dark is saturated with debris.

Such flows are the principal weather here. They build no monuments and leave no permanent architecture. Their deposits spread into fans extending beyond sight, each laid across the last. The open plain is therefore packed with buried journeys: storm sediment washed from distant continents, ash from volcanoes long extinguished, sand carried from vanished shorelines, microscopic skeletons fallen from seas that had different temperatures and currents. The landscape appears nearly featureless because it has absorbed so much history.

No constructed thing belongs here. There are no walls, anchors, cables, or wrecks. If intelligent hands ever reach this depth, they do so only briefly and poorly equipped. On some far margin, where the plain shallows toward a drowned shelf, coastal people may lower a stone weight on a braided fiber line. They can measure depth by knots and retrieve mud caught in a hollowed piece of bone or wood. They may know from the smell of the sample whether the bottom is clay or rot, and from scratches on the weight whether it struck gravel. But they cannot enter this country. Their simplest tools touch it as fingertips touch the floor of a dark room, then rise again bearing a pinch of evidence.

The laws here are ordinary and absolute. Cold slows decay but does not stop it. Pressure enters everything. Calcium dissolves below certain depths, so thin shells vanish while teeth and resistant fragments endure. Soft bodies are eaten, dispersed, or pressed into stains. Every unsupported hollow fills. Every sharp edge dulls. Anything lighter than the surrounding mud eventually rises from it; anything heavier sinks. Nothing remains exposed forever.

The sea above is not calm either. Tides flex the whole column. Distant storms send pressure through it. Seasonal changes far overhead alter the descent of food. Earthquakes travel through the bottom as abrupt shudders, lifting curtains of silt across the plain. Yet at any one point, the immediate sensations are restrained: cold water, darkness, a persistent horizontal pull, and the nearly inaudible hiss of grains passing grains.

After a great current has gone through, quiet returns quickly. The suspended cloud settles by size. Pebbles stop first. Sand follows. Fine clay remains aloft for days, turning the water opaque though there was no light to lose. A new skin forms over everything, smooth enough to preserve the touch of a fin or the track of a crawling animal.

Then the plain waits—not motionless, but between rearrangements.

In the darkness, particles continue to fall. The crusts continue to grow. Buried water is squeezed upward through the sediment. A worm advances a body-length. Somewhere beyond perception, another slope begins to loosen. The oldest landscape in the sea is continually being remade, and every remaking leaves it flatter, fuller, and more deeply occupied than before.
