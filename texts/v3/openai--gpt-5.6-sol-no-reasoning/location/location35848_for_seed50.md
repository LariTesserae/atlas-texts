The place is hidden inside a wave.

Not beneath one, exactly, nor behind it. The wave itself contains a hollow: a long chamber of air held within the upper face of an ocean storm, carried at tremendous altitude along the flank of a liquid mountain. It may be entered only for a few minutes at a time, when the water arches over itself and the pressures agree. Then a slit opens in the green wall, revealing an interior no larger than a chapel and immeasurably farther from ordinary ground.

The chamber is always under construction.

Its floor is a tilted shelf of new stone, pale gray and faintly warm at the cracks. This stone has not been eroded because it did not exist yesterday. Mineral foam precipitates from the wave’s interior, gathers like curd along invisible lines of pressure, and hardens into ledges, ribs, and little blunt promontories. Some survive for hours. Others dissolve before their surfaces lose their wet shine. There are no pebbles, no rounded grains, no ancient scratches—only raw faces, broken bubbles, crystalline seams, and delicate edges that have never yet met weather.

The shelf hangs high above the drowned world. Through the clear outer membrane of the wave, one can sometimes see the ocean far below: another ocean, darker and comparatively still, spread beneath banks of cloud. Between the lower sea and this traveling upper sea lies a blue-gray gulf of rain. Thus the chamber is submerged and aerial at once, an inclusion of breath inside an ocean climbing through the sky.

Its boundaries are water in several moods. One wall is transparent and several meters thick, crossed by rapid veins of foam. Another is a vertical torrent falling upward. The ceiling boils continually, sagging into cold translucent lobes before recoiling. Drops as large as fists shear loose, hover in the chamber’s strange pressure, and then accelerate sideways hard enough to shatter against the newborn stone.

Light penetrates freely. It is not dim deep-sea light, but the plain silver illumination of an overcast day, distorted by tons of moving water. At intervals the wall thins and becomes lucid. Shadows sharpen. The raw mineral shelf turns almost white; every suspended droplet casts a trembling lens of sunlight. Then the wave folds, and the light swings green, blue, or the brownish color of old glass. There is no reliable direction from which morning comes.

The noise is absolute.

Water strikes water with a continuous concussion that passes through bone more readily than through air. The floor drums. Cavities in the stone open and close like throats, emitting notes too low to hear cleanly and too strong not to feel. Thin mineral blades squeal as the shelf bends. When the chamber’s entrance appears, wind enters at such speed that it becomes a solid shriek. Conversation here would be pantomime. Even shouting is merely a sensation in the teeth.

Yet the hollow is not crowded. Its violence moves through emptiness. Most of the chamber contains nothing but pressured air, errant drops, and light. Objects stand far apart as if each requires a private radius of silence the place cannot otherwise provide.

Near the deepest end are three shelters.

They are low, crude things built under the lee of a stone rib: bent frames of drift-bone lashed with kelp cord, roofed in overlapping plates of translucent shell. No two plates match. One bears the spiral growth marks of an animal no longer found in the lower sea; another looks less grown than cast, its edges showing the thumbprints of whoever softened it. The shelters are too small to stand in. Their entrances face different directions, because the chamber’s gravity changes when the wave pitches.

Between them sit the scattered signs of occupation: a basalt chopper, a cup made from a sealed bladder, wedges of bone, a net woven with enormous patience from pale fibers, and shallow grooves cut into the floor. There is no metal. Fire cannot survive. Heat comes from wrapped stones lifted briefly from the forming cracks, and light, when the outer day fades, comes from a clay bowl of luminous plankton. Everything is tied down. A loose tool may become a projectile, or drift into the ceiling and remain embedded there until the ceiling turns into a waterfall.

The grooves are records. Their makers mark each time the hollow returns.

That return is uncertain. The chamber is not geographically fixed; it recurs as a pattern in moving water. It may form over a different part of the planet each season, carrying its stone shelf, shelters, and sparse inhabitants within it. Distance outside has little relation to distance inside. A hunter can leave through the slit above a tropical sea and come back minutes later with ice on their ropes. At times the entrance opens onto cloud. At times onto black water. Once, according to the longest groove, it opened against the side of a mountain, and the wave tore away enough rock to add a fourth ledge to the room.

Nothing here has had time to become soil. Vegetation survives only in the shelter of seams: black-green threads of algae, small fans of kelp no longer than a hand, and a white rootless plant that inflates when the air pressure falls. These plants do not colonize so much as endure. They are cropped close by the moving water and frequently erased. After an erasure, they return from spores sealed in the stone while it was still liquid.

Animals are rarer. Clear shrimp sometimes fall from the ceiling and paddle frantically through the air until caught by a drop. A long, narrow fish lives in the transparent wall, keeping pace with the chamber for days; its skeleton is visible and seems to contain more joints than a fish requires. It watches the shelters with one large eye. No one has managed to spear it. When the hollow is close to collapse, the fish disappears first.

There are also the bell-creatures. They arrive singly, perhaps once in several months: delicate, winged medusae that move equally well through water and air. They alight on the raw stone and pulse against it. Their touch leaves circular depressions in rock that should already be hard. The inhabitants consider them dangerous, not because they attack, but because the chamber appears to imitate them. Hours after one departs, the walls begin pulsing in the same rhythm.

This is one of the place’s few dependable rules: forms are contagious.

A knot tied in cord may later appear as a knot in the flow of the wall. A hand laid too long against soft mineral leaves not merely a print, but the beginning of another hand, extruded blindly from the stone. Repeated sounds become structures. The shelters’ frames have caused bone-shaped supports to grow beneath the shelf. The grooves recording previous returns are slowly being copied onto the ceiling, though reversed and at a much larger scale.

For this reason the inhabitants make few ornaments and avoid unnecessary repetition. They do not sing. They alter the shape of their tools whenever possible. They sleep in different postures each time. Their primitive craft is partly necessity and partly defense against the chamber learning them too precisely.

Still, the place learns.

Far below the shelf, where clear water shades into milk, incomplete structures are visible: a ladder with no destination, the arc of a doorway, a row of fingerlike columns, the rough suggestion of a crouching body. They form and unform in the turbulence. None is solid enough to reach. Some resemble artifacts that have not yet been made.

At irregular intervals, the entire hollow changes orientation. The stone shelf rises like a closing lid. The ceiling becomes a wall; the wall becomes a mile-long chute of water. Shelters strain against their lashings. Algae flatten into green writing. Every loose drop joins a single rushing sheet, and for several deafening seconds the chamber is almost entirely liquid. Its inhabitants seal themselves inside the bone frames and breathe from skin bladders while the wave rearranges the world.

Afterward there may be a new alcove. A former ledge may be gone. The entrance may open in another century of weather.

The air returns slowly, gathering first in small bright beads under the stone, then merging into a habitable hollow. Silence never returns with it. The roar resumes as if it had only drawn breath.

Beyond the transparent wall, the storm carries the chamber onward—high over the lower ocean, through cold light, bearing its few shelters and rare lives inside a volume of water large enough to erase cities. From outside it is invisible: one pale crest among thousands, breaking and unbreaking in the sky.

Inside, on rock scarcely older than a footprint, someone begins another groove.
