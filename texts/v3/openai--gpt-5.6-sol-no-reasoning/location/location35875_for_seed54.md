The city occupies a high basin where the mountains have not quite finished becoming mountains.

Ridges rise around it in black, blunt folds, their faces smooth as poured slag. There are few cracks, no scree fans, no long histories written in gullies. The stone is too young for that. It has welled upward from below in immense, nearly motionless pressures, cooling into terraces and shelves before rain, frost, or gravity can decide what shape to give it. Along the eastern rim, entire cliffs still have the swollen softness of material forced through a mold. They glisten beneath skins of ice.

Nothing grows.

There is no moss in the joints, no grass on the verges, no pale lichen lettering the walls. Water gathers on mineral surfaces without being drunk. It lies in seams, pearls beneath overhangs, and travels through the city in silver-black films. After precipitation—which is frequent, though seldom recognizable as rain—the streets remain wet for months. The atmosphere condenses directly against every exposed plane. Droplets form on parapets, bridge cables, antenna forests, and the smooth backs of the animals. They enlarge until their weight overcomes adhesion, then fall into the dark.

From above, if there were sufficient light to see by, the city would resemble a dense metallic reef submerged just beneath the surface of night. Towers stand close enough to exchange vibrations through their walls. Viaducts cross and recross at a dozen elevations. Conduits bulge from plazas and enter buildings through apertures like wells. There are neighborhoods inside the bridges, reservoirs inside the towers, and broad public chambers excavated downward into the warmest parts of the newborn rock. Construction continues everywhere, but so slowly that scaffolds are inherited as architecture. A span may take three generations to move the width of a hand into its final alignment.

The machines do not hurry. They are not idle, either.

Along the upper districts, vast frameworks lean into one another without touching. A narrow gap—sometimes no wider than a finger—runs between their black surfaces. Within this gap, matter behaves strangely. Frost rises instead of falling. Sound arrives before the impacts that produce it. Water clings in long horizontal columns, clear and trembling, held by fields whose generators are buried too deeply to map. These suspended channels serve as aqueducts, data lines, and boundaries. The inhabitants speak of them as if they were weather.

Light is rare and generally accidental. The sky offers only a diffuse bruise-colored pallor at uncertain intervals, perhaps reflected from ice fields beyond the basin. Most illumination comes from pressure: blue glimmers under a footfall, a violet line where two structural members bear against each other, a dull green pulse traveling through wet stone when machinery wakes far below. The city’s windows do not shine. They are darker rectangles cut into darkness, designed to collect differences too small for unaided eyes.

Yet the city is not empty. It is overrun.

Animals pack its streets, cling beneath its bridges, fill its cisterns, and roost in vertical layers against the towers. They are so numerous that in many districts the ground itself is never visible. Their bodies make a shifting pavement: slick shells, jointed limbs, pale insulating fur, glassy bladders, mineral plates. Some are smaller than droplets. Others occupy entire courtyards and must be built around.

No vegetation supports them. No obvious food chain rises from soil or sunlight. The smallest graze on gradients—minute differences in temperature, charge, pressure, and probability generated by the city’s buried systems. They cluster around live conduits in dense, quivering crusts. Larger animals consume these grazers, or consume the patterns they produce, or merely remain near them and grow by mechanisms no one fully understands. Predation happens, but it is not the dominant exchange. More often one creature passes through another’s field of influence and emerges diminished, while the other gains weight, memory, or a new limb.

Their abundance is deafening.

The basin resounds continuously with scraping shells, hydraulic breaths, mating knocks, sonar bursts, and the sustained choral tones of creatures anchored to architecture. Rainwater carries these vibrations through gutters and pipes until every wall seems to contain an animal calling from within. Low frequencies roll between the mountains and return minutes later, altered by the valley’s dense construction. At times all the fauna answer the echo at once. Then the city becomes a single immense throat.

The loudest creatures are not necessarily the largest. Hair-thin organisms inhabit cracks in metal cladding and produce shrieks that cut through stone. Shoals of translucent, many-legged things run along overhead aqueducts, their feet striking the held water with a sound like hard hail. In the lowest plazas, slow armored animals press their resonant undersides to the pavement. Their calls pass below hearing and reveal themselves as trembling reflections in puddles.

Movement, despite the noise and multitude, is patient.

A flock may take a year to cross a square. Wings open over several days, membrane by membrane, testing the thick cold air. Herds migrate upward through the city at a rate visible only by comparing seasonal maps. Even attacks unfold with glacial deliberation: jaws separating over hours, prey folding itself into defensive geometry, both sides surrounded by tiny scavengers waiting weeks for the outcome.

This slowness may be a consequence of the cold, but the cold is not simple. The air freezes exposed moisture, while certain stones remain wet and faintly warm from internal formation. Temperature pools in unexpected places. A stairwell may descend into sharper frost; a rooftop may hold a pocket of liquid mist. The city harvests such inversions. Its infrastructure is designed less around streets than around thermal contours, most of them drifting only a few meters per century.

Water is everywhere and almost never flowing freely. It condenses, suspends, seeps, gathers, and migrates under capillary or stranger forces. Black ponds occupy unfinished foundations. Their surfaces are crowded with breathing apertures. Rime seals canals from above, but beneath the ice thousands of bodies move in tight, soundless procession. On the western escarpment, water emerges from the rock in evenly spaced beads, as though the mountain were perforated. Each bead takes nearly an hour to swell and drop. Animals wait below with mouths raised.

There are no rivers yet. The land is too new to have learned them.

Drainage channels exist only because the city has made them, though many have been appropriated by fauna and now pulse, molt, incubate, or sing. The engineers respond by building new channels alongside the old ones. Before these are complete, other species occupy them. Thus infrastructure grows outward in paired and tripled lines: intended conduit, living conduit, replacement conduit, all interlaced until their purposes become indistinguishable.

The city’s deepest machines are said to stabilize the basin. They do not prevent geological change; they negotiate with it. When pressure rises beneath a district, foundations soften and allow the buildings to migrate. Walls loosen into grains, pass around emerging rock, and harden again. Roads bend over decades. Whole neighborhoods climb gentle slopes that did not exist when they were founded.

No demolition is practiced. Structures are persuaded to become other structures.

A tower too heavily colonized to serve as habitation may be converted into a resonator, its rooms opened to the cold so that the animals within can synchronize municipal signals. A drowned transit station becomes a pressure reservoir. A bridge that no longer reaches its opposite bank is allowed to continue growing into empty air, where eventually it may intersect some future construction—or become a nesting spine for airborne colonies.

The distinction between technology and terrain has blurred, though not completely. The youngest rock is matte and seamless; the oldest construction is jointed, numbered, and furred with instruments. Between them lie materials that revise themselves in response to stress. They resemble stone until addressed by a machine, at which point they briefly become transparent, revealing lattices that extend into dimensions visible only as color.

Above everything hangs the unlit atmosphere, dense with vapor and slow-moving fauna. Some float by displacing not air but weight, casting zones in which droplets curve upward. They pass between tower crowns like submerged moons. Their calls are long metallic descents that may last through an entire night. The smaller flying creatures cling to them in such numbers that their silhouettes appear ragged and mountainous.

There is little wind. When it comes, it moves as a broad, cold pressure front through the basin. Suspended waterways bow. Towers emit warning tones. Millions of animals turn the same side of their bodies toward it. The wet surfaces of the city roughen with frost, and for several minutes dim blue light blooms wherever the wind presses matter against matter.

Then the air stills again.

Droplets resume their patient enlargement. The cliffs continue rising by fractions. Buildings adjust their foundations. Predators close another degree around prey. Far beneath the crowded streets, machines make measurements for structures that will not be needed for centuries.

And in the almost total dark, the unfinished city listens to itself becoming a landscape.
