The city stands at the bottom of a warm inland sea.

It was not built and then drowned. Nothing here was ever cut, lifted, fitted, or planned by hands. The city grew into cityhood by other means: through the repeated choices of animals, the slow mineral labor of water, and the persistence of structures that outlived their makers. Its streets are migration channels. Its towers are reefs raised upon reefs. Its chambers were excavated by mouths, occupied by generations, abandoned, silted, and opened again.

The seafloor lies low and nearly level, a broad basin of dark marl. Across it, the settlement extends farther than the weak underwater light can carry. Even at noon, only a diluted brown-green dusk reaches these depths. At night there is no distinction between water and distance. Both become a single black substance in which shapes announce themselves by touch, scent, pressure, or brief living light.

The warm water is dense with presences.

Clouds of small fish fill the avenues, turning almost as one body. Shrimp crowd the porous walls. Eels inhabit narrow ducts and old worm-borings. Crabs pass over roofs furred with bacterial film. Rays settle in the plazas and vanish beneath dustings of silt, leaving only their raised eyes. Long, pale hunters move through upper corridors with such restraint that their passage is felt first as a slight tightening among everything smaller.

Yet the city is quiet. Water softens violence. Feeding happens in sudden, nearly soundless folds: a mouth opening, a school contracting, a plume of disturbed sediment. Most of what can be heard is intimate—the clicking of claws inside stone, the rasp of teeth on carbonate, the granular hiss of sand drawn through a burrow. From far off comes an occasional low concussion when an exhausted reef face collapses. The sound arrives through the water and the ground together, more pressure than noise.

The buildings are made of accumulated life.

Some towers began as coral colonies and have stood, in one form or another, for thousands of years. Their living surfaces occupy only the newest margins. Beneath them are stacked dead generations, riddled with passages and hardened into limestone. Other structures are broad mounds raised by shellfish around vents of nutrient-rich water. There are domes made from interlocked tubes, buttressed ridges laid down by oysters, and thin, crowded spires whose chambers were secreted by animals no longer found in the basin.

No architect governs their arrangement, but the city has districts. Water determines them.

Where a slow current descends from the north, fan-shaped colonies spread across walls to sieve suspended food. In sheltered hollows, soft-bodied animals cluster so thickly that the stone appears to breathe. Along the principal channels, the flow has scoured smooth troughs between the reefs. These are the streets: long lanes of exposed shell and dark rock, polished by passing bodies and by centuries of moving sediment. At crossings, currents form steady gyres. Husks, scales, eggs, and scraps orbit there until scavengers take them.

Open courts lie between the denser quarters. Their floors are fine black mud, interrupted by low cones and holes. They may look empty, but they are among the most inhabited places. Beneath each span of mud is another city—branching burrows packed with worms, blind crustaceans, clams, and soft creatures that never emerge. At intervals the ground exhales. Pale sediment rises in a column, spreads like smoke, and settles onto neighboring roofs.

Vegetation is scarce. There is too little light for forests of weed, and the basin lies too deep for rooted meadows. A few long ribbons of red-brown algae survive on the highest crowns, where noon sometimes reaches them. They are narrow and widely separated, always grazed close. More common are films: dark green smears on upward-facing stone, rust-colored bacterial veils around warm seeps, translucent coatings that shine when disturbed. Here, the role of foliage is taken by animals—branching corals, feather worms, hydroid thickets, and pale fans that close at the faintest pressure wave.

The city’s tallest structures approach the upper dimness without touching it. They form crowded silhouettes: crooked pinnacles, hollow arches, fused terraces. Many have been weathered into stability. The softer portions were removed long ago; the remaining forms follow the compromises of current and gravity. Arches point downstream. Towers lean together and fuse where their surfaces meet. Caverns enlarge until their ceilings fall, after which new colonies seal the rubble and begin another layer.

Erosion here is not ruin but metabolism. Parrotlike grazers grind old walls into sand. Boring sponges honeycomb foundations. Acidic films soften shell. Storm currents, descending from the unseen surface, carry weakened fragments away. At the same time, every exposed edge is being rebuilt. Larvae settle. Shells accumulate. Mineral-rich seep water cements loose grains. The city is consumed at nearly the same rate that it grows, so its outline changes while its presence remains.

There are no tools and no relics of tools. Nothing in this place can be switched on. Nothing is inert in the manner of a manufactured object. Doors are sphincters or curtains of tentacles. Windows are feeding apertures. Pipes are worm tubes, sponge canals, or the hollow bones of extinct colonies. Streetlights are living creatures.

Most of these lights are small and accidental. A disturbed comb-jelly releases a trembling blue line. Tiny plankton flash where a fin cuts through them. Lures pulse from recesses, illuminating teeth for an instant. In one quarter, colonies of minute animals cover the walls and answer pressure with green fire. When a large predator passes through, the entire avenue kindles behind it, outlining the wake as a receding architecture of sparks.

Warmth rises from the basin floor. Not violently: there are no towering black vents, only long cracks that release clear mineral water a few degrees hotter than the sea. Around them, the mud is pale and crowded. Blind shrimp graze bacterial mats; narrow fish hover with their heads toward the flow; translucent crabs defend territories no wider than their own legs. The warmth spreads through the lower streets and gathers beneath overhangs. In those pockets, eggs develop quickly and decay works quickly too.

Nothing remains unused for long.

A fallen body descends slowly through the dark, attended before it reaches the ground. Small fish take the soft margins. Crustaceans enter through the eyes and gills. By the time the remains settle into a court, the scavengers below are already rising. What is not eaten becomes wall, pavement, or soil. A shell may be shelter for one season, ballast for another animal, substrate for a colony, and finally a grain among billions.

The principal rhythm is tidal, though the distant shore cannot be sensed from here. Twice in each cycle, currents strengthen through the city. Fans unfold. Feeding crowns emerge. Schools align in the streets, all facing into the flow. Predators take positions behind buttresses. Silt lifts from ledges and fills the lower quarters with brown opacity. Then the water slackens. The suspended material settles. Animals leave their stations and resume their private routes.

Other cycles are less regular. Surface storms send cold pulses downward, followed hours later by showers of leaves, drowned insects, broken reeds, and land soil. The entire basin wakes to these deliveries. Swarms rise from cracks. Fish abandon cover. For a short time the quiet city becomes a vertical feeding ground, its inhabitants climbing through the water after falling fragments.

Then darkness closes again.

At the outer limits, the constructions thin into isolated reefs and burial mounds. Beyond them lies an open plain crossed by animal paths: shallow grooves made by rays, migrating shell herds, and heavy crawling things. The city does not have a wall. It ends gradually, by loss of complexity. Towers become knobs, avenues become channels, chambers become solitary holes. But the traffic continues far across the basin, and larvae from the central reefs drift outward on every current.

The place has laws, though none are declared. Height belongs to whatever can endure flow. Hollows belong to whatever can defend them. All surfaces must eventually become food or habitat. Darkness is not emptiness. Stillness is rarely sleep. Anything that grows must tolerate being grazed; anything that shelters must also trap; anything that dies must become structure.

From above, if there were sufficient light to see it, the city might resemble a vast, weathered relief map: ridges and basins, dense central quarters, branching roads, collapsed rings, and pale mineral suburbs around the warm fissures. From within, it is less legible. Visibility may end at the next arch. A tower is known by its current, a district by taste, a crossroads by the pressure of circling water.

Its citizens do not know they inhabit a city. They know routes, refuges, breeding grounds, ambush points, warm cracks, dangerous openings, and the exact hour when food arrives through a certain broken arch. The larger form exists only in the accumulation of those knowledges.

And through it all, the sea presses evenly—warm, black, immense—occupying every room the city has ever made.
