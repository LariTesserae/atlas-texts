The hollow lies low between two long shelves of weathered limestone, no larger than a village common. Its boundaries are clear without being abrupt: pale rock rises on three sides, rounded and undercut by old water, while the fourth opens through a narrow, level gap toward a dry plain. Nothing dramatic encloses it. The stone has simply worn into a shallow basin and remained that way for a very long time.

The ground is warm, even in the dimness. A high veil of cloud softens the daylight until there are almost no shadows. The limestone holds a muted ivory color, darkening to gray where moisture has entered its pores. Underfoot, fine brown silt lies packed into smooth, hard surfaces. It bears no tracks. In the lower places it becomes darker and cool, with a faint mineral sheen.

Water is present without abundance. Condensation gathers beneath overhangs and along the shaded faces of stone, where it forms clear beads that hang for hours before dropping. A thin rain comes occasionally, enough to deepen every color and fill a few shallow cups in the rock. These pools are seldom wider than a handspan. Their surfaces remain bare and perfectly plain—no insects dimpling them, no larvae suspended below, no animals arriving to drink.

A narrow channel crosses the hollow from the back wall to the opening. It is dry much of the time, but its floor is polished, and a dark line along its edges shows the usual reach of water. Its bends are old and settled. The banks do not crumble. During rain, a shallow sheet passes through with little force, spreading briefly across the central floor before slipping away beneath a ledge. Afterward, dampness lingers in patches, smelling of chalk, clay, and warm stone.

Vegetation survives only where the moisture is most dependable. Low gray-green mats occupy cracks beneath the rock shelves. A few wiry grasses stand apart from one another along the channel, each tuft with a clear ring of bare soil around it. Near the deepest seep grows a single cluster of broad, dusty leaves, their edges browned but not dying. There are no flowers. Lichen makes faded circles on the walls, so close in color to the stone that it appears less like growth than a slow alteration of the surface.

Nothing grazes here. Nothing nests in the crevices or moves under the leaves. There is no birdsong beyond the rim, no buzzing around the pools, no scratching in the silt. Even the plants scarcely stir; the basin is sheltered from wind. The quiet is broken only by an occasional drop striking stone, the soft passage of water after rain, and, very rarely, the small dry tick of a grass blade touching another.

At the eastern edge stand the remains of three low walls. They are made from the same limestone as the hollow, laid without mortar in broad, patient courses. Only one wall rises above waist height. The others have settled into lines of stone scarcely distinguishable from natural outcrops. They may once have enclosed a roofless shelter or marked a square pen, though there is no gate, hearth, vessel, tool, or carving to clarify their purpose. Fine silt fills the corners. Lichen crosses freely from the natural rock onto the placed stones.

The walls do not feel newly abandoned. They belong to the equilibrium of the basin now. Their fallen blocks have reached stable positions; rain follows familiar grooves through them, and the sparse plants treat their joints like any other fissure. There is no sense that the ruin is continuing to collapse. Whatever could loosen has already loosened. Whatever could be washed away has gone.

The whole place possesses that same finished quality. Its erosion is visible everywhere—in rounded lips, shallow runnels, softened corners, and hollows polished by generations of intermittent rain—but no active violence remains. The forms have been reduced to what the climate can keep. Water arrives, wets the basin, passes through, and leaves it much as it was. The small pools fill to the same margins. The damp patches return beneath the same ledges. The grasses retain their distances from one another.

Warmth persists without glare. Moisture persists without making the hollow lush. The ruins persist without becoming either building or rubble. In the muted light, the basin seems neither preserved nor forgotten. It is simply complete: a small arrangement of stone, silt, sparse leaves, and recurring water, with enough emptiness between them for every presence to remain distinct.
