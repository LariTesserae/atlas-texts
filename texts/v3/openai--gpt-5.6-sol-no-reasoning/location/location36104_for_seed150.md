The settlement occupies a shallow saddle high on the mountain, where two ridges lean close enough to make a sheltered hollow. It is no larger than a hamlet, though at first it appears immeasurable. There are no clear sightlines through it. Every span of ground, every wall-like surface, every interval overhead is occupied by vegetation.

Nothing here has been built from dead material.

The streets are narrow root channels pressed between the trunks of colossal shrubs. Their surfaces are woven from living runners: tough, pale cords packed so tightly that they bear weight like planks. Houses rise along them in overlapping tiers, grown rather than assembled. Some are hollow bulbs with thick, furred walls; others are chambers inside braided stems, their entrances formed by lips of bark that draw nearly shut against the cold. Roofs are low mats of evergreen scales. Eaves end in translucent tendrils from which condensed water falls at slow intervals.

The whole place resembles a city remembered by plants.

Its lanes turn sharply, avoiding knots of growth and recent upthrusts of stone. There are courtyards, covered passages, galleries, bridges, stairways, wells, and narrow towers, but each has a botanical origin. A stair is a series of shelf fungi hardened by frost. A bridge is an arched root that has anchored itself on both sides of a cleft. Towers are clustered flower stalks, hollow and many-chambered, grown rigid around central columns of ice. The small plazas are rosettes: concentric floors of broad leaves, their surfaces rough enough not to become treacherous when wet.

There is organization here, but no evidence of hands.

The settlement grows according to pressures and exchanges. A chamber forms wherever heat is conserved. A passage opens where moisture repeatedly travels. Structural stems thicken beneath weight; doors constrict when wind enters; damaged walls seal themselves with amber resin. What looks like planning is the accumulated intelligence of tropism, season, and mutual dependence. No single plant designed the place. The city is the shape of their agreements.

Cold is its governing fact.

Even at midday the air remains close to freezing. Mist condenses in the dense canopy and beads on every thorn, hair, rim, and vein. Thin skins of ice cover the shaded pools. Water is frequent but seldom abundant in one place: it weeps from exposed rock, runs inside fibrous gutters, and gathers under the raised rootways in black, shallow channels. After snow or rain, every surface ticks with droplets. By evening, the runoff freezes into clear seams, fastening separate plants together until the next mild interval.

Snow rarely reaches the ground. It settles first on the upper canopy—a compressed ceiling of interlaced branches only a few body-lengths above the lanes. The plants hold it there in luminous, sagging beds. Meltwater filters down slowly for days. Sometimes a branch releases its burden and a white mass drops through the dimness with a soft collapse, briefly closing an alley.

The underlying mountain is young. Here and there the living city has not yet hidden it: blue-gray stone protrudes in narrow blades, sharp-edged and unweathered. Recent cracks split the saddle. Pebbles still angular from the parent rock collect in root pockets, and the cliffs at the settlement’s boundary are raw enough to shed flakes during hard freezes. Vegetation responds immediately. Fine red rootlets enter new fractures within a season. Moss seals exposed faces. Pale crusts spread over fallen fragments, followed by miniature forests of wiry leaves.

Thus the city advances across its own unstable foundation, binding the mountain as the mountain breaks.

There are no trees in the ordinary sense. Height is difficult here, and the strongest growth proceeds sideways. Dwarf trunks as thick as pillars crawl along the ground, contorted by centuries of wind. Their bark bears nested colonies of moss, lichen, liverwort, vine, and cold-blooming parasite. Every host is also soil. Every branch carries another landscape. Dark cushions grow on pale cushions; grass sprouts from accumulated needles; small carnivorous cups sit in the wet hollows and collect midges, meltwater, and black grit.

Even decay remains crowded. A collapsed stem does not leave an opening. It becomes a warm, fibrous ridge and is occupied at once. Fungi bloom beneath its bark, root tips enter its softened center, and neighboring walls bend toward the released nutrients. The settlement has no vacant lots, no bare thresholds, no useless corners. Emptiness is treated almost as an injury.

Light arrives strained through cloud, snow, and many layers of leaf. It is blue at the outer edges and green-brown within. The brightest objects are water drops and patches of young tissue: tender shoots with an ivory translucence, unfolding in protected recesses. A few deep courtyards contain clusters of cold flowers. Their petals are nearly black, but frost outlines them in silver, making them seem cut from the darkness.

At night there is no glow except the sheen of ice under the sky. The settlement closes around itself. Leaves fold across roof vents. Hanging curtains of moss contract and thicken. The walls become firmer as their sap cools. Corridors that were open by day narrow until only the central lanes remain passable.

The place is mostly quiet. Dense growth swallows distant noises, and the mountain wind passes above rather than through it. Nearby sounds are precise: droplets striking waxy leaves, ice clicking in a stem, the minute tear of roots widening a crack. Water moves under the paths with a muffled, continuous whisper. During a thaw, the whole settlement develops a faint internal patter, as if rain were falling inside its walls.

Animals are scarce. There is too little open ground for runners and too little winter forage for large bodies. Small white insects shelter in the warmest flowers. A dark, round-bodied bird sometimes descends through a canopy vent, probes the moss chambers, and leaves before dusk. Long, silver-furred mice inhabit the dry arteries of abandoned roots, but one may pass several days without seeing one. Their tracks appear more often than they do: tiny stars impressed in frost, ending abruptly at a living wall.

Because fauna are rare, the plants perform many roles that elsewhere would belong to animals. Seed pods crawl by repeated contraction toward running water. Pollen threads loosen in response to vibration and drift through enclosed passages. Some vines recoil from frost cracks, pulling neighboring stems with them; others open chambers when they sense the carbon warmth of a breathing creature. The city notices presence slowly but unmistakably.

Its weather comes in cycles. Cloud gathers against the higher ridge, spills into the saddle, and condenses among the upper branches. The settlement becomes soaked without rain. Then wind clears the cloud, temperature falls, and every wet filament turns rigid. At dawn, as weak light returns, ice expands in stone cracks while sap begins moving beneath bark. The place creaks once or twice, softly but across its entire breadth.

Over the years, passages migrate. A familiar lane may close as roots thicken, while a parallel seam opens through a hedge. Chambers rise, divide, collapse, and are consumed. Bridges lower themselves toward new anchors. Whole districts lean away from eroding ground. There are no ruins distinct from inhabited structures, because abandonment is simply another phase of growth.

At the saddle’s lowest point lies the cistern garden. It is not a basin but a dense congregation of cupped leaves, each feeding the next. Water descends through hundreds of green vessels, purified by hairs and bitter membranes, until it reaches a dark pool enclosed beneath the roots. The surface is seldom visible. One hears it breathing through narrow holes: a low liquid movement responding to changes in air pressure.

Beyond the cistern, the growth ends abruptly at two young cliffs. Roots hang over their edges, dripping into mist. From there the settlement can finally be seen as a whole: a compact, dark-green mass fitted into the mountain hollow, roofed with snow and veined by ice, more like a single enormous organism than a collection of beings.

That impression is not entirely wrong.

In deep winter, when the water channels freeze and the upper canopy seals, sugars pass from older districts toward younger ones. Damage in one quarter changes growth in another. Flowering occurs in coordinated waves. The city conserves warmth collectively, tightening its outer surfaces while leaving a few moist chambers open at its center.

Nothing commands this. Nothing needs to.

The mountain lifts, fractures, and sheds its sharp stone. Water enters. Frost prises it wider. Roots follow the water and bind what frost has loosened. The settlement destroys and preserves its foundation at once, holding the saddle together while slowly turning it into living matter.

In the dim cold, almost without animals and entirely without tools, it continues to build.
