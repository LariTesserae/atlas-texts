The land begins high enough that distance loses its ordinary meaning.

A chain of plateaus stands above the warm low world, their summits worn nearly level by an age of weather no living people can imagine. They are not sharp young mountains. Their cliffs have no ambition left in them. They are immense, blunt remnants: rust-red tables, gray bastions, ocher islands of stone rising through miles of bright air. Between them lie chasms so broad that their opposite walls fade blue, and so deep that the forests at their bottoms resemble dark stains.

The summits are open and nearly empty. Thin grass lies flat over old rock. Here and there, low groves gather in depressions where reddish soil has survived the wind: wax-leaved trees, dense ferns, thornless succulents swollen with captured mist. Vegetation is abundant wherever the land permits it, but permission is given in isolated pockets. A traveler may cross half a day of naked sandstone and then step abruptly into a hollow crowded with broad leaves, hanging roots, pale flowers, and black moss.

Water is never plentiful, yet its traces are everywhere. It beads on leaves at dawn. It gathers beneath stone lips and in deep cylindrical potholes. Rain arrives in brief, heavy incursions, striking one plateau while the next stands dry in sunlight. The porous rock drinks quickly. Hours later, water reappears far down the cliff face, issuing from seams in silver threads. These transient falls may descend for a thousand meters without reaching the earth intact. They become mist, are seized sideways by the wind, and return upward as cool spray.

The sound is continuous and enormous.

Wind enters the chasms and is divided among countless hollows, pillars, arches, and undercut ledges. The whole range behaves like an instrument too large to be heard from outside. Deep shafts produce a bass vibration felt first in the knees and ribs. Narrow clefts scream. Caverns boom at irregular intervals as changes in pressure strike their hidden chambers. Pebbles rattle over bare shelves; trees thrash in protected gullies; distant rockfalls roll for minutes through the valleys.

At times the noise becomes so complete that smaller sounds disappear. Speech vanishes a few paces from the mouth. Thunder cannot be distinguished from the mountains’ ordinary voice. People communicate by touch, by hand signs, or by lengths of colored cloth tied to poles.

Yet the plateau tops themselves can look tranquil. Sun passes through ordinary blue sky. Shadows move at a familiar pace. Butterflies visit flowers in sheltered basins. Warm condensation gathers under broad leaves. The violence belongs chiefly to the air, which is almost never still.

The stone has reached an ancient kind of equilibrium, but the weather has not. Everything durable has already been removed or reduced to its strongest possible form. Weak layers departed ages ago. What remains is rounded bedrock, dense mineral crust, and isolated masses balanced with a perfection that looks precarious only because human lives are short. A freestanding slab may shudder in every storm and remain upright for another hundred thousand years.

Across this settled geology, change happens furiously in brief cycles. Clouds are born against one cliff, burst, and dissolve within minutes. Watercourses appear on dry rock as branching sheets, plunge from the rim, and vanish. Seeds germinate in temporary pools, flower, and cast new seed before the basins evaporate. Mats of tiny green plants swell from apparent dust after rain, turning whole slopes luminous for a day. Then the wind strips them back to brown.

The lushness belongs not to the quantity of water but to its unpredictability. Life waits everywhere.

In shaded ravines, where springs persist through most seasons, the growth is almost excessive. Tree ferns overlap in tiers. Vines bridge narrow cracks and hang hundreds of meters into empty air. Small trees grip vertical stone with exposed roots, following damp fractures downward. Their canopies form green ribbons along the cliffs—forests arranged not across land but down its walls.

Some chasms contain suspended gardens on ledges inaccessible from above or below. These green shelves may be separated by kilometers of bare rock. Their plants differ subtly from one another, as though every ledge were an island. One bears violet pitchers filled with rainwater. Another is crowded with silver reeds whose hollow stems howl in the wind. On a third, low trees produce hard, buoyant seed cases that sail between plateaus during storms.

Animals are scarce. There is too much empty air, too little dependable water, and too little protection from the sound. Small pale lizards live beneath stones and emerge after rain to drink from moss. Long-winged birds cross the gaps singly, rarely beating their wings. They ride the violent updrafts with minute adjustments, sometimes hanging motionless beside a cliff for hours. Colonies are almost unknown. Even insects appear in brief pulses: a basin may fill with translucent gnats at sunset and be empty by morning.

Larger creatures are known mostly by evidence. In the deepest forests, enormous leaves are sometimes found bitten through at shoulder height. Mud around remote springs bears three-toed impressions that end at bare stone. During certain storms, silhouettes move along the underside of low cloud, too slow to be birds and too high to be seen clearly. The plateau people do not pursue them. In a country where a trail may be erased by noon, hunting what cannot be reliably found is considered a form of madness.

Human presence is equally sparse.

A few shelters stand near dependable seeps: round huts made from fitted stone, roofed with woven grass and weighted by slabs. They crouch in hollows rather than asserting themselves against the skyline. Their doorways face away from the prevailing wind. Inside, every object is tied down or set into a niche. Sleeping mats are pegged to the floor. Water jars are squat and broad-based. Hearths are deep pits lined with dark rock, because an open flame would be pulled apart.

Metal is rare and poorly suited to this wet, mineral air. Most tools are stone, bone, hardwood, and fiber. Cliff-dwellers split dense black rock into blades, bind them into wooden handles, and polish the edges with red sand. They make exceptional rope. Fibers from several plants are soaked, beaten, twisted, braided, and finally rubbed with wax. Rope is the foundation of settlement: for roofs, ladders, bridges, nets, water scoops, warning lines, and the waist-cords without which no one approaches a rim.

There are paths, but few roads. Routes are marked by cairns too low for the wind to topple, or by shallow symbols pecked into bedrock. A spiral means shelter. Three nested cuts mean drinkable water. A horizontal groove warns that the stone beyond it sings before it breaks.

Between two of the larger plateaus hangs an ancient footbridge. It is renewed one strand at a time, so no one can say how old it is. Its ropes are thick as tree trunks, assembled from generations of finer cords; its planks are irregular pieces of storm-fallen wood. The bridge descends in a long curve into the chasm and disappears periodically into cloud. Crossing takes most of a day. Travelers do not look down—not from fear, the locals say, but because depth here can disturb judgment. The moving mist makes the cliffs seem to rise and fall, and a person staring too long may step toward what appears to be level ground.

There are older marks than the villages.

Lines of shallow cups have been carved into certain summit rocks. Rain fills them in sequence, overflowing from one to the next, and their differing tones announce how heavy a storm has been. Elsewhere, monoliths no higher than a person stand at cliff edges, all worn smooth and leaning slightly into the prevailing wind. Some are natural remnants; some show tool marks. The distinction has become unimportant.

In one broad valley, dozens of circular foundations lie half swallowed by vegetation. No walls survive above knee height. Trees grow from the rooms, and temporary streams run through old doorways. The place was not destroyed. It seems simply to have been vacated, perhaps when its spring moved to another fissure. Nothing portable remains except broken grinding stones and clay whistles shaped like birds.

The whistles are strange objects in a landscape already overwhelmed by sound. When blown, they are almost inaudible nearby, but across a chasm their thin calls can carry astonishing distances. A settlement invisible beyond cloud may answer hours later. No message is encoded beyond presence: *someone is here; someone heard you*.

Certain physical rules seem less reliable in the deepest clefts.

Falling water sometimes curves toward stone, runs along the underside of arches, then releases upward as mist. Seeds cast into strong updrafts return days later coated in pollen from plants that grow hundreds of kilometers away. Echoes do not always come from the direction of the original sound. Some repeat after such long intervals that they are mistaken for fresh events. People tell of hearing their own stone hammers from valleys they will not enter until the following season.

The simplest explanation is the shape of the country. There are too many channels, resonant chambers, and opposing winds for any one person to understand. But explanations do not quiet the unease of hearing three blows answer the two you struck.

At dusk the air usually settles for a short interval—not into silence, but into a lower, more distant roar. The cliffs retain the day’s warmth. Mist gathers in the canyons, filling them from below until the plateaus appear to float on a white inland sea. Groves darken. Pools reflect a plain sky. The rare birds find niches in the rock and fold their wings.

Then the night winds begin.

They rise from the warm depths in invisible surges. Forests on the cliff faces turn their pale undersides outward all at once. Ropes tighten. Stone chambers hum. The mist boils up over the rims and races across the empty summits, wetting everything without rain. Figures at the huts move quickly, securing lids, lowering poles, checking knots by hand because voices are already useless.

By midnight the land is in full tumult: cloud created and torn apart, waterfalls ascending and descending, foliage streaming sideways, old bridges groaning over invisible gulfs. Yet beneath this violent atmosphere, the plateaus remain what they have long become—ancient, spare, and almost impossibly stable.

The weather may remake the world every hour.

The world itself scarcely moves.
