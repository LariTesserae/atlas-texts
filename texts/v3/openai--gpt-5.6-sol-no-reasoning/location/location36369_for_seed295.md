The valley is built from rim to rim, though no builder remains in it.

At this altitude the air is warm but thin, carrying the close, mineral smell of old rain. Mountains rise around the valley as long black inclines, their summits lost in cloud. What appears at first to be bedrock is masonry: courses of dark basalt fitted so tightly that moss cannot enter the seams. The cliffs are retaining walls. The terraces are roofs. Even the broken crags above are weather-softened buttresses, built at a scale large enough to be mistaken for geology.

There is no exposed earth.

Underfoot lie worn flagstones, hollowed by countless generations of feet, claws, hooves, and falling water. Soil exists only where leaves have rotted into corners: in stair wells, along parapets, in blocked drains, and across the broad, shallow bowls of abandoned courtyards. Trees rise from these gathered pockets. Their roots travel over stone in thick cords, descend through colonnades, and enter buildings by doors and windows rather than by cracks. Ferns occupy gutters. Orchids crowd cornices. Fig trunks swell around pillars without breaking them, slowly enclosing one made form inside another living one.

The whole valley is a single inhabited structure, though its purpose is no longer legible. Galleries cross above halls; halls open onto descending gardens; gardens prove to be tiled reservoirs, empty for much of the year. There are stairs broad enough for herds and stairs so narrow that only lizards use them. Arches repeat along the slopes until darkness and vegetation hide their ends. No mechanism moves here. There are no lamps, engines, wires, hinges, wheels, or tools. Doors, where they remain, are slabs dragged long ago into sockets and now too heavy for any creature in the valley to shift. Water travels by gravity. Seeds travel in stomachs and fur. Everything that happens is done by weather, growth, hunger, mating, weight, and time.

Daylight rarely reaches the floor.

A roof of cloud rests just above the mountain rim, and below it the vegetation makes a second roof. Immense trees lean from terrace to terrace, joining their crowns over avenues and plazas. Their leaves are glossy and nearly black in the dimness. Pale undersides flash when the wind reaches them. In many places it is impossible to tell whether one stands outdoors or inside a roofless chamber: walls are obscured by vines, and vaulted interiors have been opened to the sky by ancient collapse. Noon resembles a green dusk. Dawn and evening are distinguished mostly by the direction in which birds call.

Moisture comes in intervals. Rain may pass over the upper walls for three days, drumming on leaves and running silver down every stair. Then no rain falls for weeks. The stone dries on its exposed faces but remains cool and sweating beneath the growth. Condensation gathers under lintels, along vaulted ceilings, and inside the long passages bored through the mountainside. Drop by drop it feeds cisterns sunk beneath plazas. Some are no wider than bowls. Others are black, columned lakes whose tiled edges vanish under mats of roots.

From the upper terraces, water descends through the city-valley in a chain of brief appearances. It spills from a carved mouth, crosses a courtyard as a shallow stream, disappears beneath a wall, and returns half a mile away in a stairwell crowded with reeds. During dry periods these channels shrink to isolated pools. Fish survive in the deeper ones. Frogs bury themselves in wet leaf mold. Crabs retreat into drains. Animals remember the route from basin to basin, and at dusk their migrations trace the hidden plumbing of the place.

They are everywhere.

Fruit bats pack the upper vaults in such numbers that the ceilings seem furred. At sundown they pour through the arches for nearly an hour, a warm, musky river of wings. Swifts and small dark pigeons nest in every recess. Parakeets gather in the fig crowns; hornbills knock fruit against balustrades; owls occupy empty niches once shaped for statues. Weaverbirds suspend nests from vines hanging over cisterns, thousands of little woven chambers swaying above the water.

The floors belong to other multitudes. Beetles polish trails through fallen petals. Ant columns run along mortar lines, vanish into old drains, and emerge on distant roofs. Moths coat damp walls like scales. Termites cannot eat the masonry, so they build earthen sleeves over it, carrying what little soil exists grain by grain from garden pockets. Centipedes hunt under cracked tiles. Fireflies live in the lowest halls, where their scattered green lights are sometimes the only visible boundary between air and water.

Macaques move through the middle levels. Their clans occupy separate courts, and the old architecture has become their map of inheritance: one troop owns a staircase, another the western arcade, another the fruiting trees around a collapsed dome. They know which parapets warm first, which wells keep water through the dry season, and which bridges leopards cross. Their disputes echo through the stone for hours.

Deer browse in the larger plazas. Wild pigs turn the composted gardens, uncovering buried mosaics before leaves conceal them again. Civets use narrow galleries. Porcupines sleep beneath fallen stairs. Snakes lie along warm cornices and in the hollow channels where rain once ran. In the highest, darker vegetation, cats move almost invisibly, their presence announced by a momentary silence that passes through birds and monkeys like a shadow.

But silence never reaches the whole valley.

The place is deafening. Sound collects in its masonry and is thrown back from wall to wall. Cicadas produce a continuous metallic pressure, rising until it seems to vibrate in the teeth. Frogs answer from a hundred separated reservoirs, each basin with its own pitch. Birds shriek from the canopy. Bats rustle in vaults. Monkeys bark across the terraces. Hooves strike hollow floors. Wings clap in colonnades. Water, when it comes, adds drumming roofs, overflowing spouts, gutter-roar, and the deep booming of full conduits beneath the plazas.

No single source explains the loudness. The valley itself amplifies life. A call made on one side may return from three directions, delayed and changed. Animals pause to distinguish an echo from a rival. During mating seasons, the sound becomes almost solid. Even in the driest weeks, when streams withdraw into the stone, insects fill the absence.

Change here is slow despite all that noise.

The architecture has already arrived at its weathered state. Edges are rounded where rain runs; thresholds are scooped by passage; stairs sag gently at their centers. Nothing looks newly ruined. Fallen blocks have settled into the floors and acquired gardens. Trees do not topple walls so much as negotiate with them over centuries, leaning, thickening, and redirecting their roots. When a gallery finally gives way, the event is violent and brief, but within a season leaves cover the debris, and within decades it resembles one more terrace.

Rainwater stains show the valley’s long breathing: dark fans beneath outlets, pale mineral curtains inside cisterns, green bands marking old flood levels. The main channels may alter their course only once in several generations. A blocked culvert slowly creates a marsh in an upper court. The marsh feeds a root mass. The roots lift tiles, diverting the next wet season into a lower hall. Fish eggs travel there on birds’ feet. Frogs follow. Herons learn the new pool. In this way the valley rearranges itself without intention.

There are no unnatural laws and no miracles. The strangeness lies only in the completeness of the construction and the completeness of its abandonment. Every surface was placed, cut, baked, polished, or stacked, yet nothing in the present landscape understands making. A hornbill sees a ledge. A fig sees a seam. Rain sees a slope. Generations of animals inhabit an artifact as if it were the oldest mountain in the world.

From the rim, when the clouds briefly lift, the whole extent can be seen: several miles of terraced darkness, densely wooded, descending toward a chain of reflective pools. Towers project above the canopy like worn teeth. Bridges connect ridges. Black walls contour the mountainsides far beyond the central valley, suggesting that the constructed ground continues into neighboring basins.

Then mist closes over it again.

Below, in the warm gloom, the innumerable voices continue. The stone receives them all. It has been receiving them for so long that the place seems less like a city reclaimed by nature than a vast, settled habitat whose cliffs happen to be square, whose caves happen to be rooms, and whose rivers remember the shape of stairs.
