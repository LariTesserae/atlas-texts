The place is hidden inside the mountain, though the mountain itself is manufactured.

From outside, if there is an outside, the structure resembles a high black ridge embedded in permanent cloud. Its slopes are overlapping plates of cast iron and dark ceramic, all bolted to an unseen frame. Snow lies in precise seams where the plates meet. Water beads upon every surface but seldom runs far; narrow gutters gather it, pass it through the skin, and distribute it inward. No exposed stone exists. Even the frost is seeded by refrigeration combs beneath the armor.

Deep within this fabricated summit is a chamber no larger than a freight elevator.

It is called the Rookery.

The chamber is densely assembled, scarcely containing open space. Ribbed pipes occupy the corners. Copper capillaries web the walls. Condenser fins descend from the ceiling in close ranks, wet and pale as gills. Between them sit pressure vessels, ceramic insulators, cages, relay boxes, grates, flywheels, and squat motors buried under coats of rime. Every object is within a hand’s reach of several others. Nothing appears added as decoration; nevertheless, the profusion of valves and brackets has acquired the intricacy of ornament.

A narrow maintenance ledge circles a central shaft. The ledge is slick steel punched with drainage holes. Under it lies another ledge, and beneath that another, each only a few centimeters deep. Water occupies all these levels at once: droplets suspended beneath bolts, films trembling across glass, black pools held in squared basins, ice packed around pipe collars, mist condensed on the fine hairs of electrical cables. The room is wet without possessing a stream. Its water is captive and recurrent. What drips from the ceiling is pumped upward through hidden ducts and made to drip again.

The cold is severe and perfectly regulated.

Breath blooms instantly. Bare metal takes skin. Yet the water does not entirely freeze. It remains at the exact border between states, as though the whole room were devoted to maintaining hesitation. Clear ice grows only in prepared molds: thin collars, transparent membranes, tiny roofs over screw heads. Elsewhere, droplets stay liquid despite the cold, darkened by oils and dissolved salts.

There is no soil. The nearest thing to earth is a shallow tray of graphite dust beneath one turbine housing. In it grow three strands of colorless moss, each threaded through with copper-green crystals. These are the only plants. They neither spread nor die. Inspection records, if found, would show the same three strands measured at the same lengths for longer than the machinery’s stated age.

The fauna is another matter.

Every cavity is inhabited.

Small white birds are packed among the condenser fins, thousands of them, perhaps tens of thousands, though the chamber could not plausibly hold so many. They resemble alpine swifts stripped of pigment and reduced to essentials: bead-black eyes, translucent claws, narrow hooked wings. Some perch on pipes with their breast feathers rimed solid. Some cling upside down to cable bundles. Others nest inside open electrical cabinets, arranging washers and flakes of insulation into perfect circles.

They are so close together that the walls seem feathered.

There are also blind silver eels in the coolant sumps, mites moving across the ice in red constellations, and six-legged beetles nesting in the warm interiors of motors. Pale shrimp pulse through the transparent flow meters. Minute worms occupy the water films, crowded in such numbers that each droplet has a cloudy center. The higher ledges are lined with dormant chrysalises made not of silk but of braided wire.

All of these creatures are alive. None of them changes.

The birds beat their wings continuously, but remain in place. Their bodies perform the complete labor of flight without gaining or losing a millimeter. The eels bend in the same black pools but never complete a turn. Beetle mandibles work steadily against metal, leaving no mark. Eggs remain eggs. Predators hold prey between their teeth indefinitely. Nothing feeds, starves, breeds, molts, decays, or grows. Life has been caught not in stillness, but in endless action without consequence.

This produces the sound.

The Rookery is deafening beyond ordinary endurance. The birds’ wingbeats merge into a solid, physical pressure. Motors rotate beneath them, flywheels howl, pumps hammer, relays chatter, refrigeration coils shriek as contracting metal insists upon moving without ever contracting further. Water strikes the same plates in unbroken percussion. Each droplet falls, impacts, rebounds, and is returned through the system with no measurable interval.

The noise does not fluctuate. It has no rhythm because rhythm requires difference. It is a single permanent event: thunder sustained inside a sealed box.

A person entering feels it first in the teeth, then in the sternum. Speech cannot exist here. Thought becomes difficult because the chamber seems to supply its own thought—one vast mechanical note composed of millions of smaller cries. Ear protection reduces pain but not presence. The sound travels through the ledge into bone.

Nothing in the chamber bears erosion.

This is strange because everything appears ancient. Water has touched the same rivets for ages, claws have gripped the same rails, gears have meshed through uncountable revolutions. Yet edges remain sharp from the casting. Paint retains the faint ridges left by its original brush. Welds are bright and granular. Every surface possesses the raw, provisional quality of a machine assembled moments ago and not yet worn into itself.

Even the icicles look newly poured.

The central shaft descends only nine meters, but looking down it gives the impression of immense altitude. At its bottom, a circular fan faces upward. Its blades turn at industrial speed, sustaining the birds and the mist. Behind the fan is neither floor nor ductwork but a dim white distance, like cloud seen from the open door of an aircraft. Instruments lowered into it strike a metal plate after nine meters. Eyes do not believe the instruments.

The room’s elevation is therefore internal. It is high not because of where it stands, but because the machinery enforces the conditions of height: pressure, cold, thinness, wind, cloud. A pocket summit has been constructed inside a larger constructed summit. There may be others nested below it, each chamber manufacturing the altitude required by the chamber inside.

No one knows what the installation produces.

One theory says the birds are components: soft vanes in a biological turbine, maintaining airflow where ordinary blades would ice over. Another says the machinery exists solely to preserve the birds at this exact instant. The brass gauges offer no answer. Their needles stand fixed, though they vibrate too quickly to see. Labels name unfamiliar measures—**PERCH LOAD**, **CLOUD DEPTH**, **AVIAN PHASE**, **CONTINUANCE**—and all readings remain precisely nominal.

A control wheel beside the entrance has been painted red. Its spokes are warm enough to touch. It will not turn in either direction, and tool marks show that nobody has tried.

That may be the governing rule of the place: every force is present, and every result has been withheld.

Cold without freezing. Water without drainage. flight without passage. Industry without production. Life without history. Noise without sequence. The Rookery persists in the first fraction of its own creation, packed, wet, and clamorous, forever newly assembled high inside a mountain that has never finished being made.
