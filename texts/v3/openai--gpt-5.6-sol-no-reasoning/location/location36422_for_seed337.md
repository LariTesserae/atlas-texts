The city lies just under the surface.

Its roofs are not far beneath the fields above—sometimes only a depth of two or three arm-spans—but it spreads laterally for miles, filling a broad seam between hard limestone and a younger bed of pale clay. From above there is little sign of it beyond scattered ventilation mounds, fenced sinkholes, and occasional wisps of cooking smoke escaping through cairns. Below, the seam opens into an inhabited plain: low-ceilinged in places, unexpectedly vast in others, divided by natural pillars and crowded with walls, workshops, storerooms, sleeping courts, cisterns, and narrow streets.

It is not an old cavern adapted into a city. The hollow is still being made.

At the outer edges, workers advance into the clay with wooden spades, horn picks, stone adzes, and wedges of fired hardwood. The material comes away in dense slabs. Behind them, other crews scrape the ceiling smooth, shore weak places with timber, and pack excavated earth into bricks. The city therefore grows by consuming its own boundary. Every new room produces the substance for a wall elsewhere; every passage dug at the perimeter raises another house in the center.

Nothing here has yet had time to become picturesque. There are no worn hollows in thresholds, no rounded pillars, no ancient channels cut by centuries of water. Tool marks remain sharp. Clay walls show fingerprints. Split timbers are pale where the axes opened them. Floors are level but raw, tamped hard enough to bear carts yet still carrying the parallel impressions of rammers. Even the natural stone has a newly exposed look, white and faintly powdery, as though daylight has only just reached it.

The city is densely occupied. Space is valuable not because the underground plain is small, but because excavating it is slow. Houses share walls. Roof beams pass over several properties. A potter’s kiln warms a bath chamber on one side and a nursery on the other. Grain bins are built beneath benches; beds fold against partitions; ladders rise through ceiling hatches to lofts squeezed between supports. Workshops open directly onto streets scarcely wide enough for two people to pass shoulder to shoulder. Clay jars stand in every recess. Bundles of reed, firewood, rawhide, rope, and tool handles hang overhead until the lanes seem roofed twice.

Yet the settlement does not feel dark. Hundreds of shafts reach the surface, widened at their lower ends and lined with pale plaster. They admit columns of ordinary daylight. Cloth reflectors and limewashed panels spread it sideways, so the central districts possess a cool, diffuse brightness resembling an overcast morning. The light shifts steadily across walls and floors, making it possible to tell the hour. Near noon, some shafts produce brilliant circles in which children sit to sew or sort seeds. Dust turns slowly in these beams. When a cloud passes over the land above, whole neighborhoods dim together.

Fire is used sparingly. Oil is difficult to obtain, smoke is dangerous, and fuel must be carried down. Cooking hearths are clustered beneath dedicated vents. Kilns and charcoal braziers occupy taller chambers at the margins, where heat can be drawn outward through sloping flues. Most interiors smell instead of damp clay, limestone dust, wool, stored grain, and the faint sourness of many people living close together.

Water arrives intermittently. Rain on the surface enters through stone-lined catchments and descends in chains of cisterns, the highest overflowing into those below. After a storm, the city becomes briefly musical. Drops strike ceramic basins. Gutters murmur inside walls. Overflow channels run along the sides of streets, and people emerge with bowls to catch fresh water before it reaches the communal tanks. For a day or two, condensation beads on the cool ceilings and trickles down the whitewashed pillars.

Then the sound fades.

Between rains, water is rationed from covered reservoirs. Deep in the lowest quarter, a narrow river passes through gravel beneath the clay bed, but it is unreliable and difficult to reach. Wells descend to it through cramped shafts. Water drawers work in pairs, lowering leather buckets on braided rope, listening for the softened splash far below. This river is the reason the city began here, though few inhabitants ever see it. It is less an underground stream than a moving wetness in the dark, threading through stone.

The air remains cool in all seasons. Surface winters bring a sharper chill through the shafts, while summer only makes the upper rooms slightly warmer. People dress in layered wool and felt. Walls are sometimes faced with woven mats to soften their cold. Sleeping chambers are built toward the dense center of each block, away from vents and new excavations, and several families may gather in one room during the coldest weeks.

Vegetation is scarce and deliberate. Nothing forms a wilderness underground. In the broadest light courts, shallow beds support onions, bitter greens, medicinal herbs, and pale-stemmed beans trained on string. Moss grows around leaking joints, but it is scraped away before it can hold moisture against timber. Mushrooms appear in spent straw and are tolerated if known, burned if not. A few courtyards contain small trees whose trunks rise toward open shafts; their crowns live mostly at the surface while their roots descend among the houses. From below they look like wooden columns that have unexpectedly put forth leaves.

Most food comes from the land above. Fields, grazing grounds, and orchards belong to the city even though its citizens sleep beneath them. Stair towers rise to compact surface compounds where shepherds, farmers, and watchkeepers spend the day. At dusk, they descend with baskets and animals. Goats are kept in outer chambers near the ramps; chickens in wicker pens beneath light wells. Larger livestock rarely enter except during storms or raids. Their hooves and breath overwhelm the narrow districts.

Wild animals are uncommon. Bats use a few abandoned headings, but active vents are screened with reed lattice. Small cave crickets gather near cisterns. Pale woodlice inhabit rotten braces and are regarded as warnings of damp. Rats exist, though relentless trapping keeps them wary and sparse. There are no great underground predators, no strange blind herds, nothing that properly belongs to a deep cave. This is too shallow, too new, and too thoroughly human.

The city’s life is active but quiet. Stone hammers are wrapped in hide. Carts have wooden wheels bound with rawhide rather than metal. Shouting is discouraged because voices travel unpredictably, slipping through vents and service passages to emerge far away. News is carried by runners and by soft signals struck on suspended clay tiles. Three quick notes call a water crew. A slow alternating beat warns of weak ground. At the beginning and end of a work shift, low drums sound from the excavation fronts, more felt through the floor than heard.

Silence here is practical. It allows people to hear the city changing.

Fresh clay sometimes ticks as it dries. Roof beams creak before they fail. Water may begin whispering behind a wall after rain. A dull settling thud can mean that a newly opened chamber is compacting safely—or that a support has shifted. Children learn these distinctions early. They are taught to put an ear against pillars and to report unfamiliar sounds without embarrassment.

Construction never stops. In one quarter a market is being roofed; in another, a row of homes is dismantled so the floor can be lowered. Surveyors work with plumb lines, water levels, knotted cords, and sighting frames. They map not only streets but weight: how much untouched earth lies overhead, which pillars carry which spans, where a cistern’s pressure might split a wall. Their plans are incised into damp tablets and baked for storage. There is no advanced machinery, only accumulated skill, coordinated labor, and rules enforced because everyone understands the consequence of ignoring them.

No one may dig alone. No new wall may block an airflow passage. A roof support belongs to the district, even when it stands inside a private house. Waste clay must be registered and carried where it is needed. Open flame must remain beneath a marked flue. During heavy rain, lower streets are cleared whether or not flooding seems likely. These are civic laws, but they also feel like laws of nature.

Beyond the inhabited districts lies the unfinished ring.

Here the ordered city frays into raw chambers. Survey cords cross empty ground. Timber frames stand without walls. Stacks of bricks cure in the cool air. Excavators work by daylight piped through temporary shafts, advancing across a broad front so that no single tunnel bears too much stress. The boundary is crowded with activity—diggers, carriers, carpenters, plasterers, water finders—yet only a short distance farther on there is untouched clay, compact and blank.

At shift’s end, tools are cleaned and arranged with care. Wooden wedges are dried by communal hearths. Stone edges are resharpened. Cracked baskets are rewoven. The day’s excavated volume is measured against the number of bricks formed, so material does not accumulate in the streets. Nothing is waste if it can be moved, packed, burned, woven, or built.

From a high gallery, the city appears almost like a landscape under cloudy daylight. White pillars stand in place of hills. Rooflines descend toward the reservoir quarter. Smoke rises in thin disciplined strands. People move through the packed streets in muted colors, carrying timber, jars, baskets, lambs, sleeping children. Far off, at the expanding perimeter, the repeated knock of hand tools passes through the ground.

The place is cool, close, and crowded, but not buried in the sense of being entombed. It is opening rather than closing. Each day adds a few more yards of street, another cistern mouth, another shaft through which weather can enter. Rain, light, dust, air, and human labor move continuously between the hidden city and the ordinary land above.

Nothing has been softened by age. The place is all edges, plans, wet plaster, exposed grain, and rooms whose names have not yet been decided. It is less a city beneath the earth than a city caught in the act of persuading earth to become space.
