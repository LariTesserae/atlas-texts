The highlands begin above the weather.

They occupy so much of the continent that they make their own horizon: a newly lifted immensity of black-green plateaus, serrated escarpments, and warm volcanic uplands, all standing where no range stood a few centuries ago. From the low country they appear as a dim wall reaching from one end of the world to the other. From within, they cease to feel like mountains. They are a country of vertical distances, with valleys large enough to contain separate climates and summits that vanish not into cold but into layers of warm, brown cloud.

The stone is young. Nothing has been rounded or settled. Cliffs retain the gleam of recent fracture, displaying wet faces of basalt, green glass, sulfurous crystal, and pale mineral flesh. Ridges end in thin blades. Fields of broken rock lock together so cleanly that their edges still seem to remember being one body. In places the ground has risen faster than erosion can address it: terraces lean at impossible angles, ravines stop abruptly at blank walls, and whole watersheds remain trapped in shallow basins without outlets.

There is little standing water. Rain comes intermittently and rarely reaches the earth as rain. Moisture gathers instead as condensation along the undersides of overhangs, on fern fronds, across the dark nap of moss forests. At dusk every surface beads over. For an hour the landscape glitters weakly; then the droplets crawl uphill, merge in cracks, or lift in trembling sheets toward the cloud layer.

The few rivers are temporary and enormous. They appear after distant storms, filling dry canyons without warning, warm and opaque with mineral powder. Some run for days. Some cross one valley and vanish vertically into unlit fissures. Their abandoned channels are crowded with reeds, broad-leaved succulents, and low trees whose roots can unfold within minutes when floodwater arrives. Pools remain in hollows under slabs, steaming faintly and alive with translucent swimmers. Around these pockets, vegetation grows in concentric intensities: slick algae at the center, then scarlet moss, then waist-high ferns, then dense groves of black-barked trees.

The plants are lush without making the land uniformly green. They exist in packed colonies separated by raw stone. Miles of bare lava may end abruptly at a ravine so crowded with growth that no ground is visible. Vines hang in layered curtains. Pale fronds open only in shadow. Forests climb the cliffs in narrow vertical bands wherever condensation follows seams in the rock. Their canopies knit together, detach, and sometimes migrate intact across the slope.

Nothing here appears built.

There are no roads, walls, pylons, ruins, tools, or cut surfaces. Yet the highlands possess an industrial character so strong that a traveler might constantly expect to find machinery behind the next ridge.

The mechanisms are geological and living.

Deep beneath the plateaus, chambers compress gases in regular cycles. Mountains inhale through forests of vents and exhale from fissures hundreds of kilometers away. Basalt columns descend like pistons. Mineral belts move under the ground with a slow conveyor rhythm, carrying ore from one basin to another. Vast colonies of metallic fungi refine these deposits: iron enters their root mats as rust and emerges in branching filaments of nearly pure metal. Lightning passes through these buried networks, and whole valleys dimly pulse as current is stored, divided, and discharged.

On certain nights, cliff faces begin to turn.

Not collapse or slide, but rotate in interlocking sections, each mesa moving against the next with seamless force. Valleys close. New valleys open. Water is tipped from basin to basin. Forests caught at the hinge vanish below, while other forests rise from the depths wet, steaming, and full of animals that have never seen the upper air. The movement is nearly silent. Only a pressure in the teeth announces that several billion tons of country have rearranged themselves.

The light is perpetually low. Warm clouds occupy every altitude, stained charcoal, umber, and bruised violet by airborne spores and volcanic dust. The sun appears as a larger dimness behind them. Shadows have weak edges. Bioluminescence is common but subdued: threads under bark, points along insect wings, slow green annunciations in pools. During electrical weather, a cold glow travels through the mineral strata, outlining buried structures beneath the translucent rock.

The world is crowded with animals.

Most are small enough to disappear into its density: velvet-backed grazers among the moss, glass-winged pollinators, six-legged burrowers that drink condensation directly from stone. Warm fog carries shoals of floating creatures whose bladders resemble seedpods. They feed on spores and steer with long feathered vanes, settling by the thousands onto cliff forests when the air becomes dry.

Larger animals move cautiously because the geography is unreliable. Broad-footed herd beasts follow mineral currents rather than landmarks. Their heads are crowned with sensitive plates that detect the deep turning of the plateaus. When a shift approaches, they lie flat and become almost indistinguishable from boulders. Long-bodied predators hunt along gravity seams, places where loose objects tend to roll sideways. Some never touch the ground at all, but swim through dense pockets of atmosphere trapped between mountain walls.

Life is abundant partly because extinction here is local and constant. A ravine may be sealed at noon and reopened ten thousand years older by evening. A breeding ground may briefly intersect with another version of itself and double its population. Entire ecologies arrive embedded in newly extruded hills, blinking in the low light, already adapted to conditions that have not yet developed.

Distance is not dependable.

A peak that seems twenty kilometers away may require months to reach; a far escarpment may be entered by stepping behind the right tree. Objects become larger when approached from the east and older when approached from below. Rain falling on one side of a stone sometimes appears as fossils on the other. Tracks precede the animals that make them. Echoes occur without sound, arriving instead as brief duplications of movement: a flock takes wing, and seconds later the empty air repeats the gesture.

There are regions where reality grows especially thin. Here the young stone has not decided what material it is. Basalt softens into muscle under pressure, then crystallizes when released. Ridges divide into alternate contours and occupy all of them until touched. Gravity pours visibly down slopes as a smoky distortion, pooling in depressions and making them almost impossible to cross. Trees root in these pools with their trunks extending in several directions called upward.

Despite the turbulence, the highlands are mostly quiet.

Vegetation dampens what little wind enters the valleys. Animals signal through color, scent, vibration, and changes in body heat. Geological movement occurs below the range of hearing. Even storms are muffled inside the clouds, lightning flashing without thunder. The loudest common sound is condensation falling from leaf to leaf after dusk: a sparse, intimate ticking distributed across a landscape larger than nations.

Then, rarely, the buried systems synchronize.

Every vent opens. Metallic fungal webs brighten beneath the ground. Rivers rise from dry stone, cliff forests release their airborne fauna, and plateaus begin their silent revolutions. Clouds descend in spirals through the canyons. New ridges thrust upward with unworn edges; old ones fold into warm darkness. For a few hours the entire continent seems to be manufacturing itself at impossible speed.

By morning, it is still again.

Mist clings to unfamiliar trees. Animals stand at the margins of newly opened chasms, smelling countries that did not exist the previous day. Droplets form on fresh black rock. Somewhere beyond the dim horizon, another mountain quietly begins.
