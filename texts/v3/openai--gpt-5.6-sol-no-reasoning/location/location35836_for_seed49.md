The city occupies an entire basin so low and broad that its curvature is difficult to distinguish from the curvature of the world. There is no countryside around it. From one steaming horizon to the other, construction presses against construction: terraces, retaining shells, habitation blocks, flood galleries, machine vaults, transit throats, exchanger fields, and towers whose foundations are themselves older districts. Every surface has been claimed, overlaid, bored through, reinforced, or assigned a second purpose.

There is scarcely any open ground. What appears at first to be a plaza is the roof of a reservoir; what seems a canal is a cooling trench carrying both traffic and waste heat; what looks like bedrock is often poured mineral composite, weathered for thousands of years until its manufacture is no longer obvious. The city has settled into the geological record without ever becoming still.

It is warm here, with the enveloping warmth of a stone chamber after rain. Water is everywhere but seldom expansive. It beads on the undersides of bridges, perspires through retaining walls, trickles down stairwells, gathers ankle-deep in concave roofs, and hangs as condensation inside translucent conduits. Recent showers leave the whole basin shining. They do not cleanse it so much as reveal its relief: millions of channels appear at once, silvering ledges and seams, running in braided threads through embossed signs and across old impact scars.

The water table lies just beneath the city’s lowest inhabited layers. In some neighborhoods, the floors rise and fall imperceptibly on buoyant foundations. In others, warm seepage emerges through tiled vents and is captured before it can form a puddle. The oldest foundations weep continuously. Their mineral tears have built pale lips, rippled curtains, and rounded deposits around every fracture. These formations are not removed. They have become structural, and surveyors classify them as both architecture and erosion.

There are no plants.

No moss greens the wet stone; no roots prise apart the masonry; no weed takes advantage of a gutter’s silt. Even in forgotten courtyards, the rainwater stands black and bare beneath the ordinary daylight. The absence is not sterile so much as categorical. Vegetation seems never to have belonged to this place. Decorative gardens consist of arrangements of wet glass, branching ceramics, vapor jets, and slow-growing electrocrystals. Public “trees” are heat exchangers: tall, ribbed structures whose silver canopies condense atmospheric moisture and shed it in measured drops.

Fungal films occur in sealed depths, but they are regarded more as chemical weather than life. On the exposed city, the softest things are animals.

They are everywhere.

Dense flocks of sail-backed gliders ride the updrafts between heat towers, folding their translucent bodies to shoot through slots scarcely wider than a hand. Pale burrowing colonies inhabit porous flood barriers and make the stone tremble with their synchronized rasping. Canal mouths swarm with plated filter-feeders, each anchored by a cablelike organ to the current. Warm puddles fill quickly with pin-sized, many-legged scavengers whose mirrored shells make the water look granular.

Larger creatures move along designated faunal passages woven through the infrastructure. Six-limbed freight beasts, whether bred or manufactured no one now agrees, pick their way over vibrating causeways with loads clamped along their backs. Their hides resemble rain-darkened concrete, and apertures along their flanks open and close to shed heat. Shoals of airborne scavengers gather above demolition zones, waiting for fragments rich in salts and electrical charge. In the lower conduits, something broad and blind frequently pushes against the walls, displacing whole columns of water. The city marks its passage with warning lights but makes no serious effort to remove it.

Life here has adapted not to wilderness but to saturation. It nests in speaker cavities, feeds from corrosion, follows transit schedules, and migrates according to maintenance outages. Some species depend on the city’s electromagnetic leakage as completely as sea animals depend on currents.

The sound is immense.

It is not a single industrial roar but a packed atmosphere of noises with almost no silence between them: turbines, pressure releases, wheel shrieks, impact hammers, warning choirs, animal calls, falling water, deep structural groans, and the steady thunder of fluids driven through pipes wider than streets. Freight platforms pass overhead with a noise like sustained lightning. Ventilation towers exhale in sequences that can be heard for hundreds of kilometers. Beneath everything lies the basin’s subsonic pulse, produced by millions of foundations correcting themselves against the soft, wet ground.

Conversation in exposed places is conducted by touch, light, or direct transmission. Spoken language survives mainly in interiors. The local fauna is correspondingly extravagant in its signals. Creatures flash patterned membranes, strike the ground, or communicate through narrow-band vibrations sent directly into railings and walls. During mating cycles, entire districts flicker with reflected color while the audible city continues its indifferent bellow.

The technology is advanced enough to resemble natural law, though it remains heavily maintained. Buildings lean into unfavorable loads and straighten during storms. Road surfaces flow around accidents, closing like viscous skin. Tunnels alter their internal dimensions according to traffic, becoming wider without noticeably compressing the structures beside them. Materials remember previous shapes and argue with damage. A cracked tower may spend centuries drawing fragments of itself through the surrounding city, molecule by molecule, until the wound closes.

Space is used more than once. A chamber may occupy the same measured volume as another chamber offset in phase, with separate doors opening according to local power conditions. This has not made the city spacious. It has only permitted greater density. When the phase systems fail, furniture, cargo, animals, and sometimes whole crowds are abruptly reassigned to whichever version of the room remains stable. Old districts contain memorial walls listing thousands lost not to destruction, but to incompatible occupancy.

The weather belongs to the city as much as to the atmosphere. Heat rising from the basin generates permanent convection. Clouds assemble above the central districts in a pale revolving ceiling, fed by evaporation from canals and condenser fields. Rain falls frequently and locally: one avenue may be hammered by warm drops while the next remains dry beneath a thermal plume. Between towers, winds turn violent without warning. They carry spray, grit, discarded membranes, static charge, and airborne animals in streaming bands.

Storm management is the city’s central civic act. Enormous vanes rotate along the skyline, redirecting pressure fronts. Deep below, reservoirs inhale sudden runoff and distribute its mass to keep the basin from tilting. Lightning is caught by hovering lattices and conducted into storage caverns, though some bolts evade capture and travel sideways through wet architecture. When this happens, whole facades flare blue. Creatures detach themselves from walls. Transit freezes for three or four seconds. Then every system resumes at once, and the resulting surge of noise feels physical.

Nothing here is truly empty, and nothing remains assigned for long.

A flood gallery becomes a market when dry, then a spawning ground when submerged. A residential tower lowers itself into the saturated ground to escape a wind event and resurfaces days later several blocks away. Streets divide, merge, and climb the sides of buildings in response to crowd pressure. New structures are extruded into any vacancy detected by the municipal substrate. The city does not tolerate gaps. Even demolition is a form of rapid replacement: dismantling machines consume a structure from below while its successor grows through it from above.

Yet the place is old, and its turbulence has settled into durable habits. Corners have been rounded by countless bodies. Steps are hollowed in the middle. Channels cut to drain one forgotten storm have deepened into permanent waterways. Ancient towers, no longer understood, stand enclosed inside newer buildings like fossils in stone. Their surfaces bear smooth bands where aerial migrations have brushed against them for millennia.

Erosion and repair have reached an equilibrium so intimate that it is difficult to say which process shapes the city. Rain softens a cornice; maintenance swarms reinforce the softened profile rather than restoring the original edge. Current scours a tunnel; the tunnel responds by curving with the flow. Animals bore into a wall; the wall thickens around their colonies and incorporates their chambers into its thermal system. Age is not erased. It is stabilized.

In ordinary daylight, the basin is almost comprehensible. The sky is gray-white after rain. Near structures show mineral colors—rust red, limestone cream, charcoal, dull copper—while distance reduces the city to layered blue silhouettes. Water flashes from a billion ledges. Towers fade into humid air. Moving bridges pass one another at different heights. Flocks wheel in the warm updrafts, briefly resembling torn sheets of metal.

Only at the horizon does the true scale become apparent. The dense construction continues beyond weather fronts, beyond the reach of any single transit system, beyond dialect and governance. Lightning walks across districts that maps treat as continents. Cloud shadows take hours to cross one industrial roof. Rivers enter the basin and disappear into the city without ever reaching a sea.

There may be an edge, but no one living near the center has seen it. Excavations downward find older streets. Flights upward reveal only more towers through the clouds. Travel outward passes through successive climates created by machinery, populations, and heat, each one convinced it occupies the city’s essential quarter.

The prevailing belief is that the city was built in a shallow place because shallow places collect water, matter, creatures, noise, and history. Another belief holds that the basin formed beneath the city’s weight. A third proposes there was once a world outside it, green and spacious, but this is regarded as an aesthetic myth: appealing, impossible, and faintly obscene.

Here, existence has no unused margin. Rain must travel, heat must rise, animals must nest, structures must adapt, and every vacancy must become something before the water has time to fill it.
