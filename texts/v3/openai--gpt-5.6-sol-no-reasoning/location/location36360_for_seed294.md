The whole country is underwater, though “country” may be too small a word for it. It occupies a high shelf in an immeasurable sea: a broad, constructed plateau suspended far above the true bottom. Beyond its edges the water darkens vertically, dropping through miles of cold until distance and depth become indistinguishable.

Nothing here is natural substrate. The shelf, the ridges upon it, the ravines between them, even the scattered fields of rubble are made from fitted material. Yet there are no machines, no lamps, no cables, no moving parts. If builders ever worked here, their tools have vanished more completely than their work.

The plateau is assembled from pale, dense slabs whose composition resembles neither stone nor metal. Each is gently concave, worn at its margins, and joined to its neighbors without mortar. From above, the seams form huge concentric figures interrupted by faults and erosion. Some slabs are the size of rooms; others stretch for miles. Their surfaces have been abraded almost smooth by ages of water. Relief survives only in the deepest cuts: low walls rounded into ribs, terraces softened into steps, towers reduced to blunt white stumps.

Everything bears the same ancient finish. Sharpness has passed out of the world.

The shelf stands high enough to catch immense lateral currents. Cold water, burdened with minute white particles, pours across it in unpredictable fronts. A quiet plain can become a racing river in seconds. Sediment lifts from the seams and erases the horizon. Loose blocks tumble slowly, seeming almost weightless until they strike and produce a concussion felt through the body rather than heard. Then the current ceases as abruptly as it began. The pale dust descends. Vast spaces reappear.

This is the central contradiction of the place: its forms are settled, final, and immeasurably old, while the water never settles at all.

There is little light. Far overhead lies a diffuse gray brilliance, too distant to reveal a surface. It brightens and dims without a recognizable day. The clearest illumination comes during pressure storms, when the water itself develops broad, faintly luminous bands. These pass over the plateau like weather made visible: blue-gray curtains bending around ridges, tightening through narrow cuts, and unraveling into darkness over the shelf edge.

There are no plants. No fronds, roots, mosses, or films color the pale construction. Even the smallest surfaces remain mineral-clean. In sheltered recesses there are colonies of translucent nodules, but these are animals—or something sufficiently animal to feed, recoil, and release young. They sit far apart like drops of solidified water, extending hair-fine feeding threads whenever the current carries nourishment.

Larger creatures are abundant, though the openness of the shelf keeps them scattered.

Flat, six-winged swimmers skim a handspan above the slabs, using the boundary current as if it were ground effect. Their bodies are clear except for dark knots of organs. In calm periods they move in long, orderly files along the seams. When turbulence arrives, the files burst apart and each animal anchors itself with a ribbonlike mouth.

Pale burrowers inhabit the joints between slabs. They have no visible eyes and rarely expose more than a circle of jointed feelers. The oldest seams may contain thousands, all withdrawing at once when vibrations cross the floor. The simultaneous retreat makes a dry, delicate clicking, improbably audible in the water.

Above the ridges drift loose congregations of bell-shaped grazers. There is nothing for them to graze upon the structures, so they feed directly from the turbulent water, filtering out microscopic bodies and mineral grains. They pulse only enough to maintain position. During strong currents they collapse into tight beads and are swept for miles, gathering in hollows until the hollows seem filled with dimly breathing glass.

Predators are long and spare. They move through the particulate gloom in sudden straight lines, never curving while in pursuit. To turn, they stop completely, fold themselves into a sharp angle, and launch again. Their calls are low single knocks, separated by minutes of silence. One seldom sees the caller; instead the knock travels through the constructed shelf and returns from several directions.

The loudest events are rare. Somewhere inside the plateau, beneath layers no animal can enter, pressure accumulates. A portion of the floor then lifts—not explosively, but with the slow inevitability of a continent taking breath. Seams open into black trenches. Old walls rise from beneath the sediment, already rounded and worn, as if they too have been ancient for ages before appearing. Water plunges into the openings, pulling animals, dust, and loose masonry after it.

Hours or years later, another region subsides.

Thus the landscape is continually rearranged without ever becoming new. Forms emerge already exhausted. Structures are destroyed, yet their fragments fit the world too well to look like ruins. Nothing is constructed now, and still the act of construction seems unfinished.

Toward the center of the shelf stands the nearest thing to a landmark: an enormous circular depression surrounded by low, concentric embankments. The rings are spaced too widely to comprehend from the floor. Crossing them takes days. Each ring is pierced by evenly distributed gates, but erosion and displacement have turned the original geometry slightly awry. Through every gate pours a persistent inward current.

Animals follow it.

The six-winged skimmers arrive in files. Bells roll and pulse through the openings. Predators enter alone. Tiny joint-dwellers abandon seams that their kind may have occupied for centuries and crawl inward across exposed surfaces. The migration never becomes crowded; the ring is too large, and the arrivals too dispersed. Nevertheless, all motion over the central plateau tends, gradually, toward the depression.

At its bottom is a field of upright slabs, thin as blades and taller than buildings. They stand in sparse rows with great lanes of dark water between them. Their faces are blank. When currents pass through the field, the slabs do not move, but the water among them oscillates and produces tones below ordinary hearing. The local fauna responds by becoming still. For a while even predators hang motionless, angled in the dimness.

Then turbulence enters from the outer rings.

The lanes fill with spiraling sediment. Migrating animals are lifted into columns and flung apart. The upright slabs divide each current into countercurrents, making the field a maze of invisible forces. Clear-bodied swimmers flash in and out of the dust. Bells deform around corners. Long hunters strike at shapes that are no longer where refraction says they should be. The water boils without warmth.

At the precise center, where the currents collide, there is an empty circular space.

Nothing enters it.

Not sediment, not animals, not even the faint luminous weather from above. The water there is perfectly clear and almost perfectly still. It forms a dark, vertical cylinder whose lower end meets a round aperture in the plateau. Looking into that aperture reveals neither chamber nor abyss, only a dim repetition of the concentric country overhead, as though another eroded shelf hangs inverted far below.

No creature crosses the rim. Those swept toward it regain control with sudden, desperate strength. Even dead bodies veer aside, caught by some rule in the water.

The place has other rules. Sound travels farther through the made ground than through the sea. Fallen blocks sometimes return to sockets from which they were dislodged, though never while observed closely. A gate crossed outward may open onto a different ring when crossed inward. The high shelf’s edge is always visible from its outer plains, but journeys toward it take longer than the same journeys back.

These irregularities are mild enough to become habitat. Animals route migrations around them. Predators exploit them. Generations live entirely within the lee of a single wall, feeding and reproducing under laws that fail ten body-lengths away.

There is no vegetation to soften anything and no accumulated life to disguise the underlying design. The made world remains exposed: pale planes, broad seams, worn ribs, empty gates. Yet it does not feel sterile. Life fills it sparsely but persistently, occupying currents rather than surfaces, gathering only when the storms force it together. The water is its forest, road, weather, and sky.

In the quiet intervals, the plateau appears nearly abandoned. White particles descend through dim blue. A distant file of swimmers passes over a rounded ridge. One deep knock arrives from beyond the visible plain. The constructed ground receives it and carries it away through its innumerable fitted slabs.

Then a pressure front comes over the edge.

The darkness moves first. The animals anchor, fold, or flee. Dust lifts in widening sheets. Across the ancient, seamless country, ravines reverse their flow, walls disappear, and the cold sea begins once more to remake what cannot become younger.
