The place occupies a shallow basin no wider than a small town, though from within it there is almost no sense of distance. Vegetation has taken every interval. There are no clearings, only changes in species; no paths, only seams where one living surface yields to another. Even the ground is uncertain. Underfoot lies a compressed raft of roots, leaf husks, fibrous runners, and broad overlapping pads, all buoyed slightly by dark water close beneath. A heavy step sends a slow tremor through the floor. Ten paces away, leaves shiver.

The basin is warm without being hot. Moisture hangs continually in the air, condensing along tendrils and the undersides of leaves. Rain has recently passed—or perhaps always seems to have recently passed. Water ticks from canopy to canopy long after the clouds have opened, so that the place generates its own lesser rain. It gathers in green cups, crawls down waxed stems, and disappears through breathing holes in the root floor. Here and there, narrow black channels show between mats of vegetation. They do not flow visibly, but floating pollen turns in them, proving some deep, patient circulation.

Plants provide all the architecture. Trunks grow close enough to resemble bundled columns, joined overhead by branches that have grafted themselves together. Curtains of pale roots hang from these unions, thickening with age until they reach the wet floor and become new supports. The resulting chambers are low, irregular, and densely furnished: fern shelves; mossy buttresses; stalks like clustered pipes; succulent bladders full of amber fluid; ladders of shelf fungi, though even these seem partly botanical, green-veined and photosensitive.

There is little bare mineral matter. Stone appears only in small exposures, rounded and dark, sweating steadily beneath coats of liverwort. In one corner of the basin, a low ridge of ancient concrete rises out of the roots. It has weathered into a reef. Its aggregate shows through in places like blunt teeth, but most of it is enclosed in woody tissue. Roots entered hairline cracks decades ago and widened them just enough to inhabit them, not enough to destroy the structure. The plant mass seems to preserve what it occupies.

The ridge was once part of an industrial installation. Its original purpose is difficult to read. Three squat pylons remain, each wrapped so densely in vines that they look like topiary towers. Between them runs a corroded rail or pipe, waist-high, vanishing into a wall of leaves. Ceramic insulators protrude from bark. A rusted control cabinet stands with its door open, packed solid with fine roots arranged in unnervingly parallel bundles.

Some of the machinery still operates.

It does not run continuously. At intervals too long to anticipate, something beneath the basin engages with a deep mechanical concussion. The root floor lifts by a finger’s breadth. Water wells upward through a thousand pores. Far below, flywheels or turbines begin to turn, and their vibration travels through the living mat until every leaf edge chatters.

This is the loudness of the place: not constant violence, but immense sound trapped in a small, padded world. Pumps thud underground. Water knocks in hidden pipes. Seedpods burst like snapped boards. Broad leaves shed accumulated rain all at once with the rush of overturned gravel. When the buried engines wake, resonant stems amplify selected frequencies. One grove drones at a low pitch; another gives off a dry metallic tremolo. The entire basin becomes an instrument played from beneath.

Yet the machinery is slow. Each cycle lasts hours. Pressure builds almost imperceptibly. A channel swells. Root valves open. The dark water rises through vascular forests and floods the shallow cups overhead. Then the system subsides, dripping and breathing, for days or weeks. No one appears to control it. The machinery and the plants have reached an arrangement older than memory.

In places the arrangement is plainly intimate. Copper cables have been stripped of insulation by growth, but instead of corroding away they are enclosed in translucent stems. Weak current passes through them. At dusk, clusters of leaves rotate toward buried junction boxes rather than toward the sky. Their veins brighten faintly when relays close underground. Moss around the pylons grows in precise rectangular fields, each patch differing slightly in color, as though the old switching logic has become a garden plan.

One machine rises above the vegetation near the center: a cylindrical housing perhaps three meters across, green-black with age. The plants have not concealed it. They form a circular wall around it, dense but carefully withdrawn by the width of a hand. Warm condensation coats the metal. From its top emerges a slow-turning shaft carrying no wheel, only a crown of vines braided around it.

The shaft makes one revolution in roughly a day.

As it turns, the braided vines are drawn upward, then descend on the opposite side. Their leaves pass through changing bands of light beneath the canopy. Flowers open as they rise and close as they fall. Nectar runs down grooves in the shaft and into the housing, where it vanishes through old lubrication ports. In return, the cylinder releases heat and a trace of mineral-rich vapor. Its surfaces support orchids found nowhere else in the basin: small, colorless blooms shaped like valve handles.

There are almost no animals. Now and then a tiny gnat emerges from standing water and is immediately caught by adhesive hairs. Pale worms may live in the submerged root felt, though they are seen only as disturbances. Once in several days, a narrow-backed bird passes over the basin without landing. Its call is startling because it is the only sound here that does not belong to water, plant tension, or machinery.

Pollination occurs without much fauna. Some flowers exhale clouds of dust when the ground vibrates. Others connect directly, extending temporary filaments between neighboring blooms. Seeds travel through pipes. Certain pods drop into black channels and are carried below, returning months later through overflow grilles in another part of the basin. The old factory’s distribution network has become the ecology’s circulatory system.

The dense growth imposes rules on movement. Nothing may pass quickly. Thorns are uncommon, but every surface catches: hooked hairs, coiling tendrils, soft stems that lean into newly opened space. Push through a curtain of leaves and it closes behind you, not dramatically, but with steady elastic insistence. Stand still for an hour and exploratory threads begin to rest on your clothing. Remain overnight and the threads branch.

Cuts in the vegetation do not stay open. Within minutes, nearby leaves alter their angle to occupy the new light. Within days, runners bridge the gap. Within a season, woody arches mark the wound, thicker and more elaborate than what was there before. The basin remembers disturbance as added structure. Its oldest scars are its densest places.

There are human traces, but few. A narrow metal platform remains beside one pylon, its grating filled with peat. A row of enamel warning signs has been swallowed almost to the lettering. Their visible fragments read:

**KEEP—**

**PRESS—**

**LIVING—**

Whether the last word was part of the original warning is impossible to determine.

The atmosphere carries crushed-green odors, warm iron, wet concrete, and a sweetness close to fermentation. Nothing is rotten in the ordinary sense. Dead vegetation is immediately enclosed, fermented, dissolved, and recirculated. Fallen trunks become elevated beds for new growth before they reach the floor. Even rust seems metabolized: orange stains spread into root tips, giving them a metallic red sheen.

Light arrives normally but seldom directly. The basin has a ceiling of leaves at many heights, so daylight is broken into overlapping green rooms. Reflections from wet surfaces send dim brightness upward. The result is not dark so much as directionless. Shadows blur. Noon can be recognized by warmth, not by the angle of illumination.

Along the perimeter, the land rises only slightly. A person could leave in minutes if a path remained open, yet the place resists the idea of an edge. Growth leans inward from the boundary. Drainage returns inward. Old pipes curve back beneath the basin. Even wind entering through the outer trees is divided, slowed, and turned until it circulates among the chambers.

At the lowest point is a pool the size of a room. Its surface is entirely covered by coin-shaped leaves, packed edge to edge. Beneath them, warm water descends farther than the basin’s shallow geography should permit. When the hidden engines begin their cycle, the leaves separate in a widening spiral. Not abruptly; the pattern may take an hour to form. At its center appears a circular darkness, and from that opening comes the strongest sound in the landscape: a long intake, half hydraulic suction and half breath.

The surrounding trunks incline toward it by degrees too small to watch.

Then the pool closes. The buried machinery slows. Water resumes its soft, innumerable dripping. The leaves settle against one another, filling every fraction of open surface.

Nothing happens quickly here, but nothing is still. The factory continues to manufacture something—pressure, circulation, warmth, perhaps only conditions. The vegetation continues to occupy those conditions so completely that machine and habitat can no longer be separated. It is less an overgrown ruin than a mature collaboration: a small, saturated engine-room whose walls are alive, whose production is itself, and whose long-established purpose is to remain full.
