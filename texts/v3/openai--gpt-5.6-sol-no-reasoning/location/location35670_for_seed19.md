The tableland begins above the clouds and continues long after any reasonable mountain should have ended.

It is not a summit but an elevated country: a cold, mineral plain carried on the backs of cliffs several kilometers high. From its rim, one can see lower ranges drowned in pale atmosphere, their peaks appearing as dark islands. Inland, the plateau opens into basins, ridges, and shallow valleys so old that they no longer seem carved. Every slope has relaxed toward its final angle. Every loose stone has already rolled as far as it will ever roll.

There is no water here. Not hidden under frost, not sealed in the rock, not circulating in veins below. The pale material in the depressions is not snow but pulverized feldspar and saltless crystal, dry enough to run through a closed fist like smoke. The air has almost no scent. Exposed skin loses its oils and tightens; lips split without ever feeling thirsty. Clouds occasionally pass beneath the plateau, but none cross over it.

The ground is crowded.

At first it looks like a field of upright stones: millions of narrow forms packed nearly shoulder to shoulder, occupying the plateau from rim to rim. Some are the height of a finger, some taller than a person, and some rise in branching towers above the surrounding multitude. They are not plants. They are animals, or something sufficiently near animals that the distinction is useful.

Each is enclosed in a stiff shell of gray ceramic tissue, ribbed vertically and rooted by dozens of gripping feet. The shells take many forms—spires, folded fans, corkscrews, blunt columns, lattices—but all hold perfectly still. Openings along their sides reveal layers of dry membrane as thin as onion skin. These membranes gather traces of sulfur, carbon dust, metallic vapor, and the weak electrical charge carried by high-altitude wind. Their chemistry uses no liquid phase. Reactions move across crystalline surfaces, atom by atom, with the patience of geology.

They are alive very slowly.

A breath may take sixty years. A feeding limb may extend the width of a thumbnail during the lifetime of a shepherd. Reproduction is rarer. Once in several millennia, two adjacent creatures grow until their shell rims touch. At the point of contact, they exchange a grain of patterned mineral and then separate, though no eye could witness the movement. The offspring begins between them as a blemish on the stone.

Because every visible stage is motionless, the plateau seems abandoned. In truth it supports more animal bodies per acre than a tropical reef. They stand in ranks and tangles, old individuals supporting colonies of smaller ones in the grooves of their shells. Minute plate-creatures overlap like scales. Glassy mites remain poised in the joints. Coiled hunters wait with feeding needles held a hair’s breadth from prey they may not pierce for another ten thousand years.

Nothing appears to happen, yet the landscape is saturated with intentions.

Sound is correspondingly scarce. Wind does not whistle through the shells; their openings are shaped to cancel resonance. Footsteps are swallowed by the powder between their roots. If one presses an ear to a large column, there may be a single dry tick from somewhere deep inside it. The next tick will not come soon enough to confirm the first.

Sparse among this fauna grow black, leafless organisms that are more recognizably vegetation. They spread flat against sunward stone in branching stains, converting light into chains of brittle carbon. A few produce upright blades no thicker than charred grass. The animals shelter them, though whether deliberately or merely by their presence is unclear. Around the oldest towers, the black growth forms halos, and within those halos the mineral dust is arranged in concentric rings.

There are human marks here too.

Low shelters of stacked shell fragments stand in hollows between ridges. Their builders used no mortar and cut nothing that could be broken by hand. The walls curve inward beneath roofs of hide, felt, and overlapping ceramic plates gathered from dead—or apparently dead—creatures. Entrances are narrow so that a person must crawl through without touching the nearby fauna. Stone awls, bone levers, copper hooks, and braided cords hang from pegs. Metal is precious, less because ore is scarce than because fire behaves badly on the plateau. Flames flatten into disks and sometimes remain suspended after the fuel has been removed.

No settlement contains more than a few shelters. No path remains for long, although nothing erases it. A row of footprints simply becomes harder to distinguish from the untouched ground until, under close examination, the prints appear to have preceded the walker.

This is the first rule of the plateau: movement does not necessarily produce change.

The second is that distance depends upon stillness.

While walking, a nearby ridge may remain the same size for days. If the traveler stops, it will arrive gradually, not by approaching but by becoming the ridge beside which the traveler has always been standing. The inhabitants therefore navigate by pauses. They walk with eyes lowered, halt at intervals, and wait for the country to decide where they are. Their maps are not diagrams of space but sequences of postures: kneel facing the twin spires; stand between the three hollow animals; lie flat until the western cliff can be heard.

The third rule concerns the fauna: nothing living on the plateau may be counted.

Attempts always yield a stable number, but never the same stable number twice. A field containing perhaps ten thousand columns may be counted as forty-three, or nine million, or one. Each result will remain internally correct. Marked individuals recur elsewhere without disappearing from their original places. Two adjacent shells can share an interior. A tiny creature may contain, inside its aperture, a complete view of the plateau with the observer visible as a dark upright fleck.

The people who live here do not regard these things as supernatural. They treat them as practical hazards, like cold or cliffs. Children learn not to look into more than one shell opening at a time. Hunters—though hunting is chiefly an act of waiting—tie themselves to stone anchors before cutting edible membranes, because a wounded creature may alter which side of the horizon the hunter occupies. Tools are made asymmetrical so they cannot be mistaken for their own repetitions.

Food is obtained without obvious killing. Thin outer plates are shaved from certain animals using flakes of harder shell. The exposed material grows back over centuries, so every harvest is marked with a cairn. Black ground-plants are scraped and ground into bitter meal. The people also gather pale nodules excreted at the bases of the tallest forms. These contain fats solid enough to be carved. Nothing is boiled. Nothing is washed. Meals are crushed, warmed against the body, and chewed for a long time.

The cold is steady rather than severe. There are no sudden freezes, no thaw, no seasons. Day and night pass over the tableland in equal, measured bands. At noon the shells cast clean blue shadows. At night the stars are sharp, but several constellations can also be seen beneath one’s feet, embedded in glossy seams of rock. The sun rises where expected. It does not always set into the same depth.

Across the central plateau stands the densest region, called by its inhabitants the Assembly. Here the great shelled bodies touch, interlock, and rise in terraces. Narrow passages wind among them, though “wind” may be the wrong word: the passages have existed in precisely their present curves longer than the surrounding stone. Some chambers are formed entirely from the inner surfaces of animals that appear separate from outside. Their walls are warm to the hand. Primitive carvings cover them—hands, hooks, ladders, mountain profiles, figures with many vertical bodies—but mineral analysis suggests that several carvings are older than the species that made them.

At the Assembly’s center is an empty circle.

It is the only spacious place in the crowded country, a disk of bare dark rock nearly a kilometer across. No creature crosses its edge. No black vegetation stains it. Dust does not settle there. Scattered around its perimeter are small stone platforms, each fitted with a seat and a simple copper mirror.

From any seat, the mirror reflects the circle as densely occupied.

The reflected beings are unlike the plateau fauna. They are tall, soft, and bent under invisible weight. They fill the circle without touching. They do not move. They have faces—or places where faces have been carefully removed—and each is turned toward the seated observer.

The inhabitants visit these mirrors rarely. They bring no offerings. They ask no questions. They sit until they recognize one of the figures, then cover the copper with felt and return home by a route different from the one they took outward.

Nothing on the plateau changes, not in the ordinary sense. Cliffs do not retreat. Shells do not topple. Settlements do not grow into towns. Even extinction is difficult here: dead species remain present as complete populations, their last instant distributed across the landscape. The place has reached an equilibrium so deep that cause and result lie side by side like neighboring stones.

Yet it is not peaceful.

The overwhelming life of the plateau is waiting—not for weather, migration, hunger, or spring, but for the smallest possible permission to complete motions begun before the mountains rose. Every shell holds a fraction of an action. Every needle is almost piercing. Every mouth is almost closing. Every wing, where wings exist, is almost lifting into air.

Were time to advance by one true second, the plateau would become louder than any ocean.

But the second does not come.

The high country remains under its ordinary sun: cool, dry, immensely crowded, and perfectly still.
