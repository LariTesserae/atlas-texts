Beneath the city lies a made hollow known as the Potters’ Undercourt.

It is not very deep. In several places one can hear footsteps crossing the streets overhead, softened by packed clay, brick vaults, and generations of repair. The court occupies the space of a small hamlet: a close arrangement of chambers, alleys, tanks, stairs, ovens, animal pens, and columned rooms, all enclosed beneath one broad, low roof. Nothing here is raw earth. The floor is fired tile; the walls are brick, plaster, woven reed, or fitted stone brought down from elsewhere. Even what appears to be bedrock proves, on touch, to be dark masonry polished by hands and dampness.

The place was built without much geometry. Its oldest builders enlarged it room by room, and later inhabitants filled every useful interval. Houses lean against cisterns. Cisterns support walkways. Pens occupy the warm cavities behind ovens. Niches have been carved into columns already too thin, then shored with timber ribs, then wrapped with rope when the ribs split. Passages often narrow to the width of a shoulder before opening suddenly into crowded courts. Doors overlap. Roofs serve as floors. A stair may climb three steps to a sleeping shelf, descend beneath it into a storage pit, and continue through a hatch into another family’s goat stall.

The result is dense without being large. Almost everything is near something else: baskets against jars, cages above drains, ladders across arches, sleeping mats rolled between grain bins, clay lamps set into old ventilation mouths. The architecture has been worn into rounded, accommodating forms. Brick corners have softened beneath palms and shoulders. Stone thresholds sag smoothly in the center. Channels have deepened under centuries of trickling water. Repairs are rarely removed; they accumulate, so that each wall is a history of patches—red brick under yellow plaster, yellow plaster under limewash, limewash under soot, soot scored away and covered with mats.

Water comes intermittently from above.

After rain on the surface, it begins as a fine tapping in the ceiling conduits. Then droplets gather on copperless clay pipes, on the undersides of stone slabs, and along the waxed cords hung to guide condensation into bowls. For a few hours the Undercourt becomes wet and brilliant. Gutters chatter. Cistern throats gulp. Children place cups beneath leaks whose schedules they know by heart. Every stair becomes slick, and the plaster darkens in branching shapes.

The water does not remain. Most is caught in jars or shallow tiled reservoirs; the rest travels through ancient floor channels toward a sump at the lowest end of the court. By the second warm day, the channels are dry again except for black seams of moisture. Small pockets persist beneath grates and in the bottoms of disused amphorae. These hidden wet places sustain much of the life below.

Vegetation is sparse and carefully situated. Pale moss grows around the cistern lips. Thin ferns emerge from mortar where condensation is reliable, their fronds no larger than fingers. Households cultivate trays of bitter cress beneath light shafts, and strings of bean vines climb trellises made from basket ribs. In one central chamber, where a square opening admits several hours of daylight, an old fig tree grows from an enormous ceramic vessel. Its roots cannot enter soil, for there is none; instead they occupy a packed mixture of ash, kitchen scraps, pulverized brick, dung, hair, and decayed cloth. The tree is small, contorted, and treasured. Its leaves make a dry clapping sound whenever the ventilation draft rises.

Light is surprisingly ordinary. Numerous shafts reach the surface, widening below into plastered funnels that spread daylight across walls and floors. Mirrors of polished tin are unknown, but pale limewash and shallow bowls of water help carry the illumination farther. At midday the main rooms are clear and warm, with recognizable colors: rust-red tile, blue glaze, dun rope, greenish water. Only the deeper storage cells require wick lamps. The light shifts visibly as people overhead cover grilles, carts pass across them, or rainwater pools in their mouths.

The Undercourt is loud almost from waking to sleep.

Its hard surfaces multiply every impact. Pestles strike mortars in irregular volleys. Potters slap clay against tables. Wooden mallets drive pegs into swelling joints. Goats complain from their platforms; dogs answer from below; pigeons thunder through roof vents and settle cooing in the rafters. Waterwheels no higher than a person’s knee turn in temporary runoff, knocking as they lift cups into cisterns. Vendors call through connected rooms, and their voices arrive twice—once directly and once through the drains.

The animals are everywhere. Pigeons inhabit the upper vaults in such numbers that woven screens are stretched above cooking areas. Rats travel behind wall facings and sometimes spill into the open when a repair is begun. Lean cats sleep on kiln ledges, waking instantly at the scratch of claws. Geckos cling to limewashed arches. Beetles live in grain dust; pale crickets inhabit the drains; clouds of tiny flies rise from damp compost after rain. Families keep goats, rabbits, quail, and occasionally a soot-dark pig. No pen is isolated, so feed, dung, parasites, and predators circulate continually through the court.

This abundance gives the place its ceaseless activity. Pigeons drop seeds into cracks. Goats crop the resulting shoots. Beetles enter the fodder, geckos hunt the beetles, and cats hunt both geckos and rats. During wet hours, small translucent frogs emerge from the sumps and spread through the drainage channels. They vanish again as the place dries, retreating behind glazed tiles where no hand can reach. Their sudden choruses are used as a measure of how much water has entered the court.

Heat gathers slowly in the masonry. The air remains warm even at night, especially around the communal ovens, dye pots, and pottery kilns. Yet it is not uniformly stifling. Drafts descend the light shafts and travel along the floor, carrying smells in distinct bands: yeast and hot clay near the bakeries, ammonia by the pens, damp mineral air beside the cisterns, smoke and singed hair around the workshops. In the deepest rooms condensation cools the walls enough that sleepers press their backs against them.

Everything here is maintained by hand. There are no engines, pumps, or hidden mechanisms beyond levers, treadles, pulleys, counterweights, simple valves, and gravity. Water is lifted in clay cups on rope loops. Air is encouraged through shafts with woven fans. Broken drains are tested by pouring dyed water into them and watching which wall begins to bleed color. Builders judge settlement with plumb cords and shallow water dishes. Their tools are stone, wood, bone, ceramic, and a small number of precious iron blades worn nearly to their backs.

Because the place has existed so long, its decay has become part of its design. Walls are expected to bow. Channels are expected to deepen. A cracked vault is not replaced if it can be braced, stitched, filled, or given less weight to bear. Inhabitants can read the aging structure as others read weather. A dry hiss from a brick joint means dust shifting harmlessly. A repeated tick at dusk means a timber is cooling. Fresh white powder beneath an arch means the room must be emptied. The oldest families remember no original plan and do not believe there ever was one.

The court’s principal rule is that nothing may fall without being caught.

This applies to water, embers, refuse, tools, animals, and people. Nets hang under ladders. Trays sit beneath shelves. Roof grilles are rimmed with gutters. Kiln mouths have ash aprons. Even stories told to children emphasize catching: the ancestor who caught the first rain in her apron; the mason who caught a failing beam on his shoulder; the cat who caught a fever-carrying rat before it reached the nursery.

A second rule follows: every empty space invites a use, but some spaces must remain empty so the whole may breathe. Ventilation corridors are therefore marked with handprints in blue pigment. No baskets may be stored there, no mats hung across them, no cages suspended from their ceilings. Arguments over these passages are more serious than arguments over rooms. A blocked breathway can turn three chambers smoky, spoil grain, and send the pigeons into panic.

At the lowest end lies the Basin of Returns, a tiled chamber sunken below all the others. Every drain eventually reaches it. Most days it contains only damp sediment, lost buttons, pottery chips, bones, beads, and a dark congregation of insects. After heavy rain it fills waist-deep. People descend with sieves once the water settles, recovering whatever the court has misplaced since the last storm.

The basin is the strangest part of the Undercourt, though not dramatically so. Objects found there are often more worn than they should be. A spoon lost the previous week may emerge with its handle smooth as an heirloom. Freshly broken pottery comes back with edges rounded as if by years of water. No one considers this magic. They say only that the basin is old, and that everything entering it must share, briefly, in that age.

Then the water is bailed out, the frogs retreat, the sieves are hung to dry, and the ordinary uproar resumes: hammering, bleating, bargaining, wingbeats, grinding stones, children shouting through drains, and beneath it all the patient drip of the next stored rain moving through the wholly constructed dark.
