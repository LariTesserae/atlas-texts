At this altitude the land has no horizon.

It is all slope, darkness, and distance: mountains leaning behind mountains, their upper faces lost in a permanent ceiling of cloud. Valleys fall away without revealing their floors. Ridges emerge briefly as black-green backs beneath the mist, then sink again. There are no broad glaciers, no shining snowfields. The cold here is too dry for that, except when weather comes. Instead, frost occupies the land in thin, persistent forms—silver fur on leaves, white seams in rock, needles growing from the soil during the long dark hours.

The mountains are extremely high, but ancient in appearance. Their contours have settled. Cliffs have softened into immense, sloping walls; shattered stone has knitted itself under moss and roots. Where bare rock remains, it is dark, fine-grained, and faintly metallic, weathered into rounded ribs. Nothing looks recently broken. Even the ravines appear less cut than folded into place.

Vegetation covers almost everything that can hold it.

It is not tall vegetation. No trees rise above the slopes, and little interrupts the enormous volume of empty air. The lushness lies close to the ground: mats of black heather, velvet moss, cold ferns, creeping vines, lichens thick as hides. From a distance these merge into a single dark pelt. Up close, the pelt becomes a miniature forest of translucent stalks and waxy leaves. Some leaves are nearly colorless; others are green so deep they seem black until touched by light. Their undersides carry pale pores that open only during fog.

Water is scarce in quantity but almost always present as an event.

Cloud condenses against the vegetation. Drops assemble along leaf-edges and hang there, trembling, until a passing vibration releases them. They descend through layer after layer of foliage, so slowly that rain may continue beneath the plants long after the sky has cleared. Shallow tarns occupy depressions in the rock, each one no larger than a field and perfectly dark. Thin rivers appear after storms, run hard for an hour through established channels, and then withdraw underground. In the dry intervals those channels remain vivid with wet moss, tracing green veins down otherwise black slopes.

Much of the water moves inside the mountain.

It drips through fissures and gathers in deep chambers. Sometimes a whole hillside gives a low internal shudder as reservoirs shift from one hollow to another. Springs open for a season, feed an explosion of fern growth, then seal themselves with mineral foam. The foam hardens into pale shelves, porous and cold. New plants colonize them immediately.

There are no buildings, roads, bridges, cuttings, cairns, or discarded tools. No hand has arranged the stone.

Yet the region is industrial.

The industry belongs to the landscape itself.

Far beneath the ridges, natural engines operate in enormous cavities. Pressure accumulates where water meets heated mineral beds. Gases pass through labyrinths of stone. Metallic crystals grow in branching arrays, conducting charge between strata. When the pressure reaches a certain threshold, vents along the mountainsides begin to work.

They do not erupt dramatically. A seam opens. A piston-shaped column of basalt moves a fraction downward. Vapor is drawn in through hundreds of narrow apertures. Somewhere below, hidden turbines of mineral and ice begin a slow rotation.

Their output appears across the slopes.

Dim currents travel through fungal threads and metal-rich roots. The vegetation brightens, not enough to illuminate the land, but enough to disclose its organization: faint green circuits spreading over square miles, branching, joining, fading, restarting elsewhere. Moss around the vents grows in regular bands. Ferns unfold in sequence from valley floor to ridgecrest. In the tarns, submerged organisms align into spirals and turn slowly with the induced current.

Nothing has designed these systems, unless design is simply what a process begins to resemble after several million years.

The machines reproduce imperfectly. Mineral templates form inside warm faults. Crystal lattices direct the deposition of more complicated shapes. Failed components become scree; successful ones persist and enlarge, a fraction of a millimeter each year. Some mountains contain mechanisms that ceased long ago and are now wholly overgrown. Others are young and restless, though “young” here means older than most seas.

The cold keeps everything slow until a cycle begins.

Then the slopes become intensely active.

A low-pressure front presses against the range. Fog thickens. Condensation streams from every leaf. Temporary rivers wake. The added water enters the mountain’s intake fissures, and the deep engines quicken. Electrical discharge passes soundlessly through the roots. Flowers open all at once beneath the moss canopy—small, lidless cups holding a blue-black sheen.

Animals emerge to meet them.

There are more animals here than the emptiness first suggests. They remain concealed because nearly all are low, soft, dark, and still. Pale grazing creatures move under the vegetation rather than over it, pushing tunnels through moss with wedge-shaped skulls. Their bodies are warm enough to leave winding paths of steam. Six-legged browsers cling to vertical faces, scraping lichen with rows of glassy teeth. Flocks of thin-winged animals roost inside cracks and launch only when the mountain’s charge is rising. They do not flap. They spread conductive membranes and are lifted by the electric field, drifting over the valleys like scraps of torn night.

Predators follow the cycles too.

Long-bodied hunters wait beside temporary streams for the water to drive burrowing prey into the open. They have no eyes; the flashes traveling through the root network are brighter to their buried sensory organs than daylight would be. Larger creatures patrol the high saddles in silence. Their feet divide into broad fringes that distribute their weight over moss and frost. From far away they might be mistaken for moving patches of darkness, except that the plants behind them remain folded for several minutes.

Life here rarely calls out. Sound carries too far across the empty valleys, and nearly every animal can detect vibration through stone.

The quiet is therefore crowded with tiny noises: condensation tapping leaf onto leaf, the dry contraction of frost, soft mouths cropping moss, claws settling carefully on rock. Wings make no audible beat. Rivers, when present, run beneath mats of vegetation and produce only a muted rushing, like breath heard through a wall.

The loudest noise occurs perhaps twice in a season.

One of the internal mechanisms disengages. A basalt weight drops through the mountain. The entire range gives a single deep note, too low at first to be heard. Tarn surfaces corrugate. Animals freeze. Loose frost lifts from the ridges in pale clouds. Several seconds later comes the audible portion: a subdued iron toll passing from peak to peak, dwindling into distances the eye cannot measure.

Afterward, every living thing moves.

Grazers pour from the ground. Flyers rise by the thousands, though the sky remains so large that they look sparse. Predators descend from saddles. Spores burst from the moss and are swept along newly generated electrical gradients. Streams shift course as interior gates open and close. A hillside may drain completely while another begins to weep from every fissure.

Still, there is no sense of disorder. The erosion is old and balanced. Talus falls into hollows where root mats are waiting to bind it. Streams scour only channels prepared by earlier streams. Dead animals are covered within hours by heat-seeking moss. Exposed machine-stone oxidizes, feeds lichen, and slowly returns to the mountain’s outer skin.

Day, if it exists, arrives only as a thinning of black into charcoal. The cloud ceiling gives off a diffuse gray pressure. No sun is visible. At night the darkness becomes dimensional: near darkness in the valleys, deep darkness in the sky, and the dense mineral darkness of the mountains between them. The faint living circuits are easiest to see then. They reveal slopes that seemed empty and show, for a moment, how extensively the range is connected.

A green line climbs a ridge.

Another answers from beyond it.

Then another, impossibly far away.

The pattern is not a message. It is the mountain range regulating itself—moving water, redistributing charge, opening flowers, waking predators, cooling its deep chambers. But the intervals are irregular in a way that almost suggests attention. A current may pause at the edge of a valley. The airborne creatures gather above it. Moss closes its pores. Even the hidden rivers seem to wait.

Then the current crosses, and the entire valley quietly begins.
