The basin is scarcely wider than a village, though no village stands there now. It lies low among hills so thoroughly worn down that they resemble folds in damp cloth rather than mountains. Their stone has passed beyond cliffs and rubble into a final, patient smoothness: broad backs of dark mineral, polished by ages of warm rain. Water shows everywhere without gathering decisively into a river. It films the rock, trembles in shallow depressions, and wells soundlessly through pale sand. Each footstep would fill with clear water and then, after a moment, erase itself.

The day is bright but without a visible sun. Light condenses in the air itself, silver-white and faintly green, making every wet surface luminous. The warmth is deep and even. Nothing bakes; nothing cools. Mist forms beneath leaves, under stones, and along the undersides of ruined structures, but rises only a few inches before becoming invisible again.

At the center is a marsh no deeper than a handspan. Its floor can be seen through tea-colored water: old black rock, honeycomb-pitted and crossed by seams of blue clay. Scattered islands support waist-high vegetation. There are fans of waxy leaves, copper reeds, low ferns furred with droplets, and flowering stalks whose translucent petals concentrate the ambient brightness until they seem to contain tiny lamps. The growth is lush where it occurs, but it does not close ranks. Between each thicket is open water or bare stone. The basin has the spaciousness of a carefully arranged garden, though nothing in it appears cultivated.

Life is overwhelming here, but seldom visible as an individual animal.

The shallow water is stippled by countless pinprick disturbances. Clear larvae hinge through it. Threads recoil into burrows. Minute crustaceans graze the submerged stone in such numbers that whole surfaces seem to breathe. Pale fish, each no longer than a finger joint, turn together beneath patches of reflected sky. When they change direction, the marsh flashes once and becomes dark again.

Above the water, insects fill nearly every available course through the air. They do not make the expected drone. Their wings are soft membranes, beating below the threshold of ordinary hearing, and the basin remains almost solemnly quiet despite their abundance. Clouds of them gather around the flowering lamps: blue needles, slow golden discs, insects like flecks of soot, and bead-bodied creatures borne on four transparent vanes. Occasionally an entire cloud rearranges itself into a hollow geometric shape—a sphere, a spiral, a many-sided chamber—and holds that configuration for several breaths before loosening.

Larger animals are present mostly as interruption. A reed bows although there is no wind. Something broad passes beneath the mud and leaves a line of bubbles. A six-legged wader, mottled exactly like the old rock, unfolds from what seemed to be an empty ledge. Its narrow head carries no visible eyes. It places one foot in the marsh and waits. Small lives converge on the disturbance rather than flee it. The wader drinks them through pores in its ankle, then folds itself back into stillness.

Predation here is nearly noiseless.

Along the basin’s northern rim stand three small structures, distant from one another. They might once have been shelters, instruments, or shrines. Each is a hoop of dull white material sunk halfway into the softened stone. No joints are visible. Rain and mineral deposits have covered portions of them, yet the exposed surfaces remain unmarked—not clean, exactly, but immune to the distinction between clean and weathered.

Inside the nearest hoop, droplets do not fall correctly. Condensation gathers on the upper curve, releases, and travels downward for a finger’s breadth. Then it slows, drifts sideways, and joins a rotating necklace of water suspended in the empty center. Tiny insects enter this ring and emerge hours later from another hoop at the far edge of the basin, sometimes carrying pollen from plants that grow nowhere nearby.

The structures are not dead. They have simply become ecological facts.

Roots thread through their fields. Mosses exploit slight differences in duration around them. One species of fern opens its fronds only in the moment before a displaced insect appears. Small amphibious animals lay eggs along the hoop’s inner edge, where the passing distortion prevents fungi from maturing. No creature understands the mechanism, but many have incorporated it into instinct. Migration routes bend through the basin toward these impossible shortcuts. The profusion of fauna may be partly due to them: lives continually arriving from adjacent places, seasons, or versions of the marsh.

Near the western edge, a fourth structure has collapsed—or perhaps has completed some long operation. It resembles a low scatter of black petals, each thinner than a leaf and hard enough to resist the ancient erosion that has flattened everything else. They lie in a near-perfect circle twenty paces across. Between them, vegetation grows sparsely. If a shadow crosses the circle, the petals tilt by fractions and return a little of the shadow’s darkness to the bright air. Over centuries they have accumulated a dusk of their own. At noon, the space above them is dim enough for nocturnal moths.

There are faint signs that persons once visited. A line of shallow sockets crosses a rock shelf, too regular to be natural but too worn to reveal its purpose. Beneath an overhang rests a vessel made of porous gray ceramic. It is full to the rim even in dry intervals, fed not by a spring but by condensation drawn from several yards of air. The vessel has been repaired with a band of material like transparent bone. An animal has built a nest inside it, weaving hair-fine reeds around the perpetual bead of water at its center.

No path remains. In this climate paths heal quickly, and the basin is too old to retain intentions that were not renewed.

Warm rain arrives several times each day. It begins not with clouds but with a brightening of the air. Leaves turn their pale undersides upward. Insects settle simultaneously, covering stems and stones so densely that the landscape briefly acquires a second, jeweled skin. Then large drops descend through clear light. They strike the marsh without much sound. Each impact raises a crown; each crown catches brightness; for a few minutes the basin is full of small glass architectures appearing and vanishing.

The rain activates other processes. Blue seams in the rock exhale mineral foam. Closed flowers open underwater. The buried animal beneath the mud turns in its sleep or its hunt. Hairlike organisms rise from pores in the stone and comb dissolved metals from the fresh runoff. The hoops brighten imperceptibly. Somewhere, very far away or very near in another geometry, thunder rolls once. Here it is felt as a pressure behind the teeth.

Afterward, water drains through channels too shallow to deserve the name. It passes from pool to pool, pausing at polished lips of stone, and finally disappears under the southern shelf. The flow is enough to move seeds, eggs, fragments of shell, and colonies of organisms that mimic scraps of green tissue. It is not enough to carve the basin any further. The land seems to have reached an agreement with erosion. Every surface has already yielded all the shape it can spare.

Toward evening—though the ambient light barely diminishes—the animals exchange places. Day insects settle under leaves. Other multitudes emerge from the water and from seams in the stone. Soft-bodied gliders sail inches above the marsh. Thumb-sized grazers peel luminous films from rock. Long, nearly weightless things move between the vegetation on legs as fine as fungus threads. Their passage makes no more sound than cooling metal.

Only occasionally does the quiet break. A clear click passes around the basin, repeated by no visible source. The wader snaps shut around prey. A fruit bursts and scatters seeds across the water with a patter like brief applause. Then stillness returns, though the stillness is deceptive: millions of bodies feeding, breeding, molting, filtering, hunting, dissolving, and rebuilding themselves in the bright wet air.

The basin feels neither wild nor designed. It is too old for that division. Ancient geology, abandoned engineering, and biological appetite have worn one another smooth. The machines have become weather. The ruins reproduce effects rather than memories. Animals migrate through impossible doors without surprise. Water obeys gravity except where local custom says otherwise.

Nothing dominates, despite the profusion of life. Every presence is small, dispersed, and temporary. The enduring thing is the basin itself: warm, shallow, radiant, nearly level, receiving rain after rain long after its stones have forgotten how to become any smoother.
