The city stands on the crown of a mountain so old that weather has forgotten how to reduce it further. There are no peaks here, only a high, broad table of pale mineral extending beneath a dim violet sky. Its edges descend in immense, rounded steps toward obscured lowlands. Every exposed surface has the same exhausted smoothness: bedrock worn nearly flat, saltless dust lodged in shallow basins, stones reduced to dark coins embedded in the plateau.

There is no water. None in the air, none underground, none bound secretly in sap. Frost never forms. Clouds do not gather. The atmosphere is thin, cool, and chemically dry, carrying fluorine traces and long chains of carbon dust. Human lungs, if brought here unaltered, would stiffen within minutes.

Yet the plateau is almost entirely plant.

It is not green.

The indigenous vegetation belongs to a chemistry that never learned thirst. It grows from silicates, carbon, atmospheric gases, and the slow electrical difference between sky and stone. Across the open land lies a living substrate called the pall: a gray-black membrane spread directly over the bedrock, usually no thicker than cloth. Its underside secretes molecular wedges into ancient crystal. Its upper surface is matte, softly furred, and cold enough to numb a hand through a glove. By day it drinks weak ultraviolet light. By night it opens millions of electrostatic pores and feeds on charged dust descending from the upper atmosphere.

The pall is continuous for hundreds of kilometers, but it does not make the landscape feel crowded. Its life lies flat. It resembles shadow more than growth.

From it rise sparse, enormous structures.

Some are needle groves: hollow mineral stalks a hundred meters tall, widely separated and almost perfectly straight. They taper to conductive hairs that comb charge from the sky. Others are low wheel-plants, radial organisms with bronze blades laid across the ground like dismantled turbines. Their roots are not buried but travel visibly over the pall in fine red lines, sometimes for kilometers, seeking faults where the mountain exhales useful gases.

The largest organisms have become buildings, or perhaps buildings have become the largest organisms. In the central district, the plateau bears a loose city of cathedral-like trunks. Each trunk stands alone amid broad plazas of living membrane, with several hundred meters between it and its neighbors. Their fluted walls are charcoal, ivory, or dull metallic blue. Balconies emerge as bracket fungi. Apertures tighten against dust and dilate when approached. Interior chambers are grown around load-bearing veins that pulse once or twice an hour.

No mortar is used. No part is assembled in the old sense.

The city’s inhabitants persuaded the local plant lineages to grow concepts: shelter, conduit, archive, transit, boundary. Over generations, those concepts crossed back into the wild ecology. Now a young needle plant may produce an unnecessary doorway. Root systems arrange themselves into grids where no district was planned. Empty towers develop stairwells spiraling around chambers that will never be occupied.

The place is urban without being crowded. It has avenues, plazas, civic centers, factories, observatories, and transit stations, but these stand far apart across the open plateau. Distance is part of the architecture. A public square may be three kilometers wide and contain only one singing mast, six low benches grown from the pall, and a black line marking the route to the next district.

The roads are strips where the living ground has folded itself inward, exposing glassy mineral beneath. Vehicles do not roll on them. They are drawn along controlled slopes in space, falling sideways from one district to another. A traveler waiting at a station sees no rails and hears no engine—only the sudden pressure of an arriving field. Dust jumps. Needle groves bend despite the windless air. Then a long vessel appears several centimeters above the road, dull as bone, carrying passengers inside chambers of altered gravity.

Its passing is nearly silent.

Everything else is loud.

The plateau’s plants converse through pressure and resonance. Charge-harvesting stalks boom as they equalize with the atmosphere. Their hollow bodies produce notes too low to hear cleanly, felt instead in the ribs and teeth. Wheel-plants rattle their blades to shake away insulating dust. Root cables snap taut beneath the pall with sounds like distant artillery. At irregular intervals, whole districts join in a cascading discharge: first a dry hiss underfoot, then ten thousand sharp reports racing toward the horizon, and finally the cathedral trunks sounding their deep, sustained chords.

The city is never acoustically still. Even at its quietest there is scraping, clicking, wing-thrum, subsonic organ music, and the granular rush of airborne mineral. A visitor may initially mistake this for weather. In time it becomes clear that nearly every sound has a sender and, often, a listener.

The fauna is abundant but seldom conspicuous. Water-based bodies would fail here, so the animals are dry articulations: lacquered joints, woven carbon muscle, ceramic shells, bladders filled with reactive gas. Vast numbers live inside the pall, grazing on old conductive fibers and pruning infections from its pores. When they move beneath it, the ground dimples and ripples as though something were swimming under taut cloth.

Above the surface travel the clatterbacks, six- or ten-legged herbivores with narrow bodies and tall resonant shells. They strike their limbs against hollow plants as they pass, identifying edible growth by the returning note. Flocks of foil-winged grazers circle the high towers, flashing briefly in the weak light. Their wings are sheets only molecules thick; rather than beating the air, they alternately accept and reject electrical charge, climbing the planet’s field in angular bursts.

Predators follow.

The most common are pale tripods that remain motionless for days among architectural roots. They do not chase. They counterfeit the electrical signature of a healthy feeding patch, then close their three limbs around whatever lands. Larger predators hunt through sound. Known locally as hushes, they spread broad insulating sails over sections of vegetation, damping the plants’ communication. Animals panic at the sudden silence and flee directly into waiting mouth-nets.

Thus quiet is the most alarming event on the plateau.

The light comes from a distant sun, small and copper-colored, and from the city itself. Day resembles a terrestrial dusk. Shadows are present but indecisive. The pall absorbs almost everything, making the open ground look bottomless until one notices its fine silver hairs leaning toward nearby electrical fields.

After sunset, the built trunks reveal their vascular glow. Thin lines climb their walls in amber, blue, and livid red. These are not lamps but metabolic surpluses routed visibly through the organism. Windows brighten only when occupied. Archives glow from deep inside like banked furnaces. Along the roads, directional markings crawl slowly across the ground, rearranging themselves according to traffic, migration, and the intentions inferred from approaching travelers.

Nothing here is entirely inert. Stone has been colonized down to its defects. Dust carries dormant cells and computation. The atmosphere contains microscopic seed-machines that evaluate where they land before deciding whether to become a plant, a tool, a message, or nothing at all.

The city’s advanced systems are difficult to distinguish from ecology because the distinction has been deliberately abandoned. Computation happens in branching tissues spread over whole watersheds—or what would be watersheds on a wetter world. Civic decisions are passed as hormonal gradients. Buildings dream through long cool nights, modeling future configurations in the branching growth of disposable inner fronds. Repairs arrive as pale animalcules that eat damaged matter, argue chemically over the original design, and die as the replacement structure hardens around them.

A buried network manipulates probability at very small scales. It does not create miracles so much as cultivate unlikely outcomes. Cracks tend to stop before severing important conduits. Useful mutations occur slightly too often. Falling objects favor empty ground. Travelers who become lost repeatedly choose paths that lead toward inhabited districts, though no sign directs them.

The inhabitants say the mountain is lucky.

Older records suggest luck is municipal infrastructure.

The plateau’s deep age is visible beneath all this restless life. The city is young only in its current arrangement. Under the pall lie successive urban anatomies pressed almost flat: transit loops belonging to vanished body plans, foundations grown for gravitational conditions that no longer exist, fossilized neighborhoods whose chambers are too narrow for any modern citizen. In places the living surface peels back, exposing polished layers of black architecture descending for kilometers into the mountain.

Erosion no longer works primarily from above. The air is too dry and the stone too settled. Instead, the plateau is eroded by life’s revisions. Plants dissolve obsolete walls molecule by molecule. New civic roots tunnel through old plazas. Archive organisms digest their own least-requested memories to make room for fresh ones. The city wears itself down through use, forgetting and rebuilding without rubble.

At the outer edge, where the tableland begins its immense descent, stand the oldest structures: a sparse procession of leafless towers leaning over the drop. Their surfaces have been polished by a billion years of charged dust. No openings remain. No detectable metabolism passes through them. Yet every few nights they turn, by less than the width of an atom, toward something below the horizon.

When they move, every resonant plant on the plateau falls into the same note.

Animals stop. Transit fields suspend their vessels in place. The pall tightens over the stone. For several minutes the whole high country vibrates with a roar so large it ceases to seem loud and becomes a property of matter itself.

Then the towers settle.

The clatterbacks resume their routes. Predators unfold. Road markings continue their slow migrations. The city’s windows brighten one after another beneath the cold, dry stars.

Across the ancient mountaintop, the dark vegetation goes on feeding from stone and sky, filling nearly every surface while leaving the world strangely open: a forest reduced to circuitry, a metropolis planted at immense intervals, a living wilderness that has never known rain and has no word for drought.
