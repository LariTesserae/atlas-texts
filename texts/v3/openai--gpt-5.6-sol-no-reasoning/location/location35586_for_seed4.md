The city exists inside a hedge.

From outside, the hedge appears scarcely different from the other black thickets scattered across the low marsh: a mound of thorn and winter leaf, perhaps twelve paces long, bowed under beads of cold water. No smoke rises from it. No path leads in. The surrounding ground is young and poorly settled—a skin of dark silt laid recently over older reeds, trembling underfoot and split here and there by clean-edged channels. Rain gathers in those cuts before the mud has learned to slump. Thin ice forms over them at night and breaks into bright, sharp scales whenever the water beneath rises.

One must kneel to find the entrance.

It lies under an arch of living roots, behind a curtain of oval leaves. Beyond it, the hedge opens unexpectedly into chambers, alleys, bridges, wells, pens, and stacked dwellings. Nothing is much taller than a hand. The whole settlement fits into the hollow where several ancient shrubs have braided themselves around a stone.

The inhabitants call it a city, and by their scale it is one.

Its streets are the pale ribs of leaves. Its plazas are patches of bark worn smooth by innumerable feet. Houses have been cut—not killed, but persuaded—into the pithy walls of stems. Their doors are seed-coats hinged with spider silk. Their roofs are layered scales of fungus, bark, and waxed leaf, steeply pitched to shed condensation. Elevated walkways run along thorn branches, crossing above black drops of open air. Ladders made from grass awns connect one level to another. At the lowest level, where water gleams between roots, reed rafts are tethered to moss bollards.

The city is urban not because it is large, but because almost every usable surface has been considered.

Rain is collected in curled petals and carried through grooves gnawed into wood. Sap is diverted by tiny clay valves. Ventilation shafts rise through dead twigs. Public granaries occupy dried galls, their interiors divided by walls of chewed fiber. A row of bladderwort traps, carefully disabled and reinforced, serves as cisterns. There are marketplaces in the forks of branches, watch platforms on thorns, burial gardens under translucent patches of lichen, and long communal halls beneath strips of bark lifted without severing them from the plant.

Yet the hedge remains mostly empty.

Distances between structures are large to those who live here. A bridge may stretch over an abyss of six finger-widths. A single unoccupied leaf becomes a green common, wet and windswept. Whole districts stand on separate stems, divided by darkness. At night—nearly all the time in this deep interior—only a few lamps show: foxfire sealed in snail-shell fragments, glowworm light coaxed through woven screens, or a pinprick of cold flame from a dish of marsh gas. They do not dispel the dark. They make it architectural.

The cold enters everywhere.

It condenses on ceilings and runs down the inner walls of houses. It stiffens ropes. It pearls along the hairs of leaves until each chamber seems roofed in glass. Breath hangs low. Residents wrap themselves in seed-down, moth scales, and felted plant fiber. Metal is almost unknown; a single rusted needle, found in the marsh generations ago, has been broken into an awl, two knives, and several sacred splinters. Most tools are mandible-shaped flakes of flint, fish bone, thorn, shell, and hardened wood. Construction proceeds by cutting, tying, wedging, grafting, and patiently directing growth.

The hedge does much of the building.

New rooms are begun by pinching a bud at the proper season. A wall is grown by training two shoots together. Doors take weeks to form. Bridges are roots encouraged across gaps by wet wool and darkness. When a house becomes unsafe, it is not demolished; light is admitted, and the plant absorbs it into another shape.

Thus the city is never finished. It thickens, closes, buds, and reroutes itself. Maps are seasonal documents. A lane open in autumn may be sealed by spring tissue. A square may become a flower. A nursery may split overnight when a stem swells after rain. The oldest law forbids cutting any green vein before asking where its water goes.

Plants fill the place so completely that “ground” is largely a foreign idea. There is wood beneath wood, moss upon bark, algae in collected water, liverworts spreading over the algae, and pale fungi threading through all of it. Roots pass through houses. Leaves serve as weather, terrain, roof, farm, and calendar. The citizens cultivate miniature orchards of lichen, graze molds from the shaded bark, tap sweet sap, harvest pollen, and store frost-killed aphids in wax-lined pits.

The flora, however, is only the city’s body.

Its motion comes from animals.

They are everywhere, in numbers too great to perceive at once. Mites crowd the moss like red cattle. Springtails gather in damp courtyards and erupt together when disturbed, a pale weather of jumping bodies. Aphid herds stand along tender shoots under the guard of ants. Midges sleep head-down beneath leaves, then wake in clouds during brief thaws. Snails move through lower districts with the grave force of landslides, polishing roads away with their passage. Beetles squeeze between buildings. Caterpillars arrive like slow, muscular trains and eat entire neighborhoods if not turned aside. Spiders occupy the upper spans, their webs closing avenues overnight.

The citizens themselves are difficult to distinguish from this profusion. Some say they are small, furred people. Others insist they are a kind of cricket without wings, dressed in woven husks and walking upright only within the city. At a distance one sees lamps carried in pairs, tools moving, doors opening, processions crossing bridges. Their exact forms remain obscure. In this place, personhood is less a shape than a set of agreements: one who tends a cistern, observes the truce-paths, pays seed to the granary, and sounds warning before a snail’s approach belongs to the city.

Many animals do not.

Predation occurs constantly but almost without noise. A spider trembles a line. A hunting larva closes its jaws. A shrew’s whiskered muzzle enters through the outer hedge, vast and warm, sniffing among the avenues. The city extinguishes every lamp. For several minutes the whole settlement hears only the soft, wet opening of nostrils. Then the muzzle withdraws, taking perhaps a dozen inhabitants and half a district’s aphid stock with it.

The response is practical. Bridges are restrung. The dead are named. Exposed chambers are grafted shut.

There are rules for creatures too powerful to fight. Snails must not be injured; their routes are marked with bitter sap and redirected. Ant roads are crossed only at designated places, after offerings have been set down. Spiders may inhabit the upper city if they leave three public spans clear. Wasps are given nothing and trusted nowhere. Birds are treated not as animals but as sudden changes in the sky.

Sound travels badly through the living walls. Moss drinks footsteps. Leaves soften rain. The loudest regular noises are the ticking fall of droplets, the faint working of jaws, and the creak of stems leaning under water. From below comes the occasional brittle sigh of ice fracturing in the marsh. From above, weather arrives as pressure before it arrives as sound.

Rain transforms the city.

Drops strike the outer leaves with deep, separate impacts. The hedge compresses. Water descends along established veins and accidental routes, swelling gutters, flooding low tunnels, setting rafts loose. Cistern crews climb out with thorn hooks. Gates of waxed bark are lowered. In the root harbor, black water rises until it touches the underside of the bridges.

Then the animals begin to move.

Worms push up through the silt. Beetles seek height. Ants carry their white young in disciplined columns. Springtails scatter over the water’s skin. Snails emerge from sealed hollows. Frogs approach from the marsh and sit outside the hedge like listening monuments. Their tongues enter without warning.

Afterward, the city shines.

Every hair holds a droplet. Every droplet contains a tiny inverted district: bridges below, dark above, lamps hanging like drowned stars. The temperature falls. Clear ice grows around the margins of pools and locks unwary creatures into place. By morning the inhabitants are abroad with bone chisels, cutting drinking windows and freeing ropes from frozen gutters.

The stone at the city’s center is newer than everything around it. It has a sharp face, pale and unweathered, thrust recently from the mud by frost. The hedge grew around it long ago, but only this winter did the stone rise enough to split the central roots and create the hollow in which the settlement now stands. The city is therefore ancient in custom and very young in geography. Its oldest buildings have been moved repeatedly. Its monuments are portable. Its foundations are temporary by principle.

Water seeps from one side of the stone without explanation. It does not flow from above and does not stop in hard freezes. The trickle is no wider than a thread, but it supplies the central well and keeps a patch of luminous algae alive. Some believe the stone contains a buried spring. Some believe it is a tooth belonging to an animal under the marsh. Tools placed against it overnight are sometimes found several body-lengths away, neatly arranged by size.

No one builds directly upon it.

Around the seep lies the quietest plaza in the city. Here, amid the continual eating and growing, the lamps are shaded. Hunters are forbidden. Even ants take a curving route around the stone, though no barrier blocks them. Condensation gathers on its sharp upper edge, trembles, and falls at long intervals into the well.

Each drop rings through the root chambers.

For a moment the hidden city listens: its cold houses, its leaf streets, its innumerable mouths. Then the silence closes again, green and black, while beyond the hedge the low marsh lies under rain, unaware that it contains walls, harbors, laws, famine, traffic, and a thousand lives moving between two thorns.
