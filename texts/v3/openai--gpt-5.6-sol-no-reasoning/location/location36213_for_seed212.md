The city grows in a warm basin where the ground has almost disappeared.

From the surrounding low ridges, it resembles an immense mat of greenery thrown over a shallow valley: bright crowns, dark seams, yellow-green plazas, towers braided from ascending vines. Only after watching for a while does its urban order become visible. Broad avenues divide the canopy into districts. Domes rise at regular intervals. Walls run for miles, turning at sharp corners. Bridges leap over channels of shadow. Yet there is no masonry, timber, metal, fired clay, or worked stone. Nothing here has been built by hands. The city is an arrangement the plants have entered into among themselves.

The avenues are made from roots.

They lie side by side in muscular bundles, pale where their surfaces are young and red-brown where heat has cured them. Thousands of smaller rootlets knit the gaps into a resilient pavement. At noon, the roads swell slightly, lifting in long waves as moisture moves beneath them. Intersections tighten and loosen. A traveler standing still would be carried slowly toward one district or another, not by choice or current, but by the rearrangement of the city’s circulation.

There are no animals to travel them.

No insects browse the leaves. No birds cross the open light. No worms loosen the deep tangles, and no unseen mouths consume what falls. Pollination occurs through collision, pressure, airborne dust, and the deliberate beating together of flowers. Seed dispersal is done by explosive pods or by the roads themselves. Decay is not entrusted to fungi or scavengers; dead tissue softens, liquefies, and is drawn back into neighboring roots. The whole basin smells intensely alive—green sap, pepper, hot cellulose, bruised flowers—with almost none of the sour undertone of rot.

The absence of fauna is not peaceful. It is the reason the city is so loud.

Every plant must perform its own weather.

Along the eastern wards, groves of hollow columns inhale through millions of leaf-slits. They draw the warm air inward until their chambers are full, then contract together. The discharge is a continuous roar, broad and physical, like surf heard from inside a cave. The western thickets answer with percussive cracks as seed cases burst. Vine bridges twang under changing tension. Trunks knock against trunks. Vast sheets of leaves turn edgewise and then flat again, producing detonations of applause that race across the basin.

Beneath everything is the infrasonic labor of growth: fibers tearing, roots levering themselves through packed soil, trunks winding tighter around empty cores. It is felt first in the ribs and teeth. Conversation, if there were anyone here to attempt it, would be useless.

Rain visits intermittently and seldom lasts long. The sky gathers white pressure in the hot afternoon; then a few abrupt curtains descend, drenching one neighborhood while the next remains dusty. Water rarely reaches mineral ground. It is caught at every level by cups, folded leaves, bark gutters, bladder-fruits, and suspended root nets. For hours afterward the city is jeweled with reservoirs. Clear ponds hang high overhead inside translucent leaves. Dark water collects in the hollows between roots. Condensation beads on the cool undersides of canopy vaults and falls in heavy, widely spaced drops.

By the next day much of it has been drunk, sealed away, or exhaled back into the air.

The largest waterway is not a river in the usual sense. It is a slow procession of connected basins formed by migrating plants. Great bowl-leaves overlap in a sinuous chain through the center of the city, passing water from one to another as their stalks tilt. The river may shift several streets sideways in a week. It sometimes divides around a newly risen grove and sometimes climbs. In places it flows over archways twenty meters above the ground, a suspended flood flashing between leaves, before descending through curtains of roots.

Its banks are crowded with drinking structures: needles, tongues, funnels, porous ropes, and fleshy towers that bend low at dawn. When the water passes, the whole waterfront leans after it. Buildings that once faced the river find themselves inland. Former plazas drown without warning. Dry districts crack open, releasing buried seeds that assemble new neighborhoods in a matter of hours.

Nothing here is old, though some organisms may be ancient.

The city continually presents fresh surfaces. Bark is smooth, bright, and thin. Thorns have clean translucent edges. New ridges buckle up through yesterday’s pavement without weathering or soil. Where a tower collapses, its trunk does not remain as a ruin. It separates into green ribs, each of which roots independently, making a radial suburb around the wound. There are no softened corners. Even the oldest districts are sharp because they endlessly shed their exteriors and begin again.

This gives the place an air of perpetual founding. It is always the first season after emergence.

The apparent buildings vary by quarter. Near the basin’s southern lip stand dense blocks of chambered succulents, their skins warm gray, violet, and chalky blue. Door-like openings appear along their bases at regular intervals. Inside are corridors, stairs, galleries, and high vaulted rooms, all molded from living flesh. But the entrances close at night, and the corridors branch differently each morning. A stair may lead upward until it gradually becomes the underside of a leaf. A room may narrow into a vein and carry anything inside it away as dissolved memory.

Toward the center, towers grow from braided stems around cylinders of open air. They rise quickly and sway in different directions at different heights, making the skyline appear to boil. Their upper stories are platforms of fan leaves, each bearing gardens of smaller plants. Those gardens support gardens in turn, tier upon tier, until scale becomes uncertain: a patch of moss may contain avenues and rotundas identical to those below, while the entire city may itself be growing in the shallow cup of some immeasurably larger leaf.

This possibility is difficult to dismiss because distance behaves badly in the basin.

A road seen continuing to the horizon may be reached in ten steps. A nearby tower may recede for an afternoon as one approaches it. Parallel boulevards cross without bending. Some courtyards have more perimeter than the neighborhoods surrounding them. Stand beneath certain arching roots and look upward: the sky appears below, luminous and blue, with the city’s canopy hanging from it upside down. Rain falls through these inversions in both directions, meeting in midair and spreading sideways as shining discs.

The plants do not merely occupy space; they negotiate and revise it.

Roots draw locations together when they graft. Vines sever adjacency by slipping between two surfaces. Flowers open into volumes larger than their buds and close without displacing what has entered. During periods of rapid growth, whole districts overlap. A grove of red poles may share the same ground with a field of waist-high silver cups, each fully solid, neither obstructing the other. When the overlap ends, one landscape is expelled—sometimes upward into the canopy, sometimes hours into the past.

That past is visible in flashes after rain.

Drops suspended from leaf tips show vanished versions of the city: broad fern plazas where thorn towers now stand; a flat green marsh beneath the elevated rootways; geometric orchards arranged around empty amphitheaters. Touching such a drop would make the reflected district momentarily real. The transformation seldom holds. The city rejects continuity as readily as it rejects emptiness.

Its order is undeniable nonetheless.

Plants of related function cluster together. Reservoir species line conduits. Resonant trunks assemble in long civic rows. Seed-bearing towers form defensive belts around tender nurseries. Canopies interlock into roofs above the wettest quarters. At regular intervals, enormous flowers open at street junctions, exposing radial interiors large enough to be public squares. Their petals serve as ramps and balconies. Their stamens swing like pendulums, dusting the surrounding district in luminous pollen.

There are no citizens in these spaces unless citizenship belongs to the structures themselves.

At sunrise, neighboring plants exchange pressure through root grafts. A signal passes across the basin: not electricity, not sound, but a sequence of altered tensions in living fibers. Streets constrict. Towers turn. Water is redirected. A district deprived of light is dismantled, its tissues separated and fed into the roads. Elsewhere, vacant air is enclosed by new walls. The city governs through tropism. Its laws are gradients—light, moisture, weight, crowding—and its arguments are conducted by growth.

Sometimes the arguments become violent.

Without warning the central canopy begins to rotate. Leaves align in spirals; vines pull taut; tower crowns sweep around a moving axis. The air becomes a green hurricane contained entirely beneath a calm blue sky. Root pavement heaves into ridges. Water bursts from storage bladders and travels horizontally in glittering sheets. Pod explosions roll outward like artillery. Whole facades flower at once, then strip themselves bare.

During these convulsions, forms emerge too quickly to name. A forest of ladders rises and folds into a single stalk. A plaza purses shut, becoming a fruit with all its streets inside. Miles of hedge lift as one articulated curtain and walk—not on legs, but on sequentially planted roots—across the basin. The noise becomes total. It ceases to resemble separate impacts and turns into a dense green continuum, as if the air itself were being torn and regrown thousands of times each second.

Then, just as abruptly, the motion stops.

Warm rain ticks against the arrested leaves. The sound thins to dripping, creaking, and the distant thunder of respiration. Sunlight enters the new gaps at an ordinary angle. Steam rises from bruised avenues. Fresh walls glisten. The river hangs in a place it did not occupy before. A tower slowly opens its hundreds of pale windows, each window a flower facing inward.

For a few minutes, the city appears complete.

But completion is only another unstable organ. Along the northern edge, the ground is already lifting. Fine cracks race through a plaza of interwoven roots, sharp and new. Green points press up through them in perfect rows. The rows become arches; the arches branch; the branches meet overhead. Beneath that rapidly forming arcade, depth accumulates where no depth existed.

Another district begins, loud with its own invention.
