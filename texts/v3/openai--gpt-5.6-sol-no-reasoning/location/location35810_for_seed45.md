The land is still arriving.

It rises in warm darkness as a congregation of unfinished mountains: immense black inclines thrust upward from a floor that has not yet decided whether it is a plain or a chasm. There are no valleys shaped by rivers, no alluvial fans, no softened shoulders. Every surface is primary—fresh fracture, pressure-glaze, blistered mineral, ledges sharp enough to seem conceptual. Peaks form faster than they can weather. Some stand for hours; others collapse before their bases have cooled.

There is no water here, not even hidden in the stone. The crust is composed of anhydrous salts, dark ceramics, sulfurous metals, and glassy compounds that would decompose in rain. In lower pockets, powder-fine mineral dust lies untouched by runoff, settling in pale fields between the upheavals. It resembles ash but has never burned. Underfoot it would be warm, dry, and almost weightless, slipping into fissures without clumping.

The region extends far beyond any single view. Darkness limits the eye, while elevation confuses distance. A nearby wall may rise several kilometers; a remote summit may appear close because nothing stands between it and the observer. There is almost no horizon. Instead, there are overlapping silhouettes—ridges behind escarpments behind narrow black spires—each briefly disclosed by the dull red light beneath the crust.

That light is the only general illumination. It leaks through seams as thin as scratches and through sudden rents wide enough to swallow plateaus. The glow is not the orange brilliance of open lava. It is deeper and less generous: iron-red, bruised violet, occasionally the color of heated bone. It shines upward without filling the air, catching the underside of overhangs and leaving the summits invisible.

The atmosphere is dense, dry, and warm. It contains no clouds in the earthly sense. Instead, heavy mineral vapors drift through the passes—sulfur chlorides, metallic fumes, carbon dust—moving as low, dim veils. They do not rain. Where they touch cooler high faces, they deposit thin crystalline skins: yellow-black scales, silver needles, translucent crusts that grow outward into the air. A slope can acquire a glittering rind overnight, only to shed it when the mountain flexes.

Nothing erodes in the familiar way. There has not been time.

Change comes from within. The terrain is buckled by deep pressures that move without rhythm. Whole ranges lift several meters in a slow, silent heave, then stop. New escarpments unzip across open ground. Towers of soft stone extrude from vents, fold under their own weight, and harden in contorted layers. At intervals, broad plates tilt like trapdoors, revealing incandescent depths before grinding shut again. The world does not yet possess stable drainage, stable strata, or even dependable gravity at the smallest scales: dust sometimes trembles uphill toward charged ridges, drawing fine gray streamers across the dark.

Despite this turbulence, the place is mostly quiet. Its greatest motions occur too slowly to hear. The prevailing sound is a faint granular hiss—the settling of dust, the growth of crystals, the movement of hot gas through microscopic pores. Then, without warning, the silence is broken by a report from beyond the visible ridges. The sound arrives immense and singular, like a slab of sky struck with a hammer. Echoes cross the high country for minutes. Afterward, small fragments patter down cliffs that did not exist before the noise began.

There is no vegetation. No roots enter the fractures; no lichen discolors the rock. There are no animals, no tracks, no remains of food or shelter. Even decay is absent, because nothing organic has lived here long enough to die.

Yet in a few of the warmest clefts, the mineral crusts show a doubtful kind of organization. Hexagonal films creep across sheltered surfaces, not feeding or reproducing exactly, but rearranging themselves in response to heat. When a fissure brightens, their facets turn toward it. When the ground cools, they collapse into inert black flakes. They may be chemistry on the verge of becoming appetite, or merely a repeated accident. The distinction has not yet become important.

Scattered through the range are structures.

They are too regular to pass entirely for geology and too crude to imply tools. Low rings of upright slabs occupy certain saddles, each stone fused to the ground at its base. Elsewhere, narrow stair-like terraces ascend cliff faces but terminate in blank stone. There are chambers beneath some ridges: smooth-sided, roofless hollows with three or five walls, as though architecture had begun before the idea of enclosure was complete.

No two structures are close enough to form a settlement. Miles of raw terrain lie between them. None contains inscriptions, fittings, hinges, worked edges, or movable objects. Their proportions are inconsistent—some suited to a body smaller than a hand, others to something tall enough to step between peaks. They may not have been built. The same forces raising the mountains may also be producing, rarely and without intention, shapes that resemble shelter.

One such form stands above the central salt-field: a tall, open frame made of porous black stone. It has two uprights and a lintel, though the lintel bows downward as if still soft. Beyond it is only another ridge. Seen from different elevations, however, the opening frames places that are not aligned with it: a red fissure beneath the plain, a distant crown of crystals, an unlit vertical depth. This effect may be perspective distorted by hot air. It may also be evidence that the high ground is folding in more than one direction.

The salt-field below is the emptiest part of the region. It stretches broad and pale among the mountains, perfectly dry, its surface divided into enormous irregular plates. New salt wells slowly upward along their boundaries. As it emerges, it makes delicate walls and hollow fins, some several stories high, all too young to have been carved. Warm gusts pass through them and produce a low breath—nearly a tone, though never steady enough to become music.

The field is not flat for long. Pressure waves travel beneath it, lifting one section after another. In the dimness, the pale surface seems to inhale. Plates rise, hinge apart, expose red seams, and subside at altered angles. Crystal fins topple and shatter. New ones begin growing before the fragments stop sliding.

There is weather, but no season. Dense atmospheric currents rush around the peaks, carrying heat from newly opened depths. They arrive as invisible fronts. Dust leaps from the ground. Vapors knot into rotating columns. Loose shards skate across plateaus and vanish over edges. Then the current moves on, and the disturbed particles hang for a long time in the heavy air, turning the subterranean glow into vague red halos.

Above all this is a sky with almost nothing in it. No moon, no stars visible through the dark atmospheric burden. Only rarely does the upper blackness pulse with a muted, sheetlike radiance. The highest summits answer by growing coronas of static: faint violet bristles along knife-edges, cold-looking over a warm world. The charge moves from peak to peak without thunder, sketching the architecture of the range and immediately erasing it.

The rules here are not hostile so much as incomplete. Forms precede purposes. Mountains rise before erosion knows how to name them. Chambers appear before inhabitants. Chemical patterns lean toward life, but life has not crossed the distance. The land is full of beginnings that may never continue.

Nothing watches the transformation. Nothing remembers the former shape of a ridge or mourns a collapsed plain. Still, the terrain carries a sense of expectation—not because some arrival is destined, but because every surface remains provisional.

In the warm dark, another range is pushing upward.

At first it is only a long pressure beneath the salt. Then the plates begin to tilt. A seam of red opens from one end of the field to the other, and fresh black stone, unweathered and gleaming, enters the world.
