Darkness here is not the absence of day. It is a material condition.

It occupies the basin from rim to rim: warm, wet, faintly pressurized, thick enough that distant objects disappear not by becoming smaller but by becoming less certain. Lamps do not illuminate it so much as bore short tunnels into it. A hand-lantern reveals ten or twelve paces of shining mud, black water, and mineral edges; beyond that, the darkness closes with the seamless density of poured pitch. Even lightning, when it comes, remains trapped inside the clouds and spreads through them as a dull violet bruising.

The basin is low and immense. It may once have been a shallow sea, or may be one now in an early, undecided stage. Its floor extends for many kilometers in barely perceptible terraces. Recent fractures cross it in long, sharp seams, their lips still angular and undecayed. Warm water wells through some of these cracks. Elsewhere, rain lies in broad sheets no deeper than an ankle, connected by narrow runnels that gleam only when disturbed.

Nothing has had time to become smooth.

Black shelves of newly exposed stone rise abruptly from the flats. Their faces are wet and glassy, with corners keen enough to split a boot. Cones of pale slag stand where subterranean vents have pushed up mineral foam. Ridges no taller than a person run in parallel for hundreds of meters, as if the ground hardened while wrinkled. Between them are warm marshes crowded with reeds—but the reeds grow only in isolated ranks, sparse as survey stakes. Most of the basin remains open: water, mud, raw rock, and low mats of translucent vegetation clinging close to the warmth.

Yet the place feels crowded.

Life fills every layer not occupied by plants. Small pale things pulse beneath the flooded surface. Black beetles gather by the thousands on the dry sides of stones, making those stones appear furred until the colony slowly changes position. Long-legged marsh animals stand motionless in the water, too numerous to count and too quiet to notice at first; one begins to distinguish them from the reeds only when their joints bend backward and their narrow bodies drift sideways without lifting their feet.

There are warm-water eels in the fissures, flat silver amphibians in the mud, moths with wings like damp paper, and low herds of six-legged grazers feeding on mineral films. They do not cry out. Even predation is subdued. A ripple approaches another ripple; one ceases. A shadow folds around a smaller shadow. The principal sounds remain the minute ticking of cooling stone, water dripping from unseen ledges, and, at long intervals, the hollow concussion of machinery somewhere under the plain.

The industry is scattered but regional, as though a single vast factory were buried beneath the landscape and only its necessary organs had broken the surface.

There are pumping houses on concrete islands, each no larger than a cottage. Their walls sweat constantly. Narrow pipes emerge from them, cross the marsh on low trestles, and sink again into mud. Some convey hot water; others shudder with an interior load that moves too slowly to be liquid. Rust has begun at the joints but has not spread far. The structures are recent, or perpetually recent. Their sharp casting seams, bright bolts, and unweathered foundations contrast with machinery designed in a style that suggests immense age.

At intervals, pylons rise from the basin floor. No two are quite aligned, though from certain positions they appear to form avenues reaching beyond sight. Their ceramic insulators are warm to the touch. Heavy cables sag between some of them and vanish directly into the dark above, never returning. There is no visible glow along the lines, only a faint compression in the air and the occasional sound of a distant door shutting.

Other artifacts are smaller: numbered iron hatches lying flush with the mud; ladders descending into pools; wheels mounted horizontally on stone plinths; lengths of railway track that begin and end after thirty meters. A locomotive stands on one such fragment near the western seep fields. It has no cab and no wheels. Its boiler breathes once every nine hours, releasing a dense, warm vapor in which tiny animals briefly form, mate, and dissolve before the cloud touches the ground.

Water is everywhere without ever gathering into a true river. It beads on cables, condenses on bare skin, and runs upward along certain rock faces. Pools exchange levels without channels between them. When one rises, another several kilometers away may sink by the same amount. The inhabitants—if the maintenance figures glimpsed near the pump houses can be called inhabitants—have marked these paired waters with metal tags. Each tag bears two names, one engraved normally and one cut in mirror writing.

The marsh’s rules are local and not entirely compatible.

Near the central terraces, weight accumulates. Travelers become steadily heavier while standing still, though they recover after moving on. Birds avoid landing there; instead they sleep in slow orbit around the pylons, circling for weeks with folded wings. Along the southern cracks, distance collects. A person may step across a fissure no wider than a hand and arrive an hour later with marsh water dried to salt on their clothing. Northward, reflections lag behind their owners. In the deepest pools they do not imitate at all but continue earlier gestures, repeating them with slight corrections.

No event is sudden enough to offer clarity. The breakdown proceeds patiently.

A pumping house takes twenty years to sink one meter, yet its doorway always remains the same height above the mud. A ridge of fresh basalt advances across a flooded field at the pace of growing lichen, preserving its razor edge as it goes. One pylon has been leaning for generations, never falling, its cables slackening by a finger’s width each rainy season. The animals adjust around these changes. Nests migrate. Trails bend. Entire colonies move a few centimeters each night to remain within the habitable geometry.

The vegetation is less adaptable. It grows sparsely wherever the physical laws are stable enough to permit roots. Wire-reeds surround concrete footings. Low red fans spread over iron-rich seepage. Trees are rare and disturbing: smooth, branchless trunks standing alone on mud islands, each supporting a crown somewhere out of sight. Moisture falls steadily from those invisible crowns. Occasionally a fruit arrives, dropping through the darkness with a soft, terminal splash. The fruit is warm, metallic, and filled with teeth too small to belong to any animal in the basin.

There is weather, though no sky can be seen. Warm rain begins without warning and may continue for days. It makes almost no noise; the drops descend slowly, dimpling the water like cautious fingertips. At other times condensation forms directly in the air as suspended spheres. These gather into drifting walls, millions of droplets held motionless at eye level. Animals pass through them and emerge shining. Machinery passing through leaves cylindrical absences that take hours to close.

From higher ground—one of the new stone ridges, perhaps, or the roof of a pumping station—the breadth of the place can be inferred rather than viewed. Far pumps answer near ones through the mud. Herds stir in succession across unseen flats. A tremor travels outward, touching pylons one by one, each answering with a muted iron note. The sequence continues long after it should have reached the basin’s edge.

Perhaps there is no edge. Perhaps the rim belongs only to maps.

Toward what surveyors call the east stands the largest structure: a low industrial complex spread across several artificial islands. Its tanks and gantries rise as overlapping black shapes, crowded with pipes, valves, cages, and maintenance platforms. None of it is lit. The machinery operates by touch, pressure, and the migration of animals trained—or evolved—to turn small wheels at prescribed intervals. Pale amphibians pack the gauges. Moths nest in switch boxes. Eels thread the conduits, carrying weak current in their muscles.

The complex processes something drawn from beneath the basin. Not ore, not oil, not water. The pipes vibrate with a substance that seems to have duration rather than mass. Around leakage points, stone becomes abruptly old: corners soften, rust flowers, and reeds rise, seed, and collapse in minutes. But the leakage itself spreads with extreme slowness, a dark stain advancing no more than a nail’s width in a year. Workers have surrounded each stain with white ceramic fences. Inside them, one sometimes sees the ruins of structures that have not yet been built.

The workers are seldom encountered closely. Their rubber coats shine with condensation, and their helmets have broad, opaque faceplates. They communicate by laying tools on the ground in particular arrangements. Whether they are human beneath the coats is difficult to determine, but the abundant fauna does not fear them. Marsh creatures gather around their boots. Birds follow them soundlessly. When a worker stops moving, the animals climb over the figure until it resembles a small, breathing hill.

There are old routes through the basin, indicated by raised studs hammered into stone. Following them is safer than crossing open water, but not reliably so. Some studs mark physical hazards. Others anchor the sequence of places. If one is removed, the next kilometer may occur before the previous one, or may repeat until the stud is replaced. Many are polished by use. A few are still hot from the forge.

The darkness makes all travel intimate. One proceeds from wet surface to wet surface, from the click of insects to the breath of a hidden pump. The great scale of the basin arrives indirectly: in delays, echoes, migrating pressure, and the knowledge that every nearby thing—every reed, animal, pipe, and sharp new ledge—is only one item in a crowded region continuing far beyond perception.

Nothing here hurries. Rain hangs. Stone emerges. Machines draw their impossible resource. Animals wait with inexhaustible attention. The whole wet lowland appears to be in the first stage of becoming something, though that first stage may have lasted longer than any history.

Beneath it, the buried factory turns once.

Water rises through every crack.

And in the lightless warmth, the landscape very slowly begins again.
