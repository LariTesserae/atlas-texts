The place is called a summit only because there is nowhere higher inside it.

It is a small, wholly submerged enclosure, perhaps the volume of a chapel or a clustered handful of houses, raised far above an unseen ocean floor. No mountain supports it. Beneath its lowest courses there is only black water, descending for miles. The entire landscape has been constructed: floor, walls, terraces, hollows, even the rubble accumulated in corners. There is no native rock here. Every apparent cliff is masonry, every pebble a worn fragment of something once fitted by hand.

The builders made it without advanced machinery. The blocks retain the evidence of levers, stone hammers, braided ropes, wedges driven into wet seams. Some are fired clay; others are shell-lime aggregate cast in shallow baskets. Dark glass has been chipped rather than cut. Metal is rare, appearing only as green-black pins or flattened clamps hammered from impure copper. Yet the work surrounds the water completely. It rises in steep, close-set tiers, arches crossing arches until the structure resembles both a drowned sanctuary and the inside of a colossal primitive instrument.

Its boundaries are clear. Six leaning walls enclose an irregular court, and above them a low vaulted roof gathers into a blunt crown. The summit stands several hundred handspans above the court, reachable through slit passages and flooded stairways. Openings in the outer walls admit the deep ocean through stone grilles. Thus the enclosure is not airtight or sealed, but the masonry separates it from the immensity outside. Within, one can almost imagine water behaving as air behaves in a room.

It is warm here. The blocks exhale a low geothermal heat stored in their cores. Touching them would reveal gradients: lukewarm faces, hot seams, occasional cool patches where exterior currents breathe through. Nothing boils. The warmth is old and even, less like a fire than the residual temperature of a sleeping animal.

There are no plants. No weed trails from the ledges, no seagrass bends in the openings, no film of green softens the construction. The absence is initially difficult to notice because the enclosure is crowded with life.

Small animals occupy nearly every cavity. Pale crustaceans fit themselves into chisel marks. Blind, ribbon-bodied swimmers knot gently under the arches. Transparent things like purses or bells hang in the still water, retracting when touched by pressure waves. Armored grazers move over the masonry despite there being no visible growth for them to graze. Their mouthparts scrape the blocks with such delicacy that the resulting powder hangs for days before settling.

They do not eat plants. The ecology begins with mineral warmth and the slow chemistry of the walls. In deep joints live colorless bacterial veils, too thin to see except where they distort the dull gleam of clay. Tiny fauna comb these veils from the stone. Larger fauna eat the combers; still larger ones wait in mortared hollows for the hunters. Death settles downward as a nearly invisible nourishment. Nothing grows quickly enough to be witnessed.

The density of animals does not make the place frantic. They are abundant in the manner of stars: everywhere, yet separated by darkness. Most conserve movement. A jointed creature may take an hour to extend one limb from a hole. Schools of thumb-sized fish hold themselves motionless around the warmer pillars, their fins making corrections too slight to see. Flat, soft predators lie over carvings for entire seasons, indistinguishable from folds of soot.

The largest inhabitants are long-shelled animals that circle the upper gallery. They are narrow enough to pass through doorways and too long to turn around in most corridors. Each follows a fixed route through the architecture: out through one grille, around the crown in the open sea, in through another grille, then downward through a corkscrew stair. Completing the circuit takes several days. No two overtake one another. Their shells are abraded to the same curves as the passages, as if architecture and animal have spent ages shaping a mutual fit.

Almost no sound carries from them.

The water itself is unusually quiet. The outer currents divide around the enclosure, leaving the interior in a pressure-shadow. There is no surf at this depth, no rain, no surface weather. A listener might detect the tiny tick of mouthparts on ceramic, the soft contact of shell against a lintel, or a distant granular sigh when ancient mortar relinquishes a few grains. Now and then one of the copper pins contracts in a cooler current and gives a single muted note. The whole structure seems to pause afterward.

Light enters reluctantly. Far above, the ocean may have a day and night, but neither arrives here as more than a change in the quality of blackness. The grilles sometimes hold a dim blue bruise. Many animals make their own restrained light: a row of pinpricks along a belly, a momentary amber ring, a cold flash hidden immediately beneath a shell. Illumination is treated like scent—released sparingly, meaningful to those nearby, dangerous if prolonged.

The masonry bears carvings, although erosion has reduced them to broad depressions. Faces, if they were faces, have become bowls. Lines of inscription have widened into channels where small creatures shelter. Hands have been polished into featureless paddles. On the highest terrace stands a dense group of upright figures, all handmade, all facing inward. They may once have represented builders, ancestors, prisoners, or merely posts. Water has worn each into a different stage of anonymity. Some retain shoulders. Some are tapering stones. One has become no more than a waist-high mound with a copper staple exposed at its center.

Nothing in the enclosure is untouched, but little visibly changes. Erosion here is ancient rather than violent. Water has passed through every doorway for longer than the surviving forms can account for. Corners are round. Steps sag into shallow ramps. Hard glass inserts stand proud where softer ceramic has receded around them. The oldest floor has been hollowed into basins by the minute traffic of bodies, and these basins now determine the currents, which determine where sediment falls, which determines where new animals congregate. The landscape revises itself by fractions.

There is one governing oddity: loose material tends to rise.

Not rapidly. A broken grain of mortar may rest for years before lifting. But once freed completely from the structure, mineral fragments drift upward as though the summit possesses its own reversed gravity. Fine particles gather beneath ceilings. Pebbles lodge in the undersides of arches. Larger fallen pieces ascend until they strike the crown vault, where they form inverted talus fields hanging overhead.

The fauna have adapted to this rule. Many animals lay eggs on downward-facing surfaces. Scavengers wait above a dying creature rather than below it. The old builders evidently knew the effect as well. Stairs have channels cut into their undersides, and numerous niches open toward the ceiling, accessible only by climbing or floating. What first appears to be a floor plan is only half the design. The roof is crowded with inverted courtyards, shelves, receptacles, and low parapets now buried beneath centuries of rising debris.

At the very crown is the highest room, a chamber no larger than a hut. Its threshold faces downward. Within it, fragments from every level have accumulated: pottery chips, worn tool heads, animal shells, beads of crude glass, finger-sized idols, copper scales, teeth, and unidentifiable pieces polished smooth. The debris presses densely against the domed ceiling, forming a second floor overhead. Creatures tunnel through it with extreme care.

At the center hangs an empty stone basin.

It is suspended from braided mineral cords—once rope, perhaps, since replaced grain by grain by deposits from the warm water. The basin remains level despite the reversed fall of loose objects. Its rim is barely ornamented. Every few years, a clear droplet forms on its underside, although it is already surrounded by water. The droplet grows slowly, resisting dissolution, then releases and sinks.

All nearby animals withdraw when this happens.

The drop descends through the chapel court, past the inward-facing figures and the worn steps. It gives off no light and makes no sound. Sediment rises away from its path. The blind fish tilt their heads toward it. At the lowest basin it vanishes into a fitted hole no tool can enter.

For several days afterward, warmth moves through the walls.

Then the colorless veils thicken in their hidden seams. The grazers resume scraping. Eggs open beneath the arches. One of the long-shelled animals begins another circuit of the summit.

Everything continues, quietly, within the small made world suspended over the abyss.
