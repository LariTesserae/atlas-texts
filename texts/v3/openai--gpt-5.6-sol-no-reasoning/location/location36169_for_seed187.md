The place is a hollow in an old floodplain, no larger than a village and almost perfectly level. Its boundaries are low banks worn so thoroughly by vanished rivers that they scarcely count as slopes. There is no exposed rock and almost no bare soil. Whatever sharp forms once existed have been reduced to depth without relief: fine dark sediment, compacted peat, buried channels, and the broad, patient flatness left when water has had longer than memory to finish its work.

Everything visible is plant.

The ground is a mattress of roots and old stems, firm enough to walk on in most places, though it yields slightly underfoot. Moss fills the seams. Liverworts glaze the wetter hollows. Sedges grow in dense, waist-high islands, and between them spread low mats of cranberry, pennywort, creeping willow, and cool green grasses. Even the few open pools are not truly open: duckweed covers them edge to edge, making the water look like a darker shade of land.

Above this floor, enormous old trees hold the hollow beneath a continuous canopy. They are not crowded. Only a dozen or so stand within the whole enclosure, widely separated, with empty green distances between their trunks. Each has occupied its patch for centuries. Their crowns meet far overhead, but their columns rise alone, gray-brown and furred with moss, as massive and solitary as towers in a roofless cathedral. The understory is similarly sparse. There are no thickets, no tangled young woods, only scattered fern fans and long, unobstructed aisles carpeted in living growth.

The abundance of vegetation does not make the hollow feel congested. Plants have taken every surface but have left most of the volume empty. The eye travels easily from one ancient trunk to another. Beneath the canopy there is a great deal of air.

It is cool throughout the year. Not bitterly cold, but cool in the steady manner of deep shade and wet ground. Moisture comes in intervals. Rain passes over every few days, rarely hard and rarely long. Between showers, condensation gathers on leaves and drips long after the sky has cleared. Water stands in root depressions and in the traces of old channels, then slowly retreats into peat. There is no broad stream now. Instead, the hollow contains dozens of small, temporary waters: black cups beneath uprooted boles, silver ribbons among the sedges, shallow pools hidden under duckweed.

The strangest thing about the place is only that it is so ordinary while sounding like a factory.

The noise begins before dawn and continues until after dusk. Frogs pulse from the pools in synchronized belts, their calls low and mechanical. Cicadas and crickets maintain high, hard notes in the canopy. Woodpeckers hammer resonant trunks. Wind passes through millions of narrow leaves with a constant rushing hiss. At intervals, colonies of rooks rise and settle together, producing a clatter like loose sheet metal. Bees occupy hollow limbs and send out a dense electrical droning. Water dripping into the same leaf cups makes a repetitive ticking that can be heard beneath everything else.

No machine is present, yet the sounds divide themselves into industrial layers: the bass throb of frogs, the rotating whine of insects, the percussion of birds, the conveyor-rustle of foliage. The hollow seems to operate rather than merely live.

Its production is biological and immense. Leaves are manufactured overhead in spring, sugars moved down through cambium, pollen released in clouds, seeds packed and dropped, litter dismantled by fungi and minute animals, minerals lifted from the buried flood sediments and returned again. Ant trails run like transport lines along roots. Bees commute between fixed flowering patches. Beetles bore predictable galleries through deadwood. Voles maintain tunnels in the root mattress; shrews hunt through them; grass snakes wait beside the warmest pools. Bats work the open air between the trunks each evening, and owls take over when the daylight insects quiet.

There are many animals, but few are visible at once. The open spaces remain open. Most life is distributed through layers: mites and springtails in the moss, worms in the peat, larvae under bark, birds in the crown, amphibians at the waterline. A person standing in the center might see nothing larger than a blackbird for several minutes while being surrounded by thousands of feeding, breeding, tunneling bodies.

Nothing here appears recently disturbed. Fallen limbs lie where they have always seemed to lie, softened into long green ridges. The oldest stumps have lost their woody character and become circular gardens of moss. Shallow water follows channels set down by rivers that no longer cross the plain. Animal paths are narrow but permanent. Even the seasonal changes feel less like change than stages in a repeating process: leafing, flowering, fruiting, falling, decay. The quantities differ, but the pattern does not.

There are no paths made by people, no walls, cut timber, wire, foundations, or discarded tools. The apparent machinery of the hollow has no builder and no operator. Its engines eat light. Its pipes are vessels and fungal threads. Its storage tanks are trunks, bulbs, seeds, and saturated peat. Its waste becomes feedstock almost where it falls.

Near the center is the emptiest part: a round clearing perhaps thirty paces across. It is filled only with low moss and a single shallow pool. The canopy spans it from the margins, leaving the space beneath remarkably clear. Rain reaches the pool in heavy, widely spaced drops. On still days its duckweed surface is unbroken except for the noses of frogs. On windy days leaves race around the clearing’s edge but seldom enter the middle, caught in rings of sedge and root.

This clearing gives the hollow its sense of scale. It is not wilderness without limit. One can cross it in minutes, circle its banks in an afternoon, learn the positions of each great tree. Yet it feels complete, an entire economy compressed into a green room.

The governing rule is persistence. Water may appear and disappear, animals may hatch and die, leaves may be replaced by other leaves, but the hollow permits no lasting vacancy and no lasting novelty. Every opening is covered. Every fallen thing is incorporated. The ancient flat ground has reached a form so settled that even growth does not alter it. Life here is profuse, loud, and ceaselessly active, but the landscape itself remains almost perfectly still.
