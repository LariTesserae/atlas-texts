The place is hidden beneath a low shelf of black stone, not quite a cave and not quite open ground. From outside, it appears to be only a horizontal crack at the base of a weathered escarpment. One must stoop, turn sideways, and descend a few shallow steps worn by feet into the rock. Beyond them the fissure opens into a long, hollow chamber.

It is dark, cold, and almost entirely empty.

The roof stays low near the entrance, then lifts beyond the reach of an upheld hand. Its extent can only be guessed. A lamp reveals one wall, perhaps, or a pale patch of floor; everything else withdraws into blackness. The chamber is not large in the manner of caverns, but darkness makes its few tens of paces feel unmeasured. There are no pillars, no heaps of fallen stone, no dense mineral forests. Much of the floor is bare, level bedrock, polished by a process so old and patient that its violence has been erased.

Yet the chamber is deafening.

Behind the farther wall, an underground river passes through stone.

No water can be seen. It runs in a deeper channel sealed from the chamber by several arm-lengths of rock, but its sound saturates everything: a continuous, immense concussion, too broad and low to resemble ordinary rapids. It is less like hearing water than like standing inside a drum while a storm rolls over it without end. The floor trembles faintly. Loose grit shivers into shallow seams. The chest and jaw receive the river as much as the ears do.

At irregular intervals the hidden channel narrows or swells. The roar climbs, acquires a tearing upper note, and then subsides over many minutes. These changes happen slowly enough that a visitor may doubt they are happening at all. A louder phase can last a night. A quiet phase is only quiet by comparison: speech remains nearly useless, and people communicate by touch, lamplight, and deliberate motions of the hands.

The visible chamber is dry in most places. Moisture enters indirectly. Cold condensation pearls on the wall facing the river, gathering along hairline fractures before descending in crooked threads. Occasional rain on the plateau above finds old joints in the stone and appears hours later as brief, clear dripping from the ceiling. Each drop is invisible until it crosses the light, and inaudible against the subterranean thunder.

Water collects in a handful of shallow basins. None is wider than a shield. Their rims have been rounded by generations of hands and by the slight overflow of wet seasons. The water in them is intensely cold and almost perfectly still, though rings sometimes pass across the surface when the hidden river strikes the wall particularly hard.

Life concentrates around these small reservoirs.

Mats of dark green liverwort lie pressed against the basin edges, glossy as wetted hide. Pale moss grows only where the entrance admits a weak winter-gray reflection for part of the day. Farther in, vegetation becomes stranger only in the ordinary ways of darkness: threadlike fungal growth, translucent films of algae sustained by minute traces of light, and tough little fern fronds emerging from cracks where silt has accumulated. The growth is lush at finger scale. Every damp seam contains crowded leaves and fine roots, but between one seam and the next there may be several paces of naked stone.

The chamber therefore gives two contradictory impressions. Viewed from the entrance, it is vacant. Examined on one’s knees, it is a forest.

Tiny stalks bend beneath condensation drops. Rootlets cross mud flats no larger than a palm. Pinhead fungi rise after the seasonal seepage and collapse before the rock dries again. Black springtails graze among them. Colorless worms inhabit the sediment at the bottoms of the basins. Very rarely, a small cave salamander is found beneath one of the flat stones beside the wall, cold and motionless, its skin the muted pink of an old scar. The people who visit do not disturb it. Seeing one is considered evidence that the buried river has not changed its course.

Larger animals seldom enter. A bat may cling near the outer fissure. Mice leave tracks in the fine grit and then vanish for months. Nothing nests in the inner chamber; the vibration is too constant, the food too slight, and the open floor offers no concealment except darkness itself.

Human marks are scattered through the hollow.

Near the entrance stands a low windbreak made from slabs fitted without mortar. It encloses no more than a sleeping place and has no roof. Beyond it, at long intervals, shallow niches have been chipped into the walls. Each niche holds the remains of a small clay lamp: coarse bowls pinched by hand, their rims charred where wicks once lay. Some are intact; others have softened under dripping water and slumped back toward mud.

A narrow path of pale stones crosses the chamber. They are not paving. Each is separated from the next by a stride or two, placed so that a traveler can follow them by touch when lamps fail. Generations of feet have polished their upper surfaces. At several points, loops of plaited cord hang from wooden pegs driven into natural cracks. The oldest pegs have mineral crust over them. New ones are periodically added beside the dead gray stubs of their predecessors.

There is no permanent settlement. The chamber cannot feed one, and its noise defeats ordinary domestic life. Instead it serves as a refuge, cistern, meeting place, and boundary marker for people living in the cold lowlands outside. They come singly or in small groups. Large gatherings are impossible: not because the floor lacks room, but because the roar isolates every person from the next.

The chamber has shaped its own customs.

No one shouts. Shouting accomplishes nothing and is regarded as childish.

To request water, one shows both empty hands and touches the throat. To warn of unstable footing, one presses a palm twice against another person’s shoulder. A lamp swung in a slow circle means return to the entrance. A lamp covered and uncovered three times means someone is missing.

Promises are made with foreheads touching, so that the words can be felt through the bones of the face even when they cannot be heard clearly. Disputes are taken outside. Names of the dead are scratched into soft clay tablets and left near the central basin, but the moisture gradually blunts the letters. After many years the tablets become anonymous oblongs. Older ones dissolve completely, feeding a low bank of fine brown sediment where moss grows thickest.

The chamber is believed to remember weight rather than speech.

This belief is not supernatural so much as observational. Objects left on the floor creep imperceptibly toward the river wall. A stone set upright may be found leaning after a season. Grit sorts itself into delicate bands under the perpetual vibration. Water in the basins favors one rim. The buried current, striking through the rock year after year, is slowly inclining the chamber toward itself.

Its erosion is settled and mature. Sharp forms have long since been removed. The ceiling is bowed, the walls softly scalloped, the floor worn into broad channels that no longer carry water. These are relics of an earlier course, when the river occupied the chamber openly. It abandoned this level ages ago and sank behind the wall, leaving its old bed as a hollow beside its present one.

Change now occurs almost too slowly to witness. A crack widens by the thickness of a hair in a lifetime. A basin deepens beneath intermittent drips. A clay lamp loses its rim. Moss advances during three wet years and retreats in five dry ones. The roar continues, altering in tone as unseen passages enlarge and gravel bars migrate in the buried channel.

The cold scarcely varies. In winter, vapor gathers at the entrance and freezes into thin white scales. In summer, warmer air from outside meets the stone and fills the first corridor with mist. The inner hollow remains the same: black, damp at its margins, dry at its center, trembling with remote water.

It is possible to sit there for hours and feel that nothing exists except the lamp, the nearest basin, and the pressure of sound.

Then the flame reveals some small thing: a handprint in clay, an abandoned bone needle, a salamander’s foot beneath a leaf, a row of faded cut marks counting floods. The chamber briefly contracts around that object and becomes intimate, almost inhabited.

When the light moves on, it empties again.
