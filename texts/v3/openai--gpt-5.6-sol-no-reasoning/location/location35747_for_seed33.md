At first it appears to be a city built across the summits of a mountain range.

Only later does it become clear that there are no mountains.

The towers descend farther than the eye can follow, and what seems to be ground is merely the common height at which their upper faces have weathered flat. They stand in ranks across an enormous gulf: dark mineral columns, broad as districts, separated by vertical lanes of near-perfect emptiness. Bridges cross some of the gaps. Stairways descend others. Arches join isolated roofs to façades that have no rooms behind them. Everything resembles construction, but nowhere is there evidence of tools, mortar, scaffolding, or intention. The place has streets without foundations, buildings without interiors, and plazas that are only chance agreements between neighboring heights.

It is called a city only because the mind lacks a better defense.

The altitude is extreme. Above the roofs, the atmosphere is thin, cool, and metallic, with a dusk-colored sky that never brightens much beyond charcoal violet. There is no sun in the ordinary sense. A broad, blurred radiance passes behind the sky at long intervals, revealing edges without casting dependable shadows. During these passages the upper city turns the color of old bone and tarnished silver. At other times it nearly vanishes, leaving only pale rims around parapets and the faint, deep glimmer of the chasms.

There is no water here. No frost gathers, no cloud condenses, no hidden spring feeds the cracks. The cold is a dry subtraction. Exposed surfaces shed heat until touch itself seems unwelcome. Powder collects in sheltered angles, but it is not sand in the familiar sense. It consists of scales of black salt, transparent mineral needles, and flakes so thin that they bend around a finger without breaking. On open terraces the powder arranges itself into concentric lines, as though ripples had passed through it. Nothing visible causes them.

The city is ancient enough to have entered a kind of equilibrium with its ruin.

Cornices are rounded by ages of particle wind. Stair treads sag in their centers. Towers facing the high atmospheric current have been pared into blades, while their sheltered sides retain elaborate fluting: balconies, niches, blind windows, processional ramps. Whole avenues have narrowed to ridges. Fallen material does not form rubble fields below. It drops into the streets between columns and continues falling. Sometimes, days afterward, a faint impact is felt through the soles from somewhere overhead.

That is the first breach in the world’s apparent order.

Here, down is not consistent.

Most loose things fall toward the lightless gulf. Some fall toward the nearest wall and adhere there as if the façade were level ground. A few rise slowly into the sky, tumbling until distance consumes them. The city’s old erosion records these different gravities. Drainlike grooves climb pillars. Debris fans spread vertically across walls. Door-shaped recesses occur on undersides of bridges, complete with thresholds worn smooth by traffic that could not have stood where it walked.

There are no mechanisms enforcing these changes. No engines lie under the streets, no cables sing in the towers. The rules simply vary from district to district, sometimes from one side of an arch to the other. Pebbles can be used to test a crossing, though not reliably: a stone may choose one direction while a body chooses another.

Despite the silence, the landscape is active.

High currents pass through the avenues, too thin to be heard as wind. Their arrival is known by motion: mineral dust lifting in sheets, pouring around corners, then stopping in midair. These suspended veils can remain for hours, each grain fixed with exquisite precision. When gravity alters, the entire veil collapses sideways and rushes along a wall like a dry waterfall.

Elsewhere, the columns flex.

The movement is slight but regional. At intervals, whole quarters incline toward one another by a fraction of a degree. Bridges tighten. Cracks close with a soft pressure that can be felt in the teeth. Far away, an arch releases from its anchors and sails intact across the gulf, turning slowly. It may lodge between two towers many leagues distant, becoming a new street. It may pass through a building as if one or the other were momentarily unreal.

These rearrangements make almost no sound. Noise does not travel properly at this height. A collapse nearby produces a dry click; a fracture across the city may arrive as a touch on the skin. Sometimes silence itself comes in waves. One can feel a zone of deeper quiet moving through the streets, erasing the tiny scrape of boots and the pulse within the ear. Where it passes, dust hangs still and distant structures look unnaturally close.

There is almost no life by ordinary measures.

In the deepest incisions on the sheltered faces of towers, a dull white film grows one crystal at a time. It has no cells, roots, spores, or apparent metabolism. It feeds upon differences: warmth against cold, weight against weightlessness, one geometry against another. Where a crack has two incompatible depths depending on the angle of observation, the film thickens into folded crusts. These formations are the closest thing the city has to vegetation. They spread over centuries, branching in delicate, maplike patterns.

Occasionally a patch changes all at once. It darkens, curls away from the stone, and is gone. For several days afterward, the local walls have only one side.

The fauna are rarer and less easily classified.

Small hollow creatures inhabit the underside streets. They resemble jointed rings made of translucent mineral, each no larger than a hand. They roll rather than walk, stopping at corners to press themselves flat against the masonry. Their passage leaves narrow tracks in dust, but the tracks often precede them. They appear to graze on the white difference-film, although sometimes the film increases where they have fed.

Higher in the city, solitary shapes cross the open gulf.

They are seen only against the dim sky: long, angular silhouettes with too many bends, drifting between towers without wings. Each follows the edge of something invisible, turning sharply where nothing can be seen. They may be animals, or fractures moving through space. When one passes near a structure, doorways deepen and windows briefly reveal interiors—empty halls, descending courts, rows of columns—which cease to exist as soon as the shape has gone.

No nests have been found. No carcass remains. Once in a great while, however, an entire terrace is covered with delicate polygonal shells, each open at the top. By the next dimming they have become holes instead, cut into the stone to the same depth.

The city extends well beyond any single view. From the highest accessible spires, districts can be seen tiering toward the horizon: mesas crowded with slender towers, isolated citadels leaning over gaps, amphitheaters whose seats face downward, causeways describing impossible arcs. Distance is unreliable. A quarter that seems close enough to reach by evening may recede for weeks. Another, a faint comb of structures on the horizon, may be entered by taking three consecutive left turns inside an open square.

The city has no technology, yet it possesses infrastructure.

Broad avenues connect major elevations. Repeating alcoves provide shelter from the particle winds. Shafts join upper and lower districts, though their depths depend on who enters them. Bridges tend to appear where crossings become necessary, not immediately, but after enough repeated attempts. Stone slowly extrudes from opposing ledges, advancing grain by grain until the gap is closed. Conversely, neglected roads thin and eventually separate from the world.

This gives the place a troubling responsiveness without implying intelligence. It may not be conscious. It may simply be that repetition is one of the forces here, like pressure or gravity.

A traveled path becomes shorter.

A remembered room acquires walls.

A name, spoken often enough, causes an inscription to weather into nearby stone—never in a recognizable alphabet, but with the unmistakable spacing of language.

There are few visitors and no permanent inhabitants in any familiar sense. Camps cannot remain stable. Enclosures change volume when unobserved; possessions distribute themselves among adjacent versions of the same terrace. Fire burns reluctantly in the thin atmosphere, producing a low blue line and almost no heat. Nothing can be cultivated. No water can be collected. The city offers shelter from wind but not from alteration.

Its most dangerous weather is called the Revision.

It begins when the blurred radiance behind the sky stops moving. Shadows collect along edges, not as darkness but as thickness. The skyline repeats itself in layers. Then the city starts reconsidering its own form.

For several hours, towers exchange heights. Stairways turn their flights inside out. Bridges remember older positions and stretch toward vanished supports. Weathered façades become sharp and new, while untouched walls abruptly acquire a million years of abrasion. The white films bloom across incompatible surfaces. Hollow ring-creatures roll frantically uphill in every available direction.

During a Revision, it is safest to remain beneath an arch and refuse to count anything. Enumeration gives the city choices. A plaza with four towers may have five once counted, then three when counted again, and eventually no plaza at all.

Afterward the landscape settles into silence.

Dust descends. Gravity resumes a mostly downward habit. Far districts stand in unfamiliar arrangements, but their erosion is complete, as though they have occupied those positions since the beginning. New streets bear the polished wear of generations. Fresh chasms open with ancient mineral crust along their lips.

Nothing looks recently changed.

That is perhaps the defining quality of the place: however active its transformations, each result arrives already old. The city does not become. It has always just been whatever it is now.

And in the dim, cool interval before the sky-light moves again, the vast ranks of empty architecture seem almost peaceful—an urban world without builders or citizens, weathered by laws that cannot agree, suspended at the top of an abyss whose bottom may be another sky.
