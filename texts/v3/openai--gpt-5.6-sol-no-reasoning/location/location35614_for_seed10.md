The high country has been eroded past stone.

What remains is a broad, cold tableland carried on the roots of plants: miles of dark interwoven fiber stretched between old summits, sagging into valleys and rising again in long shallow swells. From a distance it resembles tundra after rain—black-green, silvered, nearly level—but there is no reliable ground beneath it. The visible surface is a tough skin of leaves no larger than scales, layered over woody mesh, peat, bladder tissue, dormant bulbs, and pale cords descending farther than light can follow. Mountains once stood here. Their minerals have been drawn up, dissolved, eaten, and incorporated. Veins of granite persist only as grit in seed husks. Iron colors the red moss. Calcium whitens the antlerlike groves.

The land is extremely old and still violently unfinished.

There are few upright forms. Vast spaces lie open beneath the low ceiling of cloud, interrupted only by scattered plant towers or solitary tangles of sail-leaf. Yet every apparent emptiness is alive. The plains are not sparsely vegetated; vegetation has become the substrate, leaving little need for visible growth. Underfoot, if there were feet to stand here, the plain would compress with the slow resilience of muscle. Pores would close. Water would shine briefly around the pressure and then be drunk back into the mat.

Rain seldom falls cleanly. Moisture condenses everywhere at once.

It beads along black hairs, forms beneath translucent leaves, gathers inside the hollow stems that cross the plateau, and leaks from weeping escarpments of root. The atmosphere itself seems glandular. Low clouds touch the ground and snag on the vegetation, unraveling into wet streamers. Every surface drips upward as often as downward. In some basins water lies openly in long lead-colored meres, but these have no fixed shores. Rafts of bladderweed loosen, drift, and graft themselves to the opposite bank. A lake may close overnight like a healed wound, while a dry meadow nearby dilates into deep water.

The cold is persistent rather than absolute. Frost inhabits shade, hollows, and the undersides of leaves. It forms delicate white organs that the plants tolerate, use, and sometimes shed. Sunlight, when it penetrates, is too diluted to warm the whole plateau. It passes through the clouds as a gray-blue pressure, giving the wet surfaces the luster of old machinery. At the horizon, distant ridges dissolve into bands of vapor and appear flatter than they are. Perspective becomes unreliable there. A grove ten miles away may seem close enough to touch, while a nearby pool can contain a receding weather front.

Across this living floor stand the condensers.

They are plants, though at first they resemble abandoned industrial complexes: isolated assemblies of jointed columns, black vanes, ribbed drums, and stacks breathing white vapor. No hand built them. Their oldest tissues began as ordinary trunks, but over geological ages they have specialized into engines of circulation. Wind turns their crown-wheels. Flexible pistons descend through the root mat. Chambers deep below compress marsh gas and drive warm sap uphill. Mineral-laden water rattles through woody pipes. Seed pods pass along peristaltic belts, clicking from one chamber to another, graded by weight and sent down different vascular lines.

The condensers manufacture weather and themselves.

Each tower harvests cloud. Vapor catches in its high mesh, runs through chilled fronds, and drops into resonant cisterns. The impact of thousands of droplets produces a continuous metallic roar. When the cisterns fill, valves made from swollen knots wrench open, and water surges into the plain through buried veins. The release can lift acres of terrain several feet. Waves pass outward through the plant mat, slow and immense, tossing grazing animals and splitting old seams into sudden ravines.

There are no buildings, roads, tools, fences, or ruins. Yet the plateau is industrial in its metabolism. Production occurs at regional scale: water refined from air, stone reduced into sap, gas pumped from decay, heat exchanged between valleys, seeds sorted and dispatched by the million. Nothing is supervised. Nothing has ever been designed. Efficiency has arisen through age, and age has worn it into something stranger than purpose.

The loudest sound is not wind but throughput.

The plateau hums from below. Root cables under tension groan like distant ships. Hollow stems knock together in irregular volleys. Condenser groves throb, hiss, and periodically issue a bass report that rolls from valley to valley. Lakes gulp as their living banks open. Flocks rise with a roar of wings, circle, and descend somewhere that did not exist when they took flight. During pressure changes the whole country whistles through millions of stomata. It can sound like a factory heard from inside a forest, or a forest remembered by a factory.

Silence survives only in small moving pockets. One may pass across the plain like shade, extinguishing each noise it touches. Wings continue beating inside it but make no sound. Water splashes soundlessly. When the pocket moves on, the withheld noises sometimes return all at once.

Animals are everywhere, though the openness initially conceals them. Most are low, pale, and swift. Six-legged grazers travel in widely spaced herds, combing films of frost from the leaf skin. Their bodies are insulated by hollow quills that whistle when they run. Long-necked marsh browsers insert narrow heads into breathing holes and draw up strings of root lice. Silver amphibians sleep inside droplets larger than themselves. Black birds gather around active valves to catch translucent worms ejected with the overflow.

The sky has its own migrating fauna. Dense flocks of membrane-winged creatures browse the underside of the cloud deck, leaving clean channels of blue-gray air behind them. Something enormous occasionally moves above the overcast. Its weight can be inferred from the descending hollows in the clouds and from the way the condenser towers incline as it passes. It never becomes fully visible. Feathers—or structures mistaken for feathers—come down days later, each one rooting where it lands and beginning a thicket of identical barbs.

Predation is intense but rarely individual. A grazing herd may cross what appears to be a patch of red moss; the moss tightens into a sieve and takes every third animal. Birds plunge into lakes that suddenly become leaves. Root vents imitate burrows and close around anything seeking warmth. In turn, animals prune valves, clean channels, carry spores through their organs, and gnaw open the plateau when its layers grow too dense. Life here feeds on distinctions: plant and animal, terrain and organism, weather and appetite.

Those distinctions do not hold for long.

Reality is least stable near the weeping cliffs. These are ancient margins where the root table has folded over itself, exposing strata of former surfaces: compressed forests, fossil rain, generations of sky trapped as blue bands between layers of peat. Water runs down the cliff faces, but the falls are old enough to have eroded duration as well as substance. Near them, events repeat without repeating exactly. A flock bursts from a ledge, then bursts again slightly lower, then again as eggs. A fallen animal may strike the ground before it slips. Moss grows over wounds that have not yet opened.

The cliffs do not crumble. Erosion has gone beyond collapse into revision.

Their profiles change as alternatives wear away. Corners soften, distances shorten, unnecessary mineral states disappear. Stones placed in the streams emerge as rounded seeds. Bones become branching waterways. Anything remaining long enough is reduced not to dust but to its most stable living interpretation.

Storms expose the process across the wider plateau. The cloud deck churns down until it contacts the plain, erasing the horizon. Condenser towers accelerate. Their vanes rotate against the wind, pulling air from directions that briefly multiply. Water rises in sheets. The plant floor breaks into slow archipelagos and migrates. Whole ecologies are peeled up, overturned, and laid back in unfamiliar sequences: lake over meadow, frost beneath warm mud, a breeding colony inside the memory of a hill.

Lightning moves through the roots instead of the sky. Blue radiance races under the translucent ground for miles, branching and rebranching. Wherever two bolts meet, new anatomy erupts—a ring of pump-trees, a sudden organ pipe of hollow reeds, a vast flower that opens once and produces not seeds but localized gravity. Animals tumble toward it in long, astonished arcs. Rain curves around its petals. Within hours it may be dismantled by beetles, absorbed by the plain, and forgotten so thoroughly that the depression it leaves seems ancient.

Nothing here maintains a stable shape, but patterns endure. Water seeks the condensers. Herds follow warm root lines. Towers arise at wide intervals. The tableland repeatedly opens, floods, heals, and opens elsewhere. Beneath all turbulence lies the patience of a system that has survived long enough to discard permanence.

At evening—if the dim shift in the clouds can be called evening—the plateau darkens from gray-green to nearly black. Bioluminescent sap begins to pulse in the buried channels. Seen from a height, the country becomes a sparse web of cold light: arteries spanning drowned valleys, isolated towers blinking as their chambers fill, migrating herds traced by sparks disturbed under their hooves. The pulses do not synchronize. They travel, collide, cancel, resume. Sometimes they arrange themselves into the outline of a coastline larger than the plateau. Sometimes into branching forms like lungs. Sometimes into patterns that cause every animal to stop and face the same empty region of sky.

Then the old land draws water from the clouds. Its towers turn. Its cliffs weep upward. Beneath the living plain, immense pumps enter another cycle, and the high country lifts and settles by the breadth of a leaf.
