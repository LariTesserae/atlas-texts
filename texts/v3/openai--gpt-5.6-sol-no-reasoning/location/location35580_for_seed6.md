The place is called a garden only because there is no better word for a factory that grows leaves.

It occupies the upper chambers of a drowned tower, high above a seafloor no light reaches and far below the unsettled skin of the ocean. The garden itself is not large. From wall to wall it might fill a cathedral, though there are so many partitions, gantries, conduits, trunks, tanks, and suspended beds that no uninterrupted measure of it remains. Everywhere is interior. Everywhere is underwater. There is no stone, soil, shell, or timber: every surface was fabricated, and even the sediment gathered in corners consists of oxidized filings, powdered ceramic, and the black crumbs of old seals.

The tower is young.

Its plates still carry the hard angles of manufacture. Welds rise in clean silver seams. Machine-cut ribs have not yet rounded under corrosion, and bolt heads retain their numerals beneath thin green films. Where the structure has buckled, the fractures are bright and sharp. Nothing has had time to become picturesque. The ocean has only begun its work.

The chamber stands nearly full of warm, dense water. A narrow lens of compressed air survives under the central cupola, but it is inaccessible except through thickets of machinery and hanging vegetation. Below it, the water is clear enough to see twenty or thirty paces, though almost nowhere offers that much open distance. Light comes from long industrial tubes caged in brass mesh. They shine with an ordinary white-yellow steadiness, neither theatrical nor abyssal, and lend the whole submerged room the practical illumination of a workshop left operating after its workers have gone.

No workers remain. No fish entered after them. There are no worms in the seams, no crabs among the pipes, no drifting larvae, no animal eyes returning the lamps’ glow. The water is chemically hostile to flesh but exceptionally kind to plants.

Growth occupies everything.

Vines issue from ceramic collars set into the walls, their pale stems braided around coolant pipes. Broad leaves unfold from ceiling racks and press against one another until they form layered roofs. Fernlike fronds stand in vertical trays, each one dark green at the base and nearly transparent at its fine-cut tips. Long ribbons of engineered kelp pass through toothed guide wheels and disappear into narrow processing slots. Root masses fill mesh cylinders, packed so closely they resemble organs held in cages. Some roots drink dissolved metals; others wind themselves around electrical contacts and glow faintly when current passes through them.

The vegetation does not sway as ocean plants should. The chamber water moves too slowly. Leaves remain where they have grown, held in patient attitudes by near-perfect suspension. A released bubble may crawl along the underside of a leaf for an hour before finding its edge. Pollen—if that is what the yellow dust is—hangs in vertical bands between lamps. Detached tendrils descend so gradually that one could mistake them for fixed lines.

This stillness makes the density uncanny. Every handspan is occupied, yet nothing jostles. There are no darting creatures, no pulse of fins, no startled exodus from a shadow. The garden seems crowded with presences but empty of lives.

Its machines operate on similarly long intervals.

Pumps turn behind the walls, one revolution at a time. Their vibration is felt more readily than heard: a delicate pressure in the teeth, a trembling along railings. Every several minutes, a valve opens with a soft internal knock. Nutrient-rich water seeps from perforated pipes and clouds the roots with rust-red minerals. Shutters adjust by fractions, keeping each cultivation rack aligned with the lamps. Far below, something heavy changes load, and the entire tower answers with a single low note too deep to be called a sound.

Otherwise there is quiet.

A leaf touches a grille with a faint papery tick. Gas gathers in the highest folds of foliage and escapes bead by bead. Relays click inside watertight boxes. Occasionally a stressed plate gives a small, clean ping that travels through the structure and vanishes. The absence of animal noise is absolute. No song, rasp, feeding scrape, wingbeat, or breath has ever joined the machinery.

The garden was built as a closed production system, though what it produces is no longer obvious. Labels identify harvest lines for oxygen, starch, structural fiber, memory resin, coolant gel, and something translated only as *continuity*. The machinery treats these substances without haste. Leaves are pinched loose by blunt manipulators and drawn into presses. Sap moves through glass coils. Pale cellulose emerges as narrow tape and is wound around empty drums. None of it leaves the chamber. Finished materials are routed back into walls, braces, gaskets, insulation, and fresh planting frames.

The factory consumes its garden to enlarge the factory that supports its garden.

As a result, the chamber has begun to reproduce its own architecture. Roots mineralize into load-bearing lattices. Vines extrude metallic skins copied from nearby pipes. Some leaves develop rows of precise apertures and function as filters. A grove near the western bulkhead grows rectangular trunks with inspection panels in their bark. Behind these panels are not tissues but wet bundles of green conductive fiber, arranged exactly like wiring and sprouting tiny white rootlets where ordinary cables would terminate.

It is difficult to say where the designed equipment ends.

One stair climbs steeply through the room, ascending around a central column toward the trapped air beneath the cupola. The steps are pressed steel, newly made, their edges still sharp. Yet each tread bears a mat of dark moss connected by hair-thin copper strands. The handrail flowers at regular intervals into pressure gauges. Their glass faces contain no needles. Inside each is a small floating leaf whose angle indicates the load on the structure.

At the stair’s highest landing, perhaps eighty meters above the tower’s foundation but only a few meters above the lower garden floor, there is an observation alcove. The windows look outward into blue water. Beyond them stand other fabricated heights: antenna forests, scaffold pinnacles, and the upper stories of neighboring towers, all drowned. They vanish downward into depth. Upward, far beyond sight, lies the ocean surface.

The garden is therefore high and submerged at once: a summit chamber beneath an inland sea, an artificial canopy raised over an artificial abyss.

The neighboring structures are close enough that their vegetation sometimes reaches across. Thick creepers grow from external maintenance ports and span the gaps like bridges. Because there is no fauna to graze or break them, they continue until they meet another wall. There they flatten themselves against the metal, find seams, and begin the slow work of entry. A few have penetrated this chamber. Their leaves differ subtly from the resident plants: blue-black, jointed, edged with minute ceramic teeth. The garden’s sorting mechanisms do not reject them. Instead, they label them as replacement components.

This may be how towers exchange instructions.

No dramatic current ever passes through the district. No storm reaches it except as a remote alteration in pressure. Change occurs by increments: a weld darkens; a root finds a bolt; a lamp loses one degree of brightness; an air pocket becomes smaller than it was a century before. The sharp new architecture is being softened, but only by its own growth. In time, every exposed plate will wear foliage. In longer time, the foliage will duplicate every plate.

There is one rule here that seems stronger than the others: vacancy is treated as damage.

An empty patch of wall triggers seed ports. An unused pipe fills with roots. A shadowed recess acquires luminous leaves. A broken machine becomes a trellis, then a nutrient source, then a template. Even open water is gradually partitioned by transparent membranes, each no thicker than a soap film and strong enough to redirect the garden’s slow circulations. Space cannot remain merely space. The system detects it, names it, and assigns it a function.

This is why the chamber feels smaller each year, though its boundaries have not moved.

Near the bottom is the newest construction: a ring of pale shoots surrounding a square of perfectly bare floor. The shoots are all identical. They rise from sockets that did not exist on the original plans, and each carries, instead of a flower, a small sealed lamp. Their light is normal and warm. Their roots meet beneath the floor and have begun assembling something in the hollow between decks.

The process is almost silent. Now and then comes the tap of a tiny tool, though no mechanical arm is visible. Fine bubbles emerge in measured groups. Fresh-cut metal gleams through a seam.

Whatever is being made below is not hurried. The garden has warmth, power, water, and no competitors. It can take decades to tighten a screw. It can spend a century closing a gap.

Above, under the unreachable cupola, the last air lens slowly contracts. Leaves press against its lower surface and enter the dry space one millimeter at a time. Their tips change there, becoming stiff, angular, and silver. They are learning the shape of the atmosphere.

When the air is finally gone, the chamber will be completely full.

The tower will record this not as a flood, but as completion.
