The place is called the Green Throat, though it is neither a valley nor a cavern. It is a chamber suspended near the summit of a manufactured mountain: a narrow, vertical garden built inside the hollow joint of an atmospheric engine.

From one wall to the other, the Throat is only forty paces across. Its height cannot be judged. Looking up, one sees a succession of iron collars diminishing into warm haze, each crossed by ladders, conduits, and old maintenance bridges. Looking down, one sees the same architecture falling away until it is obscured by leaves. There is no earth anywhere. Every cliff is plate steel; every ledge is a riveted bracket; every apparent boulder is a casing around some ancient gearbox. Even the mountain surrounding the chamber is an assembly of trusses, ceramic armor, pressure cells, and immense blocks poured from black industrial glass.

Yet the Throat feels lush.

Vegetation has occupied nearly every surface that does not move quickly enough to shear it away. Copper-green moss pads the bolt heads. Ferns grow from the rust-filled seams between plates, their fronds trembling in the engine’s continuous pressure waves. Thick vines descend from invisible heights, rootless and braided, wrapping gantries and branching across empty air. Some are botanical; others are obsolete cables whose insulation has split and frayed into rootlike fibers. It is difficult to tell which has learned to imitate which.

There are no crowded thickets. The chamber’s emptiness is too emphatic. Plants occur in suspended islands separated by gulfs of resonant air: a flowering platform here, a curtain of creepers twenty meters away, one huge bromeliad seated alone in the bowl of a dismantled turbine. The abundance is in surface coverage rather than volume. Almost everything solid is green, but almost all the chamber is void.

The air is warm from the machinery buried behind the walls. Heat leaks through the plates in broad, mild zones. Where coolant lines pass close to the surface, cold bands form, and condensation beads on handrails, pipe elbows, and the undersides of leaves. Water is never plentiful enough to become a continuous stream. It appears intermittently: first as a glittering skin, then as drops trembling at the tips of ferns, then as a brief silver rain shaken loose when the machinery changes pitch.

These rains fall only a short distance. Leaves catch them. Cups built into the steel catch them. Animals catch them in their mouths. What escapes descends through the chamber and vanishes long before reaching any bottom.

There are pockets of standing water in the old inspection basins. Each is scarcely larger than a table, but ecologically complete. Clear shrimp graze on red bacterial films. Black-bodied water striders skate among flakes of machine oil. Tiny fish, pale and nearly transparent, live in reservoirs connected by pipes too narrow for anything larger than an eel. During the dry interval, they retreat through threaded apertures into darkness. When the condensation cycle returns, they emerge as though the metal itself has produced them.

The sound is enormous.

Deep in the mountain, compressors turn with a force that can be felt in the jaw. Their lowest note is less a noise than a condition of matter. It passes through bone, steel, water, and wood alike. Above it, belts scream in inaccessible shafts. Relays strike in volleys. Pressure valves release without warning, filling the Throat with blasts like prolonged thunder. Every few minutes an unseen flywheel enters resonance and the whole chamber takes up a metallic howl.

No one speaks here. Speech is physically possible but irrelevant.

The inhabitants communicate by touch, color, vibration, and light. Frogs cling to warm pipes and inflate brilliant yellow throat sacs; their calls cannot compete with the engines, so the sacs flash instead, catching the ordinary daylight that descends from high reflector wells. Moths settle on taut cables and send courtship signals along them by beating their abdomens against the insulation. Long-legged lizards recognize one another through patterned tremors in the catwalk grating. Even the flowers seem adapted to the noise. Several species close when the machinery quiets and open only under heavy vibration, dusting the backs of passing beetles with blue pollen.

Birds inhabit the upper collars. Their wings are short and broad, suited to sudden drafts. They do not sing. They have bright, semaphore tails and white patches under their wings, visible through foliage and steam. When a pressure purge begins, they launch together and orbit the chamber in complete apparent silence, hundreds of bodies wheeling inside a roar too great to admit any individual voice.

Although the engine runs continuously, nothing here seems hurried. The great mechanisms complete their cycles over days or months. A shaft may rotate by only a fraction of a degree each hour. Counterweights descend so slowly that vines grow across their path and are gently folded, crushed, and carried downward. Shutters adjust with geological patience. A bridge seen at one angle in childhood might have tilted into a ramp by old age.

Erosion follows these motions.

The oldest plates are hollowed by generations of condensation. Drips have worn smooth dimples into steel and cut narrow runnels through ceramic tile. Warm acidic water has softened concrete housings into shapes resembling limestone. Railings bear polished grooves where vines, stirred by constant vibration, have rubbed against them for centuries. Rust is not ruin here but settled terrain: layered, hardened, colonized, and in places load-bearing. Orange shelves project from seams like mineral strata. Whole gardens root in them.

Nothing collapses quickly. Failure is slow enough to become climate.

When a support begins to bend, its deformation may continue for fifty years. Animals adjust their paths. Roots thicken along the lower edge. Water changes course. By the time the structure reaches its final position, the ecosystem has already accepted it as a hillside. The Green Throat contains no untouched architecture and no genuine rubble—only mechanisms passing by degrees into landscape.

Daylight enters through a chain of mirrored apertures. It is surprisingly normal: neither mystical nor dim, just warm afternoon-colored illumination descending in clean slanting planes. Because the mountain stands above most clouds, the source is often direct sun. Yet the mirrors turn independently, and so noon may arrive in the chamber long after sunset outside. Shadows migrate according to maintenance schedules established by people no longer present.

Those schedules are among the place’s laws.

At the seventh pressure pulse, the eastern wall warms.

After three warmings, condensation forms on the western trusses.

When the lowest reservoir reaches a marked line, hidden pumps wake and lift the water upward—not to the highest gardens, but to a tank somewhere beyond the walls.

Every eleven days, an enormous hatch opens overhead for exactly nine minutes. During that interval the air thins, the vines rise in the draft, and the sky appears: hard blue, close and immense. Seeds, insects, and droplets are drawn upward. Some are lost to the exterior heights. Others lodge in higher works and begin new islands of growth.

The machine seems to have been built to regulate weather over lands far below, but the outside world is almost impossible to glimpse. Through cracks near the upper galleries, one occasionally sees cloud tops spread beneath the structure like a second ground. At night, there may be distant grids of light on plateaus or on other manufactured peaks. No path leads visibly to them.

There are signs that people once entered the Throat regularly. Catwalks have handrails. Numbered doors are recessed into the walls. A small control booth hangs above the central void, its windows opaque with algae. Inside are three chairs, banks of dead switches, and a paper calendar fused by damp into a solid block. But human scale ends at the booth. Beyond it, the machinery belongs to larger intentions. Pistons disappear through apertures broad enough to swallow buildings. Pipework carries substances in quantities that no nearby settlement could use. The entire chamber may be only a valve socket, a maintenance cleft, or the narrow interval between two components of something continent-sized.

Still, the Green Throat has firm boundaries. Its ecology ends at sealed bulkheads and hot bearing walls. It is a small world held at extreme altitude, a room where every material was fabricated, every horizon is nearby, and nearly all the actual space is empty.

Across that emptiness hang a few remarkable presences: one bridge furred in orchids, one descending chain the width of an oak, one solitary wheel turning too slowly to perceive. A person can watch that wheel for an hour and see no motion. Return after a week, and its spokes stand elsewhere. In the damp groove of its rim, an entire generation of bright snails has traveled several meters without ever touching stationary ground.

At long intervals—perhaps once in a season—the machinery becomes quiet.

Not silent. Water ticks from leaf to leaf. Insects rasp inside the walls. Wings brush the humid air. Small pipes ping as they cool. But without the engine’s dominant roar, these noises seem indecently intimate, as though the chamber has exposed thoughts normally kept private.

The pause lasts less than a minute.

Flowers fold. Frogs flatten themselves against the steel. Birds grip their perches. The hanging vines cease trembling and, for the first time, reveal their true weight.

Then a relay closes somewhere deep below.

The mountain strikes one immense note. Condensation leaps from every surface at once, becoming rain in midair. The birds rise. The wheel advances by a measure too small to see. Warmth returns through the walls, and the narrow green world resumes its ancient, deafening labor.
