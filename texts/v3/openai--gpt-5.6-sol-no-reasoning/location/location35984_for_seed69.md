Darkness is not an interval here. It is the climate.

Across the high interior of the continent rises a plateau so broad that its margins lie beyond ordinary travel. It stands above the lower atmosphere, yet it is warm: an elevated country under a permanent ceiling of black vapor, heated from beneath by deep volcanic strata and from within by the labor of its waters. No sun reaches the ground. No moon is known. Light exists only as an event—brief, local, and usually dangerous.

The plateau is built of ancient dark stone, worn into immense settled forms. Long escarpments descend like the steps of a drowned amphitheater. Ravines have broadened over ages into green-black corridors; mesas have slumped into rounded islands; exposed cliffs are ribbed by waterfalls that have occupied the same channels for so long that the water seems less to cross the rock than to articulate it. Everything appears finished without being still. Erosion is no longer a violence here but an old agreement among rain, root, mineral, and gravity.

Water is nearly everywhere, though rarely as open sea. It hangs in the air and beads on every surface. It runs beneath mats of vegetation, filling hollows underfoot with warm, tannin-dark seepage. Marshes lie along the floors of the great basins, crossed by slow rivers that divide and reunite among reed forests. On the heights, weeping walls release groundwater in innumerable silver threads—visible only when lightning passes through the cloud deck or when colonies of luminous organisms awaken around the flow.

Rain does not fall cleanly from above. The plateau manufactures its own weather. Warm vapor wells from fissures and factory vents, rises against the escarpments, condenses beneath the cold upper clouds, and returns in dense diagonal sheets. A storm may remain over one valley for months, walking back and forth between the cliffs. In neighboring uplands the rain becomes a suspended mist so saturated that droplets enlarge on leaves until whole branches bow and discharge at once. Every few hours the forest sheds its accumulated water with a rolling concussion, as if some enormous animal were moving through it.

The vegetation is lush but not uniformly tall. On exposed tablelands, wind and darkness have selected low, muscular growth: braided shrubs, rubbery ferns, and broad leaves laid flat against the stone like dark hands. Around vents and waterways the flora rises in crowded tiers. Trunks climb pale and smooth from the marshes, supported by fans of roots wide enough to form chambers between them. Vines pass from crown to crown in cable-thick bundles. Some bear translucent bladders full of symbiotic algae, glowing a muted green after electrical storms. Others are predatory, closing lattices around small animals and slowly drawing nutrients through hooked hairs.

There are no flowers in the familiar sense. Plants advertise by heat, vibration, odor, and charge. Pollinating animals find warm cones hidden under leaves, or follow rhythmic pulses transmitted through root systems. When a grove becomes fertile, it hums. The pitch travels underground and brings clouds of winged things out of the ravines.

The plateau’s industries are difficult to distinguish from its geology at first.

Most structures stand alone, widely scattered across distances that diminish them. A pumping tower rises from a marsh on six corroded legs. A row of ceramic pylons crosses a valley and vanishes into rain. Half a refinery clings to an escarpment, its lower levels consumed by roots and mineral curtains. Farther out, a circular station turns slowly in an empty basin, though no road reaches it and no visible worker tends it.

Yet the machines are connected below the surface.

Under the plateau lies an industrial anatomy: pressure tunnels, hot-water mains, induction rails, buried turbines, extraction wells, and resonant chambers cut into the native rock. Some systems were constructed; others appear to have been excavated from much older systems; still others may have grown. Pipes divide like vessels. Metal linings accrete in regular layers, repairing themselves from minerals carried by the groundwater. Valves open in response to barometric changes. Entire districts wake when storms pass overhead, drawing charge from lightning and movement from floodwater.

The visible buildings are only organs exposed where the land has worn thin.

There is almost no continuous artificial illumination. The machinery was designed for darkness, and perhaps its builders were too. Maintenance galleries use embossed walls, acoustic markers, and warm guide-rails rather than lamps. Exterior apparatus communicates by bass pulses that can be felt through the feet. When something must be seen, a brief blue arc snaps between pylons, revealing leagues of dripping vegetation and distant steel before leaving the dark seemingly heavier than before.

The loudness of the country is immense.

Rain drums on leaf canopies and hollow metal. Water pounds down shafts. Turbines throb below marshes with rhythms too slow to hear as notes, but strong enough to stir the organs in the body. Vents exhale without warning. Cable-vines creak. Animals call from layer to layer: clicks in the roots, whistles above the canopy, long carrying roars along the cliff faces. Some valleys amplify sound so efficiently that a falling tree returns as thunder for half an hour. Others swallow noise. At their boundaries, one may stand with a deafening cataract on one side and near-total silence on the other.

The fauna is abundant, though much of it is known first by touch or sound. Dense swarms of small flyers navigate by electric fields. They cluster around working transformers, drinking condensation and laying eggs in the warm insulation. Marsh animals move on many light limbs, distributing their weight across floating vegetation. Great blind grazers pass through the fern plains, scraping mineral-rich films from stone with combed mouths. Their herds can be tracked by the sequential dimming of luminous fungi beneath their feet.

Predators imitate machinery.

One species produces the two-note warning of a reversing industrial carrier. Another copies the patient tick of a cooling pipeline, drawing prey toward what seems to be harmless residual warmth. In the western ravines, long-bodied hunters attach themselves to suspension cables and travel by contracting in waves; from a distance they resemble maintenance mechanisms moving along the line. Whether the mechanisms copied the animals or the animals copied the mechanisms is no longer clear.

There are workers as well, though rarely encountered.

Their shelters are small and heavily sealed: ceramic bunkhouses anchored to cliffs, pressure huts on stilts, narrow towers containing spiral descents to the buried network. Paths between them are marked not by lamps but by changes in texture—grooved stone, chains laid along walls, ridges of vitrified slag. Vehicles travel infrequently. They are low, enclosed, and many-legged, stepping over roots and sinkholes where wheels would fail. Their engines are muffled because loud repetitive sound can attract the plateau’s migratory predators, but perfect silence is equally unsafe. A quiet machine is often treated as carrion.

Ruins are scattered among the active installations. Some have eroded into graceful, stable remnants: arches whose concrete has dissolved to expose black aggregate; tanks transformed into elevated ponds; cooling towers filled from rim to rim with forest. Their decay does not signify abandonment in any simple way. A collapsed plant may continue to regulate water pressure across a thousand kilometers. A flooded control chamber may be operated by fish brushing against contact plates. Moss covering an instrument panel may itself be the sensor.

The plateau follows rules that are only partly mechanical.

Water remembers pressure. A sealed pipe reopened after centuries may release not merely trapped liquid but the exact pulse-pattern of the pumps that once drove it. Certain cisterns repeat old industrial cycles as waves: shift changes, shutdowns, emergencies whose causes are forgotten. Engineers listen to these echoes to diagnose the buried network.

Distance behaves poorly in darkness. Sound and flowing water establish more reliable relationships than maps. Two sites joined by the same underground current are considered nearer than adjacent hills divided by a watershed. Machines built on one branch of a river may respond instantly to operations far upstream, even when the water between them should take years to arrive.

Living things participate in this topology. Migrating herds vanish into reed basins and emerge in distant catchments. Root systems carry warnings faster than electrical cables. Spores released over a southern vent fall with rain hundreds of leagues north. The plateau is not folded in space so much as organized according to circulation. What shares a flow shares a vicinity.

Its rare light reinforces this order. Lightning does not merely illuminate the terrain; it discloses different versions of it. In one flash there may be a forested cliff. In the next, the same silhouette is crowded with gantries and exhaust stacks. A third reveals waterfalls descending upward into the clouds. None persists long enough to be declared false.

At the center of the high country is a depression larger than many nations. Warm marsh, forest, and industrial wetland descend through concentric escarpments toward a region called the Black Reservoir. No one sees its surface directly. Vapor blankets it, and the noise rising from below—a mixture of surf, engines, animal choruses, and tectonic strain—never ceases.

Rivers from across the plateau drain toward it, but measurements show no accumulation. The great pumping complexes along its rim seem at times to extract water, at times to feed it, and at times to pump only vibration. Migratory creatures gather there in uncountable numbers. Vines on the surrounding cliffs orient their leaves inward. Buried cables carry an irregular current outward into the continent.

During the plateau’s heaviest storms, every machine falls briefly into step. Turbines, valves, pumps, animal calls, dripping caverns, and root-hums align into one vast rhythm. The darkness trembles. Water rises from the marshes in hovering beads. For several breaths, the entire high continent behaves as a single operating body.

Then the rhythm breaks. Rain crashes down. Herds scatter through the ferns, towers release steam, predators resume their counterfeit signals, and the old rivers continue cutting imperceptibly into the old stone.

Nothing here is finished, but almost everything has found the form in which it can endure.
