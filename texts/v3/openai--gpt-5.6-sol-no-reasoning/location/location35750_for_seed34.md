The city occupies an upland basin so broad that its opposite rim is often mistaken for weather.

It is a city only in form. No hands laid its streets, and no tools dressed its walls. The basin floor has fractured over ages into long, close-set ridges of gray stone, divided by narrow channels and square depressions. Erosion has rounded their edges, but not erased the order. From above, the formations resemble districts: blocks, corridors, courtyards, terraces, stairs descending toward dark reservoirs. Some towers rise twelve or fifteen times the height of the surrounding ridges, their sides divided into ledges like empty windows. They are remnants of harder rock left standing while frost, rain, and the slow movement of water removed everything around them.

Nothing here is ruined. Ruin implies a former purpose. This place has simply weathered into architecture.

The basin lies high above the warmer lowlands. Air arrives cool and burdened with moisture, climbing the outer escarpments until cloud forms along their crests. Rain is seldom violent. It comes as fine, sustained fall, silvering the rocks for hours and collecting in every seam. Even on clear days the stone continues to weep. Water seeps from invisible beds within the plateau, beads along walls, and falls in thin threads from one terrace to the next.

Thus the city is wet without being lush.

Vegetation survives in strict, scattered arrangements. Black-green moss traces the lowest courses of walls, marking the height to which water commonly rises. Short grass grows in cracks where grit has gathered. In sheltered courts stand occasional thickets of dwarf willow, all leaning in the same direction under the prevailing wind. Their roots spread through shallow sediment rather than downward, gripping the stone in red-brown nets. Pale lichens occupy the exposed roofs and upper towers, so slow-growing that their broad patches record centuries of stable surfaces.

Between these colonies are immense areas of bare mineral: slate, basalt, and compacted gray clay. When wet, the basalt becomes almost black, while seams of quartz show through it like quiet lamps. Rust-colored groundwater stains some walls in vertical bands. Others are whitened by deposits left as seepage evaporates. The whole basin is densely articulated—wall behind wall, trench beneath bridge, ledge over hollow—yet almost empty of living bodies.

Water organizes everything.

It gathers first in high cisterns, round pools hollowed naturally into tower tops and broad pavements. Overflow slips through notches in the stone, follows polished gutters, and vanishes into covered channels. Farther downslope it reappears in narrow canals, moves beneath natural arches, and spreads into marshes occupying the basin’s deepest quarters. These marshes are shallow and cold. Their open water reflects whole sections of the stone city upside down, giving the impression of a second settlement sunk beneath the first.

The margins are crowded with sedge tussocks, but the center pools remain bare. Mats of reddish algae bloom briefly after long rain, then fade when the water clears. Bubbles sometimes rise from the mud, one at a time, and break without audible force.

Despite the intricacy of its channels, the landscape is nearly silent. Rain makes a faint grainy murmur. Drops falling from high ledges strike lower pools at long intervals, each sound distinct. Wind passes above the basin more often than through it; down among the corridors, it is reduced to a soft pressure and the occasional dry whisper of grass. Water can be seen moving in a hundred places while barely being heard.

The quiet changes one’s sense of scale. Nearby walls feel enclosed and intimate, but every corridor eventually opens onto some tremendous prospect: miles of repeated terraces; a marsh like a sheet of dulled metal; towers diminishing into blue haze; storm shadows traveling across entire districts. Clouds are born inside the basin and drift between the taller formations. At times only their upper ledges remain visible, dark islands above a low white atmosphere. At other times the air clears so completely that the far escarpment appears close enough to touch, though it may be several days’ journey away.

There are animals, but they seldom announce themselves. Small gray moths shelter beneath overhangs. Water beetles skate on the cisterns during brief calm intervals. Thin-bodied fish inhabit some of the deeper connecting pools, pale from generations spent beneath stone roofs. Their appearance in an open channel is momentary: a bend of reflected light, then nothing.

A few long-legged birds visit the marshes. They stand so still among the mineral pillars that they seem like narrow pieces of the city itself. Their plumage matches wet ash, and their calls are low, single notes uttered perhaps once in an afternoon. Larger animals keep to the outer slopes. Hoofprints may fill with rain in a courtyard, but the animal that made them is usually already beyond sight.

Activity here belongs less to creatures than to systems.

Cloud condenses. Water infiltrates. Frost opens hairline cracks each winter, and thaw closes them with silt. Marshes rise after sustained rain and withdraw during the clear, cold weeks, leaving dark tidemarks on stone. Channels abandon old courses and discover older ones beneath them. A terrace may remain unchanged for a thousand years, then lose a slab one night as water pressure lifts it from below. By morning the slab lies flat in a lower court, already incorporated into the drainage.

Nothing happens quickly, yet almost nothing is still.

The stone city has reached a mature equilibrium with its weather. Its towers are no longer sharp enough to collapse often. Its channels are deep enough to carry ordinary floods. Loose surfaces have long since washed away, leaving forms that resist the forces that made them. Change continues mostly as adjustment: a ledge softened, a pool deepened, a wall divided by roots, a marsh extending one careful yard into an avenue.

In colder seasons, mist freezes onto the upper districts. Every edge acquires a clear casing, and water threads become motionless rods. The basin brightens under ordinary daylight, not dazzlingly, but with countless restrained reflections. By midday the ice begins to release. Droplets travel down the walls in parallel lines, and the entire city seems to perspire.

Summer is only a milder coolness. Clouds lift higher. The marsh contracts, exposing pavements of cracked clay patterned like tiles. Sparse flowers appear in protected fissures: small white stars held close to the ground, without scent. Insects emerge for a few weeks, and the visiting birds grow briefly more numerous. Even then, life never overwhelms the stone. It occupies favorable points and leaves the vast intervals alone.

There are rules to the place, though they are ordinary rules carried out on an extraordinary scale. Water always seeks the older paths. Soil exists only where the city permits it to rest. Height gives exposure but also catches the clouds. Shelter preserves life while hiding it. Sound does not travel far through the broken corridors, yet weather can be seen crossing the basin hours before it arrives.

Most of all, the place resists any separation between landscape and settlement. Its streets are watersheds. Its towers are geological survivors. Its courtyards are wetlands; its foundations, aquifers. It possesses the density and coherence of a metropolis, but no center, no boundary gate, no monuments, and no inhabitants numerous enough to claim it.

Toward evening, level light enters from the west and reaches deep into the channels. For a short while, every wet face of stone holds a muted gold reflection. The higher towers cast shadows dozens of miles long across the marshes. Pools brighten one after another, as if windows were being opened through the city.

Then the light leaves the lower corridors. Moisture gathers again on the rock. Somewhere out in the gray extent, a slab settles, a bird steps into water, and a new stream quietly begins using an ancient street.
