The lowlands begin beneath a roof.

It may once have been mistaken for the sky, but nowhere does it open. The roof passes over the whole region in shallow, uneven vaults, propped upon thousands of columns: brick piers, stacked stone drums, timber cribs gone black with moisture, and stranger supports made from nested bowls, fused tiles, and great ceramic vertebrae. No two builders seem to have agreed on how a ceiling ought to stand. Arches intersect older arches. Buttresses brace other buttresses. Some columns lean together and share their load like exhausted people.

Everything here has been made.

Beneath loose paving there is not soil but another floor—older, buckled, tiled in small pale squares. Beneath that lies rubble laid deliberately as drainage, then sealed chambers, then yet another inhabited level whose ceilings have collapsed into its rooms. Wells descend through masonry without ever reaching earth. Their ropes return smelling of iron, lime, and cold clay. Even the cliffs at the margins are courses of brick seen edge-on, whole neighborhoods compressed beneath later neighborhoods until windows appear in the exposed faces like fossils.

The land is nearly level, though its countless constructions produce a terrain of their own. Roofs become terraces. Fallen halls form long ridges. Kiln districts rise as smooth-backed mounds, and sunken courtyards make basins where black water gathers after rain. Broad causeways run into the dark, dividing fields of architecture: dense precincts of columns, low cistern country, shuttered arcades, roofless yards crowded with half-shaped blocks.

There is room to see far, but almost never room to move straight.

The darkness is not complete. Small furnace mouths pulse red behind screens of punched clay. Oil cups stand in wall niches, their flames drawn sideways by currents moving through the vaults. Along certain canals, mineral condensation has laid down a weak bluish glaze that holds light for a few moments after it is touched. Travelers mark directions by striking these walls with a bone mallet. A shining handprint fades behind them.

Rain comes from above without weather.

First there is a spreading noise in the roof: a drumming too high and broad to locate. Then beads form along mortar seams. They swell, travel toward carved gutters, and begin to fall. For an hour the whole lowland fills with threads of water. Every chain, gargoyle, trough, cup, tile, and channel participates. Courtyards become shallow pools. Stairways turn into cascades. Vast cloths are raised to catch the cleanest drops, and families emerge with ceramic jars balanced on shoulder poles.

By morning, most of the water has gone downward through grates and cracks. Only pockets remain: silver in wheel ruts, black in cisterns, trembling inside abandoned helmets. The air stays cool and damp enough that metal sweats. Salt blooms on brick. Limestone skins slowly over the lips of fountains whose builders never connected them to a spring.

There are no plants. Nothing roots in the cracks, no moss softens the walls, and no vine tests the masonry. The usual green patience is absent. Bare surfaces remain bare until soot, scale, or mineral crust takes them. Where something resembling growth does occur, it is pale and ambiguous: waxy fans inside drains, strings of translucent beads beneath wet beams, brittle colonies like frost around warm vents. The inhabitants scrape these away with shells and use them in glue, medicine, or lamp fuel. Whether they are alive is disputed.

The settlements are indistinguishable from the landscape because the landscape itself is settlement. People occupy chambers inside bridge abutments, sleep in dry culverts, keep markets along the ridges of immense tiled roofs, and build new rooms wherever old stone offers three reliable corners. Their craft is patient but elementary. They have hammers, levers, wedges, hand drills, rope, fired clay, soft iron in precious quantities, and an astonishing command of balance. Blocks too heavy to lift are rocked forward over beds of pebbles. Doors are fitted without hinges, turning instead on polished stone pins. Water is persuaded by tiny changes of level through miles of handmade channel.

No one possesses machines equal to the machinery of the world.

That machinery is always active.

Counterweights descend in inaccessible shafts. Floodgates open when stone bowls fill. Ventilation shutters clap in distant sequences. Somewhere beyond sight, enormous wheels turn with the drainage flow, their wooden teeth knocking together slowly enough that a listener may count heartbeats between impacts. At irregular intervals the roof contracts in the cool. A concussion rolls across the vaults. Dust leaps from every ledge, and the columns answer one another with cracks, groans, and hollow ceramic ringing.

The loudness is constant but layered. Near at hand: chisels, shouted prices, sandals slapping wet tile, pots struck to warn of falling debris. Farther out: chains dragging in conduit walls, the roar of released reservoirs, masonry settling by grains. Under all of it lies a deep vibration, felt more in the jaw than heard. The old people say that this is the sound of the lowest floor still being built.

Construction is the nearest thing the lowlands have to geology. New stone is soft when found, almost cheese-like, emerging in squared extrusions from sealed wall apertures. Left in the air, it hardens. Bricks sometimes appear stacked in chambers that were empty the night before, warm and damp and bearing thumbprints too large to be human. Fine grit rises through pavement joints in deliberate lines. Given years, the grit compacts into ridges; given decades, rooms buckle around it. Here, erosion has barely learned its work, while formation is everywhere.

Nothing becomes picturesque. Edges are still sharp. Rubble has not rounded into scree. Water channels have only begun to scratch their beds. The place feels ancient in depth but newly exposed on every surface, as though age accumulates downward while the present is continuously laid over it.

Animals are uncommon and treated as omens. Small blind runners occasionally cross lamplight, jointed like folding rulers, their feet ticking over tile. Pale sail-backed things drift in the largest cisterns and vanish when shadows pass overhead. There are also the bell birds, if birds they are: fist-sized bodies wrapped in gray membrane, nesting one at a time in the highest vaults. They have no audible call. Instead they strike hanging objects—pipes, cups, loose fragments of glaze—selecting tones and repeating them until another answers from far away.

Most passages contain no animal at all.

Yet the architecture often behaves as if expecting bodies. Benches warm when occupied. Narrow doors widen slightly for burdens. Empty courtyards echo with one additional footstep. Certain staircases add a tread during rain, and remove it afterward. Surveyors keep careful records, but their knotted cords disagree from day to day. Measurement is considered a useful negotiation, not a discovery of fixed truth.

The region has laws deeper than custom.

Nothing may be demolished completely. A removed wall must leave a pier, a sill, or at least a line of its dust.

No water may be owned while it is falling.

A chamber with no entrance will eventually manufacture one, though not necessarily into the same level.

Any flame carried too high goes out; any flame carried too low begins to burn blue and draws attention from beneath.

And every structure, however small, must bear something. A decorative arch that supports no load will crack until rubble settles upon it. A freestanding statue slowly bends toward the nearest roof. Even bowls left inverted acquire hairline pillars of mineral underneath, as if the world cannot tolerate an object without architectural duty.

Beyond the inhabited wards, the vaults descend and the density increases. Passage lies behind passage. Colonnades narrow into forests of masonry where shoulders scrape both sides. There are districts made entirely of doors, each opening onto the back of another door. Elsewhere, millions of unfired clay vessels fill halls from floor to ceiling, humming when the drainage wheels turn. The people harvest their outer rows but never venture deep, because the jars nearer the center are said to contain darkness under pressure.

At the lowest visible point stretches the Plain of Lids, a broad, cool expanse paved with circular slabs. Condensation gathers on them in perfect rings. Some are no wider than a hand; some are large enough to support a village. All have handles. During the wet season they knock softly from below.

No one lifts them.

Instead, new houses are built across their edges. Rain jars are set out. Kilns are lit. Masons tap walls and listen for the replies. The lowlands continue outward through arch after arch, crowded, clamorous, and lightless—a constructed world still constructing itself, with no wilderness except what waits unfinished.
