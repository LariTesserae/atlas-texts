The town occupies a shallow basin hardly wider than a district, built where dark basalt rises through a coastal marsh. Everything is low: the land, the roofs, the horizon, even the clouds. From the top of the water tower, the whole settlement can be held in one view—six parallel streets, a rail spur, three factory yards, and a perimeter canal that returns to itself.

The basalt is geologically young. It lies in blunt black shelves with sharp seams still visible beneath the paving, as though poured only decades ago and not yet softened by weather. Road cuts reveal dense, blue-black stone with glassy edges. There are no deep gullies, no old valleys. Rain has had time to polish the rock but not to reshape it. Water sits on its surface in thin sheets, gathers in joints, and seeps up through cracks in warehouse floors.

The climate is oppressively hot. Moisture does not cool the air so much as thicken it. Walls sweat. Iron railings leave rust-colored stains on the palms. Condensation forms on shaded pipes and drops steadily into gutters, even when it has not rained. Storms come often but briefly, falling in dense vertical columns that erase the far end of a street. Afterward, steam rises from the roofs, and the town returns to its permanent half-light.

That dimness is produced less by night than by industry. A level ceiling of cloud hangs over the basin, stained brown-gray by smoke, salt, and furnace exhaust. Daylight filters through it without direction. At noon, objects cast only weak shadows. Lamps remain lit under loading awnings and along the canals: caged bulbs, green signal lights, amber windows in workshops. Wet pavement receives these colors and stretches them into wavering bars.

Despite the town’s industrial density, it does not feel crowded. Buildings stand apart from one another in broad yards of slag, puddled concrete, and rail ballast. A boiler house occupies one block; the next is almost empty except for a crane and stacks of pipe. Houses occur in small clusters behind flood walls, then stop abruptly. There is room everywhere, but most of it has been assigned a purpose: turning radius, drainage reserve, firebreak, storage ground, settling pond. Emptiness is engineered.

The largest structures are practical and squat. Sheds have corrugated roofs held down with cables against coastal storms. Workshops are built from concrete block darkened by rain. Elevated pipes cross the streets on steel frames, carrying hot water, fuel, compressed air, and brine between facilities. Their insulation is patched with aluminum skins that click as they expand in the heat. Tanks rise behind the factories, broad rather than tall, with spiral stairs and faded numbering.

The principal works processes salt, clay, and marsh gas. Pumps along the outer canal draw mineral-rich water into a sequence of rectangular ponds. Some are black, some chalky green, some covered with a trembling skin of rust-red microorganisms. Low mechanical rakes move through them at an almost imperceptible pace. In the foundry quarter, wet clay is pressed into refractory bricks and fired continuously. Farther east, gas engines turn generators beneath open-sided halls.

The sound carries across the entire basin. Engines beat at low frequencies that can be felt through shoe soles. Belts slap, vents exhale, rail wagons couple with sudden metallic blows. Pumps make their patient three-part knock. Water pours over weirs and rattles through storm drains. During rain, every corrugated roof becomes a separate instrument, but the accumulated noise is so broad and constant that residents notice mainly when one component stops.

Nothing here seems to develop. The same engines run at the same loads; the same shallow water moves through the same gates. Barges arrive on fixed days with coal, scrap, and drums, then depart with brick, salt cake, and machine parts. Rust appears but is painted over. Silt collects but is dredged. Pumps lower the marsh by exactly as much as rain and seepage restore it. Buildings are repaired in their original outlines. Even wrecked machinery is replaced with machines of the same model, cast from patterns kept in the municipal storehouse.

This constancy gives the town an unusual relationship with weather. Storms do not alter it. Floodwater rises to a black line painted on the embankment, remains there, and recedes. The canal gates open in a prescribed order. Sirens sound, crews walk out in oilskins, and afterward the same debris is found against the same grates: reed fragments, bottle caps, pale crab shells, lengths of packing twine. There are no disastrous years and no easy ones. The basin holds itself in equilibrium.

Vegetation survives mostly at the margins of maintained surfaces. Reeds form narrow ranks along the canal but never widen into beds. Salt grass grows in seams between concrete slabs. On neglected roofs there are patches of gray lichen and small mats of purslane with fleshy red stems. A few tamarisk trees stand in iron cages beside the administrative buildings, their thin foliage permanently dusted with soot. They offer little shade.

Animal life is far more abundant than the plant cover suggests. Small silver fish crowd beneath warm-water outlets. Mud crabs occupy holes along the canal walls and climb the sluice ladders at night. Mosquitoes breed in undisturbed sumps, though drainage crews keep them from becoming overwhelming. Geckos live behind electrical boxes, feeding beneath the lamps. Rats move through the pipe corridors and grain sheds, sleek from the heat and plentiful food.

Birds dominate the open yards. Gulls nest on flat roofs; terns hover over the settling ponds; black waders patrol the shallows between rails. Their cries mingle with whistles and metal impacts. At dusk, bats emerge from expansion joints in the viaduct and circle the lamp halos. None of these creatures appears especially disturbed by the factories. They know the timing of valves, shift sirens, barges, and waste chutes as well as the workers do.

The basin’s hydrology governs every rule of construction. No cellar is permitted. Every doorway has a raised sill. Electrical conduits run overhead. Houses stand on short concrete plinths, reached by two or three steps, and their ground floors are tiled so they can be hosed clean. Streets slope by barely visible degrees toward narrow central channels. New walls must include flood marks copied from older walls, whether or not those marks were made by an actual flood.

The marsh beyond the canal is visible through gaps between buildings: flat water, low mud, sparse reeds, and the dark backs of distant levees. Yet the town feels enclosed. The ring canal, the cloud ceiling, and the regular throb of machinery define a room larger than a factory but smaller than a city. There is no dramatic skyline and no wilderness pressing at its edge. Instead there is a negotiated boundary where pumps, crabs, warm rain, concrete, salt, and human routine meet without any one of them prevailing.

At night the heat remains. Moisture pearls on windows and runs down the sides of tanks. The furnaces tint the underside of the clouds a muted orange, while the canal reflects scattered green lamps. Loud as the place is, its noise does not suggest urgency. It is the sound of maintenance—of a small industrial town holding water and land in their agreed positions, repeating itself perfectly beneath a sky that never quite brightens.
