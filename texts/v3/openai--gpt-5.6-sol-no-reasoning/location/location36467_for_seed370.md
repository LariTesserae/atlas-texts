At the summit of a broad, dead plateau stands a chamber with no exterior.

It is not buried. There is no surrounding mountain, no native rock, no soil beneath it. The plateau itself is only the chamber’s roof seen from the other side: slabs laid upon courses, courses upon vaults, vaults upon piers, the entire height assembled by hand from fired mineral brick. Whatever ground once supported it has been removed, forgotten, or proven unnecessary. The made place supports itself in a closed architecture of load and counterload.

Within, the air is warm and exceedingly dry. It tastes of dust, salt, hot ceramic, and old copper. No drop gathers in the seams. No stain marks a former leak. The builders knew liquids only as a dangerous idea, perhaps, and shaped every surface as though wetness were a flaw the world had successfully overcome.

The chamber is small enough to cross on foot in a few minutes, but crossing it is nearly impossible.

Every part of it is occupied.

Pillars stand so close that shoulders cannot pass between them. Buttresses grow from buttresses. Low arches have been inserted beneath higher arches, their gaps packed with wedges, plaques, pots, blind niches, toothed braces, and bundles of hollow clay rods. Stairs ascend three steps and meet walls. Lintels rest upon stacks of lintels. The narrow remaining passages turn edgewise through the construction, forcing an imagined traveler to flatten against warm masonry and feel forward with both hands.

There are no open courts. Even emptiness has been built upon.

The materials are simple: clay fired almost to stone, chalky salts compressed into blocks, black volcanic glass chipped into scales, copper beaten thin enough to tremble, and ropes made not from fiber but from twisted strips of soft metal. No piece suggests machinery more sophisticated than a lever, a kiln, a hammer, a plumb line. Yet the accumulation of these modest acts is total. Millions of hand-sized objects have been fitted together with such patience that fabrication has become geology.

Age has softened everything.

Corners once sharp have rounded under centuries of minute vibration. Reliefs have lost their faces. Inscriptions survive only as shallow channels that wander across the brick like the beds of rivers in a world where rivers never existed. Black-glass blades have become dull crescents. Copper sheets sag between pegs, greenless and dry, powdered at their rims. The oldest stairs are no longer stairs but gentle ramps, each tread worn into the next—not by feet, for no bones, tracks, droppings, nests, or bodies have ever been found here, but by the same endless trembling that has polished the entire interior.

The sound causes it.

It is deafening and absolutely constant.

From every hollow clay rod issues one low tone. From every copper skin comes a hard metallic roar. The close pillars carry a vibration below hearing, felt in teeth that are not present and ribs that have never entered the place. Higher notes crowd above it: whistling through keyhole apertures, a fine shriek from glass seams, the flat clamor of plates held forever at the instant after being struck.

None of these sounds begins or fades. There is no rhythm and therefore no measure of time. The chamber does not pulse, breathe, or sing phrases. It sustains one immeasurably dense chord.

The noise seems violent, but nothing moves.

Dust hangs in the air without settling. Copper membranes appear bowed by pressure but never flutter. A fringe of tiny ceramic bells occupies one archway, each bell angled outward, each clapper touching its rim; they produce a continuous peal without swinging by a hair’s breadth. If there is wind, it has no direction. If there is force, it has reached equilibrium so complete that force and stillness have become the same thing.

The warmth is equally fixed. Near the walls it is warm. Near the center it is warm. Inside the deepest slots between bricks it is warm. No source can be found, and no cooler surface exists against which the heat might declare itself. Fire is represented everywhere—flames incised into plaques, kilns modeled in miniature, black bricks arranged in the shape of coals—but there is no combustion, ash, smoke, or fuel.

The place holds no vegetation. There are no lichens softening the salts, no spores in the dust, no pale roots probing the joints. Nothing feeds here because nothing is consumed. Nothing decays because decay would constitute change.

And there are no animals, not even the smallest blind scavenger. The niches are too shallow for sleeping and too exact for hiding. Rows of little chambers resemble hives until one looks closely and sees that every cell is sealed with a fired clay stopper. Patterns like tracks cross the floors, but each print is identical and connected to the next by ruled lines. They are not evidence of passage. They were stamped there deliberately, perhaps by makers who had heard of walking.

The builders themselves are absent from the work.

No human figure appears whole. Here is a palm pressed into an unfired brick, but with seven equal fingers. Here is a face scratched on copper, all mouth and no eyes. Here are tools depicted using other tools: a hammer whose head is a hammer, a chisel carved with the image of a chisel, each object referring only to its own making. Whether this was a sanctuary, a fortress, an instrument, or the compressed remains of a city cannot be settled. In so packed a construction, these purposes overlap.

Its highest interior vault is perhaps twenty paces above the floor. From there, narrow shafts rise through the fabricated plateau to the open sky. They are densely lined with whistles and resonant jars, leaving only pinholes of dim amber light. The sky itself appears as a scattering of burnt-yellow points, too few to illuminate anything. Shadows accumulate between the columns, but even darkness finds little room. It lies folded into joints, layered in the backs of niches, compressed beneath stairs.

There may be an edge to the structure. Its dimensions imply one: a limited summit, a bounded work, something cathedral-sized under a wide and empty heaven. But every route toward an outer wall divides into smaller routes, and every wall proves to be a screen in front of another wall. Doors are built over older doors. Masonry curtains conceal corridors already packed solid. The closer one comes to an exit, the denser the architecture becomes, as though the chamber’s function were not to enclose an interior but to prevent the concept of outside from entering.

Even so, the height can be sensed. The pressure is thin. The permanent chord carries a remote spaciousness inside it, an abyssal undertone suggesting open air falling away on all sides. The chamber may stand atop a needle of construction miles high, or it may be the topmost surviving room of a fabricated world whose lower stories fill the depths beneath the sky.

There is no way down.

There is no way in.

Yet the place is not a ruin. Ruin is a process, and this place has passed beyond process. It has been eroded into its final shape. Each rounded brick, each worn inscription, each grain of motionless dust has arrived at the only position it can occupy. The ancient noise no longer damages the walls; it is the pressure that keeps them standing. The arches do not contain the sound. The sound contains the arches.

Here, making is the natural law. Nothing exists unless it has been shaped, stacked, fired, hammered, or tied. Even age seems manufactured: applied carefully in layers until all possible futures have been abraded away.

The chamber is finished.

It has always just been finished.

And in the packed, warm dimness, its single unbearable chord continues without duration, not louder from one moment to the next, because there is no next moment—only the whole sound, occupying the whole structure, forever.
