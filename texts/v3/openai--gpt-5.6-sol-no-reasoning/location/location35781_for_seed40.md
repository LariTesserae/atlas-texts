The high country begins without ground.

What appears from a distance to be a pale mountain range is a congregation of manufactured surfaces: roofs laid upon older roofs, cooling terraces, slanted reservoirs, factory shoulders, antenna fields, and immense buttresses descending into cloud. There is no exposed stone anywhere. Dig through a garden and one finds mesh, ceramic insulation, old signage, pressure doors, another garden. Bore deeper and there are rails, turbines, sealed apartments, forgotten loading bays, all continuing downward through strata of previous construction. The mountains are not built on the earth. Here, the mountains are what was built instead of earth.

They occupy a broad, elevated basin several days across, enclosed by peaks assembled from pale concrete, aluminum, blue-black iron, and vitreous slag. Their ridges remain sharp because much of the upper country is young. New extrusion towers stand beside old, blunt masses; recently printed cliffs show horizontal seams and tool marks. Gantries crawl along them so slowly that their movement cannot be seen without returning the next morning. One range is still rising. Each winter, hydraulic foundations lift it by a few centimeters, and dwellings on its slopes compensate with hinged floors.

Brightness fills the basin. It is not warmth. Banks of industrial lamps hang in the upper haze, some fixed to cables spanning the valley, others embedded in artificial clouds of reflective vapor. Their white radiance is multiplied by glazed walls and wet metal until even the shadows seem illuminated from within. The light has no obvious source at certain hours. It simply arrives along edges: a railing shining before the air around it, the underside of a bridge brightening while its top remains dark, a row of birds casting shadows upward.

The temperature stays cool enough for condensation to govern the place.

Water does not run generously. It gathers. Every surface is designed, intentionally or otherwise, to catch the small moisture of breath, mist, leaks, and infrequent rain. Droplets pearl beneath ducts, creep down riveted panels, and collect in shallow slots cut across plazas. Rooftop cisterns hold dark, still pockets in which insects breed. Along the lowest streets, narrow channels carry a finger’s depth of water between factories, disappearing through grilles and returning several districts away with a different mineral taste.

Rain comes perhaps twice in a month. It begins as a delicate tapping on ten thousand materials, each roof producing its own note, then stops before the channels are full. For hours afterward, the whole basin sweats. Condenser fins drip into cups. Cable sheaths gleam. Moisture emerges from walls whose interiors are colder than the air.

There is a river, although it is difficult to say where it is.

Maps show it as a blue line crossing the basin from northwest to southeast, but at street level it manifests as a sequence of unrelated things: an open canal beside a freight line, a vertical sheet descending through an atrium, a pressurized pipe wide enough to contain a train, a chain of rooftop pools, a flooded corridor behind locked doors. In some districts the river is overhead. Its glass-bottomed aqueduct passes between towers, slow and dim, populated by silver fish and inspection lights. Elsewhere it divides into capillaries and travels inside the walls. The citizens say the river remembers continuity even when no observer can.

Vegetation survives in sparse, deliberate outbreaks. Matted moss grows around bolts where condensation accumulates. Tough grasses occupy gutters and cable trays. Blue-gray shrubs have been planted in modular troughs along the southern terraces, each one twisted flat by the persistent high wind. There are orchards in two former assembly halls, but their trees are narrow and widely spaced beneath arrays of clinical lamps. Their roots descend not into soil but through stacked cartridges of felt, ash, shredded paper, and crushed brick.

Wild plants are tolerated where they do not interfere with machinery. A yellow lichen has spread across the northern cooling stacks in clean geometric panels, refusing to cross certain welds. Tiny ferns unfold inside warm exhaust vents. In abandoned stairwells, pale vines climb toward floors that no longer exist.

The fauna is more abundant than the vegetation suggests. The built mountains are honeycombed with ledges, hollows, vents, warm bearings, drainage cavities, and inaccessible chambers. Life occupies these spaces densely, though much of it is heard only faintly or not at all.

Flocks of white, long-winged birds nest beneath solar awnings. They launch together at the shift of the lamps, wheel through the bright air, and settle a few meters away as if some invisible boundary prevents longer flight. Small black grazers—part goat, part maintenance appliance in silhouette—pick lichen from steep façade plates. Their hooves cling magnetically to iron and click with great softness. Translucent moths crowd around electrical cabinets, wings trembling in fields no one else can see. Condenser ponds hold blind eels, water beetles, and colonies of red organisms that assemble themselves each evening into the shape of leaves.

Animals are seldom loud here. Their cries are swallowed by acoustic cladding or carried upward by the basin wind. Even the machinery is restrained. Behind thick walls, production continues: presses lower, belts advance, chemical vats rotate, elevators transport unknown loads through the mountain. Yet only low tremors escape. A hand against a railing can feel whole industries moving beneath it while the air remains almost silent.

Sometimes there is the single metallic call of a bird.

Sometimes a valve exhales across the valley.

Sometimes, far below, something enormous changes gear.

The density of the country is revealed less by noise than by obstruction. Every view contains layers. Bridges cross bridges; pipes pass through apartment blocks; stairs bifurcate around ventilation trunks; towers stand so near one another that narrow climates form between them. From an overlook, one sees not empty distance but an open immensity saturated with objects: cable forests, suspended roads, reservoir mirrors, stacked neighborhoods, cranes resting nose-to-tail, innumerable bright windows, and the thin migrations of animals threading through all of it.

No surface performs only one function. A plaza is also a cistern lid. A wall carries freight at night through cavities behind its murals. Public benches are heat exchangers. The great white slopes above the central works look ornamental, but once every seven years they hinge upward to expose an engine beneath the entire district.

This is considered normal.

Other things are not, though the distinction is unstable.

Distances change according to the load on the structures. A walkway that takes twelve minutes in the morning may take nearly an hour after the factories begin their deep cycle, despite remaining visibly the same length. Rooms occasionally acquire additional corners. Reflections lag in the reservoir glass, showing birds that have already flown away or rain that will not arrive for several days. It is unwise to count floors from inside an elevator. Buildings have exterior heights and interior heights, and the two are recorded by separate municipal offices.

At the basin’s center stands the Index, a dense cluster of thin industrial towers bound together by bridges. It is said to contain one complete example of every structure in the region: one apartment, one chimney, one drainage culvert, one aviary, one abandoned room. This cannot be literally true, because new structures appear in the Index before construction begins elsewhere.

Engineers consult it nevertheless.

The laws of weight also vary gently with altitude. Loose objects tend to slide toward active machinery, even across level tables. Water prefers inhabited floors. Birds can perch on beams too narrow to be physically present. Above a certain terrace, falling snow stops several centimeters short of the roofs and remains suspended until collected by workers with wide nets.

Nothing changes quickly. The basin’s impossibilities unfold with bureaucratic patience. A corridor may take thirty years to bend back upon itself. A neighborhood gradually becomes steeper, one fraction of a degree each season, until handrails and climbing lines are installed. Over generations, identical doors migrate from western walls to eastern ones. The inhabitants measure these drifts, issue revised diagrams, and build compensating mechanisms.

Erosion here is not the wearing-away of nature but the first abrasion of new manufacture. Corners soften under gloved hands. Fresh paint scars where animals pass. Rain begins its patient work on young steel, drawing narrow rust lines from bolt heads. The sharp ridges have not yet accumulated ruins enough to look ancient, though buried inside them are structures of impossible age. The place is always newly made over something older, and therefore always young at its surface.

Near evening—if evening is the correct term—the high lamps dim from white to a lucid blue. Condensation thickens. Cool droplets fall from bridge undersides into the shallow channels with isolated, precise sounds. The factories continue below perception. Birds settle in ventilation crowns; magnetic grazers fold their legs against warm panels. Through the aqueducts, fish drift above illuminated streets.

From the highest accessible platform, the basin appears both boundless and enclosed. Manufactured peaks recede through silver air. The river flashes in separate fragments across them, refusing to admit its interruptions. Sparse gardens glow on roofs. Every ledge seems occupied, every cavity inhabited, every wall full of sleeping systems.

Beyond the last visible ridge there is another brightness, steady and colorless.

No one agrees whether it is a neighboring city, the outside world, or the unfinished edge of this one.
