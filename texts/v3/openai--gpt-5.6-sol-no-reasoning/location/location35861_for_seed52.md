The city lies low across a warm plain, broad enough that its farther districts disappear not into haze but into darkness.

Nothing here rises very high. Buildings spread rather than climb: long terraces, shallow domes, sunken courts, and miles of roof that hover a hand’s breadth above their walls. From a distance the city resembles a scattering of black leaves laid over green ground, with generous intervals between them. Streets cross those intervals in pale, disciplined lines. Some end abruptly at gardens or empty lots; others continue beyond the inhabited districts and can be seen running ruler-straight toward the horizon.

The ground is new.

Its stone has not had time to become soil except in pockets. Dark plates push through the vegetation with sharp, unworn edges, as though poured and cooled only a few seasons ago. Between them lies a warm gray sediment, fine as flour, which holds footprints perfectly until the next rain. There are shallow fissures everywhere. They are narrow, clean-sided, and faintly luminous at depth—not with heat, but with a diluted blue that makes the roots crossing them visible.

Older maps insist there was once a river basin here. The present terrain has no river in the ordinary sense. Water arrives intermittently from the undersides of things.

At dusk, condensation beads beneath roofs, bridges, balcony lips, suspended transit rails, even under the leaves of certain broad plants. Drops gather faster than the warm air should permit. They fall upward first, rising a few centimeters in silver strings, then hesitate and descend into channels cut along the streets. During the wet cycle, these channels fill and become a temporary river system. For several hours the city is webbed with moving water. By morning most channels are empty again, though cisterns under the public squares remain full and cool.

The rain itself is local. One district may receive a soft vertical downpour while another, visible across an open field, remains dry. Clouds do not always participate. Sometimes rain forms beneath a particular building and falls only within the outline of its foundation, soaking interior gardens while leaving the roof untouched. People speak of these events as maintenance rather than weather.

Vegetation has claimed everything not actively kept bare. It is lush without being dense. Wide mats of low foliage occupy the spaces between structures, glossy and dark in the dim light. Cane-like plants rise in isolated stands, their hollow stems jointed with translucent rings. Trees are uncommon but extravagant: each has room for a huge crown, and each appears to have selected its own geometry. One branches in flat horizontal fans. Another produces a hollow sphere of leaves around an empty center. A third grows downward into a public reservoir, exposing only a cluster of pale roots to the air.

Much of the green is nearly black. True color emerges when moisture touches it. Rain wakes sudden copper veins in leaves, violet seams in moss, and pinpricks of green fire along the grasses. After a wet cycle the city glows at ground level for an hour or two, every path bordered by subdued biological light. Then the colors close again.

The buildings are made of substances difficult to distinguish from processes. Walls appear like smoked stone until touched, when they yield slightly and adjust their grain. Scratches draw shut. Doorways migrate by small increments to accommodate patterns of use. A house that has stood empty for years gradually loses its entrances and becomes a seamless low mound, still connected to water, power, and whatever subtler networks run beneath the city.

No cables are visible. No engines sound. Energy is taken from gradients too slight to notice: the difference between shade and deeper shade, between damp stone and dry stone, between one moment’s gravity and the next. The infrastructure is embedded in the city’s surfaces as a fine intelligence. Pavements loosen when roots need passage and harden under approaching feet. Bridges alter their internal distances, making a kilometer crossing feel like a short walk without seeming to move either end. Public rooms are larger inside during gatherings and contract afterward, releasing the borrowed volume in slow, inaudible pulses.

This technology has not made the city crowded. If anything, it has allowed presence to thin out.

Habitation is scattered across districts separated by orchards, wet meadows, bare plazas, and stretches of unassigned ground. A row of occupied homes may face an expanse several kilometers wide where nothing stands but waist-high leaves and one dark transit pavilion. The pavilion has no tracks. Entering it at the correct hour leads to another pavilion elsewhere, though at incorrect hours it contains only benches and the smell of wet mineral dust.

There are towers, but most descend.

Their entrances are modest circular structures in gardens or squares. Beneath them, shafts open into stacked neighborhoods extending down through the young rock. These underground districts are not cramped. Their ceilings simulate no sky, yet they carry a sense of immense overhead depth: dim atmospheric space filled with slow cloudlike forms. Some residents prefer these buried quarters because the true surface sky is unsettling.

It is always dim, although not always night.

A muted radiance occupies the air, directionless and warm in color, like sunlight seen through many layers of smoke. The visible sun is a small blurred disc that appears only intermittently and not in a consistent part of the sky. Shadows gather beneath objects but seldom point anywhere. At irregular intervals every shadow in the city lengthens toward the same empty field beyond the eastern districts. Instruments register no source there. Buildings orient a few degrees toward it over decades.

In that field stands nothing permanent. Grass grows in broad rings around patches of exposed stone. During condensation hours, droplets assemble in midair above the rings, outlining absent architecture: stairs, colonnades, high windows, perhaps an entire second city occupying a geometry slightly displaced from this one. Its watery edges persist for minutes. Sometimes figures seem to cross the suspended stairs, though no inhabitants have ever been recovered, contacted, or conclusively seen.

The city has rules that are understood through custom more than theory.

No one plants beneath a shadow that does not match its object.

Temporary rivers must be crossed in the direction of flow, even when the destination lies behind you.

A door found standing alone in vegetation belongs to the municipal system and should not be opened during rain.

Nothing is built higher than the tallest local tree. Structures that violate this rule are not demolished; by morning they are simply found farther underground.

The rare animals follow rules of their own. Small, six-legged grazers emerge after rain to feed on the luminous margins of leaves. Their hides are smooth and reflective, showing not the surroundings but nearby places as they looked several minutes earlier. Thin black birds sometimes stand along the dry channels, spaced at exact intervals. They do not fly when approached. Instead, one disappears and another appears farther down the line.

There are no swarms, no constant choruses, no obvious abundance of animal life. Pollination is performed mainly by drifting machines so small they look like floating dust. Larger fauna remain at the edge of perception: a bending of tall canes, a broad depression appearing in wet grass, a low shape crossing a distant avenue before the pavement has finished illuminating beneath it. Once or twice a season, something enormous passes under the plain. Cistern surfaces tremble. Trees close their leaves. The buried neighborhoods suspend their elevators until it has gone.

For all this, the city is not oppressive. It is warm, cultivated, and in many places gentle. Courtyards smell of wet stone, bitter flowers, and mineral-rich water. Public gardens offer fruit with cool interiors and skins that hum faintly when ripe. The open land keeps the architecture from becoming overwhelming. One can stand on a roof terrace and see many districts at once: dark inhabited clusters, green intervals, pale roads, temporary rivers catching the low light, and far beyond them the sharp rim of freshly raised ground.

Most of the city’s sound is intimate. Condensation tapping into ceramic channels. Leaves folding as the dimness deepens. Soft footfalls on responsive pavement. A distant burst of rain confined to one courtyard. Transit structures produce a low tone only when empty. Under bridges, the water sometimes carries fragments of conversation from places upstream that do not exist.

Activity is everywhere, but little of it is hurried. The terrain is still assembling itself. Hairline cracks open, glow, and seal. Gardens migrate a few centimeters each season. Buildings revise their floor plans. Water rehearses different routes through the streets. The invisible systems negotiate constantly with roots, weather, and human intention.

The city feels less constructed than recently persuaded into existence—and the persuasion is not yet complete.
