High on an old mountain lies a city that was never built.

Its streets are narrow trenches in a single expanse of pale stone. They join at squares, divide into alleys, climb shallow steps, pass beneath arches, and open suddenly into roofless courts. From above, the place has the unmistakable order of a settlement: wards arranged around a central plaza, culverts beneath the lanes, terraces like foundations, rows of low chambers along a perimeter wall. Yet no hand quarried it. No tool marks interrupt the rock. Everything has been produced by frost, roots, seepage, and spans of time so long that erosion has ceased to resemble destruction and has become architecture.

The city occupies a compact shelf near the mountain’s summit. Beyond its outer rim, the land drops into cloud. Within, distances are intimate: one can cross the whole place in an hour, if the streets permit it. The highest surviving walls are scarcely twice the height of a person. Most have been worn down to rounded partitions, benches, and thresholds. Their surfaces are smooth in some places and intricately fretted in others, full of thumb-sized cavities where softer grains have vanished.

The mountain itself is older than the peaks around it. Those neighboring summits are sharp, fractured things, still shedding cliffs into their valleys. This one has been reduced almost to a table. Its bedrock has reached a kind of final shape: broad-backed, low-angled, resistant, with no loose grandeur left to lose. At the center of the shelf stands the oldest remnant—a squat mass of dark stone like a citadel eroded nearly to its foundations. Rain has divided it into pillars and galleries. At noon, sunlight enters the cracks and makes the interior glow in thin, vertical bands.

There is little water, but dampness is everywhere.

The cold rock sweats when cloud passes over it. Condensation gathers beneath overhangs and along the undersides of stone lintels, then falls in slow, heavy drops. Fine rain arrives without warning and departs just as quickly. It does not feed streams so much as awaken the city’s hidden circulation. For several minutes after a shower, every street runs silver. Water crosses plazas in braided films, disappears into holes, and can be heard descending through buried channels. Later it emerges lower down in a row of wall-like seeps, each no wider than a hand.

In sheltered courts, these intermittent waters collect in black basins. The pools are shallow, intensely clear, and colder than the air. Their bottoms are paved—not intentionally, but with naturally jointed slabs fitted so neatly that they appear laid. When the pools evaporate, they leave rings of white mineral around the stone, marking old water levels like calendars whose units are storms.

Plant life gathers wherever moisture hesitates. The exposed streets are almost bare, but every seam carries a green line. Moss fills the lower corridors in thick cushions. Liverworts spread across the shaded courts in glossy, overlapping scales. Small ferns emerge from drain mouths, their fronds dark and delicate, trembling in winds that cannot be felt at ground level. Dwarf shrubs occupy the deeper enclosures, twisted close to the rock. Their trunks are ancient, though none is thicker than a wrist.

The central plaza is the lushest part of the city. Soil has accumulated there in a shallow bowl, fed by dust, dead moss, and windblown leaves from forests far below. During the short warm interval, it becomes a meadow no larger than a cathedral floor. Low sedges, violet bells, pale star-shaped flowers, and red mats of alpine succulents bloom almost at once. From the surrounding walls, it resembles a garden abandoned before the concept of gardening existed.

Yet the city is mostly empty.

Its structures enclose absence. Long streets contain only stone and moving air. Door-shaped openings lead into chambers without occupants; chambers open into further chambers; a turn that seems certain to reveal some civic center instead ends at a blank wall stippled with lichen. The sparse vegetation makes the void more evident rather than less. One small fern may inhabit an entire hall. A single flowering shrub may stand at the crossing of five roads.

Animals are uncommon and seldom remain visible. Tiny black flies rise from the pools on clear afternoons, dance for an hour, and vanish. A pale mountain vole lives among the mossy foundations, leaving neat clipped stems and no tracks on the stone. Once or twice a day, a bird crosses overhead—a raven, usually, or a narrow-winged hunter—but few land. The place offers no tall perch and little food.

There are, however, shell-like creatures in the wet passages beneath the city. They are translucent, nearly colorless, and carry no true shells: only ridged plates grown from mineral salts in the seepage. They move so slowly that their trails dry before they have crossed a slab. When touched by sudden frost, they draw themselves into crevices and remain there for months. Some may spend most of a lifetime traveling from one condensation drop to the next.

The emptiness is not quiet.

Wind strikes the mountain continuously. The city’s eroded openings divide and amplify it. Each passage has a pitch determined by its length and shape. Narrow alleys whistle. Round shafts boom. Low arches produce a steady throat-like note, while broken colonnades chatter in quick pulses as gusts pass through them. The central citadel contains several deep fissures that resonate together. In strong weather they generate a chord so powerful that loose water shivers in the basins and moss releases beads of moisture from its leaves.

Thunder is less common but immense. Storms rise from below, hidden inside the cloud sea, and their sound arrives through the rock before it arrives through the air. The plazas tremble. Drops fall from every ceiling at once. The city seems, for a few moments, to possess machinery—reservoirs opening, underground gates turning, vast buried wheels beginning to revolve—though there are no devices and never have been.

This impression is strengthened by the behavior of the streets.

Water and ice continue to alter them, but according to rules that are difficult to reconcile with ordinary geology. Channels deepen in one winter and seal in the next. A corridor that drained east for decades may begin draining west after a single night’s frost, though its slope appears unchanged. Pools empty uphill. Condensation forms most heavily on surfaces exposed to sunlight. Fallen stones sometimes return, over several freeze-thaw cycles, to the cavities from which they broke.

Nothing moves quickly enough to witness. The place rearranges itself by increments below perception, using rain, crystal growth, root pressure, and windblown grit as its only instruments. Return after a year, and a familiar alley will have narrowed. Return after ten, and two courts will have joined through an arch no one remembers. The plan of the city persists, but its districts migrate through the rock.

At certain hours the sound gives away these alterations. Beneath the roar of wind comes a grinding so low it is felt in the teeth. Then a crack runs across the shelf like distant gunfire. Dust rises from an empty chamber. A slab settles the width of a moss leaf. The mountain has revised itself.

Daylight here is plain and unforgiving. Under clear skies the stone is gray, the vegetation green, the remaining snow white. There are no perpetual auroras or unnatural suns. Shadows move predictably. Clouds dim the city; sunset warms its western walls; night removes color. This ordinary light makes the place stranger. Nothing is hidden by spectacle. Every impossible drain and unmade doorway can be inspected in full noon.

Cold governs all. Even in summer, shaded water skins over by dawn. In winter the city is packed with blue-white ice, but snow rarely lies evenly because the wind combs it from plazas and presses it into corridors. The architecture becomes temporarily complete: ice roofs the chambers, bridges broken streets, fills windows with translucent panes, and raises smooth towers over the vents. Melt season destroys this second city in a matter of days. Its collapse is deafening. Blocks fall through shafts; water hammers the underground passages; trapped air erupts from wells in white spray.

For that brief period the whole shelf is active. Every channel runs. Moss swells. Flowers push through snow margins. The mineral-plated seep creatures wake. Birds come to catch the flies. Frost splits another fraction from the citadel, and the wind immediately begins wearing the fresh edge smooth.

Then the water dwindles. The pools retreat into their deepest sockets. Green contracts into cracks. The streets pale and empty again.

From the valleys, the summit looks featureless—a flattened shoulder beneath the clouds. No towers interrupt its outline. No smoke marks habitation. Only when one climbs onto the shelf does the hidden order appear: the old walls, the wandering avenues, the plazas waiting for rain.

It is not a ruin, because nothing stood here before it.

It is not growing toward a city, either. The mountain has already passed through innumerable forms and settled upon this one as if remembering an arrangement from somewhere outside history. What seems ancient is not the remains of intention, but intention’s likeness, arrived at by cold and erosion alone.

The city has no inhabitants, no founder, no purpose, and no destination.

Still, through every storm, it maintains its streets.
