At the top of the warm world lies a country worn almost past the condition of land.

It is a high plateau, though little of it is level. The rock has been exposed for so long that mountains have become low backs and shallow bowls, their harder interiors standing out as ribs, knobs, and long parallel blades. No cliffs look freshly broken. Every surface has passed through innumerable seasons of abrasion and rest. Stone curls downward into stone; ledges have soft lips; valleys are broad and strangely undecided. Seen from a distance, the country resembles an immense hide stretched over buried bones.

The air is thin but not cold. Heat gathers beneath the sun and remains caught among the rock hollows, making the plateau feel drowsy and elevated at once. Breathing requires patience. Scents carry poorly except near the ground, where there is dust, warm mineral, old dung, and the faint metallic odor of moisture collecting where no rain has fallen.

Water is scarce in extent, but not entirely in influence. It appears in pockets.

At dawn, condensation pearls on the shaded faces of stones. It runs through microscopic channels, making dark threads no wider than roots, and gathers beneath overhangs in clear, bitter drops. Some basins hold shallow pools for part of the year. They are lined with black mineral skins and visited so continuously that the surrounding rock has been polished by feet, bellies, tongues, wings, and generations of waiting bodies. Elsewhere, streams exist only below the surface. One hears nothing from them. Their routes are disclosed by cooler ground, narrow bands of salt, and occasional holes from which insects emerge damp and shining.

Rain comes seldom and without violence. Clouds catch on the plateau, blur its farther ridges, and release a fine warm drizzle. The water does not transform the country so much as reveal it. Ancient channels darken. Dormant films turn green. Tiny leaves unfold from seams in the rock, and for several days the whole plateau acquires a hidden pattern, as if writing has risen through the stone. Then the moisture withdraws, and the writing fades.

Vegetation is sparse enough that each plant seems solitary, yet no plant is truly alone. Low thorn cushions occupy cracks where dust has accumulated over centuries. Pale grasses grow in widely separated rings. There are a few thick-stemmed shrubs with bark like overlapping scales, rarely taller than a crouching animal. Their leaves are small, waxed, and held edge-on to the sun. On the plateau’s higher swells stand occasional pillar-plants: leafless, gray-green columns split into many blunt fingers. Their tissues swell after rain and then contract so gradually that fissures in their skin may remain unchanged through an animal’s entire life.

There are no true forests. Instead, life has filled the open air with bodies.

From far away the plateau appears empty. Up close, it is crowded almost beyond comprehension.

Beetles pack the cooler undersides of stones in layered masses. Moths rest wing to wing inside cracks. Small translucent crabs live in damp mineral pockets far from any sea, emerging only when mist covers the ground. Flat lizards lie flush against sun-warmed slabs, their patterns indistinguishable from lichen until they breathe. Long-legged grazers move over the plateau in scattered herds, so evenly spaced and slow that at first they seem to be upright stones. Between them drift clouds of nearly invisible gnats, feeding on salt at the corners of their eyes.

Most creatures here have learned stillness.

Predators do not often chase. They resemble resting places, shadowed fissures, dead stems, or patches of gravel. A six-limbed ambusher may remain folded in a basin for months, dust settling over its back, while small animals walk across it. Broad-winged hunters ride the thin rising air without beating their wings; their shadows pass over the ground more often than their bodies are seen. Burrowing colonies build no visible mounds. They remove each grain separately and scatter it far from the entrance, preserving the plateau’s ancient contours.

The greatest animals are slow, warm-blooded walkers whose backs rise above the sparse shrubs. Their hides are deeply folded and host entire ecologies: mites in the creases, pale frogs under the throat, seed-eating birds among the knobs of old scar tissue. They follow ancestral paths between water pockets. The paths are so old that they have become shallow valleys, and the shallow valleys are so old that no one can say whether the animals made them or merely inherited the habit of the land.

Their passage is nearly silent. Their feet spread softly over stone. They call only across great distances, with notes too low to be heard clearly. One feels the sound in the sternum or sees a tremor cross the skin of a pool.

Silence here does not mean an absence of living things. It is the collective quiet of innumerable creatures avoiding waste. Wings open without snaps. Scales pass over dust. Mouthparts work in damp crevices. In the heat of midday, millions of animals may be visible, yet the loudest sound is often a bead of condensed water falling from an overhang.

There are scattered structures, though nothing in the country possesses tools.

They stand in shallow groups: low domes of fused grit, perforated towers, rings of pale stone, and thin walls wandering across ridges before ending for no apparent reason. They look built because repetition gives them order. Each dome has an entrance turned away from the prevailing wind. Each ring surrounds a patch of bare ground. Each wall contains small hollows at regular intervals.

But these are growths and remains. The domes are made by colonial animals that cement dust with saliva and die inside successive layers. The towers are the mineralized breathing organs of creatures living deep underground. The rings are left by vast circular organisms that migrate outward over centuries, consuming the scant organic matter in the soil and abandoning a sterile center. What resemble walls are the exposed spines of an ancient burrower species, each individual many miles long, dead for so long that the plateau has eroded down around its body.

Some ridges may themselves be remains. Their stone contains no clean boundary between geology and anatomy. Under certain light, one sees repeating chambers within them, filled with red dust. In the broadest valley lie smooth paired hills with a groove between them, and along that groove moisture collects even in the dry season. Herds come there to drink from tiny seeps. Nothing living remembers the organism whose weathered joint makes the valley.

This is one of the plateau’s quiet rules: life becomes landscape before it disappears.

Shells turn to pavement. Nests become hills. Migration becomes drainage. Bodies outlast species and are worn into places where later bodies feed. Even behavior can harden into geography. A million years of kneeling at the same condensation hollow has deepened it into a pool.

The country changes, but so slowly that movement is mostly understood through differences in age. A young surface is merely one whose color has not yet settled. A new channel may require ten thousand rainy seasons to become visible from the next ridge. The pillar-plants lean toward water over centuries. Colonies advance the width of a hand during the life of a grazer. Dust drifts steadily eastward, grain by grain, filling one valley while exposing the chambered bones beneath another.

Light is ordinary and therefore revealing. Morning is clear and honey-colored. Noon drains color from the exposed stone, leaving grays, chalk whites, faded ochres, and the dull black of wet mineral films. Evening restores distance. The low sun catches every rounded ridge, every ancient path, every animal back, until the plateau appears densely inscribed with parallel lines. Nothing glows unnaturally. Nothing needs to. Strangeness comes from recognition delayed: a boulder that closes an eye, a hillside that exhales, a shadow composed of wings.

At night, the warmth leaves the surface but remains in the deep rock. Animals gather over those hidden reservoirs, lying packed together in apparently empty basins. Stars are sharp in the thin air. The plateau beneath them looks still, yet countless bodies are drinking dew, exchanging places, laying eggs in mineral pores, or turning by imperceptible degrees toward the next distant pool.

From a high swell one can see nearly the whole region: worn ridges overlapping to the horizon, scattered dark basins, pale animal roads, isolated columns of vegetation, and slow herds arranged across the open ground. The view is vast but crowded. Every available refuge has an occupant; every occupant carries others; every stone has a shaded world beneath it.

And still the place gives an impression of waiting.

Not for rain, exactly. Not for an ending. It has already endured so many endings that they have become its substance. It waits in the manner of very old things whose next transformation has begun, but will not be legible for ages.
