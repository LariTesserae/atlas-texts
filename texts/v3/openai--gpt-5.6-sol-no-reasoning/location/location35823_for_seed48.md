The city lies low against a hot, shallow coast, spread across mudflats and reclaimed ground where the difference between land and water is often only a matter of pumps.

It is an industrial city, though from a distance it appears almost empty. Warehouses run for kilometers beneath a dim ceiling of haze. Rail sidings divide broad yards of black gravel. Refineries, desalination plants, rendering works, and power stations stand far apart, each surrounded by open exclusion zones and linked by elevated pipes. Roads are oversized and largely vacant. Their surfaces shine after frequent rain, holding warm water in wheel-ruts and subsided lanes. Nothing grows along them. There are no weeds in the cracks, no trees planted beside administrative buildings, no moss beneath leaking taps. Soil, where it remains, is saline clay compacted hard as fired pottery.

The heat is persistent rather than brilliant. Cloud, smoke, and coastal vapor weaken the daylight to a tarnished gray. Even at noon, lamps burn above loading docks. Condensation beads on steel tanks and trickles down their weathered sides, collecting rust before dripping into concrete channels. Rain comes in short, heavy bursts that strike hot roofs, flash into steam, and leave the whole city sweating. Beneath the paving, the water table stands near the surface. Old foundations are always wet. Tunnels must be pumped without pause.

The coast has worked on the city for a long time. Seawalls are rounded at their corners; concrete steps descend into silt where streets once continued. Culverts have widened under decades of floodwater. Rust has eaten lacework into neglected sheet metal, while salt has softened brick and exposed aggregate in the oldest buildings. The damage is not catastrophic. It has become the city’s ordinary shape. Repairs account for erosion rather than defeating it: replacement pilings are driven beside old ones, fresh pipe is strapped to dead pipe, and floodgates are built within the frames of earlier gates. The settlement endures by gradual thickening.

Almost all visible movement belongs to animals.

They occupy the city in numbers so great that the human population seems incidental. Gulls cover the flat roofs, spaced only a body-width apart. Their droppings bleach the tar and gather in gutters like plaster. Pigeons fill the warmer interiors of abandoned plants. Cormorants stand along outfall rails with wings held open in the damp heat. Waders patrol every flooded yard, stepping between bolts and broken glass to probe the mud. On the tidal margins, crabs move over one another in the drainage channels. Rats use the pipe galleries as covered roads. Feral dogs sleep beneath parked tankers and wake at dusk to search the rendering district.

The water is even more crowded. Mullet churn in oily basins. Small fish shelter beneath wharves, feeding on the dense runoff of the processing plants. Eels enter through storm drains on high tides. The warm discharge canals remain alive all year, and their concrete banks are lined not by reeds but by birds: herons, terns, rails, scavenging ducks, and flocks of dark, compact shorebirds that rise together whenever a maintenance vehicle approaches. Farther out, seals rest on half-submerged breakwaters among the remains of loading cranes. Insects accumulate wherever rainwater stands—mosquitoes in underground sumps, flies near waste conveyors, beetles in dry storage buildings—though the absence of vegetation keeps their world closely tied to animals, refuse, algae films, and industry.

There is no true plant life in the city. Repeated salt inundation, heat, polluted ground, and generations of clearing have removed it. Even algae are scrubbed from working surfaces, though thin bacterial films darken shaded waterlines and return within days. The visual palette is therefore entirely mineral and manufactured: brown water, gray concrete, red rust, yellow sodium lamps, pale salt, wet black asphalt. Any green is painted—on emergency exits, oxidized copper, or the faded stripe of a municipal vehicle.

People remain, but they move through enclosed systems. Shift buses cross the empty avenues with sealed windows. Workers pass from vehicles into control rooms by covered walkways. Heavy machinery operates behind walls: pumps turning below ground, conveyors moving through corrugated galleries, turbines rotating in windowless halls. Much of the industry is automated, and its activity is understood through signals rather than spectacle: a lamp changing color on a distant gantry, a plume of vapor beginning above a stack, floodgates lifting by degrees.

For such a populous animal landscape, the city is mostly quiet. Heat and wet air suppress distant noise. Machinery produces a low, continuous vibration that is felt more often than heard. Rain ticks on roofs. Water drains through gratings. Occasionally a train draws a long metallic complaint from the rails, or a pressure valve releases with a brief, forceful sigh. The birds are loud only in episodes. A roof may erupt into cries and wings, then settle again so completely that the drip from an overhead pipe becomes the dominant sound.

The city’s rhythms are tidal and mechanical. At low tide, mud opens beyond the seawalls, patterned with channels and crowded by feeding birds. At high tide, brown water pushes backward through the oldest drains and lifts debris in the underpasses. Rain starts the sump pumps. Heat starts the cooling systems. Factory shifts release short streams of people and vehicles, after which the roads empty once more. Predators follow these cycles: dogs wait near waste gates, gulls watch conveyor schedules, rats emerge after washdown, and larger fish gather where pumps return warm water to the harbor.

Nothing here is wild in the sense of being untouched, but much of it is beyond control. The animals have learned the timetable of the infrastructure, and the infrastructure has been altered around them. Intake grilles are reinforced against fish runs. Roof vents are screened against nesting birds. Sirens sound before waste sluices open, scattering flocks for a few minutes. Regulations require clear passages for emergency crews, yet every unused ledge becomes a roost and every warm cavity a den.

Seen from an elevated pipe bridge, the place extends as a broad, subdued geometry: tanks fading into haze, canals crossing rail yards, flooded lots reflecting the underside of clouds, and countless animals distributed over every surface that can bear them. The city seems vacant until something disturbs it. Then roofs, water, drains, gantries, and mudflats all move at once, revealing that the apparent emptiness is not an absence of life but an absence of vegetation—and that nearly everything capable of walking, swimming, burrowing, or flying has gathered here.
