The city occupies the inner wall of an immense mountain basin, so high that the outer world is hidden below a continuous white floor of cloud. Heat remains trapped here despite the elevation. The air is tropical, saturated, almost thick enough to hold fingerprints. Water beads on every surface, runs through every seam, and hangs in transparent globes beneath leaves without ever falling.

Across the basin, terraces rise in concentric tiers toward a broken rim of black and ocher stone. They resemble streets, plazas, retaining walls, stairs, and crowded districts seen from afar. Up close, none shows evidence of tools. Columns are trunks grown square; arches are fused roots; walls consist of thin mineral plates stacked with the regularity of masonry. Towers ascend in bundles of hollow reeds petrified on the outside and green within. What appears to be carved ornament is the exposed veining of rock. Doorways occur everywhere, but there are no doors, no hinges, no chambers arranged for habitation. The city is urban without ever having been built.

It is also lush. Gardens pour from roofs that are themselves living shelves of limestone. Broad leaves overlap like tiles, lacquered by condensation. Scarlet creepers stitch parapets together. Moss fills inscriptions that cannot be separated from natural fractures. Groves stand in courtyards, their canopies clipped into planes and cubes by no gardener. Pale flowers open in repeating bands along the avenues, each bloom facing inward toward the basin’s empty center.

No animals live here. No insects trouble the flowers. No birds cross the sky. There are no footprints, nests, webs, shed skins, bones, or burrows. Pollination, decay, and seed dispersal occur without agents. Fallen leaves do not rot; they soften into green paste, enter the wet stone, and reappear as new growth elsewhere. Fruit forms, darkens, and sinks intact into branches. The ecology is closed and exact, consuming nothing it has not produced.

Water is everywhere, though little of it moves. Canals lie between the streets, filled to their rims with warm, clear water. Their surfaces are perfectly level even where the channels climb steep stairways. Pools cling vertically to walls as though each facade were the bottom of a pond. Long curtains of water extend between towers, neither falling nor dispersing. In the plazas, shallow floods preserve the reflections of buildings that do not stand nearby.

The basin’s center is occupied by a lake several leagues across. From every terrace it appears directly below, regardless of the viewer’s height or position. Its water is green-black and polished, except for a permanent white concussion at its middle: the image of a waterfall striking it. There is no waterfall above.

The sound, however, is undeniable.

A continuous roar fills the basin, vast enough to erase distance. It is the combined sound of cataracts, monsoon rain, splitting cliffs, crowded streets, and stone dragged across stone. Yet nothing visibly produces it. Leaves do not tremble. Water curtains remain rigid. Condensation globes hold beneath their leaves. The roar has no pulses, echoes, approach, or fading; it is equally deafening inside a narrow root passage, on the exposed rim, beneath the surface of a canal, or with hands pressed over the ears. It passes through the body without moving air. One feels it in teeth and ribs, but the vibration never shifts a grain of silt.

Because the noise is perfectly uniform, it eventually ceases to seem loud. Instead it becomes the material from which silence would have to be cut.

The avenues follow the contour of the mountain until they arrive, without transition, on the opposite side of themselves. A stair climbed long enough ends at its own lowest step. Looking through certain archways reveals the same archway from behind, framed by unfamiliar vegetation. Some streets are broad enough to contain whole neighborhoods between adjacent paving joints. Others narrow in the distance but never become impassable; a traveler walking toward their vanishing point would diminish with them, retaining ordinary proportions relative to the walls.

The city has weathering but no history. Corners are rounded, foundations subsided, and channels worn deep, yet these conditions do not progress. Cracks contain mature roots that exert no further pressure. Landslides hang settled on slopes without having fallen. On the upper rim, pinnacles lean at impossible angles, held not by strength but by the permanence of their imbalance. Everything bears the appearance of having survived ages, though no new mark can be made. A stone lifted from the road is already back in place before the hand holding it has let go; the hand still retains its weight.

Nothing here changes. The heat does not rise or lessen. The light remains that of an ordinary late morning, diffuse and colorless beneath a sky in which no sun can be located. Shadows keep sensible shapes but sometimes attach to neighboring objects. Clouds beyond the rim remain folded in the same towers and valleys. There is wetness without rainfall, growth without development, erosion without loss.

Even attention cannot alter it. Return to a courtyard after an hour or a century and the same clear drop will remain poised at the same leaf tip. The same root will be half exposed in the same wall. The same reflection will show a pale tower absent from the skyline. Memory becomes the only unstable element, and the city seems to absorb that instability gently, redistributing it among its repetitions.

From the highest terraces, the settlement can be seen extending farther than the basin should allow. Districts overlap through translucent ridges. Bridges cross not ravines but changes in scale. Far below, avenues resemble veins in a leaf; farther still, the leaf itself grows from a plaza tree near the observer. The entire mountain may be one of the city’s towers viewed too closely. The city may be mineral growth inside a droplet that will never fall.

There are places resembling homes: cool recesses lined with fibrous mats, niches shaped like sleeping bodies, basins of fresh water, tables of level stone. There are places resembling markets, temples, reservoirs, amphitheaters, and tombs. Their arrangements imply a population so dense that every ledge would have held a presence. But no one built them, no one abandoned them, and no one is expected to return. They are forms the landscape assumes because settlement is one of its natural geometries, like branching, crystallization, or the winding of rivers.

At the basin rim, where the black rock drops toward the unmoving clouds, the roar appears to widen into something almost spacious. Looking outward reveals no horizon. Looking back reveals the green tiers, wet facades, root arches, vertical pools, and the central lake’s fixed explosion.

Above them hangs the ordinary bright sky.

Below them hangs the same sky.

And between the two, hot, drenched, deafening, and forever complete, the mountain city remains exactly as it has never become.
