The city lies beneath a plain of salt and warm stone, though “beneath” is less a matter of depth than of exclusion. The surface has sealed itself over it. Old shafts have pinched shut, stairwells end in mineral plugs, and the fractures through which the city once exchanged air with the outer world have weathered down to hairlines. What remains is a hollow no more than a few districts across: an urban chamber enclosed inside the earth, broad enough for towers, narrow enough that every sound eventually finds every wall.

Almost all of it is empty.

Buildings stand far apart on terraces carved from dark limestone. They are tall but extraordinarily thin, resembling blades driven into the cavern floor. Between them lie plazas large enough to seem ceremonial, bare except for drainage cuts and low ridges of material that looks like ceramic until it flexes underfoot. Roads cross the void in disciplined grids, yet there are no vehicles, signs, stalls, or crowds. Their surfaces are polished by something older and more patient than traffic.

The architecture has undergone centuries of erosion without becoming ruinous. Corners have softened. Facades have acquired smooth hollows where condensation repeatedly gathers and runs. Whole flights of steps have sagged into shallow ramps. The city is not decaying so much as reaching equilibrium with the cavern. Wherever stone once resisted the passage of water or air, it has been pared away; wherever pressure required reinforcement, walls have thickened.

Some of this adaptation is geological. Some is not.

The city’s construction persists in a state between infrastructure and organism. Its foundations contain a fine, warm lattice that redistributes stress through geometries too small to see. Cracks close over several days, leaving pale seams like healed bone. Towers subtly lean away from newly opened faults. Roads alter their camber before floods arrive. No central machine can be found, and no obvious power system remains. The engineering is dispersed through every surface, drawing usable differences from pressure, heat, chemistry, and the minute pull of the moon through several kilometers of rock.

There is almost no light. Along the oldest roads, isolated lines of blue-white points appear at ankle height, each no brighter than a bead of fungus. They do not illuminate the way ahead. Instead they define edges, depths, and prohibited crossings. When approached, they sometimes extinguish in sequence, leading the traveler into greater darkness. At other times they flare behind rather than ahead, as if the city considers departure more important than arrival.

Warmth rises from below. It settles in the streets and collects beneath overhangs, close and mineral-scented, while the upper cavern remains fractionally cooler. The air tastes of wet iron, old dust, and ozone. Clothing clings to the skin. Metal grows hot enough to feel alive but seldom burns.

Water is present in intervals. For long periods the city is only damp: a sheen on railings, black patches on the paving, slow droplets assembling on ceilings too high to see. Then pressure changes somewhere in the buried watershed. Condensation pearls across entire walls at once. Gutters unfold from apparently solid facades. Grilles open in plazas, and warm rain begins to fall inside the city.

It does not fall from clouds. It forms on the cavern roof and descends in huge, widely spaced drops. Each strikes with the sound of a thrown stone. Soon thousands are falling, and the hollow becomes deafening.

Water races through streets designed to receive it. Dry avenues turn into braided channels. It spills down stairwells, vanishes through doors, and fills a chain of deep cisterns under the eastern terraces. For an hour or a day, black pools occupy the plazas, mirroring only scattered guide lights and the vague undersides of bridges. Then pumps without moving parts draw the water into porous foundations. The streets empty. Mud contracts into scales. Dampness remains in pockets, nourishing the little life the city permits.

Vegetation survives chiefly along these temporary watercourses. Pale reedlike stems emerge through cracks when floods come, grow to the height of a hand, release dust-fine spores, and collapse before the paving dries. Their chemistry is neither wholly fungal nor wholly plantlike. They have translucent tissues and no visible green pigment. On warm walls, mats of rust-colored lichen spread in maps of vanished neighborhoods, avoiding active circuitry with uncanny precision.

In one sunken courtyard stands the nearest thing to a grove: nine slender organisms rooted around a permanent cistern. Their trunks are porous mineral columns knitted with living fibers. Their crowns consist of black membranes that open during rain and close into rigid cones afterward. Drops striking them produce low bell tones. Nothing in the courtyard explains why there are nine, or why the empty tenth foundation is kept clear of sediment.

Animals are rare enough that each sighting changes the apparent scale of the place. Small, long-legged scavengers move through the flood margins, their skins almost colorless, their eyes reduced to heat-sensitive pits. They feed on the brief reeds and on translucent mites that bloom in the drains. They travel alone and freeze when the guide lights brighten.

Higher up live the sailbacks: thin, warm-blooded creatures that glide between towers on membranes stretched from wrist to flank. They spend weeks motionless in vertical cracks, emerging when the city ventilates. Their calls are too low to be heard directly, but doors tremble as they pass.

Nothing seems to prey on them. Yet sometimes one vanishes in mid-glide, accompanied by a sudden clap and a ripple through the dust below. Afterward, for several minutes, nearby lights mark a circle no one should enter.

The city is loud even in dry periods. Deep below it, machinery—or geology recruited into machinery—operates continuously. There are long grinding notes like stone dragged over stone, hammering pulses from sealed transit tunnels, and abrupt metallic shrieks from towers adjusting their weight. Air moves through ducts in immense breaths. Certain intersections amplify these sounds until speech becomes impossible. Others cancel them almost perfectly, leaving tight enclosures of silence no wider than a doorway.

These silent places are part of the city’s order.

So are the roads that tilt by imperceptible degrees to guide water, and perhaps travelers. So are doors that open only during pressure drops, elevators that descend when occupied but return empty, and rooms whose dimensions change between visits. The rules are not arbitrary, though they are difficult to infer. The city appears to distinguish between movement with a destination and wandering. Those who proceed decisively find bridges aligned and passages clear. Those who hesitate encounter loops: the same plaza approached from incompatible directions, the same damp facade repeated block after block.

Carrying water upward is easy. Carrying it east is not. Loud sounds wake lights. Open flame causes nearby walls to sweat. Objects left in the middle of a road are moved to its edge by morning, though nothing is seen touching them. Damage is repaired, but additions are dismantled. The city tolerates occupants; it refuses renovation.

Near the center is a broad circular station roofed by a dome of naked rock. Platforms radiate around a shaft filled with darkness. Tracks end at its lip. At irregular intervals, a roar rises through the shaft—too vast for a train, too rhythmic for an earthquake. Dust lifts from the floor and hangs suspended. Every blue guide light in the city goes out. For several seconds the blackness becomes absolute, and the warm air draws toward the center.

Then something passes far below.

The sound climbs through the entire buried settlement, shaking water from walls and waking the sailbacks. The flexible roads tighten. Towers bow a fraction toward the shaft. Brief reeds rupture from wet seams. Ancient doors unlock with concussions that echo district to district.

When the passage ends, the city exhales. Lights return one at a time. Rain often follows.

Whether the thing below is transport, creature, engine, or natural force is impossible to determine. The city may have been built around it, or grown as an instrument to contain it, measure it, feed from it, or announce its coming. Whatever the answer, the empty streets are not abandoned. They are clearance. The plazas are not for assembly but for resonance. The towers are not homes so much as tuning forks, pressure vanes, and anchors.

The entire little metropolis is busy, but not with habitation. It is an inhabited machine whose inhabitants have become optional: a warm chamber listening to the deep earth, adjusting itself around each impossible pulse, and waiting in darkness for the next interior storm.
