At first it appears to be a marsh at night.

A dim plain extends beyond any reliable horizon, black water lying between low islands of gray material. Heat trembles over it. Pale vapor hangs knee-high in some places and rises in columns in others, as if the ground is breathing through hidden pores. Far away, weather flickers without lightning: broad pulses of diluted amber passing under the cloud ceiling.

But there is no soil here, and nothing grows.

The islands are assembled platforms—millions of them—made from fitted slabs, pressed clay, dark glass, chalky ceramic, and blocks of some porous artificial stone. They are crude in the intimate scale and impossibly sophisticated in aggregate. Every visible piece bears evidence of a hand-level technology: thumbprints hardened into mortar, peg holes, rope grooves, wedges hammered under lintels, seams packed with fiberless caulk. There are no smooth machine cuts. Nothing is poured in a single monumental mass. Yet these small pieces tessellate into a constructed world so vast that its curvature can be sensed in the slow sinking of distant towers.

There is no natural substrate beneath it. If one lifts a paving tile, there is another tile below, angled differently; beneath that, a chamber; beneath the chamber, a mat of interlocked fired-brick conduits; beneath those, another sky.

The waters are warm and shallow, though not consistently so. They collect in courtyards, spill over weirs, seep upward through stairways, and condense on the undersides of arches. In places they form immense mirror-flat basins only a finger deep. In others, narrow black channels descend between walls and have no measurable bottom. Rain falls often, but never from the same direction twice. Sometimes it drops from the low clouds. Sometimes it beads out of masonry and runs upward. Sometimes an entire district is struck by horizontal rain: a gray moving wall that passes through doorways and under bridges without wetting their windward faces.

The water has a faint mineral smell, like wet pottery and struck flint. It carries no algae, no larvae, no drifting seeds. Nothing swims beneath its skin. When disturbed, it closes over without foam.

The heat is pervasive rather than bright. There is no visible sun. Warmth leaks from mortar joints and radiates from the water after darkness deepens. Certain blocks are too hot to touch; adjacent ones sweat cold droplets. Crossing the plain means passing through large, slow climates: feverish plazas, humid galleries, scalding fog banks, then expanses where lukewarm rain evaporates before it reaches the paving. The atmosphere is dense enough to soften distance. Structures a few miles away look submerged.

Very little stands upright. Emptiness is one of the world’s principal materials.

Across the broad tiled wetlands, isolated constructions rise at long intervals: a stairway without a destination; a ring wall around an empty basin; three kiln-shaped domes leaning together; a forest of perhaps twenty stone poles separated by miles of open water. Their scarcity gives each object excessive importance. A single bridge may dominate a whole horizon. A lone gate can take half a day to reach.

None of these structures is old.

Their surfaces are raw, their edges blunt, their drainage channels scarcely scored by flow. There are no rounded pebbles, no softened corners, no sediment deltas. Water is abundant, but erosion has only just begun. The first scratches are appearing now. Fine powder, newly released from brick faces, makes pale fans beneath outlets. Hairline cracks carry the world’s first dark stains. In sheltered recesses, droplets repeatedly strike unmarked floors, beginning depressions that might become bowls in ten thousand years—if the floors remain long enough.

They rarely do.

The landscape is under continuous construction, though there are no builders.

A wall will begin in open water as a line of bubbles. Rectangular forms rise beneath the surface, at first soft and pallid, then hardening into blocks already marked with fingerprints. Mortar pushes itself into the seams. Courses accumulate one by one with the irregular rhythm of manual labor. Invisible hammers tap wedges into place. Ropes tighten around scaffolds that are not there. By dusk, a tower may stand where morning held only rain.

Elsewhere, a plaza forgets how it was assembled. Its paving loosens. Every tile lifts a hand’s breadth and rotates uncertainly, exposing darkness beneath. The pieces migrate outward in slow, scraping files, leaving behind a geometric lake. They do not collapse like rubble. They depart like workers released from an obligation.

This movement accounts for most of the sound.

For long intervals there is only the soft tick of cooling ceramic, the patter of condensation, and the low lap of water against steps. The quiet is immense enough to make small noises seem local and deliberate. Then, from beyond the mist, comes a sequence of impacts: stone on stone, spaced evenly, approaching without becoming louder. A district may groan all at once as its arches adjust to a new direction of gravity. Kilometers of chain—stone chain, each link carved separately—may drag beneath the paving for an hour.

The loudest events remain strangely muffled. When towers fall, they vanish into fog with a sound like wet cloth being shaken.

No vegetation occupies the cracks. No moss films the shaded cisterns. No lichen maps the walls. There are not even recognizable fungi. In the absence of life, mineral deposits assume ecological roles. White salts gather along warm shorelines in branching colonies. Translucent crusts spread across ceilings, retreat when touched by rain, and reappear several chambers away. Iron blooms as red disks around submerged nails, though many of the nails are stone. Delicate tubes of lime cluster at vents and open when the air pressure drops. They are not organisms, but they compete for favorable flows, smother one another, and leave layered reefs of discarded geometry.

Some patterns appear almost to forage.

Thin black fractures move across walls during the hottest hours, selecting routes from joint to joint. At night they fill with clear water and disappear. Polygonal patches of glaze crowd around sources of heat. When two patches meet, one changes color and the other fractures. These processes resemble an ecology only because the world has no other vocabulary for persistence.

Everything is made, including what would ordinarily be considered weather.

Clouds are woven from strands of vapor knotted around invisible frames. Storm fronts have seams. Seen from above—on those rare occasions when a stair ascends far enough—the rain plains are divided into enormous rectangular allotments, each undergoing a different atmospheric trial. In one, steam coils in fixed spirals. In another, droplets hang motionless until the district below is completed, then fall all at once. Elsewhere, a cloud is being unmade thread by thread, its damp filaments reeled into holes in the roofs.

Even distance is constructed. The broad empty reaches between structures are floored with repeating slabs whose dimensions subtly change. Walking north, a traveler might cross a thousand slabs and arrive only a few meters from the starting point. Walking back across the same paving may require days. Some causeways shorten when wet. Some rooms become vast when unobserved and resume their modest proportions only after footsteps enter them.

The world’s geometry is not broken so much as unfinished.

Gravity is locally installed. It pools in courtyards, runs down channels, and occasionally spills from damaged aqueducts. Under certain arches, falling rain turns sideways because “down” has been routed through the masonry like drainage. A loose object placed on a slope may remain at rest while the entire horizon tilts around it. Vertical towers sometimes cast horizontal reflections into the air.

Time, too, adheres unevenly. Fresh mortar can harden before it is mixed. Water drips from a ceiling and strikes the floor a moment before it lets go. A wall may carry the faint imprint of a doorway that has not yet been built. There are districts visible only as consequences: runoff with no roof above it, shadows with no structure, echoes from chambers still rising beneath the plain.

The primitive methods are not a sign of poverty. They are laws. Nothing may exist here unless it can be produced by the simplest sequence of gestures: lift, place, bind, strike, turn, fire, polish. The world possesses no machinery beyond leverage, tension, heat, molds, and patience. Yet it has these in inexhaustible quantity. Continents are assembled as a potter might coil a jar. Seas are carried basin by basin. Storms are tightened with rope. Horizons are measured using knotted cords that stretch from one curvature to another.

No one performs these gestures. The gestures remain.

In the deepest visible gaps, immense mechanisms can sometimes be inferred—not engines, but monumental arrangements of primitive devices. Counterweights the size of cities descend through shafts. Wooden-looking beams, though made from laminated clay, flex across abyssal chambers. Water wheels turn in air; capstans rotate without ropes; inclined planes slowly trade places. Each simple device contributes almost nothing. Together, across planetary distances, they continuously remake reality.

The turbulence is therefore everywhere and seldom frantic. It operates at several incompatible speeds. At one’s feet, a crack advances with patient delicacy. On the horizon, an entire range of artificial hills rises between breaths. A rain basin may remain unchanged for centuries while the sky above it is rebuilt every hour. Vast sections of the plain buckle, tessellate, unfold, and settle again, but always with the piecemeal hesitancy of a mason deciding where the next brick belongs.

There are places where construction outruns definition.

Beyond the low eastern spillways, matter appears in provisional states: walls that are only warmth, stairs composed of sound, bridges visible solely in reflection. Rain striking these things becomes briefly undecided. A drop may divide into several possible descents, each leaving a wet mark, though only one reaches the ground. Pools contain alternative versions of nearby structures. In one reflection a tower is complete; in another it has collapsed; in a third it was never attempted. Occasionally the reflected tower falls upward, and the real horizon shudders.

Names do not hold well here. “Brick” may mean a fired block in the morning, a unit of distance at noon, and a reddish interval of time after dark. “Water” sometimes refers to the liquid in the basins, sometimes to the downward tendency that guides it. A staircase is both an object and permission to ascend. If its top step is removed, elevation may cease to exist in the surrounding district.

This makes ruins dangerous. A broken building is not merely unstable; it may have supported a local rule. When an arch fails, echoes can lose their path home. When a cistern wall splits, containment itself leaks out, and nearby rooms can no longer keep air inside. When a bridge is dismantled, separation increases between the shores until each sees the other only as a narrow line beneath the sky.

Still, the place is not hostile. Hostility would imply an inhabitant, an intention, or at least a boundary between living trespasser and dead environment. There is no such distinction here. The landscape does not recognize life because it has never had occasion to invent the category.

It is a world before nature, though not before craft.

At the edge of the widest basin stands a low, unfinished amphitheater. Its seats face the water and are separated by broad stretches of empty paving. Rain runs down the steps in silver threads. The central platform is only half laid, exposing square sockets and warm darkness below. Across the basin, perhaps a hundred kilometers away, a row of solitary furnaces glows behind the mist. Their light is dim red, barely enough to distinguish cloud from water.

Nothing occupies the seats.

Now and then, from beneath the platform, there comes the measured tap of a stone mallet.

After seven taps, the basin’s surface lifts like a single sheet. New masonry is revealed below it: roofs, chimneys, narrow alleys, a city assembled upside down under the water. Vapor pours from its inverted streets. For a few minutes the city hangs there, reflected in the raised sea and in the wet steps of the amphitheater.

Then the water lowers.

The city is gone, or deferred, or placed somewhere beneath another sky.

Quiet returns. Condensation gathers along the newest edges. A first drop forms, swells, and falls into a surface that has never before been touched by rain.
