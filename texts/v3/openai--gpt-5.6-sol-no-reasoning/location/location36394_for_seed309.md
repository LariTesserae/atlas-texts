Below the root line, where the soil has been hollowed into warm chambers, there is a town called the Throatworks.

It is not far beneath the surface. In places, less than the height of a house separates its highest galleries from the open air. Rain can be heard passing overhead: first as a soft percussion in the roots, then as scattered drips descending through the ceiling. Yet no straightforward shaft connects the town to daylight. Every opening has been narrowed, bent, clogged, or patiently inhabited. What little light arrives has passed through leaves, loam, fungal membranes, and the translucent tissues of plants. It reaches the Throatworks green-brown and weak, barely bright enough to distinguish movement from stillness.

Movement is almost everywhere.

The town occupies a single eroded cavern, roughly the size of a walled hamlet. Its boundaries are definite. On all sides, water and roots have worn the earth down to old, hard clay, polished into pillars, shelves, bowls, and deep runnels. The cavern has existed long enough to lose the appearance of damage. Nothing looks freshly broken. Collapse scars have rounded over; abandoned tunnels have become alcoves; the floor dips into channels whose edges are smooth as pottery. Erosion has completed its argument here. The chamber is not ruinous but settled, shaped by innumerable minor pressures into a form that now seems deliberate.

Across this natural architecture stands a second city, made almost entirely of plants.

The walls are woven from roots trained through frames that have since rotted away. Streets lie between swollen buttresses of pale wood. Platforms hang from tensile ropes of vine, and narrow houses grow as hollow gourds, their shells strengthened by mineral salts drawn from the clay. Long galleries have been bent from living stems and grafted into arches. Doorways close by contracting. Bridges thicken when burdened and thin when unused. Pipes are reeds. Valves are flowers. Reservoirs are great bladderlike fruits suspended from the ceiling, pearly with condensation.

It would be misleading, however, to call the place lush in the ordinary sense. Plant life fills nearly all available substance, but does not fill the view. Most of it has organized itself into surfaces and structures: walls, roofs, conduits, cables, insulation, flooring. The open spaces are broad and dark. Buildings stand apart, connected by thin elevated walks. Enormous root columns rise singly from otherwise empty clay courts. There is room for shadows to gather between the living constructions, and for creatures to move in masses through them.

This apparent sparseness is necessary. The town must leave corridors for migration.

Every warm interval—measured not by day but by the heating of the upper soil—the fauna begins to circulate. Beetles with lacquered wing cases pour down the trunk roads. Blind, velvet-coated rodents climb counterweighted vine ladders to feeding balconies. Moths hatch from paper chambers under the eaves and fill the cavern with scales, soft impacts, and frantic powdery weather. Transparent frogs crowd the condensation gardens, pressing themselves flat against wet bark. Ant columns travel in braided lanes, some carrying leaf pulp into the factories, others removing larvae, husks, metal filings, or the neatly sectioned bodies of intruders.

There are so many animals that the city sometimes appears to be constructed from them. Sleeping bats tile the ceilings. Pale worms lace the cistern walls. The darkest passages ripple with roaches feeding shoulder to shoulder. Small crustaceans live in the brief streams after rain and seal themselves into clay capsules when the channels dry. Thin snakes, almost root-colored, suspend themselves between buildings and sway until a bird or moth strikes them. Birds do live here: short-winged, echoing things with bright mouths and no need for open sky. They nest in the warm housings of the machines.

The machines are the loudest inhabitants.

The Throatworks was built around a chain of industrial organs whose original makers are no longer obvious. Some mechanisms are iron, though so heavily patinated and root-bound that metal shows only at contact points. Others may never have been manufactured in the usual way. Flywheels turn inside woody rims. Pistons descend into sockets lined with cork. Belts of braided bast run over pulleys that secrete their own grease. Banks of copper contacts open and close beneath mats of sensitive moss. Where a conventional city might burn fuel, the Throatworks works by appetite, tension, decay, and animal motion.

At the cavern’s center stands the Press.

It is a tall but narrow structure, no larger than a chapel, assembled around a stone throat worn through the floor. Great rooted levers converge above it. With each cycle they crush cartloads of fibrous matter into dense, dark cakes. The Press exhales warm vapor and the smell of bruised leaves. Nobody agrees whether the cakes are fuel, food, building material, or offerings sent somewhere deeper. They are dropped into the throat one by one. Long after each falls from sight, a concussion returns through the stone.

The Press never works at a constant rhythm. It accelerates after rain, when swollen tissues tighten the drive-vines. It slows in dry periods, when the bladder-fruits empty and the wooden bearings contract. Colonies of heavy-backed insects provide auxiliary power by walking inside ribbed drums. Entire generations hatch, labor, mate, and die within those wheels. Their shells collect below and are ground into a hard, glossy aggregate used to repair the streets.

Around the Press are smaller workshops. One extrudes root fiber through mineral dies, producing cords fine enough for surgical binding and cables thick enough to hold a building. Another cultures luminous bacteria in glassy seedpods. A third sorts pollen by electric charge, discharging it in colored clouds through insulated reed chimneys. The air tastes faintly metallic near this quarter. Hair rises on the skin. Moths spark against the rails.

The sound is continuous and layered: the deep wooden knock of the Press; belts slapping; insect drums turning; water ticking from leaf tips into ceramic pools; frogs clacking in response to pressure changes; wings beating against stretched membranes; warning rattles traveling through hollow roots. Beneath it all is a dense biological murmur, so complete that silence would be taken as an emergency.

Communication has adapted accordingly. Residents do not rely on speech across open spaces. Messages are tapped into the tension bridges, released as scents from crushed capsules, or shown with shuttered lamps grown from luminous fruit. Some buildings flash to one another through veins in their walls. The town itself is always signaling: thirst, strain, infestation, ripeness, obstruction, heat.

Water comes intermittently. For much of the cycle the Throatworks is only damp: warm surfaces filmed with condensation, cisterns low, channels dark and empty. Then rain reaches the soil above. It does not arrive as a flood at first, but as a multiplication of sounds. Drops appear under root tips. Walls begin to sweat. Moss valves unfold. Leaf gutters stiffen and tilt themselves toward collection mouths.

If the rain persists, twelve temporary rivers cross the town.

They follow ancient grooves in the clay, dividing workshops from dormitories and curling beneath bridges polished by centuries of claws. Animals erupt toward them. Amphibians emerge from walls. Silver larvae tumble out of sealed pods. Fish no longer than fingers rise from mud bowls where they have waited in torpor, and race upstream into chambers that did not exist an hour before. Predators gather along the banks. Nets of carnivorous vine unroll over the water, taking small swimmers and anything else careless enough to touch them.

Then the rain passes. The rivers withdraw into gravel and porous rootwood. The fish bury themselves. Moisture remains in scattered pockets: a black pool under the Press, droplets in the condensation gardens, cistern-fruits overhead, little wells protected by thorn doors. Nothing is wasted. Even breath is collected in crowded sleeping chambers and guided along cool walls to bead into drinking troughs.

Warmth comes partly from the surface and partly from below. The clay floor holds a mild subterranean heat. The machines add more. So do the compressed multitudes of animals. The atmosphere is warm without being stifling, except in the hatcheries and factory throats, where damp heat can become almost bodily. There, entering a room feels like entering the mouth of some enormous resting creature.

Perhaps this is not entirely metaphorical.

The oldest residents say the vegetation of the Throatworks is one plant, divided only by local habit. A wall may fruit as a reservoir; a bridge may sprout ladders; a factory cable may flower when cut. Grafts between apparently unrelated species take with unsettling ease. Root samples from opposite ends of the town contract simultaneously when exposed to salt. Once each long dry season, every leaf in the cavern turns its underside outward at the same moment, and the city becomes briefly silver.

During that interval the machines stop.

The fauna does not. Deprived of vibration, it grows frantic. Birds fly low through the empty courts. Ants abandon their lanes. Burrowing things force their way out of walls. Predators that normally avoid the industrial quarter cross openly beneath the silent Press. The town’s few independent lamps are covered, because light attracts something from the ceiling fissures: a slow, jointed shadow that descends only when the great plant is attending elsewhere.

When the leaves turn green again, the engines restart one by one. The first piston stroke is answered by thousands of animals calling at once. The Press resumes. Bridges tense. Damp shutters open. The city seems to inhale.

No one knows whether the settlement domesticated the cavern’s vegetation, whether the vegetation grew the settlement to maintain its own hidden metabolism, or whether both have been shaped by the overwhelming animal population moving between them. The distinction may have eroded like everything else here. Tool, habitat, organism, and industry no longer remain separate categories.

There are rules, however, and the place enforces them.

Never block a migration court, even when it appears unused.

Never cut a pale root: those carry sensation.

Do not drink from a pool without disturbing its surface first. Some pools are only mouths held level.

When a bridge warms beneath your feet, move toward the nearest wall. The bridge has detected aerial hunting.

If the Press misses three strokes, climb.

If rain begins while the moths are sleeping, descend.

And if, amid the immense machinery and the cries of animals, the whole Throatworks becomes suddenly quiet, do not make a sound to test the silence. The silence is listening for exactly that.
