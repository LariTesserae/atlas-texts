The city occupies a shallow basin no wider than an old cathedral close. From the surrounding plain it appears compact and almost ordinary: a low arrangement of dark roofs, narrow towers, retaining walls, and silver utility lines gathered around a central depression. Nothing rises very high. Its ambition is lateral and inward. Buildings stand shoulder to shoulder, their foundations intertwined, their upper stories leaning into lanes so narrow that opposite windows share condensation.

There are no suburbs. At the last wall, masonry stops cleanly and the cold mineral flat begins.

The ground outside is young ground. It has not yet acquired the habits of terrain. Thin plates of basalt lie loosely fitted over pale sediment, and seams open between them without evidence of strain. Pebbles have sharp, hesitant edges. Runnels begun by brief rains travel only a few meters before losing conviction, leaving dark commas in the dust. There are no deep gullies and few rounded stones. Erosion here seems less like destruction than rehearsal: water making tentative marks, frost lifting a corner, wind testing which particles are willing to move.

The basin itself may be an unfinished landform. Surveyors disagree whether it is sinking, rising around its margins, or only becoming measurable.

Cold settles into it heavily. It is not the spectacular cold of snowfields and storms, but a persistent low temperature held by stone, shade, and the absence of broad water. Breath remains visible indoors. Metal handrails grow a skin of rime at joints and bends. Pipes sweat clear droplets even when empty. Snow is uncommon; what falls is fine and dry, vanishing into cracks or accumulating in the sheltered angles between buildings.

Moisture occurs in pockets. Rain comes occasionally, straight and brief, sounding for a few minutes on thousands of roofs. Afterward, the city releases water for days. Condensation threads down walls. Cistern mouths fill by increments. Beads gather beneath suspended conduits and fall at intervals into ceramic cups set out to receive them. In the central depression lies a black, shallow reservoir, seldom more than ankle-deep. It has no visible inlet. At dawn its surface is often complete; by afternoon it separates into glossy pools divided by ridges of salt.

The inhabitants call this water the Near River.

It is riverine not in shape but in behavior. Objects dropped into one pool may emerge hours later from another. A current can sometimes be seen passing beneath the dry ridges, bending reflections without disturbing the salt. During rain, the separate pools briefly join, and the city’s clocks lose agreement.

Construction is everywhere, though almost no one is seen building.

The oldest structures are made from black brick and a porous gray ceramic that drinks moisture from the air. Later buildings incorporate translucent alloys, dense bundles of fiber, mirror-faced stone, and surfaces with no stable thickness. Their façades are crowded with balconies, vents, narrow staircases, blind doors, cooling ribs, ducts, signal vanes, gutter gardens, and window-like recesses that open not into rooms but into weather.

Every available volume has been used. Rooms hang beneath bridges. Workshops occupy spaces inside load-bearing walls. Public passages run through the middle of residences and, in several districts, through their remembered locations. Infrastructure is layered so densely beneath the lanes that excavators no longer cut downward. They persuade the ground to disclose whichever stratum is needed.

The city’s technology is quiet. There are no roaring engines, few visible screens, no obvious sources of power. Devices resemble architectural details or geological accidents. A lintel evaluates those passing beneath it by altering the weight of their shadows. Door hinges redistribute stress into future earthquakes. Street lamps do not emit light; they remove the local possibility of darkness. Their illumination looks normal—cool daylight by day, amber-white at night—until one notices that no lamp casts a beam.

Much of the city’s machinery manages adjacency.

Space in the basin is scarce, but its engineers discovered that proximity need not be reciprocal. Two chambers may share a wall while being kilometers apart from each other. A narrow stair can be climbed for half an hour without gaining elevation. Several buildings contain the same courtyard, each from a different direction. The municipal archive occupies a structure smaller than a kiosk; inside, its aisles extend beyond the basin’s mapped boundary, though they never approach the plain outside.

These are not illusions. They are zoning.

Brass plaques mark the local rules:

**ONE PLACE PER PERSON AFTER DUSK.**

**DO NOT CROSS A THRESHOLD WHILE IT IS DEEPER THAN THE ROOM.**

**WATER REMEMBERS THE LOWEST ROUTE. DO NOT TEACH IT ANOTHER.**

**BUILDINGS UNDER FORMATION HAVE RIGHT OF WAY.**

The last warning matters most. New construction does not always proceed from foundations upward. Sometimes a finished upper room appears first, suspended between neighboring roofs, furnished and cold. Walls thicken beneath it over subsequent years. A staircase arrives one tread per season. Eventually the ground develops a footing to support what has long been present. Until then, pedestrians detour around its projected load.

Elsewhere, buildings begin as pressures. An alley becomes slightly narrower each winter. Mortar extrudes from blank walls. People dream repeatedly of a particular floor plan. When enough residents have encountered the same rooms in sleep, windows emerge in the masonry and the structure is entered into the register.

Thus the city is always under construction, but only gradually. Scaffolds remain in place for generations. Cranes rotate a few degrees per month. Unfinished arches collect frost. Materials are stacked in every spare niche: thin slabs, sealed cylinders, bricks warm to the touch, rolls of something resembling copper cloth. No dust rises from them. They are said to be raw dimensions awaiting fabrication.

The streets are paved in dark hexagonal units that adjust minutely to footfall. They are nearly flush and show little wear. Along their seams grows the city’s scant vegetation: black moss, white joint-grass, and tiny translucent rosettes whose leaves fold around droplets. In sheltered gutters, a wiry shrub sometimes establishes itself, leafless for most of the year. Its branches bear dull beads that might be fruit, ice, or lenses. Gardeners disagree and tend them without touching them.

There are also condensation gardens on the northern walls. They consist of ribbed panels, mineral wool, and patient troughs. Green algae make thin lines beneath the drips. Small lichens bloom in geometric colonies, apparently constrained by signals in the substrate. Their growth is slow enough to function as a civic calendar. Elections are held when the yellow bands meet.

Beyond the walls, vegetation is limited to low crusts and occasional clumps of silver sedge around temporary pools. Nothing forms a meadow. Roots have difficulty trusting the new soil. Some extend upward into the cold air as if searching for an older ground.

Animals are rare. Pale midges hatch above the Near River after rain and vanish before night. A few small birds nest in warm vents, but they are heard more often than seen: one dry click, then silence. There are long-bodied creatures in the service conduits, smooth and nearly colorless, which technicians classify as either vermin or diagnostic instruments. Their tracks end at solid surfaces.

Once or twice a year, a solitary animal resembling a fox crosses the outer plain. It never enters the city. It pauses at the boundary and watches a location several meters above the roofs, where nothing visible stands. Residents leave no food for it. On the mornings after its visits, one additional doorway can usually be found.

Despite the density, the city is mostly quiet. Walls absorb sound with unusual efficiency, and machinery operates below hearing. Footsteps are short-lived. Conversations seldom travel beyond the speakers. The loudest regular noise is dripping water, amplified in cisterns and hollow beams.

Yet silence is not empty here. It has layers.

In certain alleys one can hear a distant crowd, though the lane is deserted. Pressing an ear to the pavement reveals slow construction sounds from beneath: a scrape, a measured impact, a long granular settling. Some nights a train passes under the central reservoir, making no vibration, only a sequence of absences in the ambient sound. There are no tracks in the known substrate.

Bells hang in several towers, but they are not struck. At irregular intervals they remember having rung. The recollection moves through the metal as a soft inward tone, audible only in enclosed rooms.

Reality fails locally rather than catastrophically. A wall may forget which side is exterior. Reflections remain after their objects depart, often continuing small actions the originals did not perform. Distances acquire moods. A lane offended by haste lengthens; another contracts around those carrying rainwater. Names detach from places and attach to nearby materials. For several decades the western gate was called Slate, while all slate within the city answered to Western Gate.

The inhabitants have adapted through etiquette, inspection, and conservative dress. Coats are stitched with reference lines so others can tell whether a body is bending or the space around it. Everyone carries a small plumb bob, though gravity is among the more reliable phenomena. Children learn not to count windows aloud. Addresses include a street, a depth, a temperature, and a conditional phrase: *third blue door, two levels inward, below freezing, if approached alone.*

Maps are issued on brittle sheets that crack when inaccurate.

Time behaves better than space, but not perfectly. Changes are slow and often cumulative. A cup may take years to fall from a shelf, descending imperceptibly between inspections. Frost can form before the cold that causes it. An abandoned building becomes ancient over a single winter while its neighbors remain merely old. The city keeps multiple calendars, none considered authoritative. What matters is sequence: cistern filling before gate closure, gate closure before lamps, lamps before the streets begin exchanging intersections.

The landscape does not feel violent. Its breakdown is patient, infrastructural, almost administrative. There are no spectacular tears in the sky. The normal pale sun crosses at a believable pace. Clouds have familiar shapes. Shadows generally behave. The strangeness accumulates in the agreements between things—in whether a room belongs inside its building, whether a puddle is downstream from itself, whether a foundation supports the tower above it or the possibility of that tower.

At the basin’s edge, the outermost wall is still forming. It consists of alternating sections: finished stone, exposed reinforcement, dense fog, and stretches where only the shadow of a wall lies across the raw ground. Workers inspect it each morning and note which portions have become material. They do not attempt to accelerate the process. Anything made too quickly here is prone to becoming true in the wrong way.

Past that uncertain boundary lies the low plain, cold and sparsely marked. Rain darkens it in isolated patches. Thin ice forms over hollows and sometimes remains suspended after the water beneath has gone. The horizon is close, not because hills obstruct it, but because distance loses resolution beyond a few kilometers. Objects traveling outward become simpler: first textureless, then colorless, then difficult to classify. No expedition has proved the plain endless. None has found what contains it.

From outside, the city gives the impression of shelter: concentrated walls, warm windows, narrow streets, water carefully gathered. From within, it feels more like an instrument assembled around the Near River, though no one knows what it measures. The buildings crowd close to observe the dark pools. Towers incline their sensors toward them. Archives record their divisions and reunions. Every gutter eventually points inward.

During the rare full rain, when water runs openly over the young stone and the reservoir becomes a single sheet, the entire city is reflected there.

The reflection is always more complete.

It contains finished walls where scaffolds stand, mature trees in bare courtyards, animals moving through the lanes, and high structures that have not yet begun to exert their pressures. Its windows are lit. Its streets are crowded, though no sound rises from them. For a few minutes, the basin holds both the dense, cold city and whatever city is gradually arriving beneath it.

Then the rain stops. The water separates. The other place breaks into small black pools, and construction resumes at its nearly imperceptible pace.
