The plain begins before the horizon and continues beyond it: a low, hot expanse assembled entirely from fired earth.

There is no underlying bedrock. No buried continent, no natural soil, no ancient mantle pushing upward. Far below the visible surface, the same constructed material continues in courses and layers—brick beneath tile, tile beneath compacted ceramic aggregate, then enormous hollow vaults whose load-bearing arches descend into heat and darkness. Nothing here was shaped with tools, because tools have never existed. The landscape is made in the older sense that nests and shells are made: secreted, deposited, hardened, and repeated by life.

Whatever built the plain is gone.

At ground level, its construction is easy to mistake for geology. Sun-baked plates, each several hundred paces across, fit together along dark seams. Their colors vary from pale buff to iron red, with occasional reaches of cream-colored material that glare in the noon light. The plates are nearly level, but not perfectly so. Some sag toward broad joints; others rise at their edges as heat expands them. Their surfaces bear shallow ridges, dimples, and parallel textures like fingerprints enlarged to the dimensions of fields.

The land is still new. It has barely begun to wear.

Edges remain crisp. Corners have not yet rounded into pebbles. Dust is scarce except where two plates grind against one another, producing narrow fans of fine powder. There are no deep canyons, no mature drainage systems, no slopes layered with fallen debris. Rain has only started to teach water where to go. Each storm invents temporary streams, and many of these choose a different path the next time.

Heat rules most hours. In the middle of the day the air trembles over the plates, making distant seams lift and dissolve. Touching the darker ground would burn living tissue. Warm currents rise from millions of square miles at once and gather into slow atmospheric cells, so vast that the plain generates its own weather. Cloud banks form far out over regions invisible beyond the horizon. They travel for days over empty construction, shedding themselves unevenly.

Rain is infrequent but not rare. It arrives in immense, isolated curtains.

From a distance, a storm appears as a gray-blue column joining sky and plain. Beneath it, water drums softly at first, then covers the fired surfaces in a bright skin. Because the ground cannot absorb much, the rain spreads sideways. Sheets only a finger deep may run for miles, branching around minute rises and collecting in joints. Broad shallow basins fill, turning the landscape into scattered mirrors. Steam rises where the first drops strike. Later, when the plates cool, condensation beads along their undersides and in the mouths of the seams.

Within a day or two, most open water is gone. It evaporates, drains into the buried vaults, or remains in shaded hollows as warm, mineral-tasting pools. These pockets are the only dense places in the landscape.

Around them grows a sparse flora of low mats, upright threads, and bladder-like cups. There are no trees, and almost nothing casts a shadow larger than a hand. The most common growth is a dull gray-green film that occupies hairline cracks. Dry, it contracts until it resembles discoloration in the ceramic. Wet, it swells almost instantly and becomes soft and vivid, spreading translucent lobes into the passing water.

Other plants anchor themselves with fine acidic fibers that enter the surface without truly eroding it. Their bodies are simple: fans to catch morning condensation, waxed stems to endure noon, pale reservoirs buried within the joints. Some remain dormant for years between favorable rains. After a storm, they raise brief stalks bearing dust-fine spores. There are no flowers because there are no animals to visit them, and no fruits because nothing eats.

The absence of animal life is absolute. Nothing flies across the sun. Nothing burrows, grazes, calls, or leaves tracks beside the pools. The plants are stirred only by air and water. Even the smallest living motes belong to the vegetable processes of the place: photosynthetic skins, spore colonies, mineral-binding threads. Pollination, dispersal, and decay are accomplished by wind, heat, rupture, and rain.

This gives the plain a profound quiet, though it is not inert.

At noon there is the faint ticking of expansion. Each plate warms at a slightly different rate, pressing against its neighbors. A seam will sometimes produce a long ceramic murmur, too low to be called a crack, which travels beneath the feet and fades into the distance. In the evening, cooling surfaces answer with small clicks. Wind makes almost no sound over the level ground until it reaches a joint, where it whispers through buried cavities. After rain, water can be heard trickling far below, drop by isolated drop.

The construction repairs itself, though no intelligence directs it.

Deep in the vaults, heat, moisture, and living mineral colonies produce a slow slurry. Capillary pressure carries this material upward through the seams. It emerges at night as a dark glaze, spreads into gaps, and hardens after sunrise. Where a plate has pulled apart, successive films accumulate into a flexible gasket. Where a surface has buckled, the underside is dissolved and redeposited along the new line of stress. The processes are biological but impersonal, like shell formation in a creature too immense and distributed to possess a body.

Thus the plain is active without appearing busy. It flexes. It sweats. It seals itself. Water migrates through it. Vegetation wakes and collapses. Weather moves across it in systems larger than nations.

In a few places, construction remains visibly unfinished. There the level plates end at broad, shallow depressions filled with cream-colored paste. Over months, the paste develops a skin; over years, it firms into tessellated fields. Hexagonal divisions appear as it dries, though their lines are too regular to seem accidental and too variable to seem designed. Sparse plant films colonize the youngest cracks first, darkening the geometry. These are not ruins or building sites. They are places where the world is still becoming floor.

Elsewhere stand isolated forms: hollow ridges no higher than a house, immense circular lips surrounding empty basins, and long walls that emerge from the plain only to descend again after several miles. They have no clear purpose now. Perhaps they once regulated moisture or bore the weight of structures that were never completed. Their surfaces are seamless, ribbed like the interior of a shell. Wind gathers a little dust against their shaded sides, and after rain they hold narrow ribbons of water for weeks.

Because the elevation is low, the horizon is clean and oppressive. There are no mountain chains to organize distance. A person could walk toward a visible storm for days and never reach where it seemed to fall. Features that look nearby prove enormous: a dark line becomes a seam wide enough to contain a river; a pale patch becomes a basin with its own clouds; a slight sheen becomes hundreds of square miles of flooded tile.

Morning is the gentlest time. Condensation silvering the joints catches ordinary yellow light. The gray-green mats open their pores. Heat has not yet erased the furthest details, and one can see subtle changes in color running toward the horizon—older red surfaces, newer ivory ones, low blue reflections where rain remains.

By afternoon the plain is almost vacant of contrast. Plants close. Pools shrink behind white rims. The sky pales, and the fired world returns the sun’s heat upward.

Toward evening, the buried structure begins to speak in restrained pops and settling tones. Shadows gather in the seams before they gather anywhere else. Far away, a rain column may darken the horizon, moving slowly across a land with no roads, no inhabitants, and no wilderness—because wilderness implies an opposite, and here the constructed world is all there is.
