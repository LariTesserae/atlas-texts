The high basin is entirely built.

There is no buried earth beneath it, no bedrock should one pry deeply enough, no old mountain holding up the terraces. From rim to rim, and downward farther than any sounding line has measured, the country consists of fitted stone, fired clay, lime mortar, glassy slag, and hollow chambers laid one upon another. It rises like a plateau because countless generations of walls have been filled in, roofed over, and built upon. Distance disguises its workmanship. Seen from the upper parapets, the basin resembles a pale, broken upland after rain. Only nearby does every cliff reveal courses, joints, thumbprints, wedge marks, and the fine horizontal signatures of masonry.

Water is everywhere, but it owns no broad surface.

It shines in seams between paving slabs and stands in square cistern mouths packed so closely that their rims nearly touch. It beads beneath lintels, trickles through clay conduits, and weeps from the faces of walls. After the frequent bright rains, each stair becomes a narrow cascade. Courtyards fill ankle-deep and empty through thousands of little drilled holes, producing a subdued, distributed murmur. The whole basin seems to breathe out water.

Nothing grows here. No grass catches in the joints, no moss darkens the shaded channels, no bird crosses the white sky. Even rot is absent. Water stains the stone with iron-red fans and cloudy bands of salt, but leaves no slime. The only soft-looking things are mineral skins: pale crusts swelling around spouts, blue-gray deposits folded like wax, and translucent curtains forming where drops have fallen at the same point for centuries.

The light is severe without being warm. At this elevation the sky is close, clean, and almost colorless. Sunlight strikes wet masonry and multiplies until the basin is filled with a cold brilliance. Every puddle throws a small moving glare onto an arch or ceiling. Walls facing the light are chalk-white; walls turned away are lavender, silver, or the dark green of deep water, though nothing green lives there. Clouds arrive below the highest towers. They pass through streets as condensation, fastening droplets to ropes, ridges, and chipped steps. Then they withdraw, leaving the whole constructed country glittering.

The building is dense beyond the logic of habitation. Stairs climb into the undersides of other stairs. Channels cross roofs that are also roads. Narrow towers are wedged into old courtyards, and smaller chambers occupy their buttresses. Doorways open onto sealed masonry; windows look through several adjacent rooms in a straight line and finally end at a reservoir wall. There are plazas, but each plaza is crowded with low cisterns, rain tables, stone pegs, baffles, gutters, and waist-high partitions. Empty space seems to have been regarded as unfinished material.

No metal fastens any of it. The builders used fitted joints, wooden-looking forms made from fired clay, stone pins, knotted mineral-fiber cord, and wedges of dense black glass. Copper appears only as rare green stains beneath ceremonial spouts. Tools were simple: hammerstones, abrasive sand, drills turned by hand, antler-shaped implements carved from stone because there were no animals to supply antler. Yet the aggregate work is immense. Its sophistication lies not in machines but in repetition—one careful cut made a billion times.

The newest districts stand along the basin’s outer rise. They are sharp-edged and raw, scarcely weathered. Blocks retain bruises from transport; mortar bulges between them in pale beads. Here, roofs have not yet settled, and rain runs briskly along channels with crisp corners. Farther inward, surfaces soften under water and salt, but even the oldest structures are only gently worn. Erosion has barely begun its long argument with the place. A few stair noses have rounded. A few spouts have acquired stone beards. Most of the vast construction still looks recently uncovered, although no one remains to uncover it.

The basin has rules that become apparent slowly.

Water always descends, but not necessarily toward the lowest visible place. A gutter may carry rain downhill for half a mile, then feed it through a wall and upward into a cistern on the next terrace. Pressure chambers hidden below the paving accept the weight of falling water and raise a smaller portion elsewhere. There are no pumps in the familiar sense—only sealed jars, counterweighted stone gates, siphons, and reservoirs arranged with patient ingenuity. Hours after a storm, a dry tower may begin to weep from its summit. Days later, a courtyard far above the cloud line fills soundlessly from slots in its floor.

Every structure is part of this circulation. Houses, if they were houses, are shaped like vessels. Towers divide flows. Streets are spillways. Amphitheaters catch mist. Long colonnades condense water on their shaded inner faces and send the droplets into covered troughs. The packed architecture is less a city than a single porous instrument for delaying rain.

Its principal sound is the isolated drop.

One falls into a deep chamber and returns as a small, hollow note. Another strikes a clay plate with a dry click. From somewhere behind a sealed wall comes the brief sliding grind of a stone float adjusting to a rising reservoir. The sounds do not gather into noise. They remain separate across the open basin, each surrounded by enormous quiet. Wind is weak here, impeded by the saturated density of walls. When it does move, it passes through thousands of drilled apertures and makes a low breath that can be mistaken for distance itself.

There are no inhabitants and no relics of bodies. Basins contain neither bones nor husks. Kitchens, graves, beds, and pens cannot be identified. The chambers seem scaled for hands and shoulders, yet the place offers no proof that living makers occupied it. Some passages narrow until only water could continue. Some doors stand twenty feet high but only a handspan wide. Tool marks appear in positions no worker could have reached after the surrounding courses were laid.

Perhaps construction was not an event that ended.

In a few sheltered courts, loose blocks wait beside unfinished walls. Rain fills wedge-shaped clay cups fixed above them. As the cups grow heavy, they tip and release stones that strike levers. The levers tighten cords, nudge sledges, or draw abrasive lines across a block. A single cut may deepen by the width of a hair each rainy season. Counterweights rise and fall with the cisterns. Mortar—lime dust gathered from runoff and concentrated in settling bowls—seeps into prepared joints. Nothing happens quickly enough to watch, but measurements taken decades apart would show that certain walls have gained a course.

Thus the basin may still be building itself with the primitive gestures of vanished hands.

Its growth is slow and crowded. New partitions appear where water has repeatedly chosen a path. Tiles creep into alignment through cycles of wetting, freezing, and thawing. Mineral deposits seal some channels and divert flow into others, causing dormant mechanisms to wake. A stair assembled over centuries eventually reaches a doorway that has waited, inexplicably, at the correct height. Elsewhere a tower leans by a fraction and locks against its neighbor, making both stronger.

At the basin’s center, terraces descend toward a region so densely constructed that movement through it becomes impossible. Roof presses against roof. Spires stand with barely a finger’s width between them. Channels are stacked in braided layers; rain can be heard traveling below but seldom seen. The central mass rises and falls in pale ridges like a mountain range made entirely of rooms. Its highest points catch fresh snow, though the air below remains merely cool. Meltwater vanishes into the masonry before noon.

From a distance, the center appears solid. In truth it is almost all interior: millions of narrow cavities, sealed tanks, blind corridors, pressure cells, and resonant wells. The absence of life has not made it empty. It is occupied everywhere by structure, by captured water, by stored weight, by intended pathways. Even the silence is divided into chambers.

At sunset the brightness persists. Wet walls retain the sky and return it in silver planes long after the sun has dropped behind the outer works. Then cold moves down through the basin. Thin films of water glaze the upper steps, and droplets freeze at the mouths of spouts. The hidden reservoirs continue to settle. Stone gates lower by imperceptible degrees.

In the night, perhaps once in several hours, a counterweight reaches the end of its descent.

There is a single knock, deep inside the made mountain.

Afterward, somewhere high and unseen, water begins to pour into a newly opened room.
