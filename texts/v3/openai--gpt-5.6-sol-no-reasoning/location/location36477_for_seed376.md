The country occupies a high, cold basin where the mountains have been worn down to long backs and shallow escarpments. Nothing here is bare. Vegetation covers the ground so completely that geology must be inferred from its posture: a steep darkening where a cliff lies underneath, a sag where ancient scree is slowly settling, broad ribs where stone rises near the surface. From one rim of the basin to the other, the land appears upholstered in living matter.

At first it resembles a forest seen from above. Closer in, the distinction between forest, undergrowth, and soil disappears. There are no trunks planted individually in earth. Instead, a continuous woody substrate spreads over the valley in interlocked rafts, buttresses, cables, and compression arches. Upright growths rise from it like crowded black-green chimneys. Their crowns meet and graft together, making a second surface ten or twenty meters above the first. Under that canopy is another canopy, and beneath that a close, cold maze of fibrous columns. The whole basin is one vegetation mass, divided less into organisms than into rates of growth.

The upper surface is pale with frost. Leaves are small, thick, and densely tiled, each edged in silver hairs that trap condensation. In ordinary daylight the basin has subdued colors: spruce black, oxidized green, old bronze, the ivory of dead fibers, and occasional fields of red reproductive cups. Frost gathers in the hollows between leaves and remains there long after sunrise. When the air warms by even a fraction, droplets form everywhere at once. The vegetation shines briefly, then drinks most of the water through pores along its exposed surfaces.

There is little free water. Rain comes in short, slanting squalls, and meltwater sometimes travels through the basin, but streams are temporary events rather than permanent features. They appear in established channels under the living mat, felt first as a trembling and then as a cold internal rush. Here and there the substrate opens around a pool: black water in a circular throat, its edges overhung by root-felt. These pools can persist for years, then be sealed in an afternoon by explosive growth. Elsewhere, water collects in thousands of leaf-cups and translucent bladders, scattered reservoirs no larger than a hand. The valley is damp without seeming wet. Moisture is held privately.

The cold regulates everything, but it does not make the place still.

Across the basin, growth occurs with industrial regularity and catastrophic force. Sap is driven through immense vascular mains under pressure. Every few minutes, somewhere out of sight, a valve of living tissue opens. A pulse passes through the plant mass: stems straighten, membranes inflate, entire groves lean in sequence. Pollen vents from tall organs in measured columns. Seed husks split in rapid chains, each crack setting off the next until a broad hillside shivers as if traversed by machinery.

No machinery has been built here. There are no roads, towers, walls, discarded tools, or cut surfaces. Yet the valley has the organization of a factory too large to perceive as one. Vertical stacks draw air through porous tissues. Flat black leaves track the pale sun with slow mechanical precision. Deep in the mat, layered fermentation chambers generate heat, preventing the interior from freezing. Mineral-rich water is pumped uphill through braided conduits. Old cellulose is dissolved, sorted by fungal membranes, and pressed into new structural fiber. At intervals, long belts of creeping growth carry hard seed capsules from shaded interior zones to exposed ridges, where wind can take them.

The impression of industry comes not from metal but from repetition, throughput, and scale. Each form performs a narrow task. Some growths condense fog. Some quarry the stone with acid. Some strengthen spans across gullies. Some seal damage. Some exist only to buckle and tear at appointed tensions, relieving stress before a larger rupture can travel through the basin.

Nevertheless, failures are common. The system is too active to be peaceful.

A condensation tower may become top-heavy with ice and fold into the canopy, crushing an area the size of a meadow. Almost immediately, surrounding tissues brighten, divide, and surge into the wound. Pale cords lash across the gap. Sponge growth billows up from below. Red cups open in concentric rings and shed clouds of warm spores. Within hours the fallen mass is buried; within days, it has become food and foundation. On distant slopes, whole plates of vegetation detach and slide slowly over one another, exposing flashes of steaming interior tissue before the margins knit closed.

These disturbances happen almost silently. The thick plant matter absorbs impact. There is no birdsong and scarcely any insect drone. Wind rarely reaches the lower layers. The loudest ordinary sounds are minute: droplets clicking from leaf to leaf, frost ticking as it releases, the soft drawn-out creak of fibers under strain. A major rupture produces not a crash but a deep compression, more felt through the feet than heard—like a door closing far underground.

Fauna is uncommon, perhaps unnecessary. Small pale grazers sometimes appear beside the water throats, six-legged and woolly, cropping mineral crusts from the rims. They move cautiously, because the ground responds to prolonged pressure. If one stands too long in a place, sensory filaments rise around its feet. Most flee before the filaments close. Their tracks remain as dark ovals for a few minutes, then the leaves rotate and erase them.

Higher up, solitary winged animals cross the basin at great altitude. They never land on the central mat. During seed releases they circle the perimeter, waiting for the few drifting organisms they can catch in the air. Their calls, if they make any, are lost in the open cold above.

Travel through the vegetation would be possible only in the sense that travel through a sleeping animal is possible. There are firm-looking ridges, but firmness migrates. A broad woody plate may support weight in the morning and soften by noon as its internal material is pumped elsewhere. Gullies close. Horizontal trunks untwist and stand upright. A clear corridor can fill with waist-high cilia in minutes. The safest surfaces are the oldest: gray, weather-rounded bosses where wind and frost have polished many generations of bark into a dense laminate. Even these breathe faintly.

The basin's underlying shape is ancient and settled. Water, ice, and chemical roots have rounded every stone. The cliffs are not jagged but deeply fluted, their grooves inherited by the vegetation covering them. Landslides have reached their angles of rest. Drainages repeat old paths. Beneath the ceaseless biological turbulence is an equilibrium so mature that each disturbance seems already anticipated. Nothing changes the country’s overall form. Destruction moves through it like weather through grass.

Along the higher western rim, the vegetation thins just enough to reveal how it has altered the mountain. Roots have entered the rock along microscopic fractures, separated it grain by grain, and incorporated those grains into tough, ceramic tissues. Some ridges are no longer stone with plants on them but composite structures: quartz and lignin, mica and resin, basalt threaded with vascular bundles. If the living material vanished, much of the basin wall would collapse. If the rock vanished, the vegetation might retain the mountain’s shape for centuries.

In the late afternoon, sunlight enters beneath the upper canopy at a shallow angle. For a short time the internal architecture becomes visible across great distances. Veins glow amber. Membranes shine like thin ice. Vapor rises from warm decomposition chambers and moves through the forest in level sheets. Countless surfaces adjust toward the light—not together, but in waves—giving the impression that the entire valley is turning its attention westward.

Then the cold returns. Condensation hardens. Exposed valves close, and pressure is redirected below. The upper basin grows quiet enough that a single drop falling into a hidden pool can seem near, though it may be hundreds of meters away.

In darkness, the turbulence continues out of sight: pumping, splitting, dissolving, weaving, lowering one hillside and lifting another. By morning, the horizon is familiar but not identical. A crown-field has become a plain. A plain has bristled into towers. One of the black pools has vanished, while another has opened in an old depression and begun collecting the day’s sparse melt.

Nothing has arrived. Nothing has been built. Yet the cold basin works without rest, remaking itself in the weathered shape it has occupied for ages.
