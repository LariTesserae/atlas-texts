At extreme altitude, the plateau is broad enough to contain weather.

It lies above the ordinary cloud deck: a lifted province of pale stone, perhaps the exposed floor of an ancient sea, though no sea could have rested at this height. The horizon is near in some directions and impossibly far in others. Ranges of eroded pillars crowd the plateau in bands—chalk-white, blue-gray, and translucent where mineral veins have weathered open. Between them stretch shallow basins, stair-stepped ravines, and fields of thin stone fins packed almost edge to edge. There is no soil. Nothing roots in the cracks. No lichen stains the rock, no insect moves under a flake of shale. Even decay seems absent. Everything here is mineral, water, light, and duration.

The air is cool and brilliant. Sunlight falls with an almost physical pressure, sharpened by altitude until every edge casts a violet shadow. Yet the atmosphere is never dry. Vapor condenses continually on the cold faces of the pillars. Water beads on overhangs, gathers in seams, and runs downward in silver threads. After midday, brief rains cross the plateau without visible clouds: straight curtains appearing in otherwise blue air, traveling against the wind and dissolving before they reach the eastern scarps.

Water remains close to the surface. It fills long cracks beneath skins of calcite and breathes through pores in the rock. Step near certain shelves and the stone darkens around your foot, moisture rising as if pressed from a sponge. There are tarns in the larger hollows, but they seldom agree with their basins. Their surfaces may tilt several degrees without spilling. One pool climbs its northern bank in a glassy bulge while leaving the southern mud—or what resembles mud, a paste of powdered stone—perfectly dry. Another repeats the sky from three hours earlier. At dawn it holds stars.

The plateau has been weathered into an established intricacy. Nothing looks newly broken. Even fresh fractures are rounded, their interiors furred with tiny mineral needles and polished by water that has apparently flowed through them for centuries. Gullies interlock with raised channels. Caverns open upward. Stone arches sag but never fall. The whole place gives the impression not of ruin, but of an old arrangement continually maintaining itself.

Its loudness comes partly from water. Drips strike hollow slabs with the report of mallets. Hidden streams roar under thin floors. Wind passes through ranks of perforated rock and divides into hundreds of simultaneous notes: fluting shrieks, low organ pulses, dry rattles where loose pebbles dance in sockets. Thunder is common in clear weather. It does not roll across the sky; it travels underground, arriving through the soles as a series of heavy knocks.

There are other sounds with no clear source. A distant clamor like stones being quarried by a large workforce rises every afternoon from the western maze, though no workers appear and no chips accumulate. Sometimes the plateau answers itself. A shout returns not as an echo but as a repetition in the speaker’s own voice, coming minutes later from somewhere ahead. Quiet is possible only in the deepest basins, where sound can be seen as concentric disturbances passing through the air, bending sunlight as they go.

Scattered among the formations are small constructions. Most are waist-high shelters made from fitted slabs, too cramped for comfortable habitation and too deliberate to be natural. Their roofs are corbeled from flat stones. Their entrances face not east or west, but toward particular pillars, pools, or empty intervals. Inside are bowls pecked from basalt, blunt stone wedges, cordage mineralized into white curls, and plates of hammered native copper turned green-black by moisture.

The workmanship is patient rather than sophisticated. Holes have been drilled with sand and hand rotation. Blocks were split along bedding planes, then rubbed smooth. In several places, narrow bridges cross cracks that a person could easily step over. Elsewhere stairways ascend sheer monoliths and end at blank walls. Lines of fist-sized cairns thread through the crowded stone fields, yet they shift position whenever rain passes over them. Each cairn remains intact; only the route changes.

The largest structure stands near the central escarpment. It is a low ring of dark blocks enclosing a shaft. Marks around its rim resemble tally cuts, but they continue beneath later layers of stone and reappear on the opposite side in altered sequence. Water falls into the shaft from the bright, empty air above. Nothing feeds the stream. The falling column twists slowly, thick as rope, and produces a continuous roar that can be heard across half the plateau. Dropped objects do not descend with it. They drift outward, orbit the shaft once, and settle somewhere behind the thrower.

Reality loosens most clearly around the dense pillar fields.

Distances there cannot be held stable. Two neighboring spires may require an hour to walk between, while a ravine seen beyond them can be reached in ten steps. An arch approached from the south is tall enough to pass beneath; approached from the north, it is a solid wall. Reflections persist after the water carrying them has rippled away. Shadows collect in cavities and pour out at sunset like dark liquid, joining until the eastern valleys lie under a second, ground-level night.

Even gravity has weather. Most of the day it draws downward with ordinary firmness. During the coldest hour after dawn, loose droplets fall toward the nearest large mass instead: sideways onto cliffs, upward beneath arches, inward toward pillars. The change announces itself with a rising thunder as underground water abandons its channels. For several minutes the plateau wears streams across every surface. They lace the rock horizontally, split at corners, and leap gaps in bright threads. Then gravity returns, and all that suspended water collapses at once. The impact is deafening. Basins fill, ledges sheet over, and waterfalls appear on cliffs that were dry seconds before.

No organisms inhabit these waters. There are no reeds, algae, fish, flies, spores, or grazing shapes. The green films visible in sheltered cracks are copper salts. The red blooms around springs are iron. Pearly membranes on the tarns are mineral skins that crack into hexagons and sink at noon. Some formations imitate life uncannily—branching crystals, ribbed nodules, delicate tubes clustered like coral—but they neither grow nor reproduce in any familiar sense. They merely become more numerous when unobserved.

That may be the plateau’s nearest equivalent to ecology: an exchange among forms, pressures, and attention. Pools migrate. Pillars alter the wind. Sound opens hairline fractures; rain seals them with deposited stone. Copper tools left in shelters migrate slowly toward the central shaft, one green stain at a time. Cairns reroute travelers around places where distance has folded too tightly. The landscape is active not because it is alive, but because equilibrium here requires constant revision.

By evening the brightness becomes severe. The sinking sun multiplies in wet facets until illumination comes from every direction. Stone fins turn transparent at their edges. Deep beneath the plateau, outlines of buried ravines appear, full of moving water and small roofed chambers identical to those above. For a few minutes one can look down through miles of rock as through cloudy ice.

Then the sun touches the horizon and remains there.

It may remain for minutes or for several nights. Watches disagree. Water continues to drip, roar, and climb. The wind plays the perforated towers. Empty shelters lengthen their shadows toward destinations that are not present. Somewhere in the western maze, unseen hands—or the remembered effect of hands—resume their hammering.

Nothing answers them except the stone, which answers loudly, and in time.
