The land is a high, cold immensity of shale.

From a distance it appears almost level, a dark table laid beneath a dim sky, but this is an illusion caused by scale. The surface is divided into escarpments hundreds of kilometers long, shallow basins broad enough to gather weather, and trenches whose opposite rims are lost beyond the curvature of the world. Nothing rises sharply. Every ridge has been planed by ancient abrasion; every hollow has acquired the settled shape of something that has held cold for an age. Stone lies in long laminations—charcoal, iron-gray, muted blue—pressed flat and polished at their exposed edges.

Water is present, but seldom as open water. It gathers as condensation beneath overhangs, in porous seams, and along the undersides of loose plates of rock. At dusk, moisture migrates upward through the shale and beads on the surface in minute black pearls. By morning most of these have frozen. The ground then glitters faintly, not with snow but with countless lenses of clear ice, each no wider than a fingernail.

There are rains perhaps six or seven times in a long year. They arrive as thin diagonal veils and may cross a basin without wetting its floor. Where rain does reach the ground, it runs through old channels so perfectly established that the water seems to remember them. For several hours the trenches fill with steel-colored streams. Then they empty into blind gravel sinks, leaving pockets beneath the rock: cold cisterns without walls, hidden from the sky.

Vegetation survives sparsely around these buried reservoirs. There are low black rosettes with surfaces like velvet, pale jointed stems tucked into fissures, and mats of rust-colored filament which remain brittle for most of the year. After rain they soften and darken. Some lift transparent bladders to the air, opening for a single dim afternoon before collapsing again. Across the great flats, individual plants can stand kilometers apart.

Yet the country is full of animals.

At first this is not apparent. The plateau appears vacant because nearly everything living lies close to the ground or moves beneath it. Then the light changes, and the stone itself resolves into multitudes.

Along every bedding plane are creatures shaped like flat, articulated leaves. They overlap by the millions, fitted edge to edge across the shale. Their shells bear the same grain and mineral stains as the rock, and when still they are indistinguishable from it. They feed on condensation, passing droplets from one body to another through fine combs around their mouths. Moisture entering at the windward rim of a colony may travel hundreds of meters without touching stone.

In the deep basins, herds of taller animals move with glacial patience. Each stands on many narrow legs and carries a high chambered back plated in dull metal-gray scales. Vapor condenses inside those chambers during the cold night. Through the day the animals draw upon it, then release mineral pellets behind them in regular rows. Seen from above, the migrating herds resemble enormous industrial processes: conveyors advancing across an empty floor, sorting and redistributing the ground.

There are no factories here, no roads, no towers, no artifacts. The industrial quality belongs to the ecology itself.

Colonies operate as refineries. Burrowing organisms separate salts from groundwater and stack the discarded crystals in low hexagonal fields. Soft-bodied filterers inhabit fissures like living valves, opening and closing in sequence as pressure changes below. Vast breeding congregations assemble around warm faults and cover the rock in interlocking carapaces, their movements turning broad portions of the landscape from matte gray to oily silver. Electrical charges pass through them in slow pulses. During such events, blue light travels along the fault for hundreds of kilometers, section by section, like current moving through an immeasurably large circuit.

The illumination is weak enough that it does not brighten the sky. It merely reveals distance: a blue line crossing one basin, disappearing behind a beveled ridge, then reappearing much later on another horizon.

Despite their abundance, the animals are almost silent. Wings, where they exist, are membranous but do not beat; they are raised into the thin wind like sails. Feet are padded. Shells fit without clicking. Even predation proceeds without cries. A hunter may take days to close around a herd, its broad body advancing by contractions too slow for the eye to follow. The prey respond by altering course over weeks.

The loudest natural sound is the rare fracture of ice in an underground pocket. A dry knock travels through the shale, followed by a sequence of diminishing taps from distant strata. Rain makes only a faint hiss. The great colonies drink it before it can drum against the stone.

Wind is nearly continuous on the heights, but it, too, is strangely quiet. Centuries of erosion have removed the corners that would make it whistle. It passes over rounded escarpments and through broad saddles without turbulence, pressing steadily rather than gusting. Loose material is scarce. What remains has been sorted into stable pavements: black pebbles laid tightly together, each turned to the angle at which the wind can no longer move it.

The few vertical formations are remnants rather than monuments. On some horizons stand immense ribs of harder stone, widely spaced and worn blunt at the top. They might be mistaken for ruined walls, but there are no courses, no joints, no evidence of intention. They are the surviving partitions between vanished valleys. Animals gather on their sheltered sides in such numbers that the ribs seem furred from base to summit.

Above all this hangs a low-luminous sky. The sun is generally visible only as a denser patch of pearl behind layers of suspended ice. Shadows are weak and enormous. Noon resembles late evening; evening is a long descent into blue-blackness. At night, thermal currents rising from the deep faults stain the cloud deck with faint bands of amber. Beneath those bands, the fauna slowly converge.

Seen across geological time, the plateau behaves like a machine running at almost no speed. Water is lifted, frozen, carried in bodies, concentrated into salts, and returned underground. Minerals migrate through digestive systems and are laid down in new strata. Herds grind high surfaces lower by fractions of a grain. Colonies seal some cracks and widen others. The terrain shapes the animals, and the animals continue the erosion of the terrain.

No part of the process has a clear beginning. Even the oldest exposed surfaces bear trails, feeding fans, nesting grooves, and the polished routes of vanished migrations. Fossils emerge already aligned with living bodies above them, as though the present population were only the uppermost layer of one continuous organism extending downward through the plateau.

The governing rule of the place is not struggle but allocation. Heat, water, salt, and electrical charge are scarce; life is not. Every available portion is held temporarily by some body and passed onward with little waste. Empty space separates the great concentrations, but beneath that emptiness run threads of exchange: seepage paths, migration lines, conductive seams, tunnels filled with dormant young.

Thus the vastness never feels deserted. It feels occupied in reserve.

One may look across two hundred kilometers of dim, weathered stone and see nothing move. Then condensation begins. A darkening travels over the ground. Millions of flat bodies open their combs at once, herds turn imperceptibly toward the buried water, and the blue current starts along a distant fault.

There is no roar of machinery, no beating of wings, no chorus.

Only the whole plateau, quietly commencing another cycle.
