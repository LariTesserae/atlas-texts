Beneath forty metres of cold seawater lies a shallow basin in the continental rock. It is not large: from one rim to the other would be a short walk on land, perhaps the width of a cathedral close. Underwater, distance is harder to judge. The farther edge fades into blue-green suspension, and the whole depression can briefly seem much larger than it is.

The basin is simple in form. Its floor is a broad sheet of dark basalt dusted with pale silt. Low shelves descend around it in long, weathered steps, their sharpness worn away over ages until every transition has become a slope or rounded lip. Cracks run through the rock in quiet parallel lines. They are old and settled, packed with mineral deposits, and nothing widens them now. Along the northern rim, several basalt columns lean together without touching, naturally jointed remnants of a lava flow. They resemble machinery only from a distance: cylinders, flanges, repeating housings. Up close they are plainly stone.

At the centre is a field of natural vents.

They are not violent black smokers, but hundreds of narrow pores distributed through the bedrock with a regularity imposed by its cooling fractures. Through them, seawater circulates into deeper, warmer strata and returns carrying dissolved metals. Pressure drives the system. Heat maintains it. No part was built, and there are no pipes, valves, walls, or tools, yet the basin has the atmosphere of an industrial plant running behind sealed doors.

The vents release clear water only slightly warmer than the sea around it. From some comes a nearly invisible shimmer. Others precipitate thin collars of iron oxide, manganese, and chalk-white carbonate. Over thousands of years these collars have joined into low manifolds of mineral crust: elongated ridges, circular mouths, and parallel channels laid out according to the fracture grid below. There is repetition without design and production without labor. Seawater enters, minerals are sorted, heat is exchanged, and deposits accumulate so slowly that the process appears motionless.

Nothing here moves enough to disturb the silt.

The surrounding ocean is unusually still. There are no tides perceptible in the basin, no bubbles, no streaming sediment. The water has a uniform coolness except within a finger’s breadth of the vent mouths. Even there, the differences are mild and constant. The place has reached an equilibrium in which each input is answered by an equal loss. Deposits form at almost the same rate that chemical dissolution removes their outer surfaces. Thus the mineral ridges retain their established shapes: worn, blunt, and apparently permanent.

Daylight reaches the floor in an even, moderated form. It is neither abyssal darkness nor the flickering brilliance of shallow water. The surface far above filters the sky into a diffuse green-blue illumination. Because the sea is calm, there are few dancing bands of light. Shadows are soft but legible beneath overhangs and along the mineral collars. Reds have disappeared; rust is brown-black, pale growths look grey, and bare stone carries a muted violet cast. The light changes so little that the basin seems held under a single prolonged hour.

Life is almost absent. No fish cross the open water. No crustaceans pick among the cracks. There are no worms, shells, corals, or sponges, and no tracks mark the silt. Whatever made the surrounding sea habitable does not extend into this isolated pocket. The water chemistry is not dramatically poisonous, but it is austere: poor in oxygen, stable in salinity, and lacking the seasonal delivery of nutrients that might sustain an animal population.

Vegetation survives only at the margins of what can be called vegetation. On the highest southern shelf, where a little more light reaches, sparse films of dark algae stain the rock. They occur in separate patches with wide bare spaces between them. A few tough, ribbonlike fronds emerge from cracks along the rim, each no longer than a hand. They do not sway. Their surfaces are dulled by mineral dust, and their growth is so slow that they seem less like plants than markings left in the stone. The basin floor itself is mostly bare.

The quiet is substantial. There is no surf at this depth, no calling life, no rain or wind in any form recognizable from land. A listener placed here would hear chiefly the dense internal silence of water: a low pressure against the ears, the faint transmission of far-off oceanic movements, and, near the vents, a continuous subaudible vibration conducted through the basalt. It has the steadiness of distant engines. The sound never starts or stops because it is not produced by an engine at all. It is the resonance of water moving through buried fractures under constant pressure.

The industrial character of the place lies wholly in its function. The bedrock operates as a heat exchanger. The fractures serve as intake and return lines. Mineral compounds are separated and laid down in ordered beds. The field processes an immense volume of seawater across geological time, efficiently and without supervision. It has no waste heaps because its waste becomes the landscape; no maintenance because erosion and deposition correct one another; no workers because nothing living is required.

At the basin’s western edge, a smooth channel exits beneath a low stone arch. It is barely high enough for a person to pass through, though no person has. The channel is a natural overflow cut when the seabed was younger and currents stronger. Now the water on either side stands at the same pressure, and nothing visibly flows through it. Beyond the arch is only the wider sea, dim and empty.

This gives the basin a boundary without making it enclosed. Its shelves, columns, vent field, and short mineral channels form a complete small territory. There is room between every feature. The nearest columns stand several body lengths apart; each algae patch is isolated; each vent collar has bare silt around it. Emptiness is not what remains after things are removed. It is part of the arrangement.

The place does not feel abandoned, because abandonment implies a former purpose and a change of state. Nothing here has ceased. Nothing awaits repair. The basalt continues to conduct heat. Water continues its buried circuit. Metals continue to settle in microscopic layers. Sparse algae persist at the lit rim, neither spreading nor dying back. Above, the weight of the sea remains constant.

It is an untouched factory whose machinery is geology, a cool submerged chamber that has been running since long before there were observers—and whose perfect operation is indistinguishable from stillness.
