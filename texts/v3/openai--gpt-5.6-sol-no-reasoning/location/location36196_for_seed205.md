The highlands begin above the last reliable river and continue farther than maps can hold.

They are not mountains in the usual sense. Their foundation may once have been stone, but almost none of it remains visible. Over ages, plants have climbed over the plateaus, buried the ridges, bridged ravines, and built new ground upon the old. What appears from a distance to be a green continental shelf is, at close range, an immense accumulation of roots, cork, fallen trunks, seed husks, and interlocked living stems. The landscape is vegetative all the way down—or at least farther down than anyone has cut.

It stands high in hot air. The sun is strong but not unnatural, a broad white-gold presence passing through haze. Heat gathers above the canopy and settles into the hollows. At midday, resin softens on exposed bark, leaf edges curl inward, and even pale wood becomes painful to touch. Yet the place is never entirely dry. Moisture arrives indirectly: clouds snag on the higher crowns; warm winds condense inside hollow trunks; brief rainstorms travel in narrow bands; and deep in the plant-mass, sealed chambers hold black water for decades.

Water here is scattered rather than absent. It lies in pitcher leaves broad enough to ferry a person, in cisterns formed where several trees have fused, and in chains of dim pools at the bottom of root canyons. There are rivers, but few run over mineral beds. They flow through conduits grown by the landscape itself—long wooden channels lined with wax, fiber, and moss. Some carry water only after rain. Others pulse on a daily rhythm as colossal root systems lift moisture from unseen depths and release the excess downslope.

The terrain has weathered into stable forms. Great vegetative mesas rise on buttressed sides, their crowns miles across. Old root arches cross valleys in parallel ranks. Dead trunks, bleached and petrified by sap minerals, lean from slopes like eroded columns. Trails have been worn not by feet alone but by entire ecologies: migration corridors polished through bark; tunnels gnawed into fibrous cliffs; clearings maintained by grazing, nesting, trampling, and fire.

Despite the almost total abundance of plant life, the land feels spacious.

The vegetation does not form an even jungle. It organizes itself into enormous, widely separated presences. A single umbrella organism may cover a valley, but beyond its shade lies a mile of low, tawny creepers. Towering blossom-stalks stand far apart, visible from one another across the plateau. Leaf plains stretch uninterrupted between isolated groves. The emptiness is not bare ground; it is living surface kept low by heat, wind, and animals. One walks through ankle-deep blades atop a substrate perhaps thousands of feet thick.

The greatest plants make architecture without imitating buildings. Their ribs form halls. Their discarded seed shells become chambers. Their roots descend in broad stairlike tiers, though the steps were not made for human legs. Some trees grow as open lattices to withstand plateau winds, and whole flocks live inside them. Others are low and armored, each leaf a slab of waxy green several paces long. At the highest elevations stand forests of hollow spires. Warm air rises through their trunks and whistles from apertures near the crown.

Nothing here is quiet.

The first impression is not birdsong but volume: a continuous biological roar rolling across the uplands. Wings beat in migrating multitudes. Beetles pulse their abdominal plates against resonant bark. Herds of six-legged browsers tear at fibrous ground with a sound like cloth ripping without end. Hollow trees amplify mating calls until a single animal can be heard across fifty miles. At dawn, millions of canopy creatures strike seed pods in coordinated bursts, and the plateaus seem to crack apart under the noise.

The landscape itself contributes. Sap moves through major trunks with deep knocks and groans. Root mats shift under heat, releasing long wooden detonations. Wind presses through serrated leaves, producing a metallic shriek. When rain arrives, it drums on the upper leaf plains so loudly that creatures beneath them navigate by vibration rather than hearing.

Silence is rare and alarming. It means that something large is hunting, or that the air pressure has changed.

Animals occupy nearly every scale. Tiny grazers move as colored dust over sunward leaves. Mites herd fungal films along the undersides. Nectar bats nest by the tens of millions inside blossom shafts. Long-legged runners cross the open creeper plains in loose formations, each individual separated from the next by hundreds of paces, yet all turning together. Their predators travel beneath the surface, forcing blunt snouts through the root fiber and erupting only when the vibration overhead is right.

Some fauna cannot be easily distinguished from vegetation. There are slow animals that root themselves during the hot season and grow green sails from their backs. There are plants that detach upon flowering and walk toward water on pale jointed roots. Vast grazing colonies resemble patches of reddish moss until they rise at dusk and flow across the landscape. Classification is a local habit, not a rule of the world.

Even the apparent soil is animate. It breathes faintly. In places it recoils from fire. Wounds in the ground seal with translucent callus, and abandoned pits gradually close. A camp left for a season may be found embedded waist-deep in new tissue when its makers return.

Rain comes violently but locally. A blue-black cloud may form over one vegetative mesa and empty itself there while the next plateau remains under full sun. Broad leaves funnel the water inward. Cascades pour from their tips, plunge through miles of air, and vanish into the thirsty lower growth. For a few hours, dry channels wake. Amphibious creatures emerge from sealed knots in the wood, mate in every temporary pool, and disappear before sunset. By the next day, only rings of foam and strings of eggs show that water was present.

The heat quickly reasserts itself. Mist lifts from shaded ravines. Condensation gathers in the cool interiors of dead giants and runs down their walls. Travelers learn to seek water not at the lowest point, but wherever night air passes through pale wood. Some trees are reliable wells. Others drink from anyone who cuts them, pulling blood and moisture backward through the blade.

People are present, though the vastness reduces them to scattered marks.

Settlements tend to occupy old husks suspended above migration routes. A village may consist of six woven platforms inside a seed case, reached by ladders that are withdrawn when the herds pass. Elsewhere, families live in individual towers made by binding living stems and training them upward over generations. There are no broad cities. Concentrations of people attract too much attention—from predators, parasites, pollinators, and the landscape’s own slow immune responses.

Their tools are shaped from what the place provides. Bone knives are edged with glassy leaf silica. Hooks are made from thorns, cordage from vascular fiber, bowls from lacquered gall shells. Copper is known in a few places where roots have drawn it from the buried rock and deposited it in blue-green nodules, but metal is rare, soft, and often ceremonial. The most valuable implements are cultivated rather than forged: cutting vines trained to hold a keen edge, living water bladders, larvae that exude adhesive, beetle jaws fitted into wooden handles.

Fire is possible but dangerous. Dry upper fibers burn fiercely, and the living ground remembers heat. Most cooking is done in sun boxes, fermentation pits, or the steaming vents of respiration hollows. Where flame is used, it is surrounded by green wood and watched by several people. Old burn scars remain visible for centuries as sunken gray basins, slowly closing from the edges.

Ruins occur, but they are partial and ambiguous. A line of stone blocks may protrude from a root wall, polished by centuries of animal passage. Carved posts emerge from living trunks where some vanished structure has been engulfed. On remote plateaus, immense woven barriers still redirect herds, though no one remembers who made them. Their fibers have rooted, leafed, and become part of the terrain. A structure here is rarely destroyed outright. It is adopted, digested, or persuaded into another purpose.

Distances are measured by sound. People speak of the three-roar crossing, the ridge beyond the dusk drummers, the cistern where the wing-thunder begins. Visual landmarks can change as crowns unfold or herds strip a plain overnight, but recurring calls are dependable. Certain animals sing from the same resonant trunks for generations, inheriting both territory and note.

The highlands have laws that are less supernatural than ecological, though the difference may not matter.

Open water must never be approached silently; still pools host organisms that strike at an unexplained shadow but retreat from rhythm. Nothing flowering should be touched at noon. A path that has become warm beneath the feet is not a path but a tongue or feeding surface, and must be left immediately. If every nearby creature begins calling at once, one should lie flat: the sound may be camouflage against something passing overhead. If all calling ceases, one should climb.

And no one cuts a bridge-root where it crosses open air. Roots that grow over chasms carry more than sap. They transmit alarm, weather, and the slow mechanical conversation of distant groves. Severing one can alter migrations hundreds of miles away. It can also provoke movement in structures assumed to be hills.

The oldest stories say that the highlands are a single plant. The trunks, plains, root canyons, and isolated blossom towers are merely organs divided by distance. This is difficult to prove and equally difficult to dismiss. Storms sometimes trigger flowering across an area larger than a kingdom. Injuries to one plateau produce defensive growth on another. Every few decades, a wave of contraction moves through the ground from east to west, raising ridges, draining pools, and overturning entire forests before passing beyond the horizon.

From above, if anything could remain aloft long enough to see it, the region might resemble a vast green hide stretched over buried bones. Its sparse towering forms would stand like hairs. Herds would appear as dark weather. Rain would bloom and vanish across it in bruised patches. The few human clearings would be too small to distinguish.

At ground level there is only heat, distance, leaf glare, and the endless layered tumult of life: feeding, warning, mating, splitting, rooting, molting, calling. The place is established but never still. Its shapes are ancient; its surfaces change by the hour. It has reached balance not by becoming peaceful, but by allowing every appetite, defense, and disaster enough room to become part of the terrain.
