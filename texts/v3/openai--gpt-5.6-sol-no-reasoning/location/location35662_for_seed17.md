The ocean hangs above the mountains.

It is not an inverted sea, exactly, nor a flood held in place by glass. The entire upper world is water: a cold, blue-black mass beginning several kilometers above the peaks and extending beyond measurement. Between it and the land lies a layer of air so thin and level that it resembles a flaw in reality—a planetary cavern roofed by seawater and floored by stone.

The underside of the ocean is perfectly flat.

Waves do not travel across it. Storms do not wrinkle it. At that impossible boundary, the water holds itself in a smooth, trembling plane, luminous as hammered metal. Fish pass overhead with their bellies exposed to the air below. Vast pale schools turn in coordinated sheets, silvering the false sky; heavier animals move behind them, obscured by distance and water. Sometimes the silhouette of something longer than a city crosses the noon, and for hours the mountains lie under its shadow.

The land is a high plateau of recently fractured basalt. It may cover a continent, though there are no shores by which to judge it. Black escarpments ascend in abrupt tiers, their edges sharp enough to retain a wet mineral gleam. Ranges rise from ranges without foothills or soil: blades, tables, broken columns, immense fans of shattered glass. The rocks have not had time to soften. There are no rounded pebbles, no sedimentary folds, no old gullies crowded with debris. Every crack appears newly struck.

And yet nothing changes.

No cliff collapses. No fragment completes its fall. Dust does not accumulate. The place has all the evidence of a violent birth and none of the processes that should follow it. Scree fields remain poised at angles too steep to endure. Needles of volcanic glass stand for millennia without weathering. Along one northern range, a rockfall hangs visibly in the air: thousands of angular blocks separated by finger-widths of darkness, arrested between mountain and valley. Moisture beads on them but never drips.

The air gap is cold enough to burn the lungs. Frost covers no surface, because the atmosphere is already saturated and cannot be made more so; water exists everywhere as suspension, pressure, and sheen. It forms a fine mist with no discernible motion. Breath enters it and vanishes. Exposed metal takes on a white rime, though the rime never thickens.

There are no plants. Nothing green stains the basalt. No lichen softens the stone, no algae fur the wet ledges, no roots test the fractures. The only growths are mineral: pale crystalline seams extruded from cracks, branching like coral but hard and sterile, and slow domes of iron salt around electrical earths. These, too, seem caught at fixed stages of formation.

From the plateau’s highest places, the gap between rock and ocean narrows. Some summits actually pierce the ceiling.

Their tips enter the overhead sea and disappear into blue. Around each penetration, the water forms a vertical collar with no spray or leakage. Marine animals gather there in enormous numbers. They circle the submerged pinnacles, feed on whatever arrives from the unseeable depths, and descend to the boundary to look into the air.

Their abundance makes the emptiness below more severe.

Nothing walks the land. Nearly all animal life remains overhead: shoals of transparent fish, broad filter-feeders, blind white hunters, drifting colonies joined by electrical threads. The water is full of them, but the world beneath contains only their shadows. On rare ledges close to the boundary, amphibious scavengers cling upside down to the ocean’s underside. They are dark, jointed things with pressure-swollen bodies and hooked limbs. They cannot fall through. They scrape at the surface tension, collecting condensation and fragments shed by passing creatures.

Now and then one dies. Its body remains pressed against the ceiling, motionless, and is slowly dismantled by its companions. Nothing descends.

Across this continent-sized hollow stand machines.

They are sparse enough that one can travel for days without seeing one, but each is industrial in scale: pumping stations anchored into basalt, ribbed towers rising toward the suspended sea, rail termini with no rails, cooling grids stretched over chasms. Their design is severe and repetitive. Black iron, nickel plate, ceramic insulators, thick windows, ladders designed for gloved hands. Most structures are solitary. There are no surrounding settlements, no access roads, no refuse, and no evidence that workers ever lived near them.

Every machine operates.

Pistons move in polished cylinders. Turbines rotate behind grilles. Banks of relays open and close. Blue arcs leap at regular intervals between copper horns. Pipes plunge into the rock or rise until they enter the sea overhead. Nothing rusts despite the wet. Nothing overheats despite the frost. No fuel arrives, no product departs. The factories have been reduced to process without supply or purpose.

Their operation produces the landscape’s defining fact: the sound.

It is continuous and nearly beyond endurance.

The suspended ocean transmits a planetary thunder downward. Whale-deep calls, pressure cracks, distant mechanical pounding, and the innumerable motions of marine bodies combine into a dense roar across the ceiling. The factories answer from below. Compressors beat at fixed tempos; flywheels howl; transformers hum through the stone. Mountains vibrate like tuning forks. In narrow valleys the noise becomes physical, shaking moisture into halos and making teeth ache in closed mouths.

There are no quiet intervals. The sound never swells or fades. It has no approaching source, no climax, no echoing aftermath. A listener who records an hour and compares it with an hour taken centuries later finds the waveforms identical—not merely similar, but identical down to each collision, cry, cavitation bubble, and electrical snap.

That is the law of the place: events occur, but change does not.

Every turbine turns, yet completes no new revolution. Fish swim but never arrive elsewhere. Predators pursue prey across immense distances while preserving the same separation. Mist moves through beams of light without altering its pattern. One may walk, perhaps, but behind the traveler footprints vanish not by erosion but by refusal; the ground resumes the state in which it has always existed.

Even injury belongs to equilibrium. A cut bleeds at a constant rate without emptying the body. Metal struck from a railing hangs for an instant as a bright shaving, then is again part of the rail—not repaired, because it was never permitted to become otherwise. Memory remains possible, though unreliable. It may be the only accumulation the world tolerates.

Light enters from the water above. It is surprisingly ordinary: a subdued marine daylight, broad and colorless, neither dim abyssal glow nor supernatural radiance. No sun can be seen, but there is morning, noon, and evening in their proper brightnesses. They do not progress. Instead, different regions appear fixed at different hours. To the east, the ceiling shines with permanent morning. Over the central ranges lies a shadowless noon. Far west, copper light fills the mist and catches on every sharp edge, making the young mountains look briefly warm.

Walking west does not carry one toward evening. The border between times recedes at the same pace.

The machinery may be maintaining this arrangement, though no single installation appears sufficient. Some towers seem to press upward, bearing the ocean’s weight through invisible fields. Others may harvest the deafening vibration and return it unchanged. Deep shafts reveal synchronized rods descending beyond the basalt into a cold brilliance. On instrument panels, gauges hold steady at impossible pressures. Dials are labeled in several industrial scripts, none accompanied by instructions. A recurring stamped emblem shows a horizontal line between two arcs: sea, gap, stone—or perhaps a component seen in cross-section.

There are partial works as well. A bridge extends from one cliff and stops in open air, its severed end clean and newly plated. Three elevator doors stand alone on a plain, opening at intervals onto dark shafts that cannot exist beneath them. A field of anchor bolts outlines a factory larger than a mountain, but nothing was ever mounted there. At one high pass, a small glass booth contains a chair, a headset, and a lever. The headset transmits the total noise of the world without attenuation. The lever cannot be moved.

The nearest thing to weather happens beneath the ocean’s migrating shadows. The air cools by a fraction. Condensation brightens on the rock. The sound takes on a lower pressure, though its volume does not alter. Looking up, one sees fauna layered through kilometers of water: countless ordinary bodies near the boundary, stranger geometries above them, and at the limit of sight immense stationary forms that might be animals, suspended industrial platforms, or holes in the water.

Nothing descends from those depths except light and noise.

Nothing rises from the land except towers.

Between them stretches the enormous habitable impossibility: a wet, freezing atmosphere under an unfallen sea, spanning mountains too new to be ancient and machines too active to be abandoned. It is empty enough to make every structure seem accidental. It is crowded enough overhead that the sky appears alive.

The mountains wait with unworn edges. The factories labor without consequence. The animals cross the same waters forever.

And everywhere, unceasing, the ocean roars against a surface it cannot break.
