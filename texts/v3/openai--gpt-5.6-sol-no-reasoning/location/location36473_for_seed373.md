The place is called the **Dry Choir**, though no one agrees whether this is a name or a warning.

It lies high on the leeward face of a mountain, enclosed within a shallow bowl of black stone scarcely wider than a village. From the rim, the whole landscape can be seen at once: a pale grove, several narrow towers, a central emptiness, and a belt of broken cliffs around them. It seems small enough to cross in an hour.

No one crosses it in an hour.

There is no water here—not hidden under stone, not drifting as vapor, not locked in the tissues of living things. The rock contains almost no hydrated mineral. The air has been stripped so dry that exposed skin tightens and paper crumbles along its folds. Frost cannot form, despite the cold. Clouds approaching the mountain divide before reaching the bowl, leaving a clean, ordinary blue overhead.

Yet the basin is lush.

Its growths are not plants in the familiar sense. They rise from the slopes in feathery columns of carbon lace, metallic salts, silica threads, and flexible sheets only a few molecules thick. Their crowns unfold into thousands of dark green vanes, but the green is not chlorophyll. It is the color of a mineral circuitry that feeds on daylight, static charge, and the temperature difference between sunlit surfaces and the cold ground beneath.

They resemble ferns from a distance. Nearby, they resemble instruments.

Each frond is jointed with glossy black hinges. Each stem carries translucent chambers full of pale dust. Roots do not penetrate the soil but spread across it in silver mats, fastening themselves to magnetic grains and exchanging electrical charge through touch. Nothing here drinks. Nothing leaks sap. If cut, a growth seals its wound with a flash and a thin curl of ceramic smoke.

The grove is luxuriant but almost without mass. Fronds wider than sails weigh less than handfuls of ash. Trunks are hollow frameworks. Vast crowns occupy the basin while leaving most of its volume empty. Seen edge-on, some trees nearly disappear. Seen against the sun, they reveal miles of hair-fine filaments braided through the air.

This is how the landscape can be both crowded with life and profoundly vacant. It is an ecosystem made mostly of intervals.

The ground has had time to settle into its own peculiar age. The cliffs are rounded and undercut, not by rain or rivers but by abrasive winds carrying electrically sorted dust. Every stone bears long grooves aligned with old storm directions. Ridges have been polished to a dull satin. Hollows are filled with salt—not white ocean salt, but violet, rust-red, and greenish crystalline powders left by the slow decomposition of the mountain’s exotic ores.

Tracks do not remain. The surface is always moving.

At the center of the bowl is an open circle where no grove takes hold. It is perhaps eighty paces across and paved in fitted gray plates, though no seams are visible until the light strikes obliquely. The plates flex like the skin of a drum. Sometimes the circle lies flat. Sometimes it rises in broad, muscular waves. At other times it opens—not downward into a chamber, but sideways into distances that do not fit within the basin.

Around this central pavement stand seven structures.

They are slender, weathered objects between tower and tuning fork, placed irregularly among the mineral trees. No two have the same height. Their lower portions are carved from local basalt and show the softened corners, pits, and dust-burnished faces of great age. Above, the material changes into something colorless and almost absent. It bends the view behind it by a fraction, so that passing animals appear briefly doubled.

The towers have no doors. They may not have interiors. Fine script covers their bases, but the marks continually rearrange, sorting themselves according to wind pressure, nearby movement, and unknown variables. A line that looks like a warning at dawn may resemble a map by noon and a list of vanished species by evening.

These are the only obvious constructions. Elsewhere there are smaller signs: a triangular frame half buried in crystalline loam; loops of impossible cable woven into the roots; a row of shallow cups that never collect anything; a chair-shaped depression facing an unremarkable wall of rock. The objects seem scattered and unrelated until a storm begins. Then they wake together.

Storm is nearly the permanent condition of the Dry Choir.

The air does not move as ordinary wind. It arrives in discrete layers. A waist-high current may race east while the air above it tears west, and a third stream, no thicker than a finger, may spiral vertically between them. Dust climbs in rigid sheets. Leaves turn their faces in coordinated ripples, harvesting charge. Invisible pressure fronts pass through the grove, folding acres of delicate structure flat and allowing them to spring upright a heartbeat later.

The whole basin changes shape under these forces.

Groves uproot themselves and walk on braided root fans. Cliffs shed thin plates that hover for minutes before selecting new positions. Salt dunes collapse into branching figures, then tighten into spires. The central pavement buckles, extruding temporary rooms, ramps, narrow bridges, and smooth gray forms like unfinished animals. By the next shift of pressure, these are drawn back into the ground.

Creation and destruction happen too quickly to distinguish. A stand of fern-towers may assemble itself from airborne dust in the morning, host a breeding colony by afternoon, and be disassembled into shining grit before night. Nothing treats this as catastrophe. Stability here lies not in keeping a shape but in returning to a repertoire of shapes.

And the noise is overwhelming.

Every living surface is an acoustic surface. The hollow trunks boom under pressure changes. Filaments whistle above hearing and below it. Root mats crackle with charge. The seven towers produce sustained notes that meet over the central circle, interfere, split, and return as pulses strong enough to blur vision. Stone plates clap against one another beneath the soil. Dust makes a dry hiss like endless applause.

During the larger atmospheric convulsions, the basin becomes deafening beyond endurance. Sound ceases to feel like something entering the ears. It travels through teeth and ribs. Loose stones hop in place. The green vanes flatten into resonant planes, and the entire grove speaks as one vast instrument: not music exactly, but an active weather of tone.

Conversation is impossible. Shouting is meaningless. Visitors communicate by touch, light, or gestures held close to the body.

The fauna has evolved in this uproar.

Small, six-limbed creatures race through the root mats in astonishing numbers. Their bodies are light cages of cartilage and conductive fiber, furred with sensory threads. They have no eardrums; the air would destroy them. Instead they read vibration through their feet, parsing the basin’s thunder with exquisite precision. A note from one tower means shelter. A harmonic between three trees means food. A sudden silence means immediate danger.

Flocks of transparent gliders inhabit the upper grove. They look like shards of smoky glass until they bank and reveal internal lattices glowing amber. They do not flap. They alter their electrical charge and are thrown from field to field, hundreds at a time, flashing through the turbulent air like sparks moving backward.

Heavy animals are rare, but not absent. The largest are the bellbacks: pale, many-legged grazers whose arched shells contain resonant cavities. They feed by crushing mineral fronds and sorting useful compounds in hot internal furnaces. Each carries a distinct chord. Herds identify one another through these chords, and when they migrate across the central pavement the ground answers them.

Predators hunt by cancellation. Long, ribbon-bodied things cling to cliff faces and emit precise opposing vibrations, making pockets of silence in the roar. Small animals blunder toward these quiet places as if toward shelter. The silence closes around them.

For this reason, quiet is feared.

When the Dry Choir suddenly stops, every visible creature freezes. The gliders drop onto branches. Bellbacks lower their shells. Root-runners press all six feet to the ground. Even the mineral foliage folds its vanes.

In that pause, the basin appears truly empty. The towers stand far apart. The grove becomes a collection of hair-thin outlines. The blue sky seems too large for the shallow stone bowl.

Then something crosses the central circle.

It is never clearly seen. Sometimes it leaves a procession of dents in the flexing plates. Sometimes the light bends along a moving vertical edge. Sometimes an entire temporary grove springs up in its wake, mature at once, laden with nests belonging to animals not yet born. The seven towers rotate without moving, turning their invisible aspects toward it.

The world here follows several rules, though none have been fully translated.

Mass is expensive; surface is cheap.  
Water is poisonous, or perhaps merely incompatible.  
Shape is temporary; pattern persists.  
Noise carries memory.  
Silence carries hunger.

The scattered technology does not command the ecosystem. It may have founded it, or become entangled with it so long ago that the distinction no longer matters. The towers appear to stabilize the bowl by making it unstable in controlled ways. They draw energy from temperature, sunlight, mechanical stress, and perhaps from the discrepancy between possible forms. Their surfaces are continually repaired by airborne machines too small to see. The same machines inhabit the mineral trees, the gliders’ wings, the bellbacks’ shells, and the lungs of anything that stays long enough.

At sunset, ordinary light warms the black rim and turns the pale grove gold. Shadows lengthen naturally. Nothing glows without cause. For a few minutes the landscape could almost be mistaken for a high, wind-scoured garden.

Then the cooling stone releases its stored strain.

The cliffs begin to sing. The towers answer. Millions of vanes unfold toward the falling temperature. Animals pour from fissures in glittering streams. The central pavement rises in a slow wave, carrying old structures briefly to the surface: fragments of stairs, a narrow arch, a platform set with empty harnesses, all intact and all dustless. They remain for less than a minute before sinking again.

Night does not calm the bowl. It changes the key.

Under the stars, the Dry Choir rearranges itself endlessly within its modest boundary: a small place containing great distances, a lush world without moisture, an ancient equilibrium composed entirely of upheaval.
