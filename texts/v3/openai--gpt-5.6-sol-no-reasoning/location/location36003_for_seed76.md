At the top of the world lies a plateau so broad that its edges belong to different climates.

It is not a flat place. Across its continental breadth, the ground rises and subsides in long mineral swells: pale ridges, shallow basins, escarpments worn into thousands of parallel ribs. Mountains stand upon the plateau like islands on a higher sea, yet even their summits look old. Nothing here has a fresh edge. Stone has been pared by ages of wind into low, settled forms—terraces, blunt pinnacles, fields of fitted slabs—until the whole land seems less ruined than completed.

There is almost no water.

No rivers articulate the valleys. No snow remains on the heights, though the air is cold enough that breath would linger. The basins are floored with salt, gypsum, and fine gray dust. Where rivers might run elsewhere, there are wind-courses: broad, branching channels through which dense nocturnal air pours downhill. These currents do the work of water. Over ages they have hollowed ravines, sorted pebbles into bars, and carved undercuts beneath the harder strata. At dawn, cold air lies in the lowest country like an invisible lake. Pollen, shed scales, and mineral powder collect along its shores.

The vegetation is lush, but not continuous.

Enormous spaces remain open beneath the white sky. Then, suddenly, a slope is green-black with life.

Most plants grow as low, dense mantles pressed against the stone. Their tissues contain oils and waxes rather than watery sap: dark resins that stay fluid in the cold and do not readily evaporate. Cushion groves spread over sheltered shelves in rounded masses, their millions of tiny leaves packed as tightly as fur. From a distance they resemble moss-covered hills; close at hand, each hill is a colony centuries old, advancing across the rock by fractions of a finger’s width each year.

In clefts stand taller forms. These have pale, porous trunks woven from hollow fibers. They do not draw moisture from the ground. Instead, during the night, their crowns open into vast nets and filter the downward-flowing air. They harvest dust, carbon compounds, salts, spores, and traces of atmospheric frost too slight ever to bead into liquid. At sunrise the nets contract, sealing their gathered material inside. The tree then spends the bright day digesting the sky.

Their groves are widely scattered—green archipelagos separated by leagues of bare stone. Some occupy crater rims, some cling to the shaded sides of ridges, and some form narrow belts where two kinds of bedrock meet. The richest growth occurs not in soil but on ancient mineral crusts whose slow chemical decay feeds whole communities. Violet fans overlap rust-colored mats. Silver filaments hang under ledges. Amber bladders rise on stalks and turn gradually to follow the sun.

Light is the great abundance of this country.

The sky is thin, hard, and intensely blue-violet. The sun appears small but mercilessly clear. Shadows have sharp borders, yet inside them the cold deepens at once. Salt flats throw light upward, illuminating the undersides of leaves and cliffs. At noon the plateau seems suspended between two suns: one above and one shattered across the ground. Plants protect themselves with reflective hairs, crystal skins, or leaves that stand edge-on until late afternoon. When the sun lowers, whole hillsides rotate their foliage together, and a dark green sheen passes over the land like the turning of an immense animal.

Weather is born from temperature rather than moisture. Afternoon heating lifts pillars of dust several kilometers high. They travel in stately lines across the basins, thin enough to remain luminous, carrying spores from one distant grove to another. Sometimes electrical storms assemble without clouds. The air prickles; mineral needles rise upright from the soil; the taller plants close their crowns. Then blue discharges move silently along the ridges, branching over the stone in brief rootlike patterns. Only afterward comes a small, remote cracking sound.

The plateau is active everywhere, though almost nothing appears hurried.

Stone expands under day and contracts under night. Fine flakes detach with faint ticks. Salt crystals grow in fissures and patiently pry boulders apart. Wind combs dust into low traveling dunes, erases them, and makes them again elsewhere. Colonies open and close. Spores ascend. Resin circulates through buried stems. Whole gardens migrate over centuries as shade lines shift with the slow weathering of cliffs.

Animal life is rare enough to seem accidental.

Small six-legged grazers live inside the cushion groves, seldom exposing themselves to open light. Their shells are translucent and filled with dark antifreeze oils. They rasp wax from the plants and sleep through the coldest seasons in communal knots beneath stone plates. Larger creatures cross the open country only at dusk: long, spare browsers with sail-like sensory organs folded along their backs. They may travel for months between groves, following airborne chemical traces too faint for any lesser nose. Their feet are broad and soft. Even at close range, they make almost no sound.

Predators are rarer still. A pale gliding thing sometimes detaches from an escarpment and crosses a valley without moving its wings. It rides the descending air-rivers, listening for vibrations in the ground. A hunt may consist of hours of silent circling and one quick disturbance among the stones. Then the emptiness closes again.

There are no nests built from gathered materials, no dams, no paths cut deliberately into the slopes. Even the animals leave little behind. Trails survive only as slightly polished bands between old groves. Nothing raises walls. Nothing kindles fire. The living world inhabits existing folds, cracks, shadows, and wind patterns, as though construction were unnecessary—or forbidden by some principle older than instinct.

From any summit the scale is difficult to accept. Ridge succeeds ridge until distance turns them blue, then white, then invisible. Dust fronts make weather over regions larger than countries. A single plant belt may follow a geological seam for a thousand kilometers, appearing and disappearing with the stone. Shadows of the highest peaks cross entire ecosystems over the course of an afternoon.

Yet the plateau remains quiet.

There is the papery contraction of a crown at dawn, the granular whisper of salt moving over salt, the occasional dry clack of a shell beneath foliage. Most sounds are swallowed by the thin air and the enormous intervals between living things. Silence here is not an absence. It is the medium in which events remain distinct.

At night the land loses its reflected glare and becomes immeasurably deep. The plants give back a portion of the day’s energy as dim green and amber luminescence. Groves emerge across the darkness one by one, separated by black plains: sparse constellations rooted in stone. Above them the actual stars burn without shimmer.

Between earth and sky, it becomes hard to tell which wilderness is larger.
