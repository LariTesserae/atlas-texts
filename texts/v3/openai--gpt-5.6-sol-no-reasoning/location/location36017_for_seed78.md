The country begins above the clouds.

It is not a mountain range in the ordinary sense, though from far below it appears as one: a dark, serrated continent suspended at impossible height, its underside lost in storm and its upper reaches black against the stars. What seems to be stone is almost entirely vegetation. Trunks the width of valleys have grown sideways, crossed one another, rooted into their neighbors, and hardened beneath successive skins of bark. Their interlocked crowns make plateaus hundreds of kilometers across. Younger branches rise from these plateaus as sharp ridges and pinnacles, still green beneath their frost, scarcely weathered because they grew only decades ago.

There is little solid ground. The land is a forest that has become its own geology.

Its oldest wood lies deep inside, compressed into dark strata and warm with slow fermentation. Above it, pale structural roots form cliffs, arches, overhangs, and long hollow chambers. Living bark covers everything else: black and corrugated on exposed heights, red-brown in sheltered folds, silver where lichens and frost have merged into a reflective hide. Even the loose material underfoot is botanical—needles, husks, pollen flour, shattered twigs, woody scales, mats of dormant moss. True mineral fragments are rare and prized. A fist-sized stone carried up by an ancient root may become the center of a settlement’s tools and stories.

The scale produces an odd emptiness. Vegetation is nearly total, yet the world feels vacant.

One may stand upon a branch plain fifty kilometers wide and see only low black foliage pressed flat by the wind. Individual upright trees are separated by immense distances. They are not really separate trees, either, but breathing towers raised by the greater organism beneath them. Between these towers lie hollow expanses where nothing interrupts the horizon except frost ridges and the occasional white plume of transpiration. Groves occur as isolated cities of stems, divided by days of travel across open bark.

The sky is rarely bright. A dense ceiling of cloud lies both above and below the inhabited surface. The upper cloud is thin enough to reveal stars, but daylight arrives only as a dim blue stain, as though filtered through deep ice. For much of the year there is no direct sun at all. Plants live by several means: vast black leaves absorb the faint ambient light; translucent tissues conduct radiance from the exposed upper spires; symbiotic growths feed on electrical storms; deep roots draw heat and chemistry from the decaying interior.

At night—which differs from day mostly by being colder—the youngest shoots give off a weak green luminescence. It does not illuminate the landscape so much as diagram it. Veins glow under leaf membranes. Wounds in the bark shine around their edges. Fungal threads mark cracks with blue lines, and distant reproductive towers pulse slowly, one after another, across hundreds of kilometers. The pulses are not perfectly synchronized. From a ridge they resemble silent signals passing through a buried civilization.

Water is scarce in volume but persistent in touch. There are no broad seas on the heights. Moisture arrives as condensation, rime, brief hard rain, and cloud driven horizontally through the branches. Leaves are shaped to catch it. Some are broad bowls lined with wax; others are forests of fine needles that comb droplets from fog. Their collected water runs through grooves in the bark and gathers in deep knotholes, root cisterns, and hanging ponds.

These ponds are small compared with the country but enormous to the creatures that depend on them. They may occupy the dark cavity where three limbs meet, their surfaces sealed beneath clear ice for most of the year. Under that ice swim colorless larvae, flat black fish, and colonies of threadlike animals that knot themselves together when the water begins to freeze. In warmer intervals the ponds overflow. Thin streams run for hours or days along preexisting channels, then vanish into cracks, leaving new ice ribs behind.

Rain is less a season than an event. When warm cloud rises from below, the temperature changes abruptly. Frost softens. Bark swells. Millions of sealed pods open almost at once. For a few violent hours the plateau is full of running water and botanical motion: leaves turn, cups unfold, root mouths gape, and dormant animals emerge from crevices. The rain may stop before it has properly begun. The remaining droplets freeze where they hang, armoring the plants in glass.

The land is young and visibly growing. Its sharpness comes not from broken rock but from fresh formation. Branches erupt upward in splitting ridges. Root bridges thicken from one season to the next. Entire valleys close when their opposing walls graft together. Elsewhere, expanding trunks tear old plateaus apart, opening chasms whose sides show pale, wet wood. New cliffs have a clean fibrous grain, golden or cream colored, and bleed aromatic sap that steams in the cold.

Nothing here stays level for long. A traveler can return after a few years and find a familiar route tilted into a slope or lifted above the cloud line. Trails are remembered by sequence—scar, hollow, fork, pond—rather than by direction. Distances also change as the living substrate expands and contracts. During deep cold, branch plains draw taut and cracks widen. During wet thaws, they bow under absorbed water, bringing formerly separated ridges within reach.

The loudness of the place is continuous.

Wind strikes the elevated country without obstruction. It combs the low foliage into one immense hiss, then rises to a roar around the reproductive spires. Hollow trunks turn gusts into organ notes too low to be heard cleanly; they are felt in the ribs and in the soles of the feet. Ice plates chatter. Branches under tension give sudden reports like splitting stone. Somewhere, always, a root is tearing free or a young trunk is forcing its way through an older shell.

Animals add sharper sounds. Flocks of membrane-winged grazers pass through the darkness crying to maintain formation. Bark-borers drum from inside the ground. Long-legged hunters knock their hollow limbs together before a chase, producing a dry clatter that travels far across the open plains. At thaw ponds, mating colonies scream so densely that the ice vibrates.

Fauna is abundant but seldom obvious. The emptiness conceals it. Most creatures are small, migratory, dormant, or hidden within the vegetation. The exposed surface cannot support bodies in every place at once, so life concentrates around brief opportunities: a newly opened flower field, a bleeding bark fissure, a thawing cistern, a cloudfall rich in airborne spores.

Dark-furred browsers travel in lines between distant groves. Their broad feet grip bark ridges, and frost collects on their backs until a resting herd resembles a patch of low shrubs. Pale gliders nest in hanging leaves along cliff edges. They do not truly fly; they surrender to the enormous wind systems and may circle the country for months before finding a place to land. Many carry seeds hooked into their skin. Some never touch the surface after maturity, drinking moisture from cloud and feeding on aerial swarms.

The largest animals live below the plateau, moving among the pendant roots. They are seen only when lightning illuminates the cloud beneath. Then vast, slow shapes appear between the hanging forests, browsing upside down or suspended from several limbs at once. Their calls enter through the roots as tremors. Surface dwellers can hear them approaching hours before they pass underneath.

Predation is swift because shelter is far apart. Hunters wait near the few reliable water pockets or mimic frost-coated shoots along migration routes. Some are warm-blooded; others store heat generated by fermenting plant matter in chambers along their flanks. Several species hunt cooperatively by manipulating the substrate—biting through tension vines, shaking seed masts, or causing a branch to flex beneath fleeing prey. The vegetation also participates. Wounded mats close around spilled blood. Certain flowers snap shut on small animals, and some broad-leaved basins drown whatever enters them, digesting the body slowly beneath the ice.

The boundary between animal and plant is not always useful here. Many mobile creatures root themselves during the dark season and grow leaves. Some trees shed reproductive bodies that walk for years before choosing a place to become fixed. A “herd” seen crossing the horizon may be migration, seed dispersal, or the forest relocating a damaged organ.

Human habitation, where it exists, is scattered almost beyond contact.

Small shelters are tucked into bark folds or suspended inside hollow stems. They are woven rather than built: hoops of green wood, layered leaves, felted fibers, animal membrane, ice used as a temporary sealant. There is little metal. Knives are made from split shell, sharpened bone, glassy frozen sap, or the rare flakes of stone. Fire is dangerous and difficult in the wind, so communities cultivate heat-moss in insulated pits. It glows dull orange as it digests oily wood, warm enough to cook slowly or keep a sleeping chamber from freezing.

A settlement may consist of three chambers and a ladder, occupied only during the pond’s thaw. Others are solitary watch-huts on high stems, reached by braided vine lines. Old structures are hard to distinguish from growth. Abandoned baskets root themselves. Fences become hedges. A bridge, if kept alive and carefully pruned, may outlast those who trained it; neglected, it thickens into an impassable wall or wanders upward toward the light.

Marks are cut shallowly because deep wounds attract parasites. Routes are indicated by tied fibers, bent twigs, resin scents, and tuned wooden clappers. The clappers are necessary. Vision is poor, storms erase tracks, and the land itself moves. Each refuge has a sound: three hollow knocks, a rising rattle, a low reed note. In heavy weather, travelers navigate from one faint human rhythm to the next through the greater roaring.

There are older artifacts, though none are grand. A line of pegs crosses an empty plain and continues into open air where a branch once stood. Stone blades, worn almost round from generations of resharpening, are inherited by whole communities. Deep inside certain trunks are spiral cuts too large and regular to have been made by any living people. Sap has filled them, preserving the grooves as amber walls. No one agrees whether they were chambers, traps, script, or wounds inflicted before the country rose above the clouds.

The inhabitants obey practical rules.

Never camp below a glowing wound.

Never drink from a pond whose ice has no bubbles.

When the low trunks begin to sing in unison, lie flat and bind yourself to the bark; a pressure front is coming.

Do not cross fresh golden wood after dark. It is warm, fragrant, and soft enough to attract everything for kilometers.

If a grove pulses out of sequence with the others, go around it.

Above all, never assume that ground is passive.

At rare intervals, the upper cloud tears open. Moonlight or thin sunlight pours across the heights, intolerably bright. The black leaves turn toward it in a wave large enough to make weather. Frost flashes silver. Millions of winged animals rise at once. Flowers unfold over whole provinces, releasing warm vapor and pollen into the cold, and the wind becomes yellow with it.

For a few minutes the emptiness reveals its true density: every crack inhabited, every hollow webbed with roots, every distant plain crawling or opening or feeding. The country’s supposed silence beneath the roar proves to have been waiting motion.

Then the clouds close. Darkness returns. The leaves settle with a continent-wide crash, ponds skin over, hunters move toward the thawing places, and the immense living plateau continues to raise itself—branch by branch, storm by storm—into the cold.
