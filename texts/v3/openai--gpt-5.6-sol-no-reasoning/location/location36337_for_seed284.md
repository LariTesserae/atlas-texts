The city occupies a crack near the summit of an otherwise uninhabitable mountain.

From outside, nothing announces it. The peak rises in black, recently fractured blades, too young for scree slopes or rounded shoulders. Its stone is dark basalt veined with pale salts, and its upper ridges lean together like broken teeth. Far below lies a dry continental basin. No river has ever reached it. No snow remains on the summit. The air is cool because of the altitude, not because of moisture: thin, metallic, and incapable of holding warmth after the sun passes.

Halfway along the eastern crest is a fissure scarcely wide enough for a person to enter sideways. Warm machine breath comes and goes through it. Beyond, the rock opens into a steep interior shaft, and the hidden city descends.

It is less a cavern than a vertical alley built inside a fault.

Bridges, balconies, lift cages, pipes, dwellings, workshops, freight rails, and ventilation trunks fill nearly every available span. Buildings do not stand on ground. They cling to opposite walls, stack beneath one another, or hang from iron tendons driven into the mountain. Their roofs are the floors of other districts. A room may have a door in its ceiling, reached by a ladder from the room below; an entire factory may be suspended beneath a residential block, shivering gently as its presses cycle.

The settlement is only a few hundred paces across at its widest, but its depth is difficult to judge. Dim platforms recur below platforms until perspective fails. Red signal lamps drift in the darkness like embers dropped down a well. At the lowest visible point, the fissure pinches shut around machinery whose purpose no one above claims to know.

The stone is fresh and sharp. Every exposed surface shows recent violence: glassy fracture planes, glittering seams, corners unsoftened by weather. There is dust, but little erosion. Dust here is manufactured by drilling, grinding, collapse, and the constant minute adjustment of the mountain. It gathers in gray ledges along cables and window frames, then vanishes abruptly when a pressure door opens and the ventilation reverses.

There is no water cycle. Nothing drips. No stains descend from cracks, no moss rims the lamps, no condensation clouds the windows. People drink fluids brought up in sealed ceramic ampoules, reclaimed almost perfectly after use. A spilled cup is treated as an industrial accident. The absorbent powder is swept up, fired, and distilled; even the residue is accounted for.

The city’s true common substance is air.

Compressed air drives lifts, tools, door seals, message tubes, and thousands of small domestic mechanisms. Great lungs buried in the walls inhale through filtered summit vents and exhale into storage cisterns lined with copper. Pressure is wealth. Each district has its own gauges mounted in public view. When a needle falls, conversation stops. Citizens listen for leakage with the attention other cultures might give to rain.

The city is cool throughout. Near the outer fissure, metal burns the skin with cold. Deeper in, engines maintain a tolerable chill, but warmth never accumulates comfortably. Walls steal it. Railings steal it. Beds are narrow insulated boxes with sliding lids. Workshops glow around furnaces yet remain cold at the edges, as if heat here were a temporary guest surrounded by enemies.

There are no plants. Food vats use mineral-fed microbial films sealed behind opaque panels, and most residents have never seen anything resembling a leaf. Their decorative motifs are fractures, gears, concentric pressure rings, and the branching paths of electrical discharge. Public squares contain gardens of tuned metal rods and crystalline slag. In the moving air, these rods almost sing, though the city is careful about unnecessary noise.

For all its machinery, it is mostly quiet.

The engines run behind thick stone and layered felt. Gears are greased with dry graphite compounds. Citizens wear soft-soled boots because echoes can travel the full height of the fault. Voices remain low. Bells are forbidden except during structural emergencies.

The prevailing sounds are intimate: the ticking of cooling housings, a cable tightening, claws on sheet metal, the soft pneumatic cough of a door. Beneath these is a pressure too low to hear cleanly, perceived in the sternum—a vast, irregular pulse from the compressors.

Then, without warning, the whole place moves.

Turbulence here belongs not to weather but to geology and industry. The mountain is still settling around the city. The fault opens and closes by fractions, sometimes by more. A corridor that crossed the fissure yesterday may fall short today. Rails buckle sideways. Stair towers lean. Entire neighborhoods migrate on articulated frames, retracting from one wall and fastening to another while people continue eating, trading, and sleeping inside them.

No map remains accurate for long. Addresses include not only depth and wall but current tension: East Face, Level Forty-One, Third Anchorage, Compression State. Street signs rotate mechanically after each shift. Couriers memorize routes as sequences of sounds and temperatures—the humming pipe, the cold ladder, the place where the rock smells of struck flint.

Construction never ends. Crews swarm fresh fractures before the dust has settled, driving bolts into newly exposed stone. Demolition teams cut loose damaged structures, which descend on controlled cables to be dismantled in lower foundries. Sometimes a whole cluster of houses is hauled upward through the central void, windows lit and inhabitants visible within, while bridges fold away around it.

This relentless movement gives the tiny city the activity of a metropolis. Freight cages pass shoulder to shoulder. Workshops operate in stacked shifts. At narrow junctions, workers, animals, and machines wait pressed close together while load-bearing shutters decide who may pass. Markets unfold for twenty minutes on platforms temporarily judged stable, then collapse into wheeled cabinets and roll elsewhere.

Animals are everywhere.

Most are small, pale, and dry-bodied, bred or adapted for life without open water. Six-legged slate mice nest in cable sleeves and eat mineral-fed yeast wafers, insulation, and one another. Their whiskers sense air-pressure changes well before human instruments do; when they stream upward in silence, everyone follows.

Long-bodied hookcats patrol vertical surfaces using curved claws and adhesive pads. They sleep wrapped around warm conduits and wake during tremors, opening their translucent inner eyelids. Workshops keep them for vermin control, though their real value is structural: a hookcat refusing to cross a beam is considered sufficient reason to close it.

Higher in the shaft live flocks of leathery gliders. They do not truly fly in the thin air. They launch from vents and ride pulses from the compressor system, folding themselves through gaps no hand could enter. Their bodies are dark and angular, easily mistaken for scraps of cloth. They feed on airborne microbial dust and lay clusters of hard eggs in hot motor housings. At shift change they may fill the upper chasm by the hundreds, all turning at once without audible wingbeats.

The deepest levels have heavier fauna: blind burden beasts with low bodies and broad, gripping feet, used where sparks make motors dangerous. They communicate by tapping their jawbones against the floor. Handlers feel the messages through their boots. During a major fault shift, whole herds begin tapping together, and the resulting tremor runs through the city before the mountain’s own.

Life survives here by remaining contained. Skin, shell, pipe, room, district, mountain: everything important has a seal around it. Open space is considered a type of damage.

The outer world is visible only from several narrow observation slits. Through them lies an impossible drop, the basin floor dim with salt and old volcanic ash. On clear days, distant ranges seem to float in the haze. Nothing moves outside except dust. The summit casts a long, black shadow across the plain, and the city hidden inside that shadow goes on manufacturing cable, pressure vessels, ceramic seals, bearings, lamps, food substrate, and replacements for every part of itself.

Its principal product, however, is anchorage.

Deep drilling machines labor continuously at the bottom of the fault, placing immense threaded rods across the crack. Some say these hold the city in the mountain. Others say they hold the mountain together around the city. The distinction matters politically, but perhaps not mechanically.

At irregular intervals, a rod fails.

There is first a sharp, modest note, like a glass tapped with a fingernail. Every creature falls still. Warning lamps deepen from red to violet. Doors seal; bridges retract; market stalls clamp shut. Then the broken rod recoils through its housings and the mountain adjusts with terrible slowness. Walls approach. Walls withdraw. Towers bend on their joints. Dust rises in dense silver sheets. The compressors labor to keep breathable air from being crushed into one district or torn from another.

Yet even in these episodes, the city does not become loud. Its disasters occur under pressure, muffled by rock. People work in gestures and lamp signals. Animals pour through emergency grilles. Mechanisms strain with subdued groans. Somewhere, far below, the drilling continues.

Afterward there are new chambers where none existed and old streets pinched to hairline seams. The city expands into the fresh spaces immediately. Surveyors arrive first, then bolt crews, cable layers, scavengers, food vendors, landlords, and nesting gliders. Within hours the raw black rock is threaded with lamps. Within days it has a name.

The settlement’s oldest rule is written above every pressure gate:

**Nothing here is permanent except the need to fasten it.**
