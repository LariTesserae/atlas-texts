The plain lies low beneath a warm, ordinary sun.

At first it resembles a young desert overtaken by impossible growth. Black mineral plates cover the ground, fitted edge to edge as if poured yesterday and allowed to harden. Their fractures are sharp, unsoftened by weather. Pale crystals stand along the seams in fresh splinters, and shallow ridges rise only a few meters before breaking off abruptly. There are no dunes, no rounded stones, no channels left by vanished rain. Nothing here has been worn down. The land appears newly exposed, though it gives no sign that it was ever buried.

Across this fresh surface grows a dense, dry forest.

Its trunks are bundles of copper-colored fibers, flexible in appearance but rigid to the touch. Crowns spread into broad fans of translucent leaves, overlapping until the plain is roofed in green, amber, and smoky violet. The leaves are not wet. They contain no sap and possess no pores. Their tissues are lattices of carbon, silicon, and more elusive arrangements that instruments would record differently depending on which side of the leaf they approached. Beneath them crowd low mats of silver filaments, jointed stalks, mineral blossoms, and thick cushions like moss carved from graphite.

None draws nourishment from water. There is almost none to draw. Hydrogen and oxygen occur separately in the soil, carefully prevented from meeting by the chemistry of the place. The forest feeds on warmth, light, electrical imbalance, and the minute pressure exerted by empty space. At noon the fan-leaves turn their edges toward the sun, not to avoid it, but to harvest the geometry of their own shadows.

Calling this vegetation is convenient rather than exact. It germinates, branches, repairs itself, and releases drifting reproductive grains. It also performs work no natural metabolism should be able to perform. Each stand maintains a small region in which physical laws are held to slightly different tolerances. A grove may reduce inertia without changing weight. A patch of silver ground-cover may keep cause and effect separated by precisely the width of a leaf. The tallest fiber-trees stabilize distance: walk toward one and it remains twenty paces away until it chooses to admit that you have arrived.

There are no buildings. Nothing has been cut, stacked, paved, fenced, or deliberately shaped. Yet the whole forest gives the impression of technology so advanced that construction has ceased to be distinguishable from ecology. Its devices are species. Its infrastructure flowers. Its calculations take place through branching patterns and the silent alignment of leaves.

Perhaps it was engineered. Perhaps engineering is merely what life becomes here.

The forest extends for many kilometers, open in scale but crowded in every local space. Through gaps in the canopy one can see other belts of growth: dark spindle groves, level fields of glassy fronds, pale towers that may be organisms or stationary distortions in the air. Because the land is low and nearly flat, distance should be easy to judge. It is not. Features retain ordinary clarity far beyond the horizon, while nearby trunks sometimes appear softened by immense remoteness. There is always more country visible than the curvature of the plain should permit.

Light remains reassuringly normal. The sun is warm gold; shadows are cool and directional; colors do not glow without cause. This makes the violations of reality harder to dismiss. Under a leaf, its shadow may have veins not present in the leaf itself. Two neighboring stalks can cast a single shadow between them. Looking through a translucent crown sometimes reveals the same plain at another hour, though the hour outside never changes.

It is always late afternoon.

The sun does not visibly stop, yet it never advances. Shadows preserve their lengths. Temperatures remain warm and even, without breeze or accumulating heat. Fallen grains hang at fixed heights among the branches, each apparently caught in still air, although touching one allows it to complete several seconds of descent before it stops again. Nothing decays. Nothing grows in any measurable interval. Seedlings exist beside mature forms, but no observer has seen one become the other.

The place is not dead; it is held.

Change survives only as arrangement. Leaves may be found turned overnight, but there is no night. A path may close behind a traveler, though no stem is observed moving. Wounds in the mineral tissues are already repaired at the instant they are made, leaving both the cut and the unbroken surface briefly present. The forest appears to occupy a stable solution containing every necessary process without allowing those processes to pass.

Sound is sparse. There is no water to murmur, no wind to move the crowns, no soft soil underfoot. Steps make small ceramic clicks on the black plates. Occasionally the entire region emits a low, pure note too deep to locate. It does not swell or fade; one simply realizes it is present, and later realizes it is not. Near certain groves, a second footstep can be heard just before the first.

Animals are rare. Thin, many-legged creatures shelter beneath mineral fans, their bodies folded like strips of dark foil. They do not appear to eat. At long intervals, a large pale shape crosses above the canopy without wings or sound, blotting out no sunlight. Its passage can be tracked only because thousands of leaves simultaneously display their lower surfaces. There are also cavities among the roots that seem recently occupied, although they contain no tracks, castings, shed tissue, or scent.

One creature is encountered more often than the others: a thumb-sized prism that stands upright on three hairlike limbs. When approached, it presents a mirrored face. The reflection shows the surrounding forest accurately but omits the observer. If disturbed, the prism does not flee. The landscape instead acquires a second location for it, farther away, and the nearer instance becomes only a trick of perspective.

There is no obvious danger in the forest. The air is breathable, the warmth mild, the ground mostly level. No predator calls. No storms gather. But the place enforces quiet rules.

Do not walk repeatedly between the same two trees; on the fourth passage, the interval may recognize itself as a closed loop.

Do not break matching leaves from neighboring plants. Symmetry here is functional, and something else may have to become unequal to compensate.

Do not follow your shadow if it diverges. It is not lost.

Most of all, do not bring water. A droplet cannot remain merely a droplet in this chemistry. The forest reacts to liquid water as to a contradiction. Leaves turn their undersides outward. Crystals unmake their edges. Distances tighten. The droplet is separated into elements before it can fall, but for one instant the whole plain seems to remember rain—a thing that may never have happened—and every dry crown bends beneath the weight of it.

Then the late afternoon restores itself. The black plates are sharp and new. The forest is dense, lush, and utterly still. Across the low horizon, countless leaves continue their invisible work, maintaining a world that looks natural only because it has been perfected far beyond nature.
