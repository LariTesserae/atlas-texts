Beneath the public baths, behind a square stone hatch that is opened only for cleaning, lies the sun chamber.

It is not large. A person can cross it in twenty careful steps, though few do, because the chamber is filled almost to its ceiling with warm water. The floor is perhaps twice the area of an ordinary room. Four walls, a shallow vaulted roof, three pillars, and a sunken central basin make up the whole landscape. Nothing here is natural. There is no soil beneath the paving, no exposed bedrock in the walls, no sand except what has washed in grain by grain. Every surface was set by hand.

The chamber is new enough that the chisel marks remain sharp.

Its walls are faced with pale limestone tiles, fitted closely but not perfectly. Lime mortar gleams between them in narrow white seams. The floor descends by broad steps toward the middle, where water is drawn through a bronze-grated drain into lower cisterns. At the corners, clay pipes enter through round collars of packed fiber and pitch. Some pour constantly; others start and stop according to the use of the baths above. Their workmanship is simple and legible. There are no hidden mechanisms, only gravity, hand-cut channels, wooden gates, stone weights, and the changing pressure of water stored higher in the building.

Light enters through six vertical shafts. They rise to the courtyards overhead, where polished metal plates turn the daylight downward. By the time it reaches the chamber, the light has been divided into bright columns. Each column continues underwater, visible from side to side because of suspended lime dust and minute air bubbles. The whole room shines. The pale masonry reflects light repeatedly until even the spaces beneath the steps are blue-white and clear.

At noon, the illumination is almost excessive. The water casts moving nets across the vault. Every ripple becomes a band of brightness, sliding over the masonry and breaking against the pillars. The smallest flaws—an uneven tile edge, a thumbprint hardened in mortar, a shallow scratch left by a mason’s gauge—stand out with great clarity. There is nowhere for darkness to gather except inside the pipes.

The water is warm, close to skin temperature near the upper steps and hotter around the eastern inlet. It carries the mineral scent of wet stone, lime, fired clay, and faintly metallic bronze. Fresh water enters in pulses. Used bathing water arrives from above through settling boxes, already strained through woven mats and beds of charcoal. It is not dirty, though it is cloudy at certain hours with soap ash and fine dust. Layers of different warmth move through one another, bending the light.

Despite its brightness, the chamber is concealed within the bathhouse like a cavity inside a wall. People pass within a few arm lengths of it without knowing. Feet cross the ceiling. Benches scrape above. Water is poured from basins, doors close, attendants call to one another. Those sounds descend the shafts altered and enlarged, stripped of clear words. A dropped bucket becomes a hollow bronze concussion. A crowd entering the main pool arrives as rolling thunder.

Inside the chamber, the water itself is louder.

Each inlet has its own voice. One knocks irregularly in its clay throat. Another issues a steady, heavy roar. A third admits water mixed with air, producing an endless gravelly rush. The central drain draws with a deep note that can be felt through the pillars. When one of the wooden gates is opened above, pressure changes travel through the room: pipes shudder, bubbles burst from seams, and the water level lifts against the vault with a broad slap.

Then, gradually, it settles.

There are long intervals when the room is nearly empty of events. Light wheels slowly across the floor. A loose strand of packing fiber stirs beside a pipe collar. A few flakes of fresh mortar drift down and come to rest on the lowest step. Space dominates. The pillars stand far enough apart that each seems isolated, and the large central basin contains almost nothing but light.

No fish were introduced. No frogs can reach it. No insects survive the heated filtration beds, and the shafts are screened with woven copper wire. The chamber has no animal inhabitants, not even the pale, accidental creatures common to wells. Its emptiness is maintained rather than mysterious.

Life appears only as a thin green stain in two places where the shafts receive full sun. Soft films cling to the upper tiles, scarcely thicker than breath on glass. From one mortar joint grows a small tuft of water moss, dark at the base and vivid at its tips. It bends when the eastern pipe opens and slowly rises again when the current weakens. Every few weeks a cleaner scrapes it away with a wooden blade. It always returns, though never spreads far. There is too little settled silt, too much disturbance, and too little time.

The chamber’s terrain changes according to the bathhouse day.

Before dawn, the cistern is lowered. The tops of the steps emerge, glossy and steaming. Narrow waterfalls descend from one level to the next, exposing aprons of pale mineral deposit. Cleaners enter through the hatch with ropes around their waists. They carry no complex tools: wooden scrapers, clay lamps in sealed horn cases, baskets for grit, mallets, wedges, coils of cloth. They work by touch and shouted instruction, bracing themselves against the current. Their feet have not yet worn the stone smooth. The step corners remain crisp enough to bruise.

After sunrise, gates are raised by hand. Water floods back rapidly. It covers the lowest paving, then the basin rim, then the steps one by one. Air trapped between tiles streams upward in bright beads. The cleaners climb out. The hatch closes. Within an hour, their footprints are gone.

At midday, all the shafts are brilliant and the chamber is full. Water presses against the roof in places, leaving only small trembling pockets of air under the vault. Flow passes diagonally from the hotter eastern pipes toward the central drain. The sparse moss lies flat. Fine mineral dust circles each pillar but has not accumulated long enough to soften its base.

Toward evening, use above becomes irregular. A gate closes; another opens. Cooler water descends from an outdoor tank. Warm strata fold over it. The room’s currents reverse, hesitate, and resume along new paths. Light turns gold, then narrows into separate blades. For a brief period each shaft illuminates only one piece of construction: a bronze grate, a ladder cut into a pillar, a patch of charcoal-dark caulking, a mason’s mark shaped like an open hand.

After sunset, the chamber is not fully dark. Oil lamps in the baths send a weak amber glow down two shafts, and their flames are broken by the water into wavering points. The roar diminishes as the building empties. The central drain continues. Somewhere overhead, a final attendant lowers a wooden gate, and the last inflow contracts from a torrent to a rope, then to separate blows of water.

Nothing here is ancient enough to have become picturesque. The limestone has not rounded. The bronze is only beginning to dull. Mineral crusts form in hard white rims but are knocked away before they grow thick. Hairline cracks appear where hot and cool sections meet, and masons seal them with fresh mortar. The place remains visibly under construction even when no one is present. It is less a ruin than a machine made from rooms.

Its rules are plain. Water seeks the lowest channel. Heat rises. Pressure finds imperfect joints. Light follows the shafts. Stone yields slowly; wood swells; bronze stains what lies below it. Every part can be understood by a worker with a mallet and enough patience.

Yet when the chamber is full and noon strikes directly through the courtyards, its ordinary materials produce something almost ceremonial. Six white pillars of light descend through warm water into an unoccupied room. Currents cross among hand-laid stones. Bubbles flash and vanish. The walls answer the pipes with continuous, resonant thunder.

No one built it to be beautiful. Nearly no one sees it.

Still, hidden beneath the crowded baths, the little flooded chamber shines all day.
