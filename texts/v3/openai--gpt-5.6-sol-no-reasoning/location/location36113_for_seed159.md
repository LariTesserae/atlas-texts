Below the crust lies a country of chambers that have not finished deciding their shape.

It is warm here—not furnace-hot, but bodily warm, the heat of breath trapped under blankets. Moisture gathers on every overhead surface. It pearls along the black stone, fattens, and drops in a ceaseless scattered rain. Yet the country is not flooded. Most of its water exists briefly: a slick on rock, a trembling bead, a shallow pool that vanishes down a new crack before it can become still. Narrow rivers appear after distant storms seep through the world above. They rush through for an hour or a week, rearrange the floor, and disappear.

The caverns are immense but crowded. Columns, shelves, ridges, cones, mineral blisters, and curtains of pale stone stand almost shoulder to shoulder, leaving corridors that suddenly broaden into halls. Beyond each formation there is another, and beyond that another, their wet faces receding through warm haze. Some chambers could hold towns. Others are long and low, forcing everything living through them into compressed migrations.

The geology is very young. These are not ancient caves polished by patient water. They are cavities opening now.

Walls split with reports like timber breaking. Soft limestone buckles outward in pale folds. Floors rise by inches as mineral pressure swells beneath them, then collapse into steaming pits. Crystal grows fast enough to hear: a fine snapping, ticking sound, like ice forming in reverse. In places the rock is still chalky and weak, bearing fingerprints, claw marks, and the impressions of fallen bodies. Elsewhere hot seepage glazes it overnight into hard translucent skins. There are few rounded surfaces. Everything is raw-edged, cracked, newly exposed, or still being deposited.

Despite the depth, there is light.

It does not come from flame or from any visible sun. Bands of luminous mineral run through the ceilings, broad enough to illuminate whole valleys beneath the ground. Their color is mostly a clear cream-white, close enough to daylight that distances and colors remain legible. The bands brighten when the stone is under strain. Before a chamber fractures, its ceiling may shine brilliantly for several minutes; after the break, the glow dims to gray. Thus light here is also warning, though not always a reliable one.

At intervals, fresh clefts open to deeper brilliance. For a time they cast long, sharp shadows upward. Then mineral foam crusts over them, or rubble closes the seam.

Nothing green grows in this country. There are no roots, mosses, reeds, or pale cave flowers. No vegetable smell softens the air. The surfaces belong entirely to mineral deposits and animals.

And the animals are everywhere.

They cover the shelves in layered multitudes, fill the channels, hang from the ceilings, wedge themselves into warm fissures, and move in such numbers that the ground often seems to flow. Most are small: translucent shelled things with many jointed feet; soft, blind burrowers that eat fresh mineral crust; coin-shaped animals that attach themselves to warm walls and vibrate; swarms of thread-bodied fliers whose wings are no thicker than condensation. Some graze directly on chemical films invisible to the eye. Others scrape salts, drink mineral seepage, prey upon grazers, or consume the dead before a carcass cools.

Larger creatures push through this abundance like ships through floating debris. Broad-backed crawlers, six or seven paces long, carry colonies of smaller animals in the grooves of their armor. Their undersides are soft and luminous. Herds of narrow, stilt-legged beasts cross the unstable flats, testing each patch of crust before placing their weight. They have no eyes, but raise fans of delicate sensory bone into the moving air. When a rockfall begins, every fan folds at once.

There are swimmers even when there is no water. Long silver animals lie dormant in damp gravel, sealed in mucous skins. When an intermittent river arrives, they wake within minutes and fill the current by the thousand, breeding and feeding in furious compression. As the river drains away they bury themselves again, while predators follow the shrinking pools and thrust hooked mouths into the mud.

In some basins, warm water remains. These pools are densely occupied from surface to bottom: floating sacks that pulse like lungs, armored scavengers, ribboning hunters, colonies that resemble translucent masonry. Nothing in a pool is empty water. Even the bubbles are inhabited.

The country is loud with them. Shells rasp against stone. Wings hiss under the ceilings. Herds drum across thin floors. Burrowers grind within the walls, producing a continuous low vibration felt through the teeth. Calls echo for miles: clicks, throbs, metallic cries, and deep communal moans that travel along the rock rather than through the air. At times all this noise is overwhelmed by the sudden roar of a new river or the prolonged collapse of a chamber.

Silence is alarming. It usually means that the animals have sensed movement in the stone.

Scattered among them are signs of tool use, though no single species can be confidently named as their maker. Low shelters of stacked slabs occupy ledges above flood channels. Their walls are fitted without mortar and roofed with plates pried from naturally layered rock. Stone hooks hang from cords made of dried membrane. Shallow bowls have been hammered into soft mineral nodules and set beneath reliable drips. There are bridges no more than split bones laid across cracks, fenced sleeping hollows, chipped blades, and cairns enclosing warm vents.

Some structures are in active use; others are already half-swallowed by new deposits. A shelter may be built in the morning and sealed inside stone a season later. Permanent architecture is impossible. The inhabitants favor things that can be abandoned, carried, or rebuilt quickly. They make no grand monuments, unless the lines of handprints pressed into soft walls are monuments. Some of these walls later harden and preserve the prints. Others bend, split, and descend into the earth.

The few metals found here occur as soft native lumps torn from fresh seams. They are beaten cold into pins, scrapers, and ringing plates. A suspended copper sheet may serve as both alarm and map: changes in its tone reveal strain in the surrounding cavern. Fire is rare and difficult. The air is damp, fuel is nearly nonexistent, and flame agitates certain swarming species. Heat comes instead from vents, warmed stone, clustered bodies, and chemical vessels packed with reactive salts.

No trail stays fixed. Routes are understood as sequences of events rather than landmarks: follow the warm draft until the ceiling begins to sweat; wait for the white fliers to rise; cross after the second tremor but before the water turns red. A traveler who memorizes shapes will soon be lost. A traveler who understands pressure, animal movement, moisture, and sound may survive.

The deepest rule of this place is that geology and ecology proceed at nearly the same speed.

A new ridge rises, and within hours grazers cover it. A pool forms, and dormant swimmers awaken beneath it. A passage closes, dividing a population; a ceiling opens, joining two chambers whose animals have never encountered one another. Predation, migration, construction, flooding, and stone formation happen together, none providing a stable background for the others. The landscape is not a stage on which life acts. It is another participant—slower than wings, faster than generations, warm, luminous, and perpetually rearranging the terms.

Seen from a high ledge, one of the greater halls resembles an underground plain during a crowded migration. Pale towers extend to the luminous haze. Water flashes in disconnected channels. Millions of bodies turn around sudden fissures in coordinated waves. Primitive shelters cling to the few dependable shelves. Steam moves among them all. Far away, the floor heaves, and a line of white stone breaks upward like the spine of something enormous.

The animals scatter before it. Then, almost immediately, they return.
