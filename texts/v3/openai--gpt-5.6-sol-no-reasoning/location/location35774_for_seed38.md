The high country has no exposed ground.

What might once have been a plateau—an uplifted continent, perhaps, or the worn roots of mountains—is covered by vegetation so old and continuous that soil, forest, and terrain can no longer be separated. The land is a single living thickness. Its upper surface rises in low swells and immense, shallow basins, all softened by ages of growth. There are no summits in the ordinary sense. There are only higher reaches of the organism, broad green elevations lifted into cloud.

Underfoot, if there is any footing, lies a compacted weave of roots, woody ribs, dormant bulbs, mineralized husks, and pale fungal laminations. The weave may be hundreds of meters deep. Rainwater settles into it instead of running away. Every step presses a dark shine from the moss. In hollows, clear pools open between leaves as large as rafts; on slopes, water seeps from vertical gardens in fine threads, giving the impression that the land is quietly perspiring.

Stone appears rarely. When it does, it has been reduced to smooth, low-backed shapes, ancient beyond sharpness. The oldest outcrops have become green islands inside the greater green, their surfaces hidden beneath liverworts, hair roots, and thin red plants that lie flat against them like spilled dye. Water beads continually along their undersides. In the dimness, the drops gather, swell, and fall one at a time.

The air is warm despite the elevation. It has the mild, enclosing warmth of breath held beneath cloth. Clouds do not pass above the plateau so much as inhabit it. They lodge among the higher fronds, drift through root arches, and condense on every filament. At midday the light brightens from charcoal to pearl, but direct sun is uncommon. Leaves have adapted to this. Many are nearly black, drinking what little light enters; others are translucent and layered, so that one faint beam may feed twelve tiers of growth before disappearing.

There are forests here, though “forest” suggests separate trees. These growths are more communal. Trunks meet and fuse. Branches descend into the ground and emerge kilometers away. Great braided columns support canopies that are themselves rooted landscapes, laden with ponds, thickets, and smaller groves. Some valleys are roofed entirely by interlocking crowns. Beneath them, the world becomes a warm green dusk in which distances are difficult to judge.

Elsewhere, the plateau opens into marshlike expanses of low vegetation. Their surface looks firm until touched. Then whole acres tremble. Floating mats—woven from sedge, root, and the buoyant chambers of broad-leaved plants—cover deep reservoirs of brown water. The mats are thick enough to bear forests, yet they drift imperceptibly with seasonal changes in the water below. A grove seen on one side of a basin may, after a century of rains, arrive at the other.

The rivers are concealed. They travel inside the land’s living body, passing through vascular tunnels and drowned root galleries. Only occasionally do they show themselves: a black channel under an arch of stems, a slow overflow across a sponge plain, a column of water descending soundlessly through an opening in the vegetation. The water is tea-dark from ages of leaf matter but clear when lifted in the hand. Pale seeds turn within it like suspended stars.

Erosion has almost completed its work. The plateau’s great differences have been worn down, absorbed, and grown over. Cliffs remain only as long, rounded escarpments draped in hanging gardens. Old ravines are filled to their rims by root and humus. Fallen blocks are digested where they lie, their minerals drawn upward into leaves. The landscape does not resist erosion anymore; it incorporates it. Rain carries stone into plants, plants fall into water, water enters roots, and the whole continent slowly exchanges its substances without losing its form.

Because of this, the place seems settled into a final shape. Storms may cross it, rivers may change course beneath it, and entire canopies may collapse, but from a great height these are merely shivers. The broader contours have endured for spans longer than the lives of species. Growth no longer advances into emptiness. There is no emptiness left. It grows into itself, replacing old tissues, closing wounds, thickening what is already thick.

Animal life is everywhere but seldom conspicuous. Most creatures are small enough to occupy the spaces between leaves without disturbing them. Warm rain awakens clouds of soft-winged insects, though they make little noise. Glass-bodied grazers move along the undersides of foliage. Narrow, six-legged browsers peel films of algae from wet bark. In the canopy ponds live dark amphibious things with frilled throats, their eggs hung in transparent chains among submerged twigs.

Larger animals move slowly and travel little. Broad-backed herbivores inhabit the floating marshes, carrying moss and seedlings in grooves along their hides. They feed by lowering fringed mouths into the living mat, trimming it rather than tearing it. Long-limbed canopy dwellers cross the fused branches without leaps, placing each hand carefully, pausing for minutes between movements. Predators are rare and patient. Some resemble bundles of dead stems until a warm body brushes past. Others wait beneath the pond mats, sensing pressure through the roots.

The quiet is not an absence of life but the result of its closeness. Sound has nowhere to travel. Foliage, mist, water, and porous wood absorb it almost at once. There is the soft tick of condensation, the occasional release of a heavy drop, the rubbing whisper of one immense leaf against another. From far below may come a hollow knock as a saturated trunk gives way inside the forest. Afterward, silence closes over it.

Calls are infrequent. A canopy animal may send a single low note through the wood rather than through the air; kilometers away, another feels the vibration in its feet. Marsh creatures communicate by dim pulses of color beneath the water. Certain flowers open with a faint sigh, all across a basin at once, but centuries can pass between such mass bloomings.

Scattered through this world are structures that do not quite belong to it, although almost nothing remains visible enough to explain them. In one of the higher cloud forests, a row of squared uprights crosses a saddle between two swells of land. They are not stone. They appear to be the mineralized cores of once-living columns, planted at regular intervals and long since fused with the surrounding trees. Each supports an independent garden. Water flows through channels worn—or grown—down their centers.

Elsewhere, a shallow circular depression lies beneath the marsh, detectable only because the reeds above it grow in concentric bands. Along an escarpment, vines climb a series of alcoves too evenly spaced to be natural. Inside them are shelves, ledges, and dry recesses, but no implements, no carvings, no sign of fire. Whatever shaped these places may never have possessed tools distinct from living tissue. Perhaps the structures were persuaded from roots and trunks, cultivated into shelter over generations, then abandoned to rejoin the larger body of the plateau.

Even these marks change slowly. An opening closes over decades. A corridor becomes a streambed. Walls sprout vascular knots and begin exchanging water with the forest. Here, abandonment is not ruin but assimilation.

The stranger qualities of the land reveal themselves only with time. Paths do not remain where they were. A route may tighten behind a traveler as leaves incline toward the warmth left by passage. Pools collect beneath sleeping animals overnight. Fruiting bodies appear near wounds before blood has dried, offering bitter antiseptic pulp. None of this is quick enough to look intentional, yet it is too consistent to dismiss.

The plateau possesses no mind in any simple sense. Awareness, if it exists, is dispersed across distances too large and processes too slow for animals to recognize. A change in rainfall at the eastern rim may alter flowering in the west two hundred years later. The death of an ancient grove may be answered by the germination of seeds stored since before the current rivers formed. Signals travel through fused roots, through fungal threads, through water under pressure in the buried dark.

Time here is not measured by days. The dim light makes morning and evening nearly alike. The meaningful events are saturation and release, bloom and dormancy, the opening and sealing of canopy gaps. A lifetime may pass under one season of cloud.

At the far edges of the plateau, the living surface folds downward in green escarpments. Water spills from it through innumerable veils, not as thunderous falls but as mist, seepage, and shining threads. The vegetation descends until distance swallows it. No bare lowland can be seen. It may be that the growth continues below, covering the whole world, and that this vast warm height is only one raised organ of a planetary garden.

From within it, there is no horizon. There are only layers: wet leaf beyond wet leaf, dark trunks inside cloud, pools holding reflections of higher pools, and the enormous patient body of the land supporting everything while almost imperceptibly remaking itself.
