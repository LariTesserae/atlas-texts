The high country begins above the weather.

It is an open plateau crowded almost beyond passage: mile after mile of pale stone blades, rounded boulders, interlocking shelves, and close-packed mineral towers. Nothing has been built here, yet the formations fit together with the oppressive completeness of masonry. There are no clear fields and few continuous paths. Every surface is occupied by another surface—slabs leaning against columns, nodules filling clefts, terraces nested inside larger terraces. Seen from a distance, the plateau resembles a city whose streets were omitted, though no wall is truly straight and no opening was made for any body.

There is no vegetation. Not a stain of lichen marks the rock, nor moss in the wet joints, nor grass where grit has gathered. The absence is conspicuous because water is everywhere.

It lies in shallow basins no wider than a hand and in broad, mirrorlike pans between the stone masses. It beads on exposed faces beneath a white sky. It threads through cracks so narrow that the water appears motionless, a dark line drawn into the mineral. Along the plateau’s rim, hundreds of seeps emerge from porous rock and descend as silver veils. Some vanish into packed rubble; others gather in channels and pour from the escarpment into the glaring distance below. The air itself seems saturated. Cool condensation forms continuously upon the shaded sides of pillars, and fine droplets hang in recesses like clear dust.

Yet the place does not feel stormy or recently drenched. Its wetness is not an event. There are no fresh flood marks, no rising pools, no drying margins. Water occupies the same hollows with the permanence of quartz veins. Each fall holds its established volume. Each seep follows a channel polished to a dull blue-gray. The plateau and its waters have reached an old arrangement from which neither appears capable of departing.

The sound is enormous.

Water alone cannot account for it. The thin falls descending from the rim roar as if they carried whole rivers, while trickles inside the stone give out low, continuous thunder. Every packed formation catches and multiplies the noise. Narrow clefts whistle. Dome-shaped boulders hum. Deep cavities beneath the shelves resonate at pitches more felt in the ribs than heard. There is no useful silence between these tones. Together they produce a seamless pressure of sound, steady and deafening, without swell, pause, or discernible source.

Loose stones do not tremble. The pool surfaces remain perfectly smooth.

This is the plateau’s first strangeness: the noise has no corresponding violence. Spray does not billow from the cataracts. Pebbles do not roll. No gust moves through the congested passages. The air is cool and nearly still, and the deafening resonance persists as a fixed property of the place, like brightness or altitude. One might imagine that the rocks remember an ancient collapse and continue to sound it, but even that suggests a past and present the plateau does not readily admit.

Light comes from the whole sky. There is no visible sun, only a white-blue brilliance so strong that shadows are reduced to thin cobalt seams beneath overhangs. Wet stone flashes everywhere. Pale limestone, translucent silica, black volcanic glass, and metallic bands are pressed together in an intricate geology. The light enters clear crystals and is returned in hard interior sparks. It glances from the water pans until distance becomes painful to look at. Even the mist below the escarpment shines, turning the plateau into a bright island above an indistinct lower world.

The rocks bear the shapes of long weathering: fluted faces, softened rims, bowls bored into ledges, arches made broad and stable by erosion. But no erosion can be witnessed. Droplets cling without falling. Channels roar without visibly deepening. Delicate stone bridges carry the same burdens they seem to have carried forever. There is grit in the basins, graded from coarse fragments to powder, though no grain shifts and no fresh fragment can be found.

The terrain is difficult not because it is steep, but because it is full. Progress requires squeezing sideways between wet pillars, stepping from rim to rim around crowded pools, and climbing low barriers only to discover more barriers fitted closely behind them. Here and there, the congestion opens into chambers the size of valleys. Their floors are paved naturally with polygonal plates, each separated by a water-filled joint. Stone fins rise densely across them, all of nearly equal height, producing a mineral thicket through which sight travels farther than bodies could.

Beyond these chambers stand rounded massifs, pale under the omnipresent light. They are not much higher than the plateau, but their surfaces are packed with knobs and spires until they resemble crystals enlarged to mountain scale. Water descends every face in fixed silver filaments. Between the massifs lie cool, shallow lakes without beaches. Rock plunges directly into clear water, and more rock can be seen below: columns, shelves, and crowded formations continuing downward until brilliance obscures depth.

Life is almost absent.

In the deepest water, one may occasionally glimpse a colorless shape lying flush against the stone. It has no obvious eyes, mouth, limbs, or symmetry. Hours may pass without proof that it is an animal rather than a patch of translucent mineral. Very rarely, the shape is later found on another submerged face. No motion is ever observed between the two positions.

High above, amid the white blaze, solitary dark specks sometimes cross from one horizon to another. They give no call and never alter their pace. It is impossible to tell whether they are winged creatures, airborne fragments, or flaws in vision caused by the light. Nothing lands. There are no nests, bones, shells, tracks, or droppings. The plateau offers water in abundance but no visible food, no shelter softened by organic matter, and no cycle of growth or decay.

Even the mineral surfaces are sterile. Nothing films the pools. Nothing greens the seeps. Sediment has no odor beyond cold iron and wetted stone. The air tastes faintly of salt and electricity, though there are no storms.

At the plateau’s outer edge, the packed country ends abruptly. Countless waterfalls extend from the lip as straight, shining cords into a luminous gulf. Across that gulf, other high plateaus can be seen: equally crowded, equally wet, their surfaces blazing beneath the undivided sky. Their cataracts make no separate contribution to the noise. All sound arrives as one unchanging total.

Clouds fill the depths below but do not drift. Their upper surfaces are flat and radiant. Nowhere does vapor rise from them, and nowhere does a gap open to reveal the lowlands. The whole region seems suspended at one altitude between the fixed cloud sea and the fixed bright sky.

There is no windward side, no warming face, no season evident in ice or melt. The temperature remains cool without becoming cold. Breath clouds faintly and then hangs close, dissolving so slowly that each exhalation seems reluctant to leave. Water never freezes. Exposed stone never warms. A person could walk from brilliance into shadow and feel no change except the loss of glare.

Nor is there a true sense of morning or evening. The light does not move. Shadows retain their narrow shapes. The dark specks overhead, when they appear, cross a sky without direction. Distance can be measured by effort but not by time; the waterfalls continue, the resonant stone continues, and the crowded plateau repeats its variations without progressing toward any climax or center.

Still, it is not featureless. Each district has its own mineral emphasis. There are fields of red stone crowded like blunt teeth; glassy basins where water seems black beneath white light; gray labyrinths of perforated rock; long ridges armored in overlapping plates; terraces of blue mineral descending toward lakes clear as empty air. The diversity is immense, but all of it participates in the same equilibrium. No district invades another. No boundary advances. Materials meet along old, weather-softened contacts and remain there.

The loudness eventually ceases to suggest danger. It becomes something closer to pressure or gravity: a condition through which all perception must pass. One feels it in teeth, water, and bone. A shouted voice disappears instantly, though lips and throat still register the effort. A struck stone adds no audible note. Even inside the tightest recess, with hands pressed over the ears, the plateau continues to sound from within the skull.

Perhaps the water is flowing. Perhaps the bright sky is passing overhead, and the rocks are slowly wearing down, and the rare pale creatures are feeding in intervals too fine or too vast to notice. But the landscape refuses all evidence of sequence. It appears not dead, exactly, but complete—filled to capacity with stone, water, light, and sound, leaving almost no vacant space in which change could begin.
