At the summit of a drowned mountain stands a small city called Stillwater, though there is no shore within a thousand kilometers and no surface anyone has reached.

The city occupies a level shelf of black stone near the mountain’s peak. Its elevation matters even beneath the ocean: below the shelf, the slopes descend through pressure zones so severe that ordinary matter changes allegiance, while above it rises a final narrow horn of rock. The horn vanishes into darkness before reaching the sea’s upper layers. Thus Stillwater feels high without possessing a sky. Its streets look outward over precipices filled with water.

The city is not large. One could cross it in an hour if its geometry permitted straightforward travel. It consists of forty-seven structures widely spaced around a central shaft: low vaults, narrow towers, ring-shaped chambers, bridges that terminate in open water, and faceted objects whose purpose is not expressed by their form. There are broad intervals between them. Empty plazas occupy more ground than buildings, and most plazas have no monuments, only dark paving marked by thin seams of bluish metal.

Everything appears recently made.

The mountain shelf is sharp-edged, barely touched by sediment. Broken faces of basalt retain the sheen of fresh fracture. Trenches cut through the stone have crisp corners. Not even the slow abrasion of the deep sea has softened the stairs descending from plaza to plaza. The city might have been finished yesterday, yet no construction continues. There are no discarded tools, no rubble heaps, no dust suspended in the water. Whatever built Stillwater removed every sign of labor and then arrested the place at the instant of completion.

Nothing grows here.

There is no weed in a seam, no drifting algal veil, no pale forest of tube worms around the heatless vents. No mossy films soften the walls. The surfaces remain mineral, ceramic, glass, or a substance resembling compressed shadow. Where ornament occurs, it consists of equations engraved too finely to see without magnification, or grids of holes leading into depths that cannot be illuminated. The barrenness does not feel poisoned. It feels prior to fertility, as though life has not yet been introduced into the design.

The water is cool and perfectly clear. It fills chambers, streets, ducts, and interior courts without distinction. There are no air pockets. Doors do not seal; they regulate passage by altering the properties of the water inside their frames. Passing through one may make the surrounding liquid briefly rigid enough to walk upon, or remove the traveler’s buoyancy, or rotate the direction in which weight acts. Some doorways open not into rooms but into differing versions of the same room, each containing the same bare benches arranged around a black central basin.

No current moves through Stillwater.

This is the first impossibility one notices and the one that becomes most oppressive. Water usually reveals every disturbance. Here it hangs without drift. Fine grains released from a cracked ledge remain suspended indefinitely, each separated from the next by a finger’s width. A trail of bubbles outside the northern tower has not risen. They form a silver chain stretching diagonally across a courtyard, every sphere motionless, their skins reflecting a city that looks subtly different from the one around them.

Sound travels reluctantly. A struck wall gives one low note and then nothing, with no echo from neighboring structures. Movement produces only close, private noises: the scrape of a hand against stone, the muted thud of contact, the internal pulse of one’s own body. Far below the shelf, perhaps once every several days—or perhaps eternally at one fixed point in local time—there is a deep metallic groan. It does not grow louder or softer. It simply exists for eight seconds, always identical.

There are lights, but they offer little illumination. Hair-thin blue lines run beneath sections of pavement. Red points smolder in the upper faces of the towers. In the central shaft, something colorless shines downward, making nearby darkness darker instead of dispelling it. Most of the city is known by touch, sonar, and the occasional glint from an edge. Distance quickly becomes uncertain. A tower twenty meters away may seem remote enough to belong to another ridge; a bridge crossing the abyss may appear within arm’s reach until approached.

The central shaft is the city’s governing feature. It is circular, thirty meters across, and descends through the summit shelf into the mountain. Its rim bears no guardrail. Water above it is indistinguishable from water elsewhere, but anything passing over the opening slows. A falling stone takes hours to cross the first meter and never completes the second. Light bends down into the shaft and remains there in stacked, luminous layers, recording every beam ever directed inside.

Around the rim stand nine empty frames. They are made from a translucent material colder than the water and engraved with maps of places that cannot coexist: coastlines folded through themselves, streets extending across planetary interiors, constellations plotted beneath mountain ranges. When touched, a frame gives the sensation of immense rainfall striking a roof, though no sound is produced.

The city’s technology is difficult to separate from its laws of nature. There are no visible engines. Power does not seem to flow from one place to another; desired conditions are simply maintained. Pressure does not crush the hollow buildings. Metal does not corrode. Heat remains uniformly cool, without warmer machinery or colder stone. Broken surfaces do not degrade. The city’s infrastructure may be correcting reality continuously, or it may have replaced local reality with a more obedient version.

Time is similarly regulated. Nothing cycles. No lamp flickers. No mechanism advances. There are clocks carved into several walls, but their hands are structural parts of the stone and indicate no particular hour. Objects moved from their assigned locations are later found restored—not because they travel back, but because at some unclear moment the moved object becomes the copy and the original location becomes the truth.

This rule applies unevenly to visitors.

A person may leave a tool in a plaza and find it again in their hand, still also visible where it was placed. If they retrieve the second tool, one version will gradually lose physical consequence: it can be seen but not touched, then remembered but not seen, and finally will remain only as a conviction that something has gone wrong. The city does not tolerate accumulated change. It permits events but refuses outcomes.

Fauna are rare enough to seem ceremonial. Small black creatures occasionally appear along the underside of bridges. They have six jointed limbs and bodies like chips of volcanic glass. They do not swim. They step from surface to surface even when those surfaces are separated by open water, placing their feet upon invisible planes. Their movement is the only ordinary sequence in Stillwater: lift, reach, set down, shift weight. Yet if watched for long enough, each creature is found to repeat exactly the same route, including pauses and turns, without aging or feeding.

Deeper in the city lives—or is displayed—a single larger animal.

It occupies a spherical chamber at the mountain’s highest point. The chamber has transparent walls and no entrance. Inside floats a pale, long-bodied organism with neither eyes nor mouth. Rings of cartilage show beneath its skin. It curls around an object like a dark pearl, holding it gently between paired fins. The animal never moves, but observers disagree about its posture after looking away. Instruments detect no metabolism. The pearl emits a minute gravitational field directed not toward itself, but toward a point several meters outside the chamber.

There are no inhabitants in any familiar sense. Still, the city is organized for use. Corridors accommodate bodies. Recesses resemble sleeping niches. Rows of apertures in the western hall accept hands, tentacles, tools, or other manipulators. Public squares are arranged around places where something could address a gathering. Along one street, hundreds of smooth masks are set into the façades at varying heights, each turned toward the central shaft.

None depict faces. They depict absences where faces might be.

The outer edge of Stillwater ends abruptly. Pavement reaches the precipice and stops in cuts so fresh that the exposed internal layers of the road can be counted: black ceramic, silver mesh, a band of transparent material containing stationary sparks, then the native basalt. Beyond lies the open deep, sparse and unlit. The mountain falls away steeper than architecture. Here and there on the descending slope, isolated pylons stand among the rock, separated by great reaches of emptiness. Their red lights form a broken avenue into the abyss.

Travelers who follow the pylons downward eventually return from above.

This is not a loop in space. Depth gauges continue to register descent; pressure continues to increase. Yet after the thirteenth pylon, the lights of Stillwater appear beneath one’s feet, and the city is approached as though falling toward it from the dark water overhead. The mountain summit remains below. Orientation instruments display two down directions and then cease to distinguish between them.

Stillwater may be a settlement, an instrument, or a preserved instant. Perhaps it was built to survive a flood so complete that the distinction between land and ocean became obsolete. Perhaps it was placed high on the drowned mountain because its makers wished to be near a surface that no longer exists. Perhaps the city is itself the restraint holding the sea motionless, keeping an entire planet’s water from completing some ancient fall.

Whatever its purpose, it does not wait. Waiting implies change.

It simply remains: a fresh-built, empty city at the summit of a mountain under immeasurable water, cool and dark, with its silent streets held outside erosion and consequence. Above it is depth. Below it is depth. Between its buildings, every suspended particle keeps its exact place.

And at the center, in the shaft where light accumulates but never arrives, something has been falling for longer than the city has existed.
