The basin is scarcely larger than a village square, though it feels enclosed enough to be a room. Low walls of weathered limestone surround it on every side. They are not cliffs so much as broad, slumped shelves, rounded by centuries of rain and glazed dark wherever water persists. There is no visible way in except a narrow cleft at one end, and even that seems less like an entrance than a crack left between settled stones.

Everything is wet.

Water lies just beneath the pale floor and shows itself wherever the surface dips: in shallow black pools, in seams between rocks, in circular wells no wider than a handspan. It beads on the walls and gathers beneath overhangs, making the stone appear to sweat. The air is warm, close, and saturated. Moisture condenses on skin, pottery, rope, and exposed tool handles. Nothing dries here, but nothing floods either. The basin has found a lasting balance between rainfall, seepage, and drainage. Each pool keeps its depth. Each runnel follows an old course worn smooth as fired clay.

The ground is densely occupied by stone.

There is almost no bare soil and no open stretch long enough for an easy stride. Limestone knobs crowd against slabs, slabs against low ridges of deposited mineral. Pale calcite crusts have swollen around darker rocks until the floor resembles a tightly packed bed of worn bones. In places, generations of dripping water have built blunt cones beneath the walls. Elsewhere, shallow channels divide the stone into close-fitting islands. One must move sideways, placing each foot with care, or walk along the rims of the water basins.

No plants soften the place. There are no reeds at the pools, no moss on the damp walls, no roots working through cracks. Even the stains are mineral: rust-red iron, dull ochre, chalk white, and the smoky gray left by old fires. A thin translucent film sometimes coats the warmest pools, but it has no leaf, stalk, or recognizable growth. It may be no more than salts and clay arranged by surface tension.

Near the center stand several small constructions made by hand. None rises above the waist. Flat stones have been fitted into circular curbs around the clearest springs, without mortar. Beside them are three squat shelters, or remnants of shelters, built from stacked limestone and roofed with overlapping slabs. Their interiors are barely large enough for one person to crouch in. The stones are polished where shoulders and hands once rubbed them, yet there is no fresh soot, no bedding, no stored food.

A shallow niche in the enclosing wall contains a few objects: a chipped flint blade, a hammerstone, a coil of mineral-stiffened cord, and two coarse bowls whose clay is gritted with crushed shell. The bowls have been mended with drilled holes and fiber stitching. One rests under a permanent drip. Water enters it at the same slow rate that it escapes through a hairline crack, so the bowl remains forever nearly full.

This is the basin’s prevailing character: everything appears fixed at the point of almost happening.

Drops hang beneath ledges for a long time before falling. Threads of water pass through channels too shallow to be seen except by their reflected light. Warm vapor remains suspended under the shelves. The erosion is old and complete, not actively destructive. Corners have already been rounded; unstable blocks have already fallen; sediments have already found the hollows that will hold them. Even after rain, the pools rise no higher. Hidden fissures take exactly what is given.

The light comes indirectly through the cleft and through a narrow opening high in the stone. It is bluish at first and turns brown in reflection from the wet walls. Most forms are known by sheen rather than color: a curve of black water, a pale rim, the soft flash of a dripping ceiling. At midday the upper shelves brighten briefly, but the basin floor remains dim.

Sound is sparse and intimate. A drop strikes a pool. Another answers from inside one of the roofed shelters. Water whispers underground, too faint to locate. Now and then a pebble clicks beneath its own weight, settling less than a finger’s breadth. Because the enclosure is small and crowded, each noise seems close, though its source may be hidden behind a dozen intervening stones.

Animals seldom enter. A pale cave cricket may occupy a dry seam high on the wall. Tiny flies sometimes appear above the warm film of a pool, then vanish again. There are no tracks in the mineral mud, no droppings, nests, shells, or gnawed remains. The place offers water abundantly but almost nothing to eat. Those creatures that pass through do not stay.

The old structures suggest that people once did stay, at least for a time. They came for the springs, perhaps during a season when the land beyond the cleft was dry. They shaped no grand monument and imposed no new geometry. Their walls follow the existing rock. Their basins merely clarify pools that were already there. Even the path through the crowded floor is indicated only by a sequence of flattened stepping stones, each chosen rather than cut.

Now the basin holds these works in the same equilibrium as everything else. The shelters do not noticeably collapse. The tools do not noticeably decay. Warm water continues to fill the bowls and darken the curbs. Mineral deposits slowly seal old scratches, yet so slowly that the place seems changeless.

It is not dead in any dramatic sense. It is simply beyond urgency: a warm, dim chamber of stone and water where almost every space has been filled, every loose form settled, and every motion reduced to dripping.
