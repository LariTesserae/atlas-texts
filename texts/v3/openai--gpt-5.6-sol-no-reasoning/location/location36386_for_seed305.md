At first it appears to be a mountain range under full sun: white summits, black clefts, blue distances without end. Then the nearest ridge takes a breath.

The whole high country is alive.

It occupies a plateau broader than many continents, lifted so far above the lower atmosphere that the sky has darkened toward violet. Light arrives with almost no softness in it. Every spine casts a hard blue shadow; every bead of meltwater flashes like a metal shaving. There are no valleys in the ordinary sense, only compressed folds of life rising one behind another—plated backs, antler forests, feathered escarpments, colonies heaped upon colonies until the horizon itself seems furred.

The ground beneath them is young basalt, scarcely weathered since it came up from the deep. Where it can be seen, it is sharp-edged, dark, and glassy, split into fresh terraces and long cooling fractures. There is little soil. What passes for earth is mostly shed matter: down packed into felt, translucent scales, powdered shell, droppings bleached by ultraviolet light, and the slow black compost of bodies too deeply buried in the living mass to be scavenged clean.

Nothing here has been built, yet the landscape has an industrial character.

The engines are animals.

Across the plateau move herds so immense that their migrations resemble the operation of continental machinery. Six-legged grazers, low and broad as barges, advance in close ranks through gardens of silver lichen. Their mouthparts rotate in nested rings, clipping the growth with a steady rasp. Behind them come pale comb-backed creatures that consume the grazers’ waste and press it into warm, fibrous mats. Smaller organisms descend at once upon those mats: mites, burrowers, bright worms, seed-carriers. Nothing remains unoccupied for more than a few breaths. Energy passes from body to body with the efficiency of a production line, though no foreman, plan, or artifact exists.

On distant slopes, vertical chains of winged animals rise into the sky. Each flock catches the downwash of the one below, so that tens of millions of bodies form revolving columns several kilometers tall. The columns turn slowly in the severe light. They draw thin air downward, warm it with exertion, and discharge it along the ground in broad, steady currents. These biological turbines make their own local weather. Condensation forms in the cooled wake of their wings and falls as fine snow, or gathers on feathers, horns, and stalks before dripping into the darkness below.

Thus the plateau is damp without being wet.

Water exists in pockets, skins, and passing events. It lies under the fur of the land as condensation. It collects inside cupped leaves and along the grooved backs of sleeping beasts. At noon, ice sealed between bodies loosens and runs in narrow streams through tunnels of roots and limbs. Brief rivers appear beneath the herds, flowing unseen for several hours before being drunk, absorbed, frozen, or carried away in stomachs. Some cracks in the basalt hold black pools roofed entirely by vegetation. Blind swimmers live there in such density that the water has the consistency of broth.

Rain comes only occasionally. When it does, it is made locally: a bright, cold shower falling from an animal cloud beneath an otherwise empty sky. The drops strike wings before they can reach the rock. They pass downward by impact and overflow, from canopy to canopy, from hide to hide, each drop divided and recombined thousands of times. Days later, a fraction of the same rain reaches the basalt, mineral-rich and lukewarm.

Plants survive wherever animals permit them, which is everywhere and nowhere. There are no open meadows. Vegetation grows on backs, between plates, in the hollows of antlers, and through deep drifts of dormant insects. Black-rooted cushion forests spread in tight hemispheres, their outer leaves white and reflective, their interiors humid and green. Thread-vines stitch neighboring creatures together while they sleep, then snap when the herds wake. Red mosses use body heat to keep tiny reservoirs unfrozen. Some grasses never touch stone in their entire lineage; their seeds pass from hide to hide, germinating in the dense undercoat of migratory giants.

Most plants are compact, but lushness here is not measured in height. It is measured in layers. Peel back a handspan of silver groundcover and there is violet moss beneath it; beneath the moss, a mesh of roots; beneath the roots, beetle galleries; beneath those, a crust of algae glowing in the humid dark. Even bare-looking horn carries films of bacteria, minute grazing disks, and clear eggs arranged edge to edge. The plateau is packed down to microscopic scales.

Competition has made emptiness nearly impossible.

When a large animal lifts one foot, an entire temporary habitat opens beneath it. Light reaches the ground. Air rushes into the print. Flying seeds enter, followed by scavengers and heat-seeking larvae. Frost feathers blossom around the rim. By the time the foot descends again, moments or hours later, a small ecology has established itself and is crushed into the general fabric. Some species exist only during these intervals. Their whole lives—hatching, feeding, mating, laying eggs—fit within the duration of a single step.

The dominant grazers move slowly enough that their progress is difficult to see. Their strides may take half a day. Herd fronts advance by less than a valley’s width in a season. Yet their scale turns patience into force. The basalt under their paths is not worn smooth; it is newly fractured. Weight opens clean angular fissures. Pressure drives organic acids into the hot interior rock. Mountains do not crumble here so much as split, suddenly but infrequently, along brilliant fresh faces.

After such a fracture, the ecology responds over decades. First come mineral-licking swarms, coating the exposed stone in metallic colors. Then root mats enter the hairline cracks. Nesting animals settle upon the ledges in towers of interlocked bodies. Within a century, the raw cliff is hidden. This is erosion in its infancy: not yet the long rounding of landscapes, but the first bite, the first wedge of ice, the first root inserted into a world still sharp from formation.

The sound is continuous and enormous.

Near at hand there is clicking, grazing, scraping, the creak of carapaces, and the soft detonation of frost splitting shells. Beneath it lies the bass rhythm of circulation: millions of lungs drawing and releasing thin air. Farther off, migration fronts produce a low mechanical thunder. Wing-columns beat at frequencies that tremble in the ribs. Herds communicate through the basalt with pulses too deep to hear directly; loose stones hop, pools wrinkle, and sensitive plants fold their leaves as the messages pass.

At intervals, the whole plateau changes pitch.

A pressure wave travels from horizon to horizon as one population answers another. The response is not a roar but a vast modulation of existing sound: wings alter their angle, grazers pause their rotating jaws, air sacs inflate along a billion flanks. For several minutes the mountains seem to hum like energized metal. Then the message passes and the local noise resumes.

No individual appears to understand the signal. Perhaps none needs to. The plateau behaves through accumulations: slight preferences repeated by uncountable bodies, minute adjustments becoming weather, migration, and geology.

Predators are abundant but almost invisible against so much prey. Some imitate ravines, opening only when a herd crosses them. Others resemble patches of bright snow until their surfaces separate into mouths. Long transparent hunters drift within the wing-columns and feed without interrupting the flight. The largest predators do not chase. They lie beneath the packed communities for centuries, warming the overlying vegetation and drawing animals inward. When enough mass has gathered, they roll once.

A roll may take three days.

Forests tilt. Streams reverse. Millions flee across a hide they had mistaken for ground. A new dark slope is exposed to the sun, wet with condensation and dense with pale cave species suddenly born into day. Then the creature settles and becomes terrain again. Generations pass before its next movement.

Death seldom makes space. A fallen giant becomes crowded before its body cools. Scavengers arrive as dark rivers. Fungi stand up in white tiers. Plants exploit salts released beneath the skin. Nesters enter the lungs; swimmers enter the eyes. The skeleton may remain for millennia, but even its outline disappears under accumulating lives. Its ribs become sheltered valleys. Its teeth rise like young peaks, still unworn, supporting bright colonies on their windward faces.

Because the air is cold, decay proceeds slowly in the deep layers. Vast reserves of unfinished life accumulate beneath the surface—frozen fat, pollen, spores, chitin, seeds awaiting warmth. The plateau stores ancient summers inside itself. A crack, a fever, or a rare heavy rain can awaken one buried layer, and species absent for thousands of years emerge in dense, brief blooms. They may cover a region larger than a sea, transform its color for a century, and sink again without ever becoming dominant.

The light hardly relents during the long season. Snowfields and reflective leaves throw it upward; pale wings scatter it sideways. Shadows are filled by glints from eyes, shells, ice, and mica in the basalt. Even under several layers of bodies, dim radiance persists, carried by translucent tissues and living fibers. In the deepest crowded passages, organisms manufacture colder lights: green pulses, blue veins, slow amber flashes signaling pressure or hunger.

Night, when it finally comes, is not truly dark. Migrating animals climb above the plateau in glowing sheets. Their undersides shine onto the living ground, tracing the movements of the air. Predators blink in patterns across hundreds of kilometers. Heat rises in shimmering columns, and the stars appear to bend around them.

There are rules here, though no one declared them.

Nothing may remain empty.

Nothing owns the surface it occupies.

Stone is only the lowest and slowest form of habitat.

Weather can be made by agreement without intention.

A mountain may wake.

A river may travel without flowing, carried from body to body across the plateau.

Industry need not produce objects. Here it produces heat, soil, cloud, and more life.

From a great distance the high country looks almost still: a brilliant, pale-green continent under a dark sky, edged with new black mountains. Its migrations are too slow to register. Its wing-columns resemble permanent weather. Its oldest animals are mistaken for ranges, and its youngest rock for wounds.

Only by watching for years would one see the entire surface shift.

Only by listening through the stone would one know it had already begun.
