The high sea has no shore.

It occupies the upper atmosphere of a vast world, suspended where mountains would ordinarily end and weather would begin. Beneath it lies a depth no swimmer has measured: cool blue-green water descending into clouded darkness. Above it, if “above” still means anything here, there is more water, faintly silvered by a remote and evenly distributed light. There is no surface. Pressure does not increase downward, nor does buoyancy favor any direction. The sea is held in perfect suspension, a planetary volume without tides, currents, thermoclines, or falling.

Within it grows the Crown.

The Crown is not a forest rooted in soil. It is the soil, the horizon, and nearly everything solid enough to touch. Its oldest bodies are continental plants whose trunks extend for thousands of kilometers through the water. They do not rise or branch according to gravity. Instead they form enormous open lattices: dark cables, pale ribs, broad membranes, and sparsely spaced knots from which leaf systems unfold. One may travel for days across an empty blue gulf and see only a single green filament on the horizon. Yet that filament, approached, proves wider than a river and densely composed of braided living strands.

Vegetation fills almost all the world’s substance while leaving most of its volume open.

The largest trunks are weathered into stable shapes. Their outer layers are ridged and hollowed by an erosion that ended long ago. Water once moved here. It carved flutes into bark, bored smooth tunnels through woody masses, and combed the edges of leaves into long parallel fingers. Then motion diminished, slowed beyond measurement, and ceased. The scars remain perfectly clear. Splinters hang halfway detached but never break loose. Curtains of shed epidermis stand extended in the water, neither sinking nor folding. Every surface bears the settled signature of ancient flow.

Now nothing changes.

No leaf opens. No creature grows. No body decays. Predation occurs, but it is not an event. Birth is abundant, but it never begins. Death is everywhere, but it never completes. The landscape exists in a state so exact that all its processes coincide with their own cancellation.

A translucent grazer hangs among the leaves with its mouth closed around a frond. The bite is perpetual. Plant sugars cross one membrane while an equal measure returns through another; the frond remains whole, the animal remains hungry and fed. Farther out, a ribbon-bodied hunter encloses a cloud of minute swimmers, each swimmer held just ahead of digestion. Spores occupy the water in immense but sparse constellations. They are always germinating, their first root hairs extending no farther than the width of a cell.

This stasis is not silence.

The Crown is deafening.

Every plant is an engine.

Along the great trunks, bands of contractile wood hold themselves under tension. Chambers in the bark compress water against mineral valves grown from silica and iron. Leaf membranes maintain electrical potentials powerful enough to make the surrounding water glow faintly at their edges. Vast rootless bulbs perform the work of lungs, pumps, turbines, and refineries—not because they were designed, but because the ecosystem’s metabolism long ago acquired industrial scale.

No one built these mechanisms. There are no walls laid stone upon stone, no tools, no roads, no wreckage of makers. A pumping station the size of a mountain is a fruit. A refinery is a colony of mosses. Transmission lines are black vines whose sap carries charge through filaments of copper accumulated molecule by molecule from the sea. Cylinders turning within woody housings are seed organs. Rows of identical pressure sacs, stretching beyond visibility, are the vegetative segments of one plant.

They operate without moving.

Each turbine is held at the exact point of rotation where thrust, drag, intake, and discharge balance. Yet its note continues: a low mechanical bellow conducted through the water and through the living skeleton of the Crown. Valves thunder without opening. Cavitation crackles permanently in polished chambers, bubbles poised between formation and collapse. Electrical organs emit an unbroken transformer hum. Tensioned fibers sing like cables in a wind that no longer blows.

The result is a stable roar, immense and without rhythm.

Sound has no onset here. It is part of the geography. Some regions are shaped by bass pressure so deep that soft-bodied animals flatten into discs. Elsewhere, needle leaves sustain piercing harmonic fields, and passing through them feels like entering cold, bright metal. Where trunks intersect, their tones interfere, producing quiet hollows: enormous lens-shaped sanctuaries in which the surrounding machinery cancels itself. These are not truly silent. One hears the fine ticking of crustaceans’ joints, the hiss of gas exchange across leaves, and the continuous choral rasp of billions of feeding mouths.

The water is cool enough to numb exposed flesh but not cold enough for ice. Its temperature is identical everywhere. Light is similarly even: neither dim abyssal gloom nor brilliant tropical glare, but the calm illumination of an overcast day, present throughout the volume without any discernible source. Leaves cast shadows, though there is no sun. The shadows fall in different directions according to the local electrical orientation of the Crown, so that every grove possesses its own private geometry of noon.

Color gathers chiefly around life. The open water is a restrained blue-gray. Old load-bearing trunks are black, umber, and mineral green. Young tissue—though nothing is truly young—is vivid: fern gold, translucent lime, bruised violet, the cold red of deep-water algae. Many leaves are several kilometers broad and only a few cells thick. Seen edge-on, they vanish. Seen face-on, they divide the sea into luminous countries.

Between these living continents are great empty intervals.

The fauna is abundant in number but dispersed across the Crown’s scale. A shoal of a million silver animals may look like a pinch of filings in an oceanic chamber. Long-legged browsers stand on trunk surfaces, gripping bark with radial feet; because there is no useful down, they stand on every side at once. Balloon-bodied filterers occupy the open water at distances of tens of kilometers from one another, each attended by small ecosystems of cleaners, parasites, pollinators, and scavengers. Blind armored things inhabit the ancient erosion tunnels. Their shells fit the channels so exactly that it is impossible to tell whether the plants shaped the animals or the animals once shaped the plants.

Some creatures resemble pieces of unfinished machinery. There are fish with ceramic teeth arranged like gears, worms that insulate themselves in resin, and broad colonial animals that rest across electrical vines and feed upon voltage. A transparent species with hundreds of branching limbs bridges the gaps between leaves. Its nerves flash in orderly sequences, always the same sequence, never reaching an end. In the quieter hollows drift soft bells containing miniature gardens; plant and animal tissues have mingled so thoroughly that taxonomy becomes a question of which process one chooses to observe.

Distances deceive. With no surface, bottom, current, or gravity, there is no natural direction and little sense of speed. The Crown supplies orientation through its anatomy. Travelers speak in terms of sapward and bloomward, toward the blackwood or away from the singing membranes. “High” means approaching the oldest photosynthetic strata, where the ambient light seems fractionally whiter. These regions are called the summits, though one may enter them from any direction.

At the highest summits, the vegetation opens.

There are cool blue expanses thousands of kilometers across, bounded by sparse arches of living wood. Here the industrial roar becomes broad and remote. Gigantic leaves stand far apart like green moons. Each supports a halo of animals held in permanent migration around it: grazers, hunters, spores, and glittering pollinators arranged in rings. Nothing advances along those rings, yet the posture of every body declares flight.

The oldest scars of erosion are found there. Whole ridges have been rounded smooth. Caverns open through trunks large enough to contain weather, though no weather now develops. Inside them hang stationary banks of plant-born mist—water within water, distinguished by oils and dissolved gases. Sheet lightning extends from wall to wall and remains branched across the chamber. Its illumination does not flicker. Thunder is continuous and has polished the inner bark into resonant curves.

There are no ruins because nothing here was ever constructed, and nothing has been lost.

There is no wilderness in opposition to industry. The factory and the forest are the same organism viewed at different distances. Its mechanization is as innocent as flowering. Its noise is birdsong enlarged to continental power. Its cables seek light; its furnaces digest minerals; its production lines manufacture pollen, resin, oxygen, toxins, shells, sugars, and exact replacements for cells that never quite die.

What is produced is consumed in the same instant and quantity. What is consumed remains present. Heat released by one reaction is absorbed by its reciprocal elsewhere, with no delay. Every pressure has its counterpressure. Every sound is sustained by an equal, inaudible cancellation that prevents it from doing work. The ecology is crowded with relations but empty of history.

Perhaps the Crown once changed. Its worn channels and streamlined forms imply storms, migrations, seasons, and a directional sea. Perhaps its biological engines accelerated until they achieved a perfect planetary regulation. Or perhaps the scars are not memories at all. They may simply be permanent features of a world that contains the appearance of age without ever having had a past.

There is no method of deciding.

A mark cut into the bark would already have been there. A displaced animal would still occupy its former place while also seeming to travel. A measuring device, if such an artifact could exist here, would join the equilibrium and report one unvarying value forever. The landscape admits observers only as another balanced process: seeing and being seen, disturbing and restoring, arriving and having always been present.

Thus the high sea persists—cool, illuminated, uproarious, and still.

Across its immeasurable blue chambers, the plant continents hold themselves open. Their engines thunder. Their leaves remain spread. Animals swarm without crossing the spaces between one moment and the next. Ancient watercourses score the wood, recording motions that cannot recur and may never have happened.

Nothing waits.

Nothing finishes.

The whole living ocean is caught neither in sleep nor in paralysis, but in a perfection of activity so complete that change has nowhere left to go.
