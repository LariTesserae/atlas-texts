The place is a hollow near the top of a mesa, scarcely larger than a walled hamlet. By day the surrounding plateau drinks the sun until its stone becomes painful to touch. By night—if night is what this is—the heat does not leave. It remains stored in the black rock and breathes upward through fissures, carrying iron dust, sulfur, and the bitter odor of old shells.

No open sky can be seen from inside.

The hollow is packed from floor to rim with animals.

At first they resemble a single dark substance, a deep granular fill poured into a volcanic cup. Then the substance rearranges itself. Backs rise and sink. Plates slide under plates. Jointed legs find momentary purchase on horns, carapaces, sleeping flanks. Wings unfold only halfway before encountering other wings. There is no bare ground except where the ground itself is alive.

Most of the creatures are small enough to hold in two hands, though a few broad-backed forms occupy whole terraces. They have evolved without water and without anything that could be called vegetation. Their tissues run on hot oils, sulfurous waxes, and slow mineral reactions. They do not drink. They graze the stone.

The mesa’s basalt contains narrow bands of pale salts and metallic sulfides. Scrapers rasp these seams with glass teeth. Borers dissolve pits using acidic breath. Heavier beasts swallow loose rock, digest its reactive fraction, and pass smooth black pellets behind them. Mites consume the pellets. Needle-mouthed things consume the mites. Everything else consumes whatever becomes trapped, injured, overheated, or briefly still.

Yet the hollow is mostly quiet.

Noise is dangerous here, and wasteful. Bodies communicate through pressure: a touch passed foot to foot, a shiver transmitted across a thousand interlocked shells. Alarm travels as a tightening of the entire colony-like mass. Courtship is a slow alternation of warmth and shadow. Territorial threats are made by exposing reflective patches no larger than fingernails. Only occasionally does an audible sound escape—a dry click, the whisper of scales, a single low booming note from somewhere underneath. Afterward, silence spreads in a ring.

The darkness comes from the animals themselves. Above the hollow, millions of leathery bodies cling to one another in suspended layers, anchored to the inward-sloping rim. They are not quite flyers. In the thin high air, flight would be expensive, so they form living canopies and glide only when rising heat is strongest. Their overlapping membranes block the sun almost completely. A few narrow shafts penetrate the canopy, but these are immediately crowded by heat-feeding creatures that stand upright in the light and turn translucent.

What illumination remains is dull and internal. Many species bear pits of red radiance along their sides, not true lamps but exposed organs hot enough to glow. Others have mirrored faces that borrow these embers and scatter them through the press. Seen from the rim, the hollow pulses faintly, as though coals had been buried beneath beetle shells.

The terrain below the swarm has been shaped over ages into concentric shelves. None are architectural in origin, though they resemble worn stairs. The animals made them by feeding: softer mineral strata were eaten away, harder bands left standing. Claws, bellies, and migrating weight polished every surface. Ancient runnels descend toward a central throat, but they never carried water. They conduct bodies. During mass movements the population flows down them in ropes and sheets.

At the hollow’s center stands the only constructed thing: a ring of stone uprights, each roughly the height of a person, leaning inward above a plugged fissure. No tool marks remain, if there ever were any. Heat has cracked the uprights; mineral breath has glazed them; generations of rubbing animals have rounded their edges. They may be ruins, or natural slabs once rearranged by a species that no longer exists. The current inhabitants treat them simply as warmer stone.

The plugged fissure governs the place.

Deep beneath it, pressure accumulates. Hot gas climbs through pores in the seal, feeding pale crusts of sulfur and iron salt. For long periods the leakage is gentle. Grazers assemble around the uprights; predators bury themselves among them; egg cases are cemented into the hottest cracks. Then, without warning, the throat exhales.

The first sign is not sound but expansion. Bodies nearest the center swell as gas enters their breathing chambers. The pressure signal passes outward. The packed multitude begins to climb.

It does not panic. Panic would create useless space. Instead each creature participates in a precise, ancient rearrangement. Armored feeders brace themselves into ramps. Hook-limbed young ascend their backs. Broad membrane-bearers detach from the roof and crawl toward the rim. Predators flatten into crevices while prey walk over them under temporary truce. The whole hollow turns slowly inside out.

Then the gas arrives: furnace-hot, oxygen-poor, laden with black mineral powder. It pushes through the central shelves and lifts the lighter animals in a dense spiral. Wings open. Shells tumble. Glowing bodies rise toward the roof, where the canopy parts just enough to release them into the dark above the mesa.

For several minutes the sky fills with life.

The exhalation is nearly silent, a vast soft rush like sand sliding under cloth. Animals circle over the high plateau on the thermal column, mating, hunting, exchanging parasites, scattering heat-resistant egg grains. Some descend into neighboring fissures. Most fall back into the hollow as the breath weakens. The canopy closes. Darkness returns.

Below, the shelves are different. A few bodies have been crushed. Others lie stunned and are dismantled almost at once. Fresh mineral dust coats the stone. Feeding resumes.

There are laws here, though nothing declared them.

No corpse remains intact for long.

No patch of warmth stays unoccupied.

Open space is an injury that bodies hurry to close.

Noise belongs to the trapped, the dying, and whatever moves beneath the floor.

And water—should even a drop arrive from some impossible weather—is not relief. It is a violent reagent. Salts bloom, oils curdle, dormant spores rupture, and whole feeding chains converge upon the wetness. The oldest shells bear pale scars from rains separated by centuries.

Around the mesa, the desert is apparently empty. Its slopes descend through ash, glassy scree, and wind-planed red stone. Nothing grows. Nothing crosses the salt flats below. The wind leaves long comb marks that may persist for decades.

Only at the summit does the world seem crowded beyond capacity: a dark cup of hunger, heat, and patient movement, established so long that animal and terrain have become the same weathered structure. The hollow does not contain an ecosystem so much as the ecosystem contains the hollow—holding its shelves together, roofing it against the light, grinding its walls inward grain by grain.

Sometimes, beneath all those interlocked feet, the ancient plug shifts.

Every creature freezes.

From far below comes one quiet knock.
