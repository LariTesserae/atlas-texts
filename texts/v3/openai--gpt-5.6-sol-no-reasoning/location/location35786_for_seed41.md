Below the hardpan, where the roots of the sun-baked plain lose their direction, there is a chamber called the Green Hollow.

It is not deep. In several places the surface lies only a few body-lengths overhead, but the intervening stone is dense and old, packed with reddish clay and seams of pale mineral. The hollow occupies the breadth of a small grove. Its boundaries are clear: a low vault above, a floor sunken into warm mud, and walls rounded by water that no longer flows continuously. Nothing here is immense, yet the density of life confuses distance. Curtains, stems, mineral folds, and suspended roots divide the chamber into many intimate spaces. One may cross it quickly and still feel that it contains hundreds of rooms.

The heat is constant. It gathers beneath the earth and cannot escape, so the air has the close warmth of breath held behind closed lips. Skin becomes wet almost at once, though open water is scarce. Moisture exists as condensation: pearls trembling on root hairs, greasy films over stone, droplets swelling beneath leaves. Occasionally one falls. In the encompassing quiet its impact seems precise and solitary.

Rain reaches the hollow indirectly. During storms on the plain, water presses through cracks and old burrows. For several hours the ceiling begins to seep from a hundred points. Thin threads descend, briefly silver in the weak light, and the floor’s shallow basins fill. By the following day most are empty again, drunk by roots, lapped by animals, or drawn back into the hot air. Only three deeper pools persist through the dry intervals. Their water is dark with tannin and clear at the edges, where pale blind shrimp graze among drowned filaments.

The chamber was made by erosion, but erosion here has matured into architecture. The former current left smooth channels along the walls and scooped shelves at several heights, recording floods older than the vegetation. Ribs of harder stone remain between softer hollows. Their edges have long since lost any sharpness. Mud banks slump and settle, roots brace them, and the next seepage opens a path around rather than through. Destruction and repair have reached an equilibrium: every fallen piece becomes a dam, every dam becomes a garden, every garden slows the water that feeds it.

Most plants descend into the hollow rather than rise from it.

The ceiling is crowded with roots from the surface scrub—ropes as thick as limbs, red-brown cords, transparent feeding threads, and flat braided masses that cling to damp stone. Some reach the floor and thicken there, forming columns. Others hang freely, ending in tassels just above the mud. Their tips grow fastest when rain is approaching, responding to pressure and moisture before the first drop falls above. The whole ceiling then appears to lower by a finger’s breadth.

Between these roots live the hollow’s own growths. Broad waxy leaves unfold from pockets of silt on the walls. Their color in the dimness is nearly black, though bruised tissue shines an intense green. Mats of velvet moss cover the flood shelves. Small translucent fungi emerge in clusters after seepage, soften within hours, and are eaten before they collapse. There are also bladder-like plants rooted underwater, each bearing a pale cup at the surface. The cups catch condensation and, sometimes, tiny animals. They digest both.

No sunlight directly enters. Light leaks down through a few narrow fissures, stained amber by soil and roots. It produces columns no wider than reeds, visible mostly because of the insects turning within them. Elsewhere there is a weak living radiance. Fungal threads glimmer under barklike root skins. Pinpoint larvae pulse blue-green when disturbed. A creeping lichen marks old waterlines with a dull gold haze. These lights do not illuminate the chamber so much as reveal its layers one at a time: a wet eye, a curved shell, the underside of a leaf, then nothing.

Animals are everywhere, but the hollow is remarkably quiet.

The mud is densely written with tracks—split toes, dragging tails, the stippling of many feet—yet movement is cautious. Heat rewards stillness, and sound attracts hunters. Beetles fold their legs beneath mirrored shells. Long-bodied amphibians lie in root creases with only their nostrils exposed. Pale crabs occupy holes in the banks, sealing the entrances with mud during the driest weeks. Tiny fish survive in the permanent pools; when seasonal puddles form, some travel between them overland, flexing through wet moss in the dark.

The most numerous creatures inhabit the ceiling. Moths sleep among root tassels. Soft-bodied mites graze condensation films. Small geckos, mottled like mineral deposits, hang upside down and take insects without an audible snap. Above them cling colonies of compact, furless animals with membrane-thin ears. They are not quite bats: their forelimbs are made for climbing rather than flight, and they move through the root canopy hand over hand. At long intervals one gives a single dry click. Other clicks answer from distant parts of the chamber, mapping passages that sight cannot.

Predation is frequent but subdued. A frog vanishes beneath a leaf. A thread-snake draws a sleeping moth into a crack. Ripples cross a pool after something pale surges upward. There are no prolonged chases; the terrain is too tangled. Life proceeds by ambush, concealment, and short decisive movements.

At the center of the hollow stands its one conspicuous remnant of building: a low ring of fitted stones around the largest pool. No mortar remains, if there ever was any. Roots have entered the seams and pushed the ring out of true, but its circular intention is still unmistakable. Beside it, three shallow steps ascend into a wall and stop there. Mineral flow has softened their faces. One bears a pattern of parallel grooves, perhaps carved, perhaps merely the exposed structure of the rock.

Nothing else indicates who shaped the stones. There are no tools, vessels, hinges, or metal stains. The chamber has absorbed the ring so completely that animals treat it as geology. Crabs burrow beneath it. Moss thickens on the shaded inner face. During seepage, water beads along each fitted joint, outlining the old construction before the lines disappear again.

The place follows a cycle more exact than seasons on the surface. First comes the long hot hush, when pools withdraw and leaves turn their waxed faces inward. Then the roots begin to sweat. Larvae wake in sealed mud. The climbing colonies descend toward the water. Hours later, rain enters—not as a torrent, but as innumerable fine leaks. Fungi open. Amphibians call only once or twice, low enough to be felt through the ground. Eggs are laid in every temporary basin. Predators gather. For a brief interval the Green Hollow is in rapid motion while remaining almost silent.

Then the seepage ends. Droplets become less frequent. Temporary streams separate into beads and vanish. New creatures hatch quickly, feeding on one another as their pools contract. Those that can burrow descend. Those that can climb move into the roots. Those able to endure seal themselves in leaves, mud, or translucent cases against the stone.

The hollow grows quiet again, but never still. Condensation crawls. Roots tighten imperceptibly. Mud settles beneath old stonework. In the pools, blind shrimp continue tracing the dark margins. Somewhere in the dense partitions, too softly to locate, something clicks once and waits for the chamber to answer.
