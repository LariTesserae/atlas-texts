The sea has no bottom anyone would call natural.

Far beneath the weathered surface—below shipping, storms, and the seasonal drift of ice—the water opens into an engineered abyss. Its floor is a continuous plate of steel and poured mineral composite, laid in sections so immense that their seams appear on maps as straight dark valleys. The entire basin was manufactured: walls, slopes, trenches, reefs, channels, even the sediment. There is no exposed bedrock. Wherever the plates have split, another layer of construction waits underneath.

The place is vast enough to have its own currents and internal weather. Cold rivers of dense water descend from distant cooling towers, travel for hundreds of kilometers, and strike warmer industrial outflows. At their borders, the water turns cloudy. Slow fronts form. Curtains of silt advance across open tracts, and localized storms of bubbles climb toward the unreachable brightness overhead.

Despite the depth, there is light.

Rows of lamps are fixed to the ceiling of the sea: white industrial suns mounted on pylons, cables, and suspended trusses. Some face downward from buoyant platforms; others rise from the floor on lattice towers taller than mountains. Together they establish a broad approximation of daylight. Their illumination is plain and functional, neither theatrical nor uncanny. Shadows fall where shadows should. Colors remain recognizable: gray steel, yellow safety paint, rust-red runoff, blue-green water, the silver sides of fish.

The lamps follow work cycles rather than astronomy. Banks brighten one by one across the basin, creating a dawn that takes hours to travel from east to west. At the end of the shift, they dim in the same order. Maintenance outages make temporary nights hundreds of kilometers wide. Animals gather at their borders, following the moving edge of light.

Most of the constructed seafloor is empty.

There are long plains of ribbed plating on which nothing stands except inspection posts and numbered hatches. Cable conduits cross them in perfectly straight lines. Access roads, intended for vehicles that no longer operate, remain visible beneath a skin of pale manufactured sand. Here and there, bolt heads rise like isolated boulders. The distances between installations are so great that a swimmer can travel for days without seeing anything larger than a pipe marker.

The emptiness is not peaceful. Sound travels too well.

Pumps beat beyond the horizon with a slow, bodily rhythm. Turbines produce steady bands of vibration, felt in the jaw before they are heard. Pressure doors close with reports like remote artillery. Chains drag through guide wheels. Cooling fans transmit their drone through the structure and into the water. Wherever two currents meet a truss, the whole assembly hums. At intervals, warning horns sound for operations taking place far away; their tones arrive delayed, distorted, and directionless.

Close to a working complex, the noise becomes overwhelming. Water screams through reduction valves. Intake grilles howl. Loose panels chatter against their fastenings. Schools of fish scatter at the starting of a compressor, then regroup as soon as the sound becomes constant. Life here fears change more than volume.

The principal structures are factories built to operate underwater. They stand in widely separated clusters, each one a city of pressure vessels, pipe galleries, tanks, chimneys, and crane towers. Their architecture is blunt because it was designed for force: rounded chambers, thick doors, external ribs, replaceable panels. Yellow gantries encircle black cylinders. Windows are few and small. Rail lines run inside transparent maintenance tubes, linking assembly halls to mines that do not excavate stone but harvest old layers of machinery beneath the basin floor.

Above each factory hangs a dirty artificial sky of exhaust bubbles. The bubbles collect under broad capture roofs, merge into trembling silver lakes, and are drawn away through vertical ducts. Where a roof has failed, gas escapes freely. Columns rise for kilometers, breaking into finer beads until they resemble snow traveling upward.

The basin is young, or at least newly rebuilt. Its edges have not had time to soften. Trenches retain sharp angles. Artificial embankments show fresh tool marks. Collapsed panels have bright torn edges with little corrosion. On slopes, the manufactured sediment lies thinly and slides easily, exposing geometric foundations beneath it. Every strong current redraws these deposits. Dunes form against railings, migrate across floor markings, and vanish into intakes.

There are only sparse gardens. Vegetation was introduced as filtration infrastructure rather than scenery: tough ribbons of kelp attached to ceramic racks, low mats of algae around nutrient outlets, and occasional fields of seagrass rooted in shallow trays. The plants never conceal the construction. Each patch is bounded by frames, valves, and feed lines. Beyond the cultivated plots, bare metal resumes.

Animals are far more successful.

Small fish fill the illuminated water in restless, coordinated clouds. They browse on bacterial films growing over warm pipes and graze the algae kept alive by leaking fertilizer. Larger fish patrol the outer margins of the lamp fields. Crabs inhabit drainage slots. Eels settle inside unused cable ducts, facing into the current with only their heads exposed. Mussels crowd bolt recesses until maintenance crews scrape them clean. Pale shrimp rise when the lights dim and descend again at the artificial dawn.

The largest animals travel between industrial districts. Broad-backed rays glide above the plating, using the warmth of buried conduits as migratory routes. Tuna follow the great circulation loops. Sharks wait near intake exclusion zones, where disoriented fish accumulate against vibrating screens. They are ordinary sharks, neither engineered monsters nor symbols—simply predators exploiting a dependable machine.

Life concentrates around failure. A leaking heat exchanger becomes a reef. A broken nutrient pipe flowers green within weeks. An abandoned crane acquires mussels, anemones, fish, and hunting eels until it is biologically busier than an intact habitat module. Repair crews erase these accidental ecosystems as part of routine work, and new ones begin elsewhere.

The water never rests. Pumps maintain basin-scale circulation, lifting deep water through towers and releasing it along the upper levels. Thermal plumes bend beneath crosscurrents. Automated dredges crawl over the floor, vacuuming sediment from channels and laying it down in designated fields. Cargo submersibles move between plants with lamps blazing, pushing blunt pressure waves ahead of them. Their wakes arrive long after they have passed, stirring pale clouds from otherwise empty plains.

In some sectors, fabrication continues. Vast movable frames lower new plates into gaps in the floor. Welding vehicles travel along the seams, producing white arcs visible through kilometers of dark water. The welds cool quickly and contract with deep metallic knocks. Fresh structures look almost painfully precise: clean edges, unclouded glass, paint without scratches. Within months they carry films of bacteria. Within years they host shells and rust stains. Yet erosion is kept from advancing far. The machinery renews the landscape faster than water can age it.

The rules are practical and absolute. Currents reverse when scheduled. Daylight begins when the grid supplies it. A valley may be drained, sealed, and replaced. Migration routes persist only so long as the pumps that create them. Warm seasons occur downstream from reactors; winters spread during shutdowns. Geography is an operating condition.

There are storms, though no wind reaches this depth. When several circulation stations change state together, opposing flows collide over the central plain. Sediment rises into rolling fronts. Kelp racks bend flat. Fish seek the lee of towers, while larger animals turn into the current and hold position. The lamps become diffuse disks in the murk. Machinery disappears a hundred meters away, but its noise grows louder through the turbulence.

Then the pumps settle into their new pattern. The cloud advances, thins, and drops its load over another sector. Plate numbers reappear. The white light clears. Schools reform around the pylons.

From any single point, the basin seems sparse: a lamp tower, a line of piping, a few distant fish, and an empty plain disappearing into blue. Its fullness becomes apparent only over time. Currents bring migrations. Sirens announce cargo movements. A dredger crosses the horizon. Predators arrive at the shift change. Lamps brighten in the distance like weather moving in.

Everything here has been placed, but almost nothing remains only what it was made to be. The sea uses the factories as coastline, the pipelines as reefs, the waste heat as climate, and the work schedule as its calendar. It is not reclaiming a natural world beneath the construction; there is no older ground to reclaim. It is making an ecology directly upon the machine, in the cold, loud water of an artificial ocean large enough to feel inevitable.
