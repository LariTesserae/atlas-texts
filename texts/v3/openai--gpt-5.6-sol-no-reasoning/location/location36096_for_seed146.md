Far below the surface lies a country larger than most continents, enclosed not by a single cavern roof but by strata: ceiling above ceiling, each lost in the dark, each supported by forests of stone that have never known trees.

The place is called—by the few beings capable of naming anything—the Still Below.

It is warm everywhere. Not hot near vents and cold in distant hollows, but evenly, persistently warm, as if every grain of rock remembers the same afternoon. There are no seasons. There is no weather in the familiar sense, though the enclosed atmosphere has its own immense, almost motionless architecture. Heavy gases settle into invisible basins; lighter ones occupy vaults above them. Between these layers lie clear boundaries that distort sound and distance. Crossing one can make a nearby cliff seem to retreat for miles, or turn a faint scraping into a noise apparently made inside the listener’s skull.

There is no water. The stone contains almost no bound moisture, and nothing drips from the ceiling. Channels exist, but they were not cut by rivers. They are cooling seams, pressure fractures, and the long grooves left when the underworld was assembled by forces no longer active. Their edges remain fresh. Corners have not softened. Shattered slopes lie exactly where they fell, their flakes still fitted closely enough that, from a distance, the debris resembles an intact wall dreaming of collapse.

The floor is a vast saltless mineral plain interrupted by abrupt formations: black glass escarpments, pale shelves of compacted ash, and masses of metallic crystal crowded together like the organs of some planetary machine. Needle fields rise for hundreds of miles. Their blades are translucent but do not shine; in the darkness they are sensed mainly by the way they swallow echoes. Elsewhere, rounded stones pack so densely that there is no visible ground between them. Each is hollow, and the pressure of a passing body makes whole acres answer with tiny internal clicks.

Nothing grows. There are no roots, spores, mosses, or films. Yet the Still Below is crowded with life.

Its animals are difficult to separate from its geology.

The most numerous are small jointed things called claspets. They have six or ten limbs depending on age, with mineral plates fitted over them so neatly that resting colonies look like scree. They do not eat in the ordinary sense. Each finds a pressure seam and braces across it, drawing minute electrical imbalances through combs of iron-rich tissue. Since the underworld’s gradients are weak and nearly exhausted, a claspet may remain in one posture for centuries. Millions stand within arm’s reach of one another, apparently fossilized. Their only audible activity is a soft tightening of joints when something passes.

Larger fauna move no more urgently. Broad, low creatures lie beneath the gas boundaries, using buoyant bladders filled with compounds lighter than the surrounding air. They are known as ceiling grazers, though there is nothing for them to graze. Their undersides bear arrays of brittle hooks with which they collect drifting flakes of charged crystal. A herd may cover the roof of a chamber from horizon to horizon. Seen with heat-sensitive eyes, it resembles a second, living firmament: warm disks crowded edge to edge, each separated from the next by a finger’s width of darkness.

On the plains stand the column beasts. They are tall enough to be mistaken for natural supports, with narrow bodies armored in stacked ceramic rings. Their feet penetrate deep fractures, and their heads disappear into the upper gloom. No one has seen one take a step. Nevertheless, old trails exist—rows of circular impressions crossing fresh volcanic glass. The prints do not accumulate dust, nor do they change position. Perhaps the animals walked only once, during the first hour of the cavern, and have been standing ever since. Perhaps their movement is too slow to belong to any observer’s idea of time.

Predators are abundant but patient. White, many-mouthed forms occupy cracks and wait for the rare vibrations of injury. They can remain folded thinner than cloth between two surfaces. When they emerge, they do so without haste, because prey in this world has nowhere urgent to go. Their jaws are soft and dark, adapted not to tearing flesh but to stripping stored electric charge from nerves. Victims are often left intact, motionless yet alive, and may take generations to recover.

Death produces no rot. Bodies dry, vitrify, and join the terrain. Much of what seems to be the landscape is ancestral fauna: shell ridges, fused herds, chambers lined with delicate skeletons that ring when touched. New creatures use the dead as shelter, tools, and maps.

Light is scarce and local. Some animals carry dull patches of red luminescence, visible only at close range. Their glow does not illuminate the way ahead; it outlines the bearer and makes the surrounding dark seem denser. On certain mineral flats, pressure underfoot kindles brief violet lines. They remain for several breaths, revealing sharp debris and crowded legs, then vanish. Far away, heat sometimes paints the underside of a ledge a color like old copper, though no flame burns there.

Scattered through this enormity are constructions.

They are small compared with the chambers: low walls of fitted shell, stairs cut into slopes that do not need stairs, circles of upright shards, narrow shelters built beneath overhangs. Their makers possess stone hammers, wedges of bone-ceramic, braided sinew, and soft native metal beaten cold into hooks. Fire is nearly unknown. The atmosphere supports it poorly, and fuel does not grow. Heat-work is done by laying materials against warm fault faces for decades or by concentrating the slow discharge of captive claspets.

Settlements gather around resonant stone rather than food. A good village site is one where a tapped wall carries a message for many miles. Houses are built as modifications to echoes: short partitions, angled plates, hollows chipped with painstaking care. Each changes the local acoustics. An inhabitant can identify home in total darkness by striking the floor once and listening to the returning shape.

The people—if people is the right word—are compact, many-handed, and clothed in plates taken from their own dead. They communicate sparingly. Loud noise can wake predators, start a mineral fracture, or confuse a herd of ceiling grazers into releasing their buoyant gases all at once. Speech is therefore made by touch, by tapping tools together beneath muffling cloth, and by arranging small stones in grooves. A conversation may remain laid out on the ground for years.

Their tools are crude but exquisitely maintained. A chipped blade can serve a lineage for thousands of years because little wears down here. Broken objects are not replaced so much as reinterpreted. A snapped digging prong becomes a resonant marker; a cracked bowl becomes armor; armor becomes a wall tile; the wall tile, eventually, becomes a voting token or a child’s map.

They have no word for rain. Their equivalent of ocean means a place too broad for sound to return.

Beyond the inhabited ledges lies such an ocean: the Great Absence, a plain so level and wide that settlements at its margin cannot detect its far boundary. It is not empty. It is packed with motionless fauna—millions upon millions of narrow-bodied creatures standing upright, each facing the same unknown direction. Their shells absorb vibration. No echo returns from among them. Travelers enter by keeping one hand on a guide cord stretched between stone stakes, but the cord extends only a short distance. Past the final knot there is no practical navigation.

At intervals across the Great Absence stand isolated towers, apparently built by hand from precisely stacked black flakes. Each tower is no taller than a dwelling, yet its shadow, when seen by thermal sense, descends rather than extending across the ground. Excavation beneath one revealed an identical tower upside down, then another below that. The diggers stopped after the seventh. Nothing suggested a foundation.

The underworld appears young. Its glass remains sharp, its fractures clean, its mineral faces unclouded. Yet everything living within it behaves as though the world is immeasurably old. This contradiction troubles its tool-users. Their simplest explanation is that formation occurred recently but lasted forever: the Still Below came into being in one prolonged instant from which it has never entirely escaped.

That may account for its deepest law.

Nothing here changes unless touched, and even touch changes less than it should. Tracks remain. Sounds sometimes remain too. In sealed hollows one can hear taps made before the listener was born, repeating at long intervals without diminishing. A slain animal may fall, but the warmth it displaces persists as a standing shape. A wall dismantled stone by stone leaves behind an acoustic wall that continues to reflect footsteps.

Because of this, construction is dangerous. To make a shelter is to add something nearly permanent to the world, including its absences. Builders work with ceremony and fear. Before setting a stone, they test not only where it will stand but where its remembered position may linger if it is later removed.

There are places ruined by too many former arrangements. One enters what appears to be an open chamber and collides with the echoes of vanished pillars. Paths overlap paths that no longer exist. Old walls divide new rooms without being visible or solid. Animals become trapped in the memory of enclosures. Settlements abandoned such sites, leaving behind tools embedded in no material surface and doorways that open only into earlier versions of the dark.

Most of the Still Below, however, rests in equilibrium. The gases do not circulate. The warm stone does not cool. Fauna draw tiny energies from one another and return almost exactly what they take. Birth is rare, death rarer, movement exceptional. The densely packed world holds itself like a breath that was never inhaled.

Its quiet is not emptiness. Listen long enough and it becomes populous: the occasional glassy click of a claspet tightening its grip; a distant tool tapping a question through fifty miles of rock; the nearly inaudible settling of a predator unfolding; the dry chime of shell against shell in a herd that may not have shifted since the cavern opened.

Above all of it is the unreachable surface, assumed rather than known.

Below it are further ceilings.

And below those, according to the deepest resonances, something vast enough to support the whole underworld—or to be mistaken for support because it has not yet moved.
