Beneath the salt plain is a chamber called the Warm Choir.

It is not a cavern in the ordinary sense. No river opened it, and no patient dissolution rounded its walls. The chamber was made abruptly when a buried mineral seam changed phase and contracted, leaving a hollow roughly the size of a cathedral transept. Its fractures are still young. Black splinters jut from the ceiling; shelves of rose-colored ore end in knife edges; the floor is divided into clean polygonal plates as if it cracked this morning.

Nothing falls from those edges. Nothing wears down.

The Warm Choir has remained exactly as it is for longer than the settlements above can account for.

The air is dry enough to preserve fingerprints. It tastes of copper, hot dust, ozone, and the bitter sweetness of machine insulation. Moisture is absent not merely as weather but as a category. No condensation forms on the pipes. No sweat survives on skin. Anything damp brought through the access shaft gives a faint hiss and becomes a lace of mineral salts before reaching the bottom.

The heat is steady and close. It comes neither from below nor from the machinery scattered through the chamber. Every surface—metal, stone, shell, bone—is equally warm. There are no cool corners and no thermal currents. Flags hang limp. Smoke, when made, gathers into compact gray shapes and remains where it was released.

The chamber is crowded.

Thousands of narrow mineral pillars fill it from floor to ceiling, some natural, some fabricated, most impossible to classify. They stand so closely that passage between them requires turning sideways. The natural columns are faceted basalt and translucent amber spar. The artificial ones are iron cylinders ribbed with ceramic collars, inspection plates, flywheels, relay boxes, toothed rims, and thick bundles of fabric-wrapped cable. Between the two are hybrid forms: crystals that terminate in screw threads, pipes that branch according to botanical laws, stone fins stamped with serial numbers deep inside their grain.

All are linked.

Chains sag between columns. Copper busbars bridge gaps. Black filaments grow from rock to machinery and from machinery back into rock. Their arrangement gives the impression of a factory assembled around a forest, then fossilized before anyone decided which part was meant to replace the other.

There are only a few recognizable structures. Near the access shaft stands a corrugated-metal booth with one cloudy window and a lever too large for a human hand. Farther in, a narrow gantry crosses above the columns, ending abruptly against an uncut wall. A brick enclosure surrounds three rotary engines, though no doorway was provided. White arrows have been painted on the floor at intervals, all pointing inward toward different centers.

The machines run.

A flywheel turns inside its cage without accelerating or slowing. Banks of hammers rise and strike bronze plates. Pistons work in open cylinders, polished rods vanishing into sealed stone. Relays chatter in cabinets whose cables have been severed. A conveyor belt carries seven identical black pellets around a tight loop and feeds them back into its own hopper. Every mechanism repeats a short action, and every action produces noise.

The noise is immense.

It is not chaotic. The same impacts occur in the same sequence: three iron blows, a long electrical growl, the clack of relays, a turbine’s rising shriek that never rises beyond one particular note, then a deep concussion felt through the teeth. Smaller sounds fill the intervals—scraping claws, dry wing cases, ceramic ticking, the whisper of grains moving inside sealed pipes.

After several minutes, the entire sequence repeats exactly.

A listener can use it as a clock, but it does not measure anything. The interval never gains or loses a fraction. The chamber has no perceptible day, season, decay, or accumulation. Dust disturbed by a boot hangs briefly, then returns grain by grain to its former place. A cracked object remains cracked, but the crack does not spread. Fuel gauges do not descend. Bearings turn without wearing. The seven pellets circulate, never chipped and never misplaced.

This permanence governs the animals as well.

They are abundant, packed into every recess of the Choir: ivory mites clustered on warm rivets, flat six-legged lizards wedged beneath cable trays, translucent moths resting on ceramic insulators, beetles with iron-colored wing cases marching along the chains. Blind creatures like jointed ribbons coil inside the hollow columns. Pale, long-eared mammals sit among the foundations, their mouths sealed by smooth skin; they seem to feed by pressing their bellies against vibrating panels.

None of them breed. None die. None grow.

Their movements are exact. At the first iron blow the moths open their wings. During the electrical growl the lizards cross from the western pipes to the eastern ones. When the turbine reaches its fixed high note, the pale mammals lift their heads. A storm of beetles then pours down one column, divides around a red warning lamp, and vanishes beneath the floor plates. At the concussion, all wings close.

Then the pattern begins again.

The animals are not mechanical. Opened specimens have warm organs, dry blood like red powder, nerves, stomachs, lungs. Yet each bears tiny signs of manufacture: a stamped numeral on the inner shell, a braided copper hair rooted in the spine, a ceramic bead behind an eye. Conversely, several motors contain chambers like hearts, and the insulation stripped from certain wires contracts when touched.

Vegetation survives sparsely in this congestion. Chalk-white stalks emerge from cracks near the engines, rarely more than a handspan tall. They have no leaves. Their skins are pleated like bellows, and their root systems wrap around electrical grounds rather than seeking moisture. When the relays chatter, the stalks expand; when the bronze plates are struck, they release a haze of metallic pollen.

The pollen never settles anywhere new. By the end of the cycle, every grain has returned to its original anther.

Deeper in the chamber, where the pillars nearly touch, grow three rust-red fans as broad as doors. They resemble fungi, except that their flesh is laminated mica and their undersides are covered in minute warm teeth. The animals avoid them. Each fan is connected by a rootlike cable to the same buried switchbox. The switchbox is padlocked. The key hangs beside it.

The key cannot be lifted.

Nor can the great lever in the booth be moved, though it rests loosely in its slot. Nothing is visibly restraining either object. Tools can break the booth, cut the lever, or excavate the wall around it, but after the next concussion the damage is gone and every fragment occupies its previous place. Living visitors are not restored in this way. Their footprints disappear; their thirst does not. A wound remains. Time works normally in them and nowhere else.

That is the central danger of the Warm Choir. It is stable, but it does not grant stability.

Those who enter become the only aging things in a world without change. Their breath and movements fall messily across the immaculate cycle. Their voices interfere with the fixed harmonics and return from the columns as unfamiliar sentences. If they remain for several repetitions, the fauna begin making allowances: a beetle route bends around a boot, a moth opens half a second late, one of the sealed-mouth mammals turns to watch.

These deviations vanish at the concussion. The animals resume their original paths.

But during the next cycle, another creature makes a different allowance.

After many hours, the chamber seems to be testing every possible response to the intruder, one at a time, while retaining none of what it learns. A lizard climbs the visitor’s clothing. A ribbon-creature spells a spiral around their feet. The hammers omit one blow, leaving a silence so abrupt that the ears ache. In one cycle only, all the pale mammals stand and point their closed faces toward the brick enclosure.

Inside that enclosure, the three rotary engines continue turning.

Between them is a fourth foundation, empty except for four fresh bolt holes. The holes are sharp-edged, without dust or corrosion. Their spacing matches no known motor. From beneath the foundation comes a regular pressure, not quite a vibration: the sensation of something repeatedly arriving against the underside of the floor.

It arrives at the same instant in every cycle.

Nothing progresses. Nothing emerges.

Above, the salt plain remains quiet, hard, and apparently lifeless. Only a few ventilation stacks mark the Choir’s location, each no taller than a person and capped with a rustless iron hood. Their mouths exhale no heat. If one kneels beside them, however, the machinery can be heard far below: hammer, growl, chatter, shriek, concussion.

Around the stacks stand sparse white stalks identical to those underground. They cast short shadows in the warm, dim air beneath the plain’s permanent dust veil.

At every concussion, all the stalks lean toward the vents.

Then they straighten again.
