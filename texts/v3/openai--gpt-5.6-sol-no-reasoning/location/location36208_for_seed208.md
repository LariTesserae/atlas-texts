The land lies low beneath a ceiling of slate-colored vapor, an immense shallow basin worn almost past the point of having features. Whatever mountains once enclosed it have been reduced to long ribs under the mud. Whatever rivers once crossed it have forgotten their channels. Water moves everywhere now, but seldom with direction: over flats, through porous stone, beneath thin mineral crusts, gathering in basins no deeper than a handspan and vanishing again into cracks polished by ages.

There are no plants.

Nothing rises green from the ground, no moss softens the rocks, and no roots hold the pale sediment together. The basin’s colors are those of old matter exposed to water: gray clay, black flint, chalk, rust, and the dim violet sheen of wet volcanic glass. In places, wide pavements of stone have weathered into fitted polygons. Rainwater stands in their seams, making each slab appear outlined in lead. Elsewhere, the surface is covered by rounded pebbles so anciently tumbled that they have become nearly identical—smooth, flat ovals spread for leagues like counters on a game board.

The light has no visible source. It leaks through the cloud layer as a weak, metallic dusk, enough to catch on wet surfaces but not enough to throw a clear shadow. Distance dissolves quickly. A low escarpment half a league away resembles a dark tide; a structure farther out might be a boulder until one comes near enough to see the stacked joints.

The world is never quiet.

The deafening sound comes first from the sinkholes. Across the basin, widely separated vents breathe through the rock. Water drains into some and is forced from others, according to pressures deep below. Each opening has its own voice: bass thunder, hollow drumming, a continuous mineral shriek. Together they make a vast dissonant chord that passes through the ground as much as through the air. Loose stones vibrate. Pools tremble in concentric rings even when there is no wind.

At intervals, the buried aquifers shift. Then an entire district bellows.

Water punches upward through old fissures in white, cold columns, hammering the flats with such force that speech becomes impossible. Vapor rolls outward close to the ground. Within minutes the eruption subsides, leaving new fans of gravel, collapsed crusts, and sheets of water sliding across terrain almost too level to carry them. The sheets meet opposing flows, wrinkle, and spread sideways. They may take days to find an outlet.

Rain adds a finer noise: constant ticking on stone, hissing on pools, rattling against the thousands of empty shells that collect along the margins.

For although the basin is without vegetation, it is crowded with animals.

Most are small and hard-bodied. They live from the mineral chemistry of the water and from dense microbial veils too thin and dark to resemble growth. Gray comb-creatures graze these invisible films from submerged rock, scraping in ranks. Needle-legged waders stride above them, their bodies little more than translucent bladders held between jointed stilts. When a pressure wave passes through the ground, whole colonies lift at once. Their glassy wings make a roar like thrown sand.

The pools are busy with pale, eyeless swimmers. They trail luminous feelers through the silt, tasting heat and dissolved metals. Some cluster around the weeping rocks, where deep water beads continuously from stone faces. Others follow the brief floods, crossing the flats in black, writhing fronts before the water drains away. Predators pursue them: broad, low things with shovel-shaped skulls, able to skate over mud by spreading their ribbed feet. They strike sideways and swallow prey in numbers rather than one at a time.

Larger animals are fewer but impossible to ignore. Shellbacks migrate between the permanent basins, walking in dispersed files through the rain. Each carries a resonant, bowl-shaped carapace several paces wide. Falling water drums on these shells, and the animals answer by altering chambers within them, producing notes that travel for miles. A herd approaching through fog sounds like an avalanche trapped underground.

No one knows whether this is communication, navigation, or merely an involuntary response to weather. Other creatures react to it. Swimmers bury themselves. Winged colonies rise. The shovel-headed hunters turn their faces toward the sound and wait.

Along slightly higher shelves stand the basin’s scattered works.

They are low structures, never towers: rings of fitted stone, roofless shelters, causeways raised only a little above the flood, and cairns built around holes that breathe warm mist. Their makers used no mortar. Every block was selected by hand, chipped with harder stone, and set so precisely that even after immense weathering the joints remain narrow. Bone wedges, flint hammers, fired-clay scoops, and short blades of hammered native copper can still be found under ledges. The copper is green-black and thin from repeated sharpening.

Some shelters are still occupied.

Their inhabitants remain close to the stonework during the loudest eruptions, communicating by touch, hand signs, and cords tied in patterns of knots. Voices are of little use here. They wear cloaks made from overlapping shell plates and boots wrapped in pale hide. They do not build fires in the open; fuel is scarce in a world without wood, and what can be burned must be rendered from animal oils or dried microbial sediment gathered from deep caves. Lamps are shallow stone cups with wicks of twisted gut. Their flames are small, blue, and carefully hooded from the wet air.

The basin people build chiefly to alter sound and water.

A wall may look useless until a vent begins to roar; then its curved face turns the concussion away from a sleeping hollow. Stone funnels collect condensation. Grooved tables separate edible silt from poisonous precipitate. Rows of upright slabs mark safe footing across mud that can liquefy without warning. Certain circular chambers are tuned to the shellbacks’ calls. When a herd passes, the chambers resonate, shaking clean water from the ceilings into waiting jars.

Nothing is built for permanence in the usual sense. Permanence belongs to erosion here, not resistance. Any object that projects too boldly is worn down, undermined, or shattered by pressure. The oldest constructions survive because they imitate the final forms of the land: low, rounded, hollow, and open to being crossed by water.

The inhabitants say the basin has already endured its ending.

According to their marks, the ridges were once sharp and the waters ran in narrow lines. There may even have been tall growth, though no clear representation of it remains—only repeated branching figures scratched into buried stones. Now the landscape has arrived at a condition so old that change rarely accumulates. Floods rearrange pebbles; the next floods return them. Pools fill with sediment and then open from below. Escarpments retreat, but so slowly that generations memorize the same cracks.

Time does not seem absent. It seems exhausted.

Yet the basin is active at every moment. Water surges and withdraws. Animals hatch in sudden multitudes, hunt, mate, shed shells, and die. Mineral skins bloom across pools overnight, bright as oil, then fracture into rafts and sink. Rain thickens to a roar and thins to suspended droplets. Fog crawls along the ground, revealing and concealing the sparse landmarks. Far-off vents begin new notes while old ones choke into silence.

After the heaviest rains, the entire plain becomes a shallow mirror. Only the stone rings, the backs of migrating animals, and occasional black ridges remain above it. The sky and ground join into one dim medium. Creatures appear to move through cloud beneath cloud, and the scattered human works seem less built upon the landscape than briefly caught in it.

Then drainage begins.

Across the mirror, countless whirlpools form. Their combined sucking joins the thunder underfoot. Shells clatter toward dark openings. Waders lean against the accelerating flow. People lash themselves to cairns and lift their tools overhead. By dawn, if dawn can be distinguished from any other hour, the water has fallen again, exposing the old polished flats.

Everything is wet. Everything is sounding.

And over the near-featureless distance, beneath the weak gray light, the basin resumes its long labor of becoming no younger and no more finished than it already is.
