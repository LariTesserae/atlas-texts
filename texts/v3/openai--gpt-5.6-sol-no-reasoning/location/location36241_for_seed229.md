The Wind Orchard occupies a hollow near the summit of a broad, dry mountain, no larger than a village and enclosed on three sides by low cliffs. From above it resembles a dark green bruise in the pale stone. Nothing flows into it. No springs rise there, no rain reaches it, and beneath the thin soil the mountain is solid salt-veined basalt.

Yet the hollow is lush.

The plants are not watered. They live on wind.

Tall, narrow trees stand far apart across the basin, each with a crown like an open hand. Their trunks are braided from gray fibers and hollow from root to crown. At dusk, warm air climbs through them, making the trees inhale. Their leaves—thick, black-green membranes stretched between ivory ribs—snap open and strain particles from the gale: dust, spores, insect scales, volcanic salts, scraps of skin, the fine flour of distant plains. Whatever the mountain air carries, the orchard eats.

There are no quiet leaves here. The foliage claps, cracks, rattles, and booms. Some crowns produce a low horn note when the wind enters them at the correct angle. Others chatter like teeth. The oldest trees have worn apertures in their trunks and sing in several tones at once. During the evening updraft the entire basin becomes an instrument audible from the foothills: a disorderly cathedral of bellows, drums, wooden cries, and long animal moans.

Between the trees grow dense islands of lesser vegetation separated by expanses of bare, ocher soil. Rope moss knots itself around stone but does not spread across the open ground. Fan-shaped shrubs turn continually into the wind. Pale bladder-vines inflate and roll from trunk to trunk, anchoring briefly with thorny hooks before the next gust tears them loose. In sheltered cracks are beds of crimson velvet, soft to the touch and warm from slow internal fermentation. The luxuriance occurs in clumps and towers rather than carpets. Every living patch is crowded with itself; between patches lies deliberate emptiness.

The basin floor has long since settled into shallow wind channels. Pebbles move along these grooves in restless files, polishing the basalt to a dull shine. The cliffs are rounded and undercut, not by water but by centuries of airborne grit and the scraping passage of migratory root masses. Salt blooms from shaded rock in brittle white antlers. They grow over decades, shiver apart in a single loud night, and begin again.

The air is warm even after sunset, but the light remains dim. A permanent canopy of high mineral haze covers the summit. The hidden sun appears as a tarnished copper disc, illuminating edges more readily than surfaces. Leaves flash briefly when they twist. Wings and shells glint, then disappear. Nothing in the hollow is evenly visible at once.

Its animals are numerous, though few remain in one place.

Flocks of six-legged gliders pour over the cliffs whenever the wind changes. Their translucent sails fill with dust, turning amber, green, or violet according to where they have traveled. They land by colliding with the trees and folding themselves around branches. Trunk-lizards chase them through the hollow cores, their claws ticking behind the deeper music of the wood. Fat, eyeless burrowers surface in bare spaces to swallow moving pebbles, digesting the films of microorganisms upon them before coughing the stones back into the wind.

Most conspicuous are the bell-throats: long-legged grazing animals no higher than a person’s knee, with inflated resonant sacs beneath their jaws. They browse on bladder-vines and call continuously to maintain their herds in the gloom. One herd may sound like a hundred clay pots struck with padded sticks. Predators imitate these notes imperfectly. The difference is subtle but absolute; when a false tone enters the chorus, the whole orchard erupts into running, flapping, snapping motion.

Even the plants respond. Shrubs flatten. Leaves reverse themselves. Vine-balls rip free and bound across the basin. For several moments the landscape seems to dismantle itself and flee.

Then it gathers again elsewhere.

Nothing here has a permanent arrangement. The trees migrate slowly by killing the windward half of their root systems and growing rapidly on the other side. A mature tree may cross the hollow in two human lifetimes, leaving a trail of fibrous pits. Shrub islands split, merge, and rotate. Colonies of velvet growth vanish beneath the soil when exposed and rise years later under a different rock. Animal nests are built to be abandoned. Paths are real only for an afternoon.

The few human structures obey the same expectation.

Along the northern cliff stand three low shelters made from fitted stone and stitched hide, each barely large enough for two sleepers. Their walls lean into the prevailing wind. Doorways are narrow and curved, and roof ropes pass through holes in the rock rather than being tied to stakes. No mortar is used; anything rigid eventually cracks. Farther downslope are the remains of older shelters—circles of polished stones, blackened hollows, a rack of weathered bone whose purpose has been forgotten.

The orchard-keepers visit in small bands during the season of strong ascent. They carry stone knives, bone awls, woven masks, and bronze hooks precious enough to be inherited by name. They do not cut living trees. Instead they collect shed membrane, fallen salt-antler, empty insect sails, and the resonant knots expelled from old trunks. These knots are used as horns, weather gauges, and voices for the dead.

Their ladders are woven rather than built. Their baskets have lids. Every tool is tied to the wrist.

Fire is possible but difficult. Open flame is snatched sideways and distributed in sparks, so cooking is done inside clay jars packed with heat-bearing stones. There is almost no drinkable liquid. The inhabitants obtain moisture from the bodies of waxy fruits and from soft nodules harvested inside the wind trees. To cut such a nodule without permission is considered worse than theft. A tree that loses too many can no longer strain the air; it begins to whistle thinly, then fills with dust and suffocates upright.

The place has one governing rule: anything unfastened belongs to the orchard.

This applies to tools, food, names spoken too loudly, the bodies of the dead, and sometimes living people. A sudden vertical gust can lift an adult from the ground and carry them into the canopy. The keepers do not immediately attempt rescue. They wait to see whether the trees release the person. Some are dropped within minutes, bruised and coated in pollen. Others are passed from crown to crown until they disappear into the haze. Days later their voice may be heard among the horn notes, though whether this is mimicry, haunting, or merely grief finding a pattern is not discussed.

The hollow is most turbulent near the turning of the light. Warm currents rise from the plains and cold upper winds descend from the hidden peaks. They meet inside the basin.

Then the orchard remakes itself.

Dust columns walk between the trees. Bladder-vines tumble overhead like a second, low sky. Gliders hatch in such numbers that branches bend under them. Burrowing animals pour from one hole and vanish into another. Salt antlers ring and explode. Whole shrub colonies crawl on hooked roots toward fresh shelter. The ground trembles—not violently, but with countless small decisions.

Amid this turmoil, there are intervals of peculiar balance. A wind tree may hold a single note. The bell-throats answer from the dimness. Every loose leaf aligns edge-on to the gale, and for one breath the roaring hollow becomes clear enough to hear a stone rolling along its ancient groove.

Then something shifts high above, beyond sight.

The note breaks. The animals cry out. The orchard tears loose once more, and its green, crowded islands begin their ceaseless migration across the dry mountain bowl.
