The town lies below the ordinary ground, in a broad limestone hollow roofed by stone and roots. It is not deep enough to feel abyssal. Warmth from the surface still reaches it, rain still finds it, and in a few places old shafts show a distant gray coin of daylight. Yet the place has its own weather. Moisture condenses on the ceiling, gathers along root tips, and falls in heavy drops. After storms above, water runs down every stair and wall. Gutters overflow, cisterns knock and fill, and the lowest passages become shallow canals.

Everything has long since been occupied by plants.

The original streets remain, but only as channels between masses of growth. Brick walls can be found by pressing a hand beneath moss, liverwort, and layers of fine white roots. Iron railings have become trellises as thick as hedges. Roofs are mattresses of fern and sedge, their drainage pipes wrapped in vines. The town’s masonry provides only the hidden skeleton. Its visible surfaces are bark, leaf, root felt, damp humus, and the dark green sheen of algae.

There is no uncultivated space. Every landing, lintel, pipe, and window recess carries some useful or tolerated growth. Old warehouses have become warm green chambers in which broad-leaved plants rise in ranks from flooded floors. Courtyards are packed with reed beds. Workshops sit inside woven shells of living willow. Even the lamps are held in cages of trained roots, which tighten by slow degrees around ceramic insulators without crushing them.

The place is small enough to cross in an hour, though the density of passages makes it feel larger. Its boundary is clear: a ring of old stone where the cavern narrows and the roots become too compact to penetrate. Within that ring, construction has accumulated vertically. Walkways cross over roofs; pipes run beneath gardens; ladders descend through former kitchens into pumping rooms. There are streets above streets, but none are open to the sky. What appears to be an alley may be the gap between two immense root buttresses. What appears to be a garden wall may conceal the side of a factory.

The central district surrounds a reservoir called the Basin, a black, rectangular body of water built into the deepest part of the hollow. Rainwater, seepage, and industrial runoff arrive here through separate channels. Stone partitions and dense beds of rushes divide it into stages. The water enters cloudy, then passes slowly through gravel, charcoal, root mats, and pale floating plants. By the far end it is clear enough to reveal tiled steps descending farther than the lamps illuminate.

No fish disturb it. No insects skate on its surface. There are no birds in the rafters, no rats in the mills, no frogs in the drainage trenches. Animal life is simply absent.

This does not make the town quiet. On the contrary, its loudness is continuous.

Pumps labor in vaults beneath the Basin, lifting water back through the upper neighborhoods. Belt drives slap and hum inside the factories. Turbines turn in narrow races. Condensation drums on metal roofs, water chatters through grates, and the old ventilation engines draw air through root-clogged shafts with a resonant, tidal roar. Each sound is steady enough to become part of the structure. A person may fail to notice the pumping hall until a machine is stopped; then the missing vibration feels like a floor giving way.

The machinery is heavy, practical, and old. Flywheels revolve behind iron mesh. Switchboards are faced with dark stone and glazed ceramic. Copper conductors run overhead on brackets, green with oxidation wherever the damp reaches them. Motors are rebuilt rather than replaced, and most bear layers of paint beneath films of algae. The mechanisms move slowly because speed is dangerous here. Fast machinery tears roots, bursts saturated soil, and throws moisture into electrical housings. Everything turns with deliberate torque: screw pumps, presses, rollers, cable drums, ventilator fans.

Factories produce modest things in great regularity. There are mills for pressing plant fiber into board, vats for pulping old roots, looms that weave cordage from stems, kilns that char woody waste into filter carbon, and presses that extract oils, resins, dyes, and starch. Nothing depends on animal products. Leather is replaced by laminated bark and waxed cloth. Glue comes from boiled tubers and resin. Grease is rendered from seeds. The resulting odors—sap, hot cellulose, wet stone, machine oil, fermentation—hang in the warm air and separate into districts.

The architecture was designed for water rather than against it. Door thresholds are high. Floors slope toward channels. Stairs have grooves worn down their centers by centuries of runoff. Walls are rounded at corners so that streams cannot undermine them. In the oldest quarter, erosion has softened bricks into shapes like loaves and hollowed handrails into smooth troughs. Mineral deposits build small white terraces beneath persistent leaks. These formations are not removed unless they block a passage. The town has learned that whatever water repeatedly attempts should usually be given a route.

Vegetation serves as both crop and infrastructure. Dense root beds brace unstable ceilings. Climbing stems bind masonry. Moss filters dust from ventilation currents. Succulents are planted around warm engines to draw humidity from the air, while ferns indicate leaking pipes long before a pressure gauge does. Curtains of leaves divide rooms and damp sudden drafts. Along certain corridors, roots have been trained into raised, resilient walking surfaces over the wet stone. They yield slightly underfoot and heal where they are cut.

There are rules for living construction. A load-bearing root may not be severed. New pipes must bend around established trunks. Structures are inspected by touch as much as by measurement: a healthy wall-root should feel cool, taut, and faintly springy. Any surface left bare must be planted within a season, because exposed mortar cracks quickly in the constant condensation. Open flame is confined to the kiln quarter, where vegetation is kept wet and low. Elsewhere, heat comes from insulated steam lines and electric coils.

Light is sparse and local. Lamps illuminate worktables, intersections, gauges, and stair treads, but little else. Their amber circles overlap only in the busiest streets. Beyond them, leaves absorb rather than reflect the light, so every planted doorway seems deep. Pale fungi would be an obvious presence in such dampness, but the town is too meticulously managed for them to dominate; molds are scrubbed from food chambers and encouraged only in sealed compost vaults. The greenery remains the main darkness—layer upon layer of leaves, from glossy black-green to the washed gray of submerged stems.

Day and night are weak concepts here. Surface light enters through shafts, but clouds and foliage reduce it to a gradual brightening. Time is marked more reliably by shifts of machinery, reservoir levels, and the slow response to rain. Several hours after a storm begins above, the ceiling starts to weep. Later the wall channels quicken. Later still, the pumps deepen their note as the Basin rises. The whole town receives weather as a delayed message.

Change is measured over years. A stairway narrows as roots thicken on both sides. A workshop relocates when a supporting trunk claims its floor. A canal fills with silt and becomes a reed bed; a maintenance passage beside it becomes the new canal. Mineral crust seals an old leak while water discovers another route a few meters away. The settlement does not expand much. It rearranges itself inside its stone boundary, patiently exchanging room between industry, water, and growth.

Warm air pools in the lower quarters. It carries a permanent mist that beads on skin and tools. Clothing never becomes entirely dry. Paper curls unless waxed; wood swells unless seasoned in the hot galleries near the kilns. Metal rusts rapidly, and so every exposed fitting is painted, oiled, wrapped, or deliberately allowed to corrode to a stable surface. Nothing here can be merely installed and forgotten. Maintenance is not an interruption of life but its central rhythm.

At the outer edge of town, where the cavern roof slopes down, the buildings become low and embedded. Workshops merge with root banks. Pipes disappear beneath moss. The final lanes end at green walls dense enough to seem solid, watered from within by countless fine seeps. If one parts the leaves, there is no wilderness beyond—only stone, packed soil, and the intertwined foundations of the settlement.

The town is enclosed, crowded, and loud, but not frantic. Its machines turn; its water circulates; its plants occupy every available thickness of air. Nothing moves quickly except the occasional overflow after rain. The rest is a settled negotiation, old enough that no one distinguishes clearly between the town and the growth covering it. The stone holds the hollow. The roots hold the stone. The pumps keep the roots from drowning. Water wears every part of the arrangement, and every part has learned the shape of that wearing.
