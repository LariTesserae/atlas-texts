The place occupies a shallow saddle near the summit of a young mountain, no larger than a secluded grove. Black ridges rise around it in a broken ring. Their faces are sharp, unworn, and faintly glassy, as though lifted from the earth only yesterday. Yet nothing here appears unfinished. The fractures have all reached exactly the depth they require. Every loosened stone has already found its final resting place.

Cold water lies just beneath the ground.

It darkens the thin soil and wells up wherever roots or crystal seams have opened a path. There is no central pool, only scattered manifestations: a lens of water between two slabs, a trembling skin over moss, droplets suspended under fern fronds, narrow runnels descending the rock without ever arriving below. The cliffs weep continually. Their tears hang at regular intervals, each bead swelling to the same size, falling the same distance, and vanishing into the same patch of saturated earth.

Mist fills the saddle to the height of a person’s chest. Above it, the peaks stand in a dim blue twilight. Below, stems disappear into milk-colored obscurity. The air condenses on fur, bark, feathers, and bare stone, but the wetness never accumulates. It is a damp place held in perfect balance: always receiving water, never flooding; always draining, never drying.

The vegetation is lush but widely spaced. Great cushions of black-green moss occupy the hollows like sleeping animals. Pale ferns rise alone from the mist, each several paces from the next, their fronds broad, wet, and intricately divided. Low conifers cling to cracks in the ridge, twisted less by wind than by some fixed idea of wind. Their needles are almost silver in the poor light. Between them grow translucent stalks bearing clusters of blue bladders. These pulse with no visible rhythm, contracting and swelling while remaining, somehow, the same size.

There are flowers, though they resemble apertures more than blossoms. Each is a dark circular opening edged with soft white petals. Looking into one reveals not pollen or stamens but a moving assembly line passing through immeasurable depth: belts, pistons, hanging hooks, incandescent vats, toothed wheels as tall as cliffs. No two flowers show the same section of the works. Put an ear near one and the saddle’s underlying thunder becomes momentarily distinct—the repeated strike of presses, the whine of bearings, the heavy rolling of metal sheets.

Nothing constructed stands in the landscape. There are no roads, cables, chimneys, housings, ruins, or discarded tools. Nevertheless, the entire place is industrial.

The machinery has no location. Its vibrations pass through the mountain without shaking so much as a grain of soil. Engine noise issues from wet stone. A fern unfolding produces the hiss of a pneumatic valve. Water running beneath the moss clanks with the cadence of chain conveyors. From the mist comes the synchronized roar of furnaces drawing breath, although the air stays cool and smells only of rain, iron, crushed leaves, and a faint mineral ozone.

At intervals—intervals that do not pass, but are simply present—the surrounding rocks emit a deep metallic impact. It is loud enough to be felt in teeth and ribs. Birds rise at the sound. They are always rising at the sound.

Hundreds inhabit the small saddle, despite the spaciousness of its vegetation. Slate-backed thrushes stand on isolated stones, wings half-open. Long-tailed white birds hang above the marshy ground as if caught at the top of a wingbeat. Tiny red finches crowd the high branches while leaving impossible stretches of empty branch between each body. Their calls overlap into a shrill mesh, repetitive but not rhythmic. Each call is heard in full. None begins or ends.

Amphibians occupy every damp crevice. Their skin is deep green and patterned with markings like rivet heads. They inflate their throats, and from each comes the sound of a distant motor turning under load. Snails with iron-gray shells graze the rocks. Where they pass, they leave trails of clear water rather than slime, and within each trail minute sparks travel steadily uphill.

Large animals are present only in fragments of view. Antlers move behind the mist without heads beneath them. A dark, six-legged mountain goat appears between two firs, but only the spaces between its legs can be seen; its body is an absence shaped by rain. There are broad pawprints in the moss, always filling with water, though nothing ever makes them. Schools of thin silver fish swim through the air near the cliff face. They turn around invisible obstacles and flash like loose blades. Their movement produces the dry ticking of cooling metal.

Predation occurs, but not as an event. A pale hawk is fixed in a descending curve above the saddle. Beneath it, a hare is fixed in the act of turning. The distance between them is full of feathers, fur, fear, pursuit, impact, feeding, and renewed pursuit—all states occupying the same narrow reach of air. Neither animal grows hungry nor escapes. Both are alive. Both have already died. The ecology does not cycle; it exists as a completed diagram.

This is the governing law of the saddle: processes are permitted, change is not.

Water falls, but no pool rises. Roots grow, but no plant enlarges. Young volcanic stone remains young without weathering. Animals feed without consuming and breed without multiplying. Moss repairs every footprint at precisely the rate the foot depresses it. Mist drifts across the enclosure while its every fold remains in the same place. Sound travels, yet never fades. If a pebble is dislodged, it may tumble forever down a slope only three paces long, striking the same buried ledges in an endless sequence without approaching the bottom.

The apparent machinery obeys the same rule. It operates at immense capacity and produces nothing that was not already here. Its furnaces burn without heat. Its presses descend without crushing. Its unseen turbines turn, driving water through roots and veins, lifting mist, articulating wings, pumping the blue bladders on their stems. Everything is production; there is no product. Everything is powered; no fuel is spent.

Near the center of the saddle stands its only truly open space, a patch of shallow black water perhaps ten steps across. Sparse reeds grow around it. The surface reflects neither sky nor mountains. It reflects an interior ceiling: steel beams fading into sooty distance, lamps glowing dull orange, suspended pipes trembling with pressure. The reflection is loud. When the pond is approached, the unseen factory floor seems to approach from below, and the water acquires depth—not downward, but sideways, opening into miles of wet industrial dusk.

Fish occasionally break its surface. For a moment their bodies are made of polished brass, stamped with serial numbers in an alphabet that cannot be looked at directly. Then they enter the cool air and become flesh. Before falling back, they split into multiple versions of themselves: egg, larva, adult, skeleton, fossil, machine component. All strike the water at once, making a single round ripple that expands indefinitely without becoming larger.

No sun crosses this place. The light is dim and uniform, as if filtered through raincloud and deep water together. Droplets catch a weak pewter gleam. The palest things—fungi under roots, bellies of hovering fish, the eyes of frogs—seem illuminated from a neighboring reality. There are shadows, but they do not correspond consistently to bodies. Some shadows resemble gears. Some resemble workers bending at stations. None of the workers themselves can be seen.

The boundary of the saddle is easy to recognize. It is where the fresh ridges lean inward and the industrial roar becomes perfectly intelligible for one step. At that line, every animal turns its head. Every flower-aperture widens. The water on the rocks pauses halfway through falling.

Beyond the ridge lies ordinary high country: cloud, scree, wind, and ravines descending toward forests. It can be seen clearly. But leaving is difficult, because the moment before departure has become one of the saddle’s permanent processes. A traveler may lift a foot over the black edge, may feel warmer air touch the face, may even see loose gravel skipping downhill outside.

The lifted foot does not come down.

Behind, the small wet grove continues its tremendous labor. Birds call, engines strain, frogs pulse, presses fall, and the mountain weeps into soil that has been saturated forever. Nothing deteriorates. Nothing concludes. In the cold dimness, the whole landscape runs flawlessly, manufacturing the same instant without end.
