At the summit of a young mountain, enclosed by a rim of black stone, there is a wet garden of machinery.

The place is no larger than a hamlet. From the valleys below it appears almost impossible: a dark notch near the mountain’s needle-point, sometimes flying a white banner of cloud. Only when one reaches the final ridge does the interior reveal itself—a steep-walled basin sunk into the summit, warm, green, and full of water. Its boundaries are immediate. Sheer rock rises on three sides; on the fourth, the basin ends at a precipice where runoff escapes through iron grilles and falls for more than a kilometer without touching anything.

The mountain is geologically new. Its faces are not rounded or settled but split into blades, columns, and glossy fractures. Basalt ribs thrust upward through the vegetation. Pale mineral seams look freshly opened, showing blue-gray, rust-red, and sulfurous yellow beneath the dark skin of the rock. Scree has not yet become soil. Stones still retain corners sharp enough to cut cloth, and after rain the cliffs shed small fragments with brittle, ceramic clicks.

Yet the summit basin is lush. Warm groundwater rises through fissures and keeps the shallow soil wet. Moss blankets the safer slopes in thick green cushions. Ferns crowd every shaded seam, their fronds overlapping like wet feathers. There are dwarf trees with broad, waxy leaves, their roots gripping pipes and stones indiscriminately. Creepers cross from rock to gantry, tying the industrial remains to the living walls. In the lowest hollows, stands of copper-colored reeds emerge from opaque pools.

The atmosphere condenses on everything. Droplets gather beneath railings, cables, leaves, valve wheels, and the lower lips of cliffs. The rocks weep steadily even after weeks without rain. Water travels down them in delicate threads, disappears into moss, then emerges elsewhere warm and faintly metallic. Puddles never quite clear. Their surfaces carry films of iron red, mineral white, and occasional rainbow traces from lubricants too old to identify.

Across this cramped basin runs a pumping works.

It is not a single factory so much as a scattered machine fitted directly into the mountain: three squat turbine houses, a ladder of settling tanks, covered conduits, rusting hoists, and a narrow-gauge rail line that begins at a sealed tunnel and ends abruptly above the abyss. The structures are small, because there is no room for grandeur. Every shed is wedged against a cliff. Every platform has been shaped around some obstruction of stone. Stairs double back steeply, passing beneath pipes and between tree trunks. In places one must turn sideways to proceed.

The oldest walls are built from local black rock, fitted with unusual precision. Newer sections are steel and poured concrete, though “newer” here may still mean generations old. Orange corrosion blooms around bolts. Electrical insulators of green ceramic stand along the ridgeline, clean and bright under rain. Thick cables descend into the basin, humming softly enough to be felt through wet handrails.

Some part of the works is still operating.

Deep underfoot, pumps turn at a grave, unhurried rate. Each stroke travels through the summit as a long vibration: a gathering pressure, a heavy impact, then a diminishing shudder among the pipes. The rhythm is slow enough that visitors initially mistake it for irregular thunder. After an hour, its order becomes undeniable.

Above that pulse lies constant noise. Water rings against metal roofs. Overflow channels roar beneath grates. High winds scrape the outer cliffs and force themselves through cracks in the buildings, producing low organ tones. Loose sheet metal worries at its fastenings. A turbine, hidden in one of the lower vaults, gives off a continuous hard whine that rises whenever the summit pools fill. Conversation is possible only at close range.

Nothing moves quickly despite the sound. Wheels turn by degrees. Counterweights descend over days. Reservoir levels rise and fall no faster than the growth of moss along their depth marks. A bank of enormous gauges occupies one sheltered wall, and their needles scarcely shift within a human visit. The whole installation seems less like a busy factory than a single industrial action being performed with immense patience.

Its purpose is obscure until one reaches the eastern brink.

There, water from the basin is collected in a narrow stone throat and passed through filters of gravel, roots, and tarnished mesh. It enters a row of pressure vessels sunk into the cliff. From these vessels, pipes vanish vertically downward through the mountain. No outflow can be seen. On still days, however, warm vapor rises from cracks thousands of meters below, and distant forests along the lower slopes remain green even through the dry season.

The machinery may be feeding the mountain, rather than draining it.

Old maintenance plates call different chambers by the names of organs: Upper Lung, West Kidney, Second Heart. The labels might be workers’ humor, but the arrangement reinforces it. Pipes branch like arteries. Tanks swell and contract with changes in pressure. Inspection hatches exhale damp mineral heat. Near the central pump house, the rock itself is warm enough to keep pale orchids flowering throughout the year.

The basin’s few animals are easy to miss. Small black frogs live inside drain mouths, emerging only during heavy rain. Colorless moths collect around the amber lamps in the turbine shed. Once or twice a day, a mountain swift cuts through the basin at tremendous speed, vanishes over the rim, and leaves the vegetation shaking in its wake. There are no large grazers, no fish in the pools, and almost no insect chorus. The lushness therefore feels oddly unconsumed. Leaves accumulate without being chewed. Fruit ripens, darkens, and drops untouched into the water.

Human traces are scattered and intimate. A narrow bunkhouse leans beside the upper cistern, containing six iron bedsteads and a stove. Waterproof coats, stiff with age, still hang from numbered hooks. Chalk calculations remain beneath the control room windows, repeatedly revised until the figures become a pale fog. Someone planted herbs in cut-down oil drums: mint survives; rosemary has become a woody thicket. On a high catwalk hangs a small bell, green with corrosion. It is connected neither to a clock nor an alarm circuit. Wind cannot reach it, yet it sometimes rings once between pump strokes.

Paths through the basin obey the machinery rather than the terrain. Red-painted rails indicate surfaces safe to touch. White stones mark ground that may collapse into warm cavities. The locals of the lower valleys—when they speak of the summit at all—say one must never close a valve entirely, never sleep while facing downhill, and never drink water that has passed through a pipe. Rainwater is harmless. Spring water is usually harmless. Pipe water, they insist, has already been assigned somewhere else.

Weather arrives abruptly at this elevation. Sunlight can be clear and ordinary one moment, bringing out every shade of wet green and rust; then cloud spills over the rim and reduces the world to one room. During these intervals the basin loses its precipice and seems enclosed on all sides by luminous gray walls. Moisture beads on eyelashes. Lamps glow in the daytime. The machinery’s sound becomes close and bodiless.

When the cloud withdraws, the scale of the mountain returns all at once. Beyond the railings there is open air, a gulf of blue distance, and ranges folded below ranges. The summit garden, so crowded within itself, becomes a tiny wet pocket suspended above an immense dry world. Its overflowing plants lean outward over the abyss. Water streams from their roots and breaks immediately into mist.

At evening the turbine houses illuminate one by one. Their windows are not bright—only a muted amber among ferns and black stone—but the electricity is steady. Condensation shines on the pipes. Needle-thin waterfalls catch the last daylight beyond the grilles. Far below, clouds turn pink while the basin has already entered shadow.

Then the deep pump completes another stroke.

The summit trembles. Reeds bow over the pools. Drops fall from every cable at once, as though shaken loose by a shared decision. Somewhere inside the mountain, a vast quantity of water is moved a little farther along a route no one can see. Afterward the place resumes its loud stillness: engines humming, cliffs weeping, vegetation closing patiently around the iron.
