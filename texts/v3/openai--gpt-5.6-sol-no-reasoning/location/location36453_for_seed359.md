The enclosure is called the Low Garden, though there is no high garden and nothing here has ever grown from soil.

It occupies a shallow octagonal basin perhaps two hundred paces across. Its rim rises no higher than a house, enough to interrupt the horizon and make the place feel private. Every surface is constructed. The ground consists of fitted slabs: cream ceramic, black glass, porous brick, hammered copper, and a pale substance resembling bone but retaining the warmth of sun-fired clay. There is no natural substrate beneath them. Lift one slab and another lies below, then a course of little vaults, then further floors diminishing into darkness. Roots descend through prepared holes only to find more architecture.

The basin is warm and dry. No channel, well, dew trap, or buried reservoir exists. Water is not scarce here; it is absent as a category. The glazed troughs bear no mineral trace of it. Vessels shaped like cups are used for shade, music, incubation, or measures of time. Thirst, among the animals, is answered by other means.

The garden is lush nevertheless.

Its vegetation crowds nearly every seam. There are fans of pleated copper leaf, low thickets of green-black rods, scarlet bladders on braided stems, and trees built around stacks of hollow ceramic vertebrae. Their tissues contain waxes, oils, mobile salts, and slow-pulsing gels that do not evaporate in the heat. Some draw nourishment from differences in temperature between adjacent tiles. Others push hair-fine filaments into metal and feed on tarnish. Mat colonies digest glaze a century at a time. A grove of squat, ocher pillars survives on pressure: the footsteps of passing creatures compress chambers beneath them, and each compression drives a minute ration of sap upward.

Nothing has roots in the familiar sense. Plants fasten themselves with clamps, hooks, suction disks, knots, or tiny masonry. One species lays bricks around its own base as it grows. Another unthreads floor mosaics and reassembles the pieces into a trellis behind it. The oldest vines seem handmade because they are: their new shoots are pinched, folded, and tied by the previous season’s leaves, which remain animate for months after falling.

Growth is thickest toward the center, where the basin’s floor descends by three broad steps. Here the vegetation forms walls, aisles, compartments, and low ceilings. It is possible to cross the entire garden without seeing more than six paces ahead. Warm leaf-surfaces brush both shoulders. Pods hang at eye level in clustered ranks. The density feels less like wilderness than storage—an overfilled workshop in which every tool has bred.

Yet much of the basin is newly made.

Edges remain keen. Chisel marks are fresh in the retaining walls. Statues lack weathering. Corners that ought to have been softened by generations are sharp enough to cut skin. The copper has only begun to darken, and fine red dust still gathers beneath drilled holes. No erosion acts uniformly here. A staircase may be ancient at its top and newly quarried at its foot, despite being made from one continuous block. Cracks terminate in midair. Fragments lie beside objects that are not yet broken.

The builders are present, though no one agrees which creatures they are.

Small six-legged animals travel the galleries in patient files. Their bodies are wrapped in overlapping scales of fired clay, each scale stamped with a simple mark. They carry splinters, seeds, wedges, and lengths of fiber. At intervals they stop to enlarge the garden using stone hammers held between their front limbs. They know leverage, abrasion, binding, kilns, and the geometry of arches. They do not know wheels, or perhaps dislike them. A rolling object may move in a direction unrelated to the slope, making wheels unreliable.

Above them, broad-winged things rest beneath the artificial trees. Their wings are thin wooden frames covered in translucent skin. When folded, they resemble shutters. They feed by settling over warm tiles and sealing their bodies against the surface, absorbing heat and metallic vapors. Their droppings are tiny cold beads—not liquid, not solid, but pockets from which warmth has been removed. Gardeners collect these beads in felt-lined baskets and bury them around heat-sensitive sprouts.

There are also ribbon-bodied grazers, ivory mites that polish the walls, burrowing masks with no visible animal behind them, and herds of knee-high tripods. The tripods have soft gray hides stretched over cane skeletons. They migrate around a single circular path, completing one circuit every thirteen days. Nothing blocks them. If a wall is erected across the route, they emerge from its farther face without damaging it.

Most of the garden’s life is quiet. There is no running water, no wind in the sealed basin, and little birdsong. The prevailing sound is contact: claws ticking on tile, dry leaves rubbing, knots tightening, the occasional faint knock of a hand tool. Pods open without a pop. Predators kill without cries. Even falling stones seem to lower themselves for the final finger-width.

Sound does not always remain where it is made. A hammer blow in the western gallery may be heard hours later under the central trees. Footsteps sometimes precede the animal responsible. In one colonnade, silence accumulates visibly as a fine blue coating. Scraping it away releases old noises all at once—wingbeats, breathing, a dropped chisel, one brief laugh in a voice no living species can produce. For this reason the colonnade is cleaned only on appointed days.

The light is ordinary enough to be disturbing.

It has the color and strength of late morning, casting clear shadows toward the basin’s eastern wall. There is no sun. The open sky above is a flat depth of warm gray, and nothing crosses it. Shadows remain consistent for weeks, then rotate by a few degrees during a single quiet night. Some objects possess more than one. A white stone near the southern rim casts the shadow of a kneeling person, and that shadow tends nearby plants with careful finger movements. Seeds placed within reach of it are sorted into viable and unviable piles by dawn.

The garden’s people—if the clay-scaled tool users deserve that word—have built their settlements into the enclosure’s retaining wall. Each dwelling is a niche closed by a woven mat. Inside are sleeping shelves, dry kilns, tool stones, racks of edible wax, and ancestral tablets impressed with tracks. No flame is used. Heat is borrowed from black tiles and carried indoors in thick-walled jars. Meals consist of sliced plant resins, mineral foams, shed wing membrane, and pale fungal wafers that grow on copper without moisture.

Their technology is intimate and exact. They shape materials by tapping, rubbing, scoring, folding, and waiting. A doorway may take three generations to cut. A living hinge can be trained in six years. They mend broken walls with a mortar made from dust and animal breath. Their greatest machines have no moving parts: chambers whose proportions persuade gravity to lean slightly sideways, ovens that fire clay with yesterday’s heat, and racks that make two identical objects exchange their damage.

Nothing in the Low Garden is mined from elsewhere. There is no elsewhere that can be reliably reached.

The eight gates in the rim all open back into the basin. Each returns by a different route. The northern gate leads through a corridor lined with carvings and emerges at the west wall after twenty minutes. The bronze gate returns the traveler immediately, but older. A low door behind the pillar grove opens into the same garden before its vegetation was planted; entry is forbidden because footprints left there appear as pits in the present floor. The largest gate shows a salt-white plain under black stars, but anything passing through it enters the garden again from above and falls gently into the foliage.

Thus the enclosure supplies its own raw material by revision. New masonry appears along walls that have been patiently measured, drawn, and named. A tile unobserved for long enough may become unfired clay. Broken tools are left in designated niches, where they gradually turn into smaller, more specialized tools. The dead are placed beneath blank floor panels. Years later those panels acquire doors.

Change is slow, local, and difficult to distinguish from error. A ceramic tree adds one ring every few decades. The tripod herds wear a groove into the circular path, though the groove sometimes migrates away from the path entirely. Species adapt not only to heat and food but to exceptions in causality. Certain mites live exclusively on effects that have lost their causes. A narrow purple vine flowers wherever something almost happened. Predators wait beside repeated mistakes.

Despite this, the place does not feel chaotic. It has customs deeper than consistency.

No object may remain wholly useless. No empty space stays empty indefinitely. Every edge invites a fastening; every fastening becomes habitat. Things made alike begin to share properties. Things named too precisely disappear behind their names. Damage travels more readily than matter. Time prefers corridors but dislikes stairs. Gravity is strongest beneath sleeping creatures. The garden observes these rules with the serenity of climate.

Near the center stands its densest structure: a small roofless pavilion entirely engulfed by living growth. Its columns are bundled reeds cast in bronze; its floor is a mosaic depicting the Low Garden in minute detail. Looking closely, one can find the pavilion represented within the mosaic, and within that pavilion another mosaic, continuing past the power of any lens. The tiny gardens are not identical. Fauna move in some. Fires burn in others. One is flooded with a bright substance for which the inhabitants have no word.

At noon—or at what the fixed light permits them to call noon—the garden’s animals gather around this image. The winged shutters cling to columns. Mites emerge from carvings. Tripods pause along their endless circuit. Clay-scaled builders set down their hammers. Even the plants rotate their leaves toward the mosaic.

For several minutes, nothing makes a sound.

Then, from immeasurably far below, through layer after layer of depicted gardens, comes one soft tap.

A little later, somewhere in the basin, a new corner appears.
