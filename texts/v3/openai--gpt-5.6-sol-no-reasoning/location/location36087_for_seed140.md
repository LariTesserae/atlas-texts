The place is a high basin no wider than a village, scooped from the summit of an immeasurably old plateau. Once there were mountains here. Time has worn them down to broad shoulders and low, rounded shelves, leaving this shallow hollow cupped above the clouds.

No light enters it.

The darkness is not merely night, nor the shadow of an overhang. Above the basin, the sky is permanently sealed by warm black cloud, while the rock itself seems to swallow every stray photon. Lamps can be carried in, but their beams shorten quickly, reddening and fading after a few paces. Vision becomes intimate: a wet leaf, a hand, the silver bead of water at the end of a root. Everything beyond is inferred from sound and touch.

The air is warm and saturated. Moisture condenses continually upon skin, bark, metal, and stone, though rain falls only at long intervals. Water is everywhere without gathering into anything as decisive as a lake. It occupies seams. It films the rock. It hangs in moss and in the deep cups of leaves. Beneath the thin soil, it moves through old channels with a pressure one can feel through bare feet.

The floor of the basin is a single ancient surface, softly concave and almost level. Erosion has passed beyond violence here. All edges have been negotiated away. The exposed stone is dark, fine-grained, and smooth as bone handled for generations. Shallow runnels cross it in branching patterns, but these too have become broad and gentle, carrying water at the speed of oil. In some places the plateau’s original mineral bands have emerged as pale curves, the remnants of formations older than the surrounding terrain. They make the ground resemble a map whose country no longer exists.

Vegetation grows lushly wherever there is purchase, yet the basin retains a curious emptiness. There is no continuous forest. Instead, immense distances—twenty paces, fifty—separate individual concentrations of life. A tree stands alone in each pocket of soil, spreading low and wide rather than rising, its leaves heavy, black-green, and warm to the touch. Ferns collect around weeping rock faces. Pillows of moss occupy depressions. Ropes of pale vine descend into cracks and vanish into the water below.

Between these islands lie open expanses of bare stone.

The emptiness is partly engineered. Each grove appears to respect an invisible boundary. Roots approach certain lines and turn aside. Spores carried across them fail to germinate. Fallen leaves decompose rapidly on one side and remain intact for years on the other. These boundaries partition the basin into rooms without walls: humid garden, naked pavement, reed hollow, fungal court, and the central clearing.

Only the animals cross freely.

They are abundant, though seldom seen whole. Their calls fill the darkness. Clicking colonies live in holes under the stone and coordinate in waves, thousands of small hard bodies striking in sequences that pass from one edge of the basin to the other. Broad-winged creatures move overhead, navigating by pulses too low to hear directly; each pulse shudders in the ribs and releases beads of water from the leaves. Tree-dwelling animals whistle with the pitch and regularity of kettles. Frogs—or things occupying the ecological place of frogs—produce deep wooden knocks from the water seams.

The result is loud almost without pause. Darkness turns the basin into an instrument. Stone carries every impact. Underground water booms in distant chambers. Wings slap moisture from the air. At intervals, one solitary animal issues a long descending cry, and dozens of species become silent to listen. Then the clicking begins again.

Near the basin’s rim stand the remains of seven structures.

They are small, each scarcely larger than a sleeping shelter, and set so far apart that one cannot perceive two at once. Their shells are seamless and dull, neither metal nor ceramic, with doorways worn low and rounded by an age of entry. Vines grow across some but not others. Inside, the walls remain dry despite the saturated atmosphere.

The structures possess no visible controls. Their function appears only through effects. One turns sound aside, leaving a person within it able to hear events occurring on the opposite side of the basin while nearby calls vanish. Another keeps a single shallow pool suspended vertically against its inner wall; tiny translucent animals swim in it, indifferent to gravity. A third contains a depression that produces fresh soil at a rate of one finger’s depth every century. Roots wait around its threshold.

The largest structure stands in the central clearing, though “largest” means only the size of a modest chapel. It has sunk halfway into the plateau. Its roof is open to the black sky, and its interior contains a circular shaft filled not with water but with a denser, colder darkness. Objects lowered into it return wet and fractionally older. Seeds come back sprouted. Fruit returns soft. Polished metal develops a patina. Living hands feel the passage as exhaustion, then hunger, then the calm after hunger.

No one knows whether the structures maintain the basin or merely observe it. They emit no heat and consume no apparent fuel. Yet the invisible garden boundaries converge upon them, and beneath each is a slow vibration identical to the rhythm of the subterranean water.

Change here is difficult to witness. Growth proceeds, but patiently. A new leaf may take a month to unfold. Water requires days to cross a slab. Animals live active lives within this slowness, feeding and mating and calling, but the larger ecology seems to breathe on another scale. Every few decades, condensation thickens into rain. For several continuous weeks, water descends through the darkness in warm, heavy drops. The runnels fill. The ground’s pale mineral lines gleam faintly—not by reflected light, but with a residual radiance too weak for sight and detectable only as color behind closed eyelids.

During these rains the animals gather in the empty spaces. They emerge from cracks, trees, shafts, and roofless structures until the bare pavement is crowded with unseen bodies. Their calls combine into a roar that can be heard from the valleys below, where it is mistaken for thunder trapped inside the mountain.

Then the rains cease. Water sinks back toward the hidden channels. The creatures disperse. The basin returns to its spacious arrangement: lone trees, isolated gardens, smooth stone, seven scattered rooms, and darkness laid evenly over all of it.

Nothing in the place seems unfinished. Even decay has become stable. The ruins do not collapse. The plateau no longer sharpens or breaks. Dead wood softens into soil exactly where soil is permitted, and nowhere else. The basin has not escaped time; rather, it has traveled so far into age that time has ceased to behave like damage.

It is less a wilderness than a final condition—warm, wet, crowded with voices, and almost empty of forms.
