At the top of a mountain range, where the peaks have narrowed to fins and splintered teeth, there is a hollow no wider than a village green.

It lies almost impossibly high. On every side, the land falls away at once: cliffs descend through streaming cloud, and valleys appear only in brief openings thousands of feet below. Yet the hollow itself is low and cupped, an old bowl worn into the summit rock. Its enclosing walls shelter it from the worst of the wind. Seen from the surrounding ridges, it resembles a dark thumbprint pressed into the pale crown of the mountain.

Water gathers here.

It does not form a lake. The basin is too shallow, too fractured, and too densely lined with old turf. Instead, water travels everywhere just beneath the surface. Each step darkens the moss. Clear beads swell along roots and vanish again. Runnels thread between flat stones, divide, rejoin, and pour over the cliff in dozens of narrow falls. After rain, the whole hollow seems to weep. Even in dry weather, mist condenses on the cold walls and keeps them glossy.

The terrain has three levels. The lowest is a marshy floor of black peat, sedge, and ankle-deep pools. Around it rises a broken ring of stone shelves, broad enough in places for a person to walk. Above those stand the enclosing crags, weathered into rounded pillars and deep vertical grooves. This is not raw or newly shattered country. Every crack has been tested by centuries of frost. Loose material has already gone over the edge. What remains is stable in the mountain’s severe sense of stability: worn, seated, and unlikely to move except during the rare great thaw.

Nothing is crowded. Despite the wetness, the basin feels empty.

Plants occur in isolated societies with bare space between them. A cushion of alpine flowers inhabits one stone and no other. A miniature grove of crooked mountain ash occupies a sheltered cleft, seven trees in all, their pale trunks bent in the same direction. Elsewhere, tough grasses grow in circular mats around seeps. Broad, dark leaves emerge from the peat like wet leather. Lichen maps the rock in rust, silver, and sulphur-yellow, but even these colonies stop at clean boundaries, leaving expanses of bare grey stone.

The openness makes each living thing conspicuous. A single red blossom can be seen across the whole basin. So can a grazing hare, a shed feather, or the white bones of a small animal caught among the reeds.

There are many animals, though few remain visible for long.

Cloud-swifts nest in holes in the outer cliffs. They enter the hollow in sudden numbers, filling it with wings, then vanish over the rim as if poured away. Thick-furred marmots live beneath the dry shelves and whistle from stone to stone. Pale frogs breed in the cold pools; their calls rise at dusk in rattling choruses. Beetles with blue-black shells work through the moss. Tiny fish, scarcely longer than a finger joint, occupy the largest springs, where they hold themselves against the current beneath curtains of waterweed.

Above all are the cliff goats. They come down in small bands to lick minerals from the weeping rock. Their hooves have polished certain ledges to a dull shine. During the birthing season, the hollow fills with bleats and answering cries from animals hidden by folds in the ground.

Predators arrive openly. Lean eagles circle close enough for the movement of their feathers to be seen. A small high-country fox patrols the marsh margin, placing each foot carefully on grass tussocks. It hunts frogs, chicks, and young marmots, and is itself harried by the nesting birds. Nothing here stays peaceful for long. Grazers move through the flowers; insects follow dung and bloom; birds descend upon insects; storms drive the birds into crevices; sunlight brings everything out again.

The loudness of the place is startling.

Water strikes ledges below the rim with a continuous, unseen booming. Wind plays in the vertical grooves of the cliffs, producing low flute notes that alter as clouds pass. The birds scream. Marmots whistle. Frogs clatter in the peat pools. During rain, every leaf and stone contributes its own percussion, while the summit walls catch the noise and throw it back across the little basin. Even on a clear day there is scarcely silence. The smallest sounds—the snap of a seedpod, the clink of a goat’s hoof—carry from wall to wall.

Only beneath the stone shelves is the world quiet. There, the peat muffles footsteps and the mountain’s noises become a distant vibration.

Signs of people are scattered around the hollow, though no one seems to live there now.

Near the eastern rim stands a low shelter built from fitted slabs, half buried in turf. Its roof is made of overlapping stone plates weighted with boulders. The entrance is so narrow that a person must crawl inside. Within are an old hearth, a few soot marks, and sleeping hollows lined with decayed grass. A bone needle lies in a crack beyond the reach of rain. Beside the doorway, someone once carved a row of short vertical cuts, perhaps a count of days, animals, or storms.

Across the marsh, three forked wooden posts stand around a spring. They have silvered with age but remain upright. Plaited grass cords hang from one of them, renewed more recently than the shelter. Small offerings have been pushed into the peat nearby: bird bones bundled with red thread, polished pebbles, a shallow cup hammered from soft copper and repaired twice with crude rivets.

There is also a path, though it appears only intermittently. Flat stones cross the deepest ground. A notch has been cut into one wall. A few handholds have been chipped along the outer approach, where the mountain drops beneath the climber’s heels. Whoever comes here carries little and builds less. The hollow does not permit weight easily. Timber is scarce, fuel scarcer, and anything tall attracts wind and lightning.

At noon, under ordinary daylight, the place is sharply legible. Shadows sit beneath every tuft. Pools reflect the blue or grey of the sky. Looking outward is more difficult: the surrounding world is either lost in cloud or revealed with dizzying clarity. Nearby stones seem intimate enough to touch, while the next visible ground may be miles away. The basin is a room whose walls open directly onto distance.

Weather changes it by the minute.

Cloud spills over one rim and drains through the opposite notch. Rain crosses in visible curtains. Hail rattles briefly and melts into the warm skin of the peat. In colder hours, ice skins the pools but leaves the springs open and steaming faintly. Sunlight can follow a storm so quickly that every surface begins to glitter before the thunder has stopped. Water rises, pours, sinks, and reappears elsewhere. Flowers close. Frogs submerge. Swifts crowd the lee side of the crags. Then wind returns and sets the whole place in motion again.

There is one peculiarity to the hollow, minor enough that it may go unnoticed at first. Water does not always seem to obey the slope.

Certain rivulets climb a short distance across tilted stone before entering cracks. Droplets sometimes move sideways along the cliff face, joining one another in trembling lines. The people who left the posts appear to have marked these places. Each upward-flowing seep bears a small triangle pecked into the rock.

The effect is not dramatic. Nothing floats; nothing falls upward. It is more like a local preference in the stone, as though the mountain’s buried pressures draw water toward particular veins. Moss grows especially thick around those veins, and animals seek them out. The cliff goats lick them. The pale frogs lay eggs beneath them. When thunder passes directly overhead, the rivulets quicken and the triangular marks become dark with moisture.

From the center of the basin, all its boundaries can be seen. It is a modest place: a marsh, several shelves, seven trees, one shelter, a spring marked by posts, and a ring of rock. Yet the emptiness around it gives it an immense solitude. The hollow is not expansive, but it is suspended above expanses. It feels less like part of the mountain than something the mountain has held in one hand for a very long time.

Life comes into that hand, drinks, breeds, hunts, shelters, and departs.

The water remains, moving softly beneath everything, even when the wind and wings make the summit roar.
