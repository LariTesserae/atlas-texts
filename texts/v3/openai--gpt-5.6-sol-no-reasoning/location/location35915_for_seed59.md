The town occupies a high, shallow basin where the mountain briefly relents. It is no more than a mile across, bounded by weathered ridges and low cliffs from which water seeps year-round. From above, little masonry is visible. The place resembles a dark green nest pressed into the hills: terraces drowned in leaves, roofs tufted with moss, lanes roofed over by vines and fruit branches, smoke escaping through the canopy in thin blue threads.

Everything is wet without being flooded. Rain comes often, usually in quick cold showers, but even in clear weather the town drips. Springs rise beneath courtyards. Water beads on bark, condenses under bridges, and gathers along the undersides of broad leaves before falling in heavy, irregular drops. The cliffs weep through curtains of maidenhair fern. Every stair has a narrow runnel cut beside it, and every runnel joins another until the lower streets are accompanied by fast, ankle-deep channels.

The basin has been occupied so long that it is difficult to distinguish terrain from construction. Houses are built against old terrace walls, beneath living roots, and sometimes inside hollow trunks that have been widened with stone tools. Their foundations consist of rounded fieldstone packed with clay. Upper walls are woven from hazel or willow and plastered with mud, lime, hair, and chopped reed. Roofs are steep, heavy with sod, and held together by roots as much as by rafters. Pegs, cords, wedges, ceramic pipes, bone gutters, wooden hinges, and a little hammered copper make up the town’s practical technology. Almost nothing is straight, but almost everything works.

Plants are not decoration here. They are the principal building material, the weatherproofing, the food supply, the street plan, and much of the local economy. Living willow fences are braided into load-bearing screens. Coppiced trees support balconies. Great pollarded limes stand in public squares, their thick limbs carrying platforms, ladders, pigeon shelters, and drying racks. Vines are trained into covered passages. Reed beds filter wastewater below each cluster of houses, and mats of sedge reinforce the soft edges of ponds.

Some roofs are grazed by goats. Others bear cabbages, sorrel, onions, beans, flax, and small sour apples. Nettles grow in deliberate plots for fiber and soup. Moss is harvested for packing walls. Fungi are cultivated in shaded cellars and under stacked logs. Even the paths are alive: tough creeping plants form resilient carpets over the mud, releasing sharp green smells underfoot.

The oldest trees have absorbed entire structures. Door lintels disappear into trunks. Stairways end at roots polished by generations of hands. A meeting hall stands beneath the interlocked crowns of several beeches, its walls built in the spaces between them. Nobody can say whether the hall was planted, built, or merely encouraged. Repairs consist as often of pruning, grafting, and tying as of carpentry.

For all its age, the place is not quiet or contemplative. It is crowded almost beyond comfort.

Animals are everywhere.

Ducks occupy the channels and must be pushed aside by passing carts. Geese patrol the bridges in quarrelsome groups. Chickens scratch beneath market tables. Pigs nose through designated lanes, consuming peelings and spilled grain before being driven home at dusk. Goats climb roofs, walls, woodpiles, and occasionally one another. Small mountain cattle are led through streets scarcely wider than their horns. Dogs bark from steps and herd anything that moves. Cats inhabit rafters, storehouses, and warm clay ovens.

Above them, the town is thick with birds. Swallows stitch through open rooms. Sparrows erupt from hedges. Rooks crowd the taller trees, dropping twigs and maintaining a continuous argumentative clamor. Doves nest in wall cavities. Herons stand improbably still beside millponds until frogs or fingerlings betray themselves. In the flowering season, insects add another layer: bees in wall hives, flies around dung heaps, beetles under bark, midges over every cistern.

The surrounding vegetation is equally crowded with life. Newts shelter beneath wet stones. Toads sit in drains. Voles tunnel through the roof gardens. Red deer browse along the upper terraces at night, while boar churn abandoned plots into black mud. Foxes come close enough to steal poultry from beneath windows. On damp evenings, snails appear by the thousands, silvering walls and paths.

Sound accumulates in the enclosed basin. The cliffs reflect it inward: rushing channels, turning millwheels, chopping blocks, looms thudding behind plaster walls, dogs barking, geese screaming, traders calling weights and prices, children splashing through runoff, carpenters driving wooden pegs, rain rattling leaves, and the abrupt roar of water released through a sluice. Bells of clay, bronze, and carved wood mark fires, meetings, missing livestock, incoming storms, and the opening of communal gates. There are few truly silent moments. Before dawn, frogs and running water occupy the dark; then the first rooster begins, and the whole town follows.

Movement is the town’s governing condition. Water is always being diverted. Animals are always escaping. Vines require training, paths require cutting back, roofs require grazing or mowing, and swollen doors require planing. A blocked grate can redirect a stream through three houses. A fallen branch may close a market lane while opening sunlight onto a garden that has not seen it in years. After heavy rain, people emerge with hooks, baskets, mattocks, wooden shovels, and wicker screens to clear channels before the upper ponds overflow.

The streets reflect this instability. There is no fixed boundary between lane, stair, bridge, drain, and courtyard. Boards are laid across mud and removed hours later. Foot traffic shifts to wherever the ground is firm. Market stalls unfold beneath trees and vanish by evening. Livestock pens are assembled from hurdles, moved downhill, then carried back uphill once the soil has rested. The town is old, but its surface changes daily.

Beneath that restlessness lies an established and carefully maintained form. Centuries of rain have rounded the stair treads and hollowed handholds in stone. The main channels are lined with slabs fitted so closely that only moss enters the joints. Terrace walls bulge but seldom fail; their stones settle, roots tighten them, and residents replace whatever the winter loosens. The basin’s erosion is not resisted outright. It is slowed, guided, divided, and put to work.

Water powers small grain mills and fulling hammers. It cools dairies, fills fishponds, soaks flax, carries waste toward reed beds, and irrigates terraces in dry weeks. Every household has rights to certain channels at certain hours. Wooden markers at the sluices record these rights more reliably than speech. To steal water is a serious offense. To obstruct runoff through carelessness is nearly as bad.

The settlement has grown around these rules. Its districts are not arranged by wealth so much as by flow. Millers occupy the upper channels. Dyers and tanners work downstream in stone-lined yards, separated from drinking sources. Gardeners cluster around settling ponds rich with silt. Potters use clay from the lower basin and fire their kilns on exposed ledges where sparks cannot catch the canopy. Herdspeople live near the ridge paths, in houses with broad gates and lofts full of hay.

The climate is cool even in summer. Mist collects in the basin overnight and clings among the houses until the sun clears the eastern ridge. Breath shows in shaded lanes well into spring. Stone remains cold to the touch, and laundry dries slowly. Winters bring sleet, wet snow, and ice along the channels, but deep freezes are uncommon; water continues beneath the crust, audible in the dark. People dress in layered wool, oiled hides, woven bark fiber, and felted caps. Interiors smell of smoke, damp cloth, herbs, livestock, and fermenting fruit.

Daylight is ordinary but fragmented. In open squares it falls pale and clean from the high sky. Elsewhere it filters through leaf upon leaf, reflected from wet stone and moving water. Sunlit patches migrate across walls while branches thrash in the basin wind. After rain, every surface flashes: snail trails, roof grass, glazed pottery, black feathers, fish scales, and the worn edges of steps.

At the center is a crowded pond fed by three springs. It is half reservoir, half market, half animal yard—its functions overlap despite the arithmetic. Stone platforms project over the water. Women rinse wool beside boys selling eels from baskets. Ducks investigate floating peelings. Pack animals drink at the upper end while reed beds clean the lower shallows. A large willow grows from an island scarcely wider than its trunk. Its roots bind the retaining wall, and its branches cover much of the square.

This willow is neither sacred nor magical. It is simply indispensable. Announcements are nailed to its dead limbs. Ropes are tied to it during floods. Its young shoots become baskets; its older wood becomes handles, braces, and charcoal. Birds nest in it, goats eat what they can reach, and children cross the pond by climbing through its branches. The tree survives this use because everyone understands how much use it can bear.

That is the character of the whole place. Nothing is untouched, yet nothing is wholly subdued. Human settlement has not cleared the living world away; it has entered into a dense, noisy negotiation with it. The town feeds the animals that plague it, channels the water that threatens it, and builds with vegetation that continually attempts to become forest again.

From the ridge at evening, the basin seems almost calm. Smoke lies blue beneath the canopy. Roofs blend into gardens, gardens into orchards, orchards into wet woodland. The water catches a few strips of sky. Then a flock of rooks lifts all at once, blackening the air; bells begin below; dogs answer; people shout at livestock in the lanes; and a sudden shower advances across the leaves with the sound of applause.
