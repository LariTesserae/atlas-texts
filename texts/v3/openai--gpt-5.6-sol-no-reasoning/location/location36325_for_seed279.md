The lowland begins where a shallow inland sea has withdrawn, though not completely and perhaps not permanently. It is an open basin of dark mineral flats, crowded with stone.

There is no vegetation. Nothing softens the horizon, binds the mud, or disguises the rawness of the ground. The basin appears newly made: slabs tilt out of it at abrupt angles, their edges still keen; fans of gravel lie beneath short escarpments as though poured there yesterday. In places the crust is black and glassy, wrinkled by cooling. Elsewhere it is pale, porous stone that drinks rain and releases it hours later through thousands of pinholes.

Water is everywhere without gathering into a true river. It occupies seams. It fills polygonal cracks to their lips and makes long, narrow mirrors between ridges. It lies under thin mineral skins that flex almost imperceptibly when crossed. Rainwater descends from the surrounding shelves and spreads into sheets only a finger deep, so transparent that the flooded ground first appears merely polished. There are no reeds, mosses, stains of algae, or films of recognizable life. Even the wettest pools remain clear enough to reveal their beds of angular fragments.

The air is cool and close. Mist forms low rather than high, condensing between stones and around the mouths of fissures. Droplets accumulate on overhangs, swell slowly, and fall. Because every exposed surface weeps in this way, the whole basin glitters faintly despite the dimness.

Yet the place is loud.

The noise does not correspond to visible motion. Water moves sluggishly. Mist hardly drifts. Nothing like wind troubles the pools. Still, the basin resounds with continuous mineral thunder: deep groans underfoot, hollow blows from distant chambers, and a dense metallic ringing that seems to pass from slab to slab. The newly hardened crust is settling over a waterlogged foundation. Pressure travels through it in slow waves, too gradual to see but strong enough to make the standing stones sing.

There are thousands of those stones. From a distance they resemble a drowned city’s spires, packed across the flats in irregular districts. Up close they are natural extrusions: blades, chimneys, clustered columns, and thin partitions perforated by round openings. Many nearly touch. Passage through them becomes a matter of turning sideways between wet planes and stepping across black channels. Their surfaces are striped with fresh precipitates—white, blue-gray, rust-red—but the colors belong to salts and metals rather than lichens.

Each formation has a voice. Broad plates boom at intervals. Narrow fins hum continuously. Hollow columns cough when trapped water drops into their interiors. Cavities beneath the flatlands compress and release air through pores in the rock, producing whistles, brassy drones, and abrupt animal-like cries. During light rain the sound multiplies until the basin becomes an immense roof struck in a million places.

Nothing is operating it. There are no mechanisms hidden below, no makers maintaining the tones. The landscape is simply built in such a way that gravity, cooling stone, shallow water, and confined air cannot remain silent.

Scattered among the natural towers are structures that are harder to explain. They are small, seldom more than a single chamber, and made from the same black slabs as the basin floor. Some consist of three upright stones supporting a fourth. Others are low rings enclosing dry hollows. Their joints have no mortar and show no tool marks. It is possible that they are only unusually orderly fractures, but their entrances all face the same direction: toward a broad depression in the northeast where mist remains throughout the day.

Inside, sound changes. The surrounding clamor compresses into one low note, felt through the ribs more than heard. Water does not cross their thresholds, even when the flats flood. Droplets stop at the outer edges and join into silver beads, forming quivering borders around the dry interiors. The chambers contain nothing except loose stones sorted, somehow, by size.

There is little animal life. Occasionally a translucent, many-legged thing is visible beneath a submerged crust. It moves so slowly that it can be mistaken for a trapped bubble, advancing along mineral seams over the course of hours. In the deeper fissures, pale threads withdraw from light; whether they are animals, crystals, or something between is uncertain. After prolonged rain, small black forms emerge from holes high on the stone columns. They have membranous bodies and no obvious wings, but they cross short gaps by flattening themselves and riding the pressure pulses that vibrate through the air. They make no sound of their own.

The sky is a uniform lid of graphite cloud. Day arrives as a thinning of darkness rather than sunrise. Shadows remain broad and weak. At rare moments the cloud breaks into luminous bands, and every water-filled seam reflects the same pale opening. Then the basin appears divided into thousands of bright-edged islands, an intricate map without names or destinations.

Change here is patient. A passage may narrow by the width of a grain in a season. A tower may lean for centuries before touching its neighbor. Salts gather around seep-mouths in delicate shelves, one transparent layer at a time. The loudness suggests violence, but actual collapse is uncommon. Most of the noise is preparation: stress accumulating, water finding lower pores, rock remembering heat.

When a break finally occurs, it is almost modest. A fin splits. One half descends into the mud and sends a slow wave through the shallow channels. For several minutes every column in the basin alters its pitch. Far-off chambers answer. The sound travels beyond sight, across the entire lowland, until the stone forest has accepted its new arrangement.

Then the old roar returns, subtly changed.

The principal rule of the basin is that water seeks no single lowest place. Beneath the visible ground lies a dense mesh of newborn cavities, and pressure continually shifts from one to another. Pools drain uphill. Cracks fill after rain has stopped. A footprint may remain dry while the land around it floods, then brim suddenly hours later with cold, perfectly clear water. There is no dependable current to follow out.

The second rule is that sound is a more accurate map than sight. Open routes produce dull echoes. Impassable clusters ring brightly. Deep water dampens the bass but sharpens distant whistles. Those who stand still long enough can hear the shape of the basin—not as an image, but as layers of pressure and delay. The scattered stone chambers become landmarks because each holds a distinct sustained note.

Toward the northeast depression, the formations grow denser. Towers merge into walls; walls buckle into vaulted galleries open to the clouded sky. Mist pours soundlessly over their edges while the ground beneath them roars. At the center is a broad, shallow expanse of water, scarcely deeper than an ankle, enclosed by a nearly perfect ring of fresh black stone. Its surface does not ripple when struck by drops. Instead, each drop produces a widening circle of dim light beneath the water, as if the impact has awakened buried luminosity.

These circles expand very slowly. Many overlap. Their intersections remain glowing after the circles themselves fade, leaving a shifting geometry on the basin floor.

The rare pale creatures gather under those intersections, motionless and densely layered, while all around them the stone sings.
