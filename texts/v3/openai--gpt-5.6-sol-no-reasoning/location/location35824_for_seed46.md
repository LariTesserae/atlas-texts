High in a young mountain, before the mountain has learned the shapes of cliffs and valleys, there is a pocket of green heat.

It cannot be reached from the surface. It is not a cave, because no passage leads into it, and not a hollow in the usual geological sense. Rather, it is an omission embedded in the mineral: a room-sized interval where the mountain’s substance has not yet decided to exist. Around it, raw stone presses inward—dark, seamless, scarcely fractured, bearing none of the fluting or rubble that water and weather would eventually make. The walls look freshly generated. Pale crystals continue to appear on them, point first, extending by fractions over the course of years.

The pocket is high above any sea and dry beyond ordinary desert dryness. There is no groundwater, no condensation, no buried ice. Heat travels through the enclosing rock from deep reservoirs of magma, holding the interval at the temperature of a furnace room. Yet nothing burns. There is little oxygen and no wind, only a dense, transparent atmosphere of sulfur compounds, metal vapors, and electrically excited gases. Breathing, if there were anything here that breathed, would be impossible.

Across the floor grow scattered columns of vegetation.

They are green only approximately. Their upright fronds have the deep, light-swallowing color of oxidized copper, with edges that flash yellow-white when they turn toward the chamber’s illumination. Each organism stands alone in a generous circle of bare black mineral. There are perhaps eleven of them, though counting is unreliable: looked at from one side there are nine, from another fourteen, and in the reflectionless center of the pocket there seems to be only one plant repeated at different ages.

They possess no roots. Beneath each trunk, fine metallic filaments terminate a hair’s breadth above the floor. The gap is constant, even when the floor slowly rises. From it comes a faint violet corona, almost too dim to see.

Their leaves are broad, pleated surfaces, lush in area but sparse in number. They unfold like the cooling fins of engines. Instead of water, a warm liquid alloy circulates through branching channels in their tissues—mostly gallium and unfamiliar low-melting metals—driven by rhythmic electromagnetic contractions. Their cells are neither cells nor machines. Thin mineral membranes sort ions, stack charge, and manufacture new layers of leaf from vapor. Under magnification, every surface is busy with assembly lines: crystal gates opening and closing, molecules passed along rails of electric potential, imperfections detected, dismantled, and reused.

No hand designed these processes. No builders installed them. Industry is simply what life became here.

The vegetation does not form a forest so much as a distributed factory whose components happen to resemble cycads, ferns, and young palms. One column concentrates sulfur. Another plates the floor with nickel in a disk around itself. A third produces hair-thin ribbons that crawl outward for decades before dissolving back into gas. The oldest carries a crown of translucent vanes nearly touching the unseen limits of the pocket. Their shadows are clean and ordinary, which makes everything else more disturbing.

Light comes from nowhere remarkable.

It is broad, neutral, and steady, resembling afternoon daylight beneath a clear sky. There is no lamp, aperture, flame, or luminous organism. The walls do not glow. Objects are simply illuminated, each from an angle corresponding to a sun that would have to be many kilometers inside the mountain. Every few centuries the angle shifts slightly. The plants turn with it, their great folded leaves moving so slowly that the motion is known only by the displacement of shadows across the stone.

There are no animals, no mites in the pleats, no larvae, no pollinators, no hidden scavengers. Nothing flies. Nothing calls. Decay is not performed by mouths or fungi but by voltage gradients. A fallen leaf remains intact for centuries, then separates all at once into shining sheets, powders, and neat beads of molten metal. These migrate over the floor without haste and are taken up by whichever plant requires them.

The quiet is nearly complete. At long intervals a leaf makes a soft ticking sound as it expands. Static whispers along a frond. Somewhere beyond the wall, rock undergoes a phase change with a low, padded knock. Each sound seems disproportionately significant, as though the place had been listening for it.

Once every forty-three hours, all the plants emit the same deep industrial hum. The note begins below hearing, rises through the floor, and holds for eleven minutes. During this interval, the chamber’s geometry becomes operational rather than spatial. Distances turn into functions. The far wall is no longer farther away than the near wall; it is merely later in a sequence. Shadows detach from their plants and arrange themselves in parallel ranks. Surfaces acquire tolerances. Empty air divides into invisible compartments, each performing one step of an unknown production.

The product is the mountain.

Grain by grain, the pocket manufactures its surroundings. The plants extract elements from the atmosphere, refine them through their metallic sap, and project completed crystal lattices outward through the walls. The enclosing rock is therefore younger than the hollow it contains. Strata radiate away from this hidden chamber like growth rings, though no geologist on the surface would interpret them that way. Faults are laid down in advance of stresses. Ore bodies are deposited as spare parts. Fossilless layers wait for histories that have not occurred.

Erosion has scarcely begun because the land itself is unfinished. Outside, if “outside” can be said to exist while the cycle is active, the summit is still rising: a blunt expanse of hot stone with no gullies, no scree, no river-cut faces. Wind passes over it but has not yet established a direction. Dust has not accumulated enough to soften its edges. The mountain has height without age.

Inside, the manufacturing proceeds at the speed of geology. A finger-width of new wall may take a thousand years. The plants do not hurry. Their metabolism is calibrated to tectonic pressure, stellar cycles, and the gradual cooling of buried metals. Individual leaves persist longer than species do elsewhere.

Yet the pocket remains small. No matter how much mountain it produces, its interior never exceeds a few intimate strides. Attempting to cross it reveals the deepest failure in its reality. One can approach any plant, but not the bare center between them. A step toward that center becomes a step along the wall; another arrives behind the walker; a third is found already completed. There is no barrier. Space simply refuses the destination.

The plants exploit this. Their rootless filaments hang into the inaccessible center, drawing from it electricity, hot vapor, and occasionally materials that have no stable existence outside the chamber: blue iron, negative ash, crystals whose facets meet before their edges do. Such substances last only long enough to enter the living refinery. By the time they emerge as rock, they have become ordinary basalt, quartz, sulfur, copper.

This may be the purpose of the place, if purpose is not too constructed a word: to take what cannot be real and process it into what can.

Nothing observes the work. There are no footprints and no devices, no ruins, conduits, bolts, or inscriptions. The precise rhythms and industrial chemistry have arisen without civilization. The pocket is untouched because touching it may be the event that has not happened yet—the future erosion that will someday open the mountain, admit air, and expose this hot green chamber to a sky whose sunlight it has imitated for millions of years.

Perhaps then the plants will die.

Perhaps the summit will discover that it was hollow.

Or perhaps the opening, when it finally forms, will reveal only solid stone, because the pocket will have completed its production and manufactured itself out of existence.
