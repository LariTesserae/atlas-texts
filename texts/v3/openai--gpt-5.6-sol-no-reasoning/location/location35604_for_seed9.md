At the top of the city stands a manufactured marsh.

It occupies no more space than a market square, though its folded construction gives it the depth of a hillside. From the lower door to the upper parapet, the ground rises steeply through terraces, narrow basins, dripping galleries, and flights of shallow steps. There is no soil beneath it, no bedrock, no buried seam of anything that was not brought here, mixed here, riveted down, cast into molds, or grown on purpose. Even the mud is an industrial product: black mineral paste stirred in vats below and pumped upward through ceramic arteries.

The place is warm despite its elevation. Moist air from the city rises along the tower faces and condenses against the marsh’s cooler metalwork. Water pearls on railings, gathers under leaves, and runs continuously from one level to the next. At intervals, hidden valves open with a soft internal knock. A terrace floods ankle-deep; another drains. Threads of water emerge from weeping concrete and creep down walls furred with moss.

Rain is frequent here, though not always from clouds. A gantry spans the highest basin, carrying perforated pipes and a slow traveling carriage. It releases curtains of droplets in measured strips, watering one sector at a time. From below, the moving rain looks almost natural. Up close, each shower begins and ends at a ruler-straight boundary.

Everything is young.

The concrete is still pale and scarcely stained. Brick channels retain the sharp ridges of their molds. Copper spillways are only beginning to green. No gully has had time to deepen, no root has pried open a joint, and no current has rounded the dark aggregate scattered across the pools. The landscape has erosion in principle but not yet in history. Water searches for routes the designers did not anticipate, testing seams, lingering in bolt holes, building tiny deltas of manufactured silt behind screw heads. It is a place just starting to discover how it will wear out.

The terraces are crowded with vegetation. Broad-leaved taro and dwarf bananas lean over cisterns. Sedges rise through grated floors. Ferns spring from wall pockets shaped like bricks, and vines are trained along fine wires until it becomes difficult to tell which lines are stems and which are supports. Moss occupies the wet faces. Liverworts shine on shaded ceramic. In the warmest corners, pale flowers open beneath ducts that exhale humid air from the machinery rooms.

Nothing grows from an unprepared surface. Every root enters a cartridge, trough, suspended mat, or crack filled deliberately with substrate. Yet the planting has already begun to exceed its diagram. Runners bridge drainage gaps. Roots trail into the wrong reservoirs. Seeds germinate inside lamp housings and around valve collars. Leaves overlap inspection marks. The constructed marsh is becoming lush enough to conceal the fact that it is entirely constructed, though it never quite succeeds.

Its boundaries are close and unmistakable. On three sides, waist-high walls drop sheer to roofs far below. On the fourth stands a service building of ribbed glass and painted steel. Beyond the parapets, the air is bright, and clouds travel almost level with the upper basin. The city beneath is often hidden by warm haze. When it clears, streets appear as narrow scored lines, and the marsh seems less like a rooftop garden than a moist green chamber accidentally suspended in the sky.

The machinery does not dominate by noise. Most of it works beneath the floor: low-speed pumps, settling drums, heat exchangers, nutrient mixers. Their presence comes through vibration. A hand laid on a parapet can feel water being lifted. Grates tremble faintly before sluices open. Occasionally a motor starts with a restrained hum and a chain moves somewhere behind a wall, then quiet returns.

The loudest sounds are small ones: drops striking leaves, overflow ticking into a steel basin, reeds brushing a cable, bubbles rising through mud. Wind makes the taller plants hiss. Deep in the plumbing, air passes with a sound like someone turning over in sleep.

There are few animals. A handful of copper-colored midges gather in the warm mist, and minute snails have appeared in one shaded channel, though they were not included in the inventory. A single black bird sometimes lands on the rain gantry to drink from the bolt cups. Its arrival changes the scale of the place. For a few minutes, it becomes not a facility but a wet highland clearing. Then the bird departs, dropping into the bright distance, and the terraces belong once more to plants, water, and machinery.

More elusive things move in the filters. Workers speak of translucent larvae found downstream of sealed tanks, each bearing a row of dark geometric marks. The official explanation is contamination in the imported moss. No one has explained why the marks resemble the perforations in the irrigation pipes, or why the larvae appear only when the pumps reverse at dusk.

The marsh follows a complex daily metabolism. In the morning, condensation is harvested from mesh sails and sent into the upper pools. Around noon, shade panels unfold, not to darken the whole enclosure but to cast moving islands of cover across plants sensitive to the high-altitude sun. In late afternoon, the mud beds warm. At dusk, nutrient water rises through porous tiles, making the ground gleam from within. Overnight, the terraces drain slowly into clarifying chambers, where roots, charcoal, sand, and revolving metal screens strip the water clean before it is returned uphill.

Thus the same water is always descending and being raised, but never in quite the same condition. It passes through leaves, fog, pipes, mud, reservoirs, and air. It becomes rain, runoff, sap, vapor, and coolant. The marsh is less a place containing water than a machine for teaching water different forms.

Its paths are narrow. Visitors move single-file between wet walls and crowded planting beds, ducking beneath pipes and curtains of aerial roots. Every few steps the route changes material: perforated iron, warm brick, rubber mesh, glazed tile, translucent panels with water flowing underneath. There are handrails everywhere, slick with condensation. Some terminate abruptly in foliage because the plants have obscured unfinished stairs. Others surround empty rectangles where future pools are marked but not yet installed.

Construction continues while the landscape operates. Fresh modules arrive by crane: sealed cubes of wet substrate, bundles of reed plugs, prefabricated cascades, replacement pump housings. Sections are dismantled and rebuilt before weather can mature them. A terrace may be marsh on Monday, scaffolding on Wednesday, and a steaming fern hollow by the weekend. The place is complete in its artificiality but incomplete in every other sense.

This gives it a peculiar law: nothing here is original, but nearly everything is becoming unforeseen.

Water escapes its assigned channels. Plants graft themselves across manufactured boundaries. Mineral crusts bloom around nozzles. Rust begins where rust was calculated not to begin. Fine sediment gathers into the first soft contours, the embryonic suggestion of banks and shoals. The marsh has not yet been shaped by erosion, but erosion has entered it as a future—quietly, drop by drop.

Standing at the central pool, one can see nearly the whole landscape at once: the upper rain frame, the descending green terraces, the glazed service wall, the bright rim of sky. It is intimate, crowded, warm, and high. Mist catches ordinary daylight without making it theatrical. Pipes shine between leaves. Young concrete sweats. A pump turns somewhere below.

The whole small world is busy making the first marks upon itself.
