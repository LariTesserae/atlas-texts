The city occupies a high basin where the mountains have collapsed into broad shelves of pale stone. From a distance it appears almost empty: a few black towers, long roofs of oxidized metal, and thin lines of pipe crossing miles of open mineral ground. The structures are too widely spaced to form a skyline. They seem less like neighborhoods than instruments placed across a vast work surface.

Everything is wet, though there are no rivers and almost no standing lakes. Water is held close to the surface by the basin’s dense clay and salt strata. It rises through cracks, beads on walls, and gathers beneath sheets of loose stone. After noon, when the heat is greatest, vapor climbs from thousands of shallow vents and condenses against anything cooler than the air. Roof edges weep continuously. Elevated conduits rain onto the plazas below. Some streets shine beneath a permanent film no deeper than a fingernail, reflecting the white sky with such intensity that travelers wrap their faces in dark cloth.

No plants soften the place. There is no moss in the joints, no weed beneath the rails, no windblown seed caught against a curb. The ground is mineral from horizon to horizon: cream-colored marl, iron-black glass, green copper runoff, red sediment laid down in repeated fans. Even the open reservoirs are bare along their margins. Their hot water laps against ceramic revetments and shelves of sulfur without reed, scum, or floating leaf.

Yet the basin is crowded with animals.

They are most visible on the flats beyond the city: great dispersed herds of six-legged waders moving through the shallow seep fields. Their hides are smooth and reflective, gray where dry and nearly silver when wet. They lower broad mouths to the ground, not to graze, but to sift warm mineral water for microscopic life too small to color the pools. Behind them come narrow-bodied followers that pick salts and parasites from folds around their joints. Pale flyers circle above, rarely calling, their translucent wings held rigid on columns of hot air.

The absence of vegetation makes every movement legible. A herd crossing ten kilometers away can be followed from the city terraces. Predators cannot approach under cover, so they have become patient and geometric. Long, low creatures lie aligned with cracks in the stone, sometimes remaining motionless for days. Others hunt from flooded service trenches, with only their glassy breathing ridges above the water. When a chase begins it is brief, brilliant, and nearly silent: silver bodies scattering over white ground, dark limbs opening, spray lifting and vanishing into steam.

The city was built around condensation.

Its oldest quarters sit against the northern escarpment, where caverns breathe cold air from deep inside the mountain. Massive stone fins channel the basin’s hot vapor into these cavities. Water forms on their shaded surfaces and descends through enclosed gutters to cisterns below. Later districts expanded the principle with metal cooling towers, pressure engines, buried turbines, and lattices of ceramic pipe. The result is an urban system spread over an enormous area, its components separated so that one does not warm another.

There are pump houses in the open flats, squat and windowless. There are distillation yards with hundreds of shallow black trays. There are elevated switching stations reached by narrow stairs, and abandoned furnaces whose walls have slumped into smooth vitrified mounds. Residential structures cling to the lee sides of industrial buildings, using their shade and exhaust draft. Streets widen suddenly into bare plazas where heat can disperse. Whole blocks may contain only a single tower and a tiled catchment court.

The machinery is always working, but quietly. Rotors turn behind thick walls. Pumps flex slowly under the ground. Valves open with soft metallic ticks. The city’s engineers learned long ago that vibration attracts burrowing animals from the wet strata, and that loud, regular impacts can bring entire colonies upward. Machines therefore use balanced drives, water cushions, and long belts that transmit force without hammering. Wheels have broad ceramic rims. Doors are weighted to close without striking their frames.

Silence here is not emptiness. It is civic discipline.

People speak at close range. Signals are given with reflected light, colored shutters, or pressure changes in narrow pipes. Cargo trams move on wet rails with little more than a hiss. Even the factories seem contemplative: arrays of polished arms dipping into hot baths; centrifuges turning beneath stone lids; kiln mouths sealed behind successive chambers. At shift changes, workers emerge in pale heat-cloaks and spread across the open streets without bells or whistles to summon them.

The loudest natural sound is water striking water. It drips in cistern halls, trembles through drainage slots, and occasionally falls from the escarpment in brief curtains when underground chambers overflow. Thunder is rare, despite the moisture. Clouds form low and bright, more like suspended glare than weather, and release fine hot rain that evaporates from the upper terraces while still falling. On the lower flats it reaches the ground and runs in quick branching threads, rearranging the colored salts before sinking out of sight.

The heat is severe and constant. At this elevation the sunlight is hard, almost white, and shadows have blue-black edges. Exposed metal cannot be touched. Water in the southern basins approaches scalding, while shaded condensation galleries remain cool enough to numb the hands. The whole city is organized around these violent local differences. A person may walk from blistering pavement into cold mist within a dozen steps, then emerge onto a bridge where the air dries sweat instantly.

At dawn, condensation turns every cable into a string of bright droplets. The towers glitter. Millions of tiny airborne animals rise from the seep fields, nearly invisible except where they bend the light into moving veils. Flyers pass through them with open mouths. Along the reservoir rims, shell-backed creatures climb from the water to warm themselves on the tiles, leaving complex tracks in the mineral film. Maintenance crews read these marks carefully. Certain patterns indicate clean water. Others mean a pipe has begun leaking underground.

By afternoon, the basin is a white furnace. Activity retreats below roofs and into service tunnels. Only the largest animals remain on the flats, standing knee-deep in hot seepage to shed heat through their wet limbs. Far off, cooling towers exhale narrow columns of vapor that do not drift much in the still air. They stand over the city like temporary architecture—immense, luminous, and continually rebuilding themselves.

Evening brings no green scent, no insects in grass, no rustle of leaves. The air smells of rain on stone, hot clay, metal oxide, and faint sulfur. Shadows lengthen across the terraces until isolated buildings seem joined by darkness. Animals come into the outer districts to drink from overflow channels. People leave shallow bowls in designated courts, not from affection alone but because the animals consume mineral films that would otherwise clog the drains.

There are strict rules at these courts. No sudden light. No striking metal. No crossing between a drinking animal and open ground. Predators sometimes follow the herds into the city, and the citizens do not kill them unless they enter a dwelling. The predators are part of the drainage ecology: they keep the burrowers moving, preventing colonies from sealing the warm outlets. What appears wild and what appears engineered have long since become inseparable.

The basin is old enough that erosion no longer feels destructive. Corners have rounded to stable curves. Runoff has found permanent routes through stairs and courtyards. Stone walls are furrowed where centuries of condensation have passed over them, but the channels now protect the surfaces beneath. Abandoned districts do not become overgrown. They simply become smoother. Roofs sag, pipes mineralize from within, and rooms slowly fill with translucent deposits until they resemble natural caves.

In some ruins, animals live among immobilized machines whose gears have become solid wheels of calcite. Warm water continues to pass through them. At night, pressure variations make the sealed pipes flex by fractions of an inch, and droplets fall in sequences that can be mistaken for deliberate messages.

The inhabitants insist they are only pressure noises.

Still, the oldest condensation stations are never dismantled. Their outputs are no longer measurable, and no one remembers where all their buried lines lead. They stand far apart on the eastern shelf, black against the glaring flats, shedding cold water in the hottest hours of the day. Herds gather near them but do not drink. Flyers circle their roofs but never land.

Between those stations and the city lies a long reach of wet, empty stone, bright enough to erase distance. Across it move animals, workers, steam shadows, and the silent carts of the industrial roads. Nothing grows. Almost nothing decays. Water rises, condenses, runs, disappears, and rises again, carrying the basin’s heat through an ancient city that has learned to survive by making as little disturbance as possible.
