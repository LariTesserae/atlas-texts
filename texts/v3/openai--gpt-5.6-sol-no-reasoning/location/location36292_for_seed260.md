The region is called the **Unfinished Heights**, though “region” implies borders it does not possess.

It begins as a cool, dim plateau and then repeatedly fails to remain one.

Black escarpments stand at impossible elevations, their summits hidden not by cloud but by distance: the upper stone becomes smaller and paler until scale ceases to apply. Between them lie broad salt-colored basins, shelves of pumice, and steep valleys filled with something resembling vegetation. There is no river, no snowmelt, no rain trapped in the hollows. The air carries almost no vapor. Exposed minerals remain powder-dry even beneath the deepest growth.

The ground is young. Not young in the ordinary geological sense, but visibly unfinished.

Cliffs end in soft, blunt margins, as though they have not yet been sharpened by weather. Talus is rare. Ravines begin abruptly and stop after a few hundred paces. Some slopes retain the smooth convexity of things pushed up from below; others show parallel grooves where the world appears to have been combed while still pliable. At the edges of the plateau, veins of pale rock extend into open space like ribs awaiting the rest of an animal that will never come.

There are no fossils.

There are not yet enough past events here to make one.

Across this raw geology grows a sparse but vigorous flora. From a distance it appears lush: islands of deep green, violet-black, and tarnished silver spread over the dun stone, dense within themselves and separated by enormous reaches of emptiness. Up close, little of it resembles a plant.

Tall fronds rise from cracks in the rock, each made of overlapping mineral scales. They are cool to the touch and flexible only when no one is looking directly at them. Mats of thread-thin glass root into bare cliffs and branch outward in intricate fans. Their growth has no relation to gravity. Some climb. Some descend. Some extend horizontally over gulfs without support, forming trembling hedges in midair.

The most common growth is the **hushreed**, a waist-high cluster of hollow gray tubes. It has no sap and needs no water. It feeds on vibration.

This is fortunate, because the Heights are never quiet.

A continuous roar fills the landscape. It has no single source. The mountains produce it simply by existing: an immense, layered sound like rockslides, foundries, storms inside caverns, and enormous wings beating just beyond sight. The ground shudders beneath it. Loose dust dances in geometric patches. The air presses against the body in slow pulses powerful enough to blur vision.

Speech is possible only mouth-to-ear, and even then words arrive damaged.

Yet nothing seems to move quickly. No stone falls. No gale crosses the basins. The mineral forests stand nearly still. The deafening noise belongs to processes so slow that their violence has been spread across centuries. A cliff being raised by the width of a fingernail produces a century-long thunder. A new valley opening over ten generations sounds like metal torn continuously in two. The world is under construction, but its tools are duration and pressure.

Here, sound is not the consequence of change.

Sound is the change itself.

The vegetation uses it. Hushreeds funnel the roar into their buried bulbs, where vibration is converted into a chalky fuel. Glass-mats orient their branches toward particular frequencies, and whole slopes alter color when the mountains’ tone deepens. Black fan-plants grow near fault mouths and open one blade every few years. In this way the flora is lush without moisture, thriving on the endless acoustic expenditure of an unfinished world.

There are no animals. No insects hide beneath the scales. No birds cross the tremendous spaces. Nothing grazes, hunts, pollinates, or decomposes. Dead growth does not rot; it gradually loses its shape and becomes ordinary stone. New growth does not seed. Instead, when two distant tones align, a crack somewhere may darken, and years later a shoot will be standing there.

The emptiness between living patches is severe. One may walk for days across smooth ash-colored terraces and encounter only a single tuft of silver filaments clinging to a vertical face. Then, beyond a ridge, an entire valley will be crowded with towering laminae, folded green forms stacked to the horizon like a forest designed from leaves alone. The absence of fauna makes such abundance feel ceremonial, prepared for an audience that has not evolved.

Scattered among the heights are huts built by human or near-human hands.

They are low, thick-walled structures assembled from fitted slabs, hide-like sheets peeled from dead fan-plants, and pegs of polished volcanic glass. Most stand alone. A day’s walk may separate one from the next. They have no doors facing the valleys, only narrow entrances turned toward the rock, and each contains a shallow pit lined with braided hushreed. The braid muffles the roar enough for sleep, though never enough for silence.

Their makers have few tools: stone mallets, wedges of horn-colored mineral, cord spun from glass-mat fibers, bowls cut from soft basalt. Metal occurs in the cliffs, but often refuses to remain metal after being removed. Copper hammered at noon may be green stone by dusk; a blade sharpened on one side may become sharp on the other instead. The inhabitants therefore trust materials that have forgotten how to change.

No inhabitants are commonly seen.

Signs of them persist: handprints pressed into raw cliff faces, paths marked by upright shards, racks holding bundles of dried fronds, and strings stretched across gullies to measure changes in distance. Some huts have smoke holes despite there being no fuel and no evidence of fire. Others contain ladders leading downward into solid floor.

The ladders are used.

At certain times—determined not by sun or season, but by a drop in the underlying roar—a climber may descend through one of these floors. To an observer, the climber appears to become thinner, then flatter, then very far away, while remaining within arm’s reach. If they return, they may carry a piece of lower country: warm sand, a wet pebble, an animal bone. Such objects do not last. Water hisses into white dust. Bone opens like a flower and becomes a stand of mineral reeds. Sand rises grain by grain and fixes itself to the ceiling in the pattern of an unfamiliar constellation.

The sky is dim and without a dependable source of light. It has the color of iron seen through smoke. A pallid band sometimes lies along the eastern horizon, but east itself migrates. Shadows point uphill. At great elevation, shadows occasionally detach from their objects and continue climbing alone.

Distance is equally unreliable.

Some summits require months to reach and can still be touched by extending a hand from the basin floor. A hut visible nearby may remain the same apparent size after a week of approach. Two peaks separated by a narrow notch may stand on opposite sides of the region, while valleys hundreds of leagues apart share the same echo. Travelers navigate by sound, counting the delay between pulses in the mountain roar. But the echoes sometimes return before the initiating sound, warning of cliffs that are only about to form.

These warnings matter. New land arrives without spectacle. For decades there may be an empty gulf between two plateaus. Then a faint plane appears in it, transparent as breath on glass. Dust begins to cling to the plane. Mineral fibers root along its underside. With each century-long peal of thunder, the plane gains thickness and color. Eventually it becomes a pass, already bearing a weathered footpath and perhaps the ruins of a hut older than the stone supporting it.

This is how the Heights expand: not outward, but into places that afterward claim always to have been present.

Erosion has barely begun. The first winds are weak and indecisive, wandering in short loops. They move dust from one side of a stone to the other, then return it. Cracks are fresh, edges unsoftened. Yet here and there stand isolated arches, fluted pillars, and needles so exquisitely worn they seem ancient. These are not remnants of erosion. They are prototypes—forms the land is considering. Nearby cliffs may copy them imperfectly over the next thousand years.

Coolness lies over everything. Not frost, not winter, but the cold of material awaiting a final state. Bare skin loses warmth slowly. Breath does not fog. The vegetation is colder than stone and sometimes leaves numb patterns where it brushes the hand. In the highest gardens, temperature ceases to be evenly distributed: one side of the body may stand in mild air while the other faces a cold so profound it feels like an opening rather than a condition.

There are places where the breakdown is worse.

One basin contains a forest visible only in the spaces between its trunks. On a western wall, every crack opens onto the same small room, no matter how shallow the crack. A stairway near the central escarpment has been carved upward for thirty-seven steps; the thirty-eighth is always the step currently being climbed. Above it, patient figures in reed cloaks can sometimes be seen ascending without gaining height.

And beneath the unending roar, there is a second sound.

It is almost silence, but not quite: a measured interval, recurring every several days, in which the landscape seems to listen. Hushreeds close. Glass forests bow toward the ground. The impossible shadows stop climbing. For one fraction of a moment the thunder drops away, revealing how immense the open country truly is.

In that interval, a single sharp knock can be heard from somewhere under every mountain.

Then the roar returns, a little deeper than before. A new ledge has appeared on a distant face. A pale shoot stands where there was bare stone. One of the scattered huts has acquired another room.

The Heights have advanced, slowly and deafeningly, toward whatever they are becoming.
