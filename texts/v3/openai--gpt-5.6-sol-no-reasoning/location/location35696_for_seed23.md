The town occupies a high bowl in the mountains, no wider than a short walk from rim to rim. Its walls are the bowl itself: steep shelves of pale stone, rounded and fluted by water until they resemble the inside of an enormous, weathered shell. There is no forest above it and scarcely any grass. Black moss occupies the seams. Low silver weeds grow where roofs meet rock, their leaves no larger than fingernails. Otherwise the slopes are bare—cold stone, wet stone, ice-glazed stone, all glaring beneath a white and relentless sky.

Water is everywhere, but nowhere broad enough to be called a lake. It emerges in threads from the walls, gathers under doors, runs down stairs, beads on ropes and skin, and drips from projecting ledges in perfect rows. The ground is paved not against the water but for it. Every lane is a channel. Every stair has a groove worn along one side. Courtyards are shallow basins crossed by stepping stones, and the lower chambers of houses are built with openings through which streams pass freely.

The water has been doing this longer than the town has existed. It has softened corners, hollowed thresholds, and polished generations of hand-cut masonry to the sheen of bone. Some buildings are so eroded that they appear grown rather than constructed. Doorways lean into the direction of drainage. Pillars are narrow at the middle where spray strikes them. Faces carved over old lintels have lost all expression except deep eye sockets filled with clear water.

The settlement is dense, almost without empty space. Houses crowd one another in ascending tiers, sharing walls, roofs, gutters, ladders, and foundations. Most are made from flat local stone fitted without mortar, with seals of moss, felt, clay, and animal grease pressed into the gaps. Their roofs are low and massively weighted, yet they shine with runoff and thin ice. Covered bridges span lanes scarcely wide enough for two people. Passages burrow beneath houses and emerge in other courtyards. There are niches within niches: storage holes, sleeping recesses, shrines no larger than a cupped hand, pens built under stairs, platforms suspended over channels.

Nothing is mechanically powered. The most intricate devices are wooden sluice gates, stone counterweights, bone pegs, rawhide hinges, basket traps, and toothed waterwheels cut by hand. Metal is rare: a few greenish knives, copper needles, fishhooks passed through families. Yet the town possesses an old sophistication. It understands pressure, overflow, freeze, thaw, and the patience of gravity. The whole settlement is a water instrument assembled from crude materials and corrected over centuries.

It is never still.

Streams change course hourly. A chute clogs with ice, a cistern overtops, a retaining wall gives way, and suddenly three lanes flood while another runs dry. Residents hurry across roofs to pull woven screens from spillways or hammer wedges into gates. Whole facades can be dismantled and rebuilt before dusk. Walls are made with deliberate weak points so that floodwater will take an empty shed rather than a sleeping house. No one expects a street to remain where it was. Addresses are given by watershed: above the blue spill, behind the goat drain, two ladders below the cold spring.

This turbulence ought to make the place thunderous, but it does not. The porous rock swallows heavy sound. Falling water slips into deep holes lined with moss and vanishes with a breath. People speak close to one another and rarely raise their voices. Footsteps are muffled by wet felt wrapped around boots. Even collapsing masonry produces less noise than it should—a brief grinding pressure, a soft concussion, then the tiny ticking of stones finding new positions.

The brightness intensifies the quiet. The high air is clean after near-constant weather, and every wetted surface throws back the sky. Sunlight arrives hard and blue-white through breaks in racing cloud. It flashes from channels, ice films, wet feathers, fish scales, and the pale eyes of the inhabitants. There are few shadows at midday. Light enters from above, below, and through reflections in the flooded lanes until the cramped town seems illuminated from inside its stones.

The cold is profound. Water remains liquid only because it rises from depths warmed by the mountain, but it loses that warmth quickly. Steam gathers in low courtyards, condenses on walls, and freezes higher up in translucent skins. A person can place one hand in a warm spring and the other on ice. Buildings breathe visibly through roof vents. At night, entire cascades stiffen into ribbed pillars; by noon they break loose in dazzling sheets.

Animals occupy every layer of the town. There are more of them than plants, and perhaps more than people.

Small white fish swim through street channels and shelter beneath thresholds. Flat, many-legged crustaceans cling to submerged walls. Pale eels knot themselves in warm cisterns. Black mountain birds nest in chimney cavities and hop silently along gutters, taking insects from the vapor. Thick-furred rodents live in communal warrens behind retaining walls, tolerated because they sense underground shifts before stone collapses. Blue-backed salamanders sleep under kitchen slabs. Horned goats, compact and shaggy, stand on roofs like carved ornaments and descend by routes no human can follow.

At certain hours, clouds of minute winged creatures rise from the warm water. They should perish immediately in the cold, but each carries a droplet around itself—a trembling sphere that catches the sun. For several minutes the town fills with floating points of brilliance. Birds pass through them with open beaks. Then the insects descend, and their discarded droplets strike roofs like the faintest rain.

The larger creatures are stranger. Beneath the central quarter lies a network of flooded caverns. Something moves there, slowly enough to be mistaken for geology. It pushes water uphill. At irregular intervals the springs pulse in sequence from one side of town to the other, and residents stop work to count the pulses. Seven is ordinary. Eight means gates must be opened. Nine means everyone climbs.

No one speaks of ten.

The town’s oldest structure stands over the principal spring: a circular building with no roof and walls worn smooth by mist. It might once have been a temple, reservoir, council chamber, or all three. Its floor is a pool of impossible brightness. Pebbles at the bottom cast shadows upward, onto the sky. Objects lowered into it do not always return with the same shape. A straight stick comes back gently curved. A dead fish may return swimming for a few breaths. A knotted cord comes back untied but remembering its knots: when stretched between two hands, it bends around absent loops.

The people use this water sparingly. They do not worship it, exactly. They maintain it.

That is the governing principle of the town. Nothing here is believed to be permanent, only maintainable. A house is not an object but an agreement among stones, water, weather, neighbors, and time. Ownership extends only as long as one keeps runoff from harming those below. If a roof sheds ice into another family’s courtyard, the roof is partly theirs. If a channel feeds three cisterns, all three households must clear it. Children learn drainage maps before family histories.

The law is cut into thin slate tiles and hung where water can touch them. As the inscriptions erode, their force diminishes. A rule lasts only while it remains legible. Important laws must therefore be recarved again and again, and each recutting changes a word or a line. There are no ancient commandments here, only ancient revisions.

Beyond the bowl, the mountain falls away abruptly into cloud. Paths leave by narrow notches, but landslides erase them often. From the highest ledges the town looks like a complicated handful of gray objects caught in a stone cup—roofs, bridges, pens, ladders, people, birds, steam, ice, and shining water packed almost too closely to distinguish.

The weather circles the rim at extraordinary speed. Snow may fall on one quarter while another burns in clear sun. Curtains of rain cross the town in less than a minute. Wind rarely reaches the streets directly; instead it exerts pressure from above, making door skins flex and pools dimple without apparent cause. Clouds pour over the rim and fill the upper lanes, while lower courtyards remain bright. Sometimes a rainbow forms vertically in the central shaft of mist and stands there like a colored post.

In the clearest light, the erosion of the bowl reveals its settled form. Every shelf, hollow, and channel belongs to a pattern too complete to seem accidental. The town has adapted to it, but did not make it. Seen from above, the roads follow branching lines like vessels. The houses cluster in the spaces between them like scales. The central spring occupies the place where an eye might be.

During the rare moments when all gates are open, every stream turns inward. Water races through the narrow town, carrying chips of ice, feathers, bowls, fish, toys, and urgent reflections. People climb ladders. Goats leap to higher roofs. Birds fill the white sky without calling. Walls flex; bridges lift; stairs vanish under foam. The entire settlement appears on the verge of being dismantled.

Then the hidden caverns exhale.

The water drains through the central pool. Loose stones settle into new arrangements. A few rooms are gone, a new channel has opened, and fish glitter in courtyards that were dry that morning. Steam rises everywhere. The sun returns with devastating brilliance.

Before the wet walls have stopped trembling, the inhabitants descend with baskets, levers, cords, and wooden mallets. Quietly, rapidly, they begin learning the town again.
