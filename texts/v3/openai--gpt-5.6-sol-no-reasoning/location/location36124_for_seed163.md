The high country begins above the last belt of ordinary forest. From there the land opens into a broad, cool tableland, bright beneath a sky repeatedly rinsed by rain. Its horizons are far but not immense: a linked country of shallow basins, green escarpments, wet saddles, and pale ridges, large enough to contain weather but still legible from any height. On clear mornings, sunlight crosses the whole plateau at once. Every leaf and standing pool answers.

It is difficult to say where the ground ends and vegetation begins. Much of the plateau has no exposed soil. Living mats several meters thick cover the young rock: moss, sedge, liverwort, low fern, and dense rafts of interlocking roots. Some mats are firm enough to carry a truck. Others flex underfoot, squeezing cold water through their seams. In the hollows, plant matter spans ponds like green skin, translucent at the edges and dark where old stems have accumulated beneath it.

Trees are scarce but vegetation is not. The climate and wind keep growth low, horizontal, and continuous. Shrubs travel along the ground in braided limbs. Cushion plants form broad, hard islands patterned with tiny white flowers. On sheltered slopes, giant rhubarb-like leaves rise shoulder-high in loose colonies, each leaf holding a lens of rainwater. The steepest faces wear veils of trailing roots. These hang from the cliff brows, catching mist and directing it downward in thousands of silver threads.

The stone beneath is newly exposed and barely articulated. It has not yet acquired the deep cuts and elaborate ruins of an old mountain landscape. Ridges remain blunt. Valleys are wide, shallow beginnings rather than finished channels. Here and there the vegetal covering has split to reveal raw gray slabs with sharp mineral faces, stained only faintly by iron and the first films of lichen. Water runs over them without having carved more than narrow grooves. Pebbles are uncommon. Sand gathers only in a few pale fans below recent fractures.

Water is everywhere, though seldom in great depth. It rests near the surface of the living ground, trembles in hoofless pools, condenses on cables and leaves, and wells from the rock wherever pressure finds an opening. Rain passes often across the plateau in bright curtains. Even afterward, under hard sunlight, the air remains full of fine suspended droplets. Cliffs continue weeping for days. Shallow streams do not so much cross the land as spread through it, dividing into countless clear filaments among roots before collecting again.

The plateau’s overwhelming sound comes from machinery.

Far apart across the green are industrial pumping stations: low buildings of corrugated alloy, concrete footings, vents, tanks, and tall skeletal frames. No town surrounds them. Each stands alone in a cleared patch, linked to the next by narrow service tracks and pipelines raised above the soft ground. Their paint—white, safety yellow, faded red—is startling against the vegetation.

The stations draw water from deep fissures and redistribute it through the plateau. Their turbines turn at a low but immense pitch that can be felt in the sternum before it is heard. Pressure valves discharge with prolonged roars. Cooling fans beat the air. At intervals, a pump engages somewhere beyond sight, and its vibration travels through the saturated plant mat until pools ripple kilometers away.

Close to a station, conversation is impossible. The noise does not feel quick or frantic; it is continuous, patient, almost geological. Great engines maintain steady revolutions for months. Their sound fills the basins and reflects from wet escarpments, accumulating into a broad metallic thunder. Even at the remotest overlooks there is usually a hum beneath the wind, as if the mountain itself were powered.

The industrial system is sparse but regional. Pipes vanish into root beds, cross gullies on simple trestles, and enter rock through concrete collars. Substations stand on isolated knolls, their ceramic insulators shining after rain. A cable railway climbs one eastern face, moving freight so slowly that its platform can appear motionless for minutes at a time. There are occasional maintenance huts, instrument masts, and old foundations being reclaimed by moss. Nothing is crowded. Between installations lie long kilometers of wet green openness.

The machinery does not dominate visually as completely as its sound suggests. Vegetation climbs its supports and grows in roof gutters. Moss pads the pipelines without seeming to corrode them. Some structures have been deliberately designed as trellises: condensation fins feed curtains of fern, runoff channels empty into planted basins, and warm exhaust vents support patches of unfamiliar broadleaf growth. From a distance, an operating station may look like a handful of pale geometric objects set down in a limitless botanical surface.

There are few animals. Small black midges appear above the pools during still intervals. Pale moths rest under metal ledges. Once in a while a narrow-winged bird crosses an entire basin without landing, its cry lost at once in the engines. There are no herds to open paths, no chorus of frogs, almost no visible grazing. Leaves fall and soften where they lie. Seeds travel primarily by wind and water. Pollination is handled by the rare insects, by shaking rain, and by mechanisms within the flowers themselves.

The absence of animals gives the vegetation an untouched completeness. Nothing has bitten the edges from the great leaves. Fruits ripen and collapse beneath their parent stems. Moss towers stand untrampled. Delicate films persist over puddles until the next downpour breaks them. Human tracks, where they exist, are more conspicuous than wildlife trails: parallel tire impressions slowly filling with water and bright new growth.

The light is exceptionally strong for a wet, cool place. Cloud layers are thin and mobile, and the high elevation leaves little haze between sky and ground. After rain, reflections multiply until brightness seems to come from below as well as above. Pools flash white. Droplets bead every blade. The metal installations throw small intolerable glints. Shadows remain blue and cold, especially under the suspended root mats, but exposed slopes can be dazzling.

Temperature changes are mild and deliberate. Nights bring silver condensation and occasional thin ice around the pools. By midday, the ice loosens from the leaves and floats in clear fragments. Warm seasons do not brown the plateau; they only deepen its greens and increase the frequency of flowering. In colder months, growth slows but does not vanish. Water continues to move under the plant cover, and the pumps continue to turn.

Nearly everything here proceeds gradually. The mountains are young, but their making is not spectacular. Stone rises by imperceptible measures. Roots investigate new cracks. Water begins channels that may take centuries to become streams. A station abandoned at the edge of one basin has been subsiding into the living mat for decades: first the steps disappeared, then the lower walls, then half the windows. Its roof remains visible, supporting a miniature bog that flowers brilliantly each year.

The principal rule of the plateau is that weight must be distributed. Concentrated loads puncture the root substrate and sink into cold black water. Buildings stand on wide rafts or many shallow pilings. Vehicles use broad mesh wheels. Walkways are latticed so plants can grow through them. Travelers learn to read differences in green: silver sedge indicates firmness; glossy moss conceals depth; red-stemmed cushions gather where stone lies close beneath.

Another rule is that water cannot be permanently owned. A channel cut in one season closes with roots in the next. A drained basin swells again after rain. Pipes carry enormous volumes, but seepage and condensation continually return a portion to the plateau. The factories may guide the water, lift it, cool it, pressurize it, and send it over the escarpments to settlements below, yet the high country remains saturated. The system seems less like an imposition than a temporary organ grown within a much larger body.

Seen from the western rim, the landscape is an expanse of brilliant green chambers separated by low raw ridges. White rain moves across one basin while sunlight floods the next. Pump towers stand at wide intervals, exhaling vapor. Pipelines draw thin straight lines through terrain otherwise without geometry. The noise rises from everywhere—turbines, fans, spillways, pressure gates—vast enough to erase small sounds, steady enough to become a kind of silence.

Under that thunder, the plateau is still being assembled. Water lays down dark organic layers. Plants turn open pools into ground. Young streams test their courses over unweathered stone. Foundations settle by millimeters. Roots enter concrete seams. Nothing appears hurried, yet nothing is finished. The whole bright highland hangs between marsh and mountain, garden and utility, raw surface and inhabited earth.
