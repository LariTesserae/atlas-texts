At first it appears to be a plain.

It lies low beneath a white sky, extending farther than distance can properly account for: level, brilliantly lit, almost without shadow. There are no hills in the familiar sense, no exposed rock, no rivers, no ponds or damp hollows. Nothing glitters with water. The horizon is a thin green seam, though one may walk toward it for weeks and find it no nearer.

The ground is alive.

Not merely covered in vegetation, but made of it—an immense load-bearing tissue engineered into floors, roads, terraces, foundations, ducts, and buried machinery. Its upper surface resembles overlapping leaves the size of city squares. They are pale jade, waxy and cool, fitted so precisely along their edges that little dust can enter the seams. Underfoot they yield by a fraction, then stiffen. Pressure travels away through them in dim concentric changes of color, informing structures hundreds of kilometers distant that something has stepped there.

There is no soil. Beneath the leaves are further leaves, then braided trunks, cellulose beams, lignin vaults, cork insulation, root cables, fungal-looking conduits that are not fungi but specialized organs of the same designed organism. Descending through a maintenance pore reveals no geological bottom—only successions of constructed life: green decks, black structural fibers, amber resin chambers, pale growth foundries, and deeper still the old machine strata where pistons the diameter of villages move once every few days.

The place is called many things, but none of its names translate cleanly. The closest is **the Fabricated Prairie**, provided *prairie* is understood to mean a continent assembled from botanical components, and *fabricated* to mean both manufactured and untrue.

It has never rained.

Its plants contain almost no water. Their chemistry runs on chilled oils, fluorinated sap, volatile salts, and carbon gels that remain flexible in the cool air. What looks like dew at dawn is a bloom of clear resin extruded to repair microscopic fractures. It hardens quickly beneath the fierce light, giving older surfaces a glazed, weathered finish. Broken stalks do not bleed; they expose bundles of silver-green filaments carrying slow electrical charge and fragrant solvent.

The atmosphere is dry enough to preserve a fingerprint for centuries. Breath disappears without mist. Exposed organic matter does not rot so much as become lacquered, then incorporated.

Every visible plant is also an industrial object.

There are forests of columnar growth towers whose ribbed trunks house distillation stacks. Their crowns spread into enormous light-catching vanes, thin as petals and bright as polished bone. There are low fields of conveyor vines carrying seed-capsules from one horizon to another. Along them, fruitlike containers travel in orderly pulses: sealed bulbs full of enzymes, replacement organs, machine spores, compressed gases, pigments, and coded knots of hereditary material.

Groves are factories. Meadows are storage systems. Flowers are instruments.

Some blossoms turn all day to follow sources of light that cannot be seen. Their faces are crowded with mirrored stamens, lens-petals, antennae, calibration hairs. At irregular intervals a whole province of them opens at once. No sound accompanies this movement. Millions of structures, each larger than a house, unfold in complete silence and flood the plain with reflected brilliance. For several minutes the landscape appears to be made of white fire. Then the flowers close, and somewhere deep below, a generation of power cells begins to swell.

The few conventional machines are old and partly digested by their surroundings. Gantries stand wrapped in muscular ivy. Rail lines flex like tendons. Extraction rigs kneel on many jointed legs and insert ceramic drills into mature resin basins. Their motors are electrically driven, meticulously insulated, and nearly silent. A person nearby might hear only a faint granular clicking, like frost settling on glass.

Nothing here uses combustion. Flame is considered an ecological catastrophe and perhaps a logical error.

The coolness is constant but not dead. It gathers in broad currents a few meters above the plain. Air slides through the vegetative architecture in immense, slow circulations generated by thermal petals and buried fans. These winds are usually too weak to feel. Their passage is visible instead: fields change angle in ordered bands, leaf after leaf tilting by a degree, until a dark green line crosses the continent. Sometimes two such lines meet and form a standing crease in the air. Loose seeds drift toward it and remain suspended, accumulating into impossible vertical shoals.

This is the closest thing the Prairie has to weather.

Other cycles pass through it. There are conductivity seasons, when the ground carries charge and hair rises from the skin. There are pale months when the canopy withdraws pigments to its buried reservoirs and the world becomes ivory. There are construction fronts: kilometers-wide zones where the surface softens, buckles, and grows new architecture overnight. Columns thrust upward without noise. Bridges splice themselves between distant crowns. Walls inflate from leaf membranes and become woody by morning. Doors appear in places that had no interior the previous day.

All of it is built, but the builders are not readily found.

There are no untouched places. Even apparent decay has been designed. The weathered plains, with their split bark plates and sun-clouded resins, are maintained as sacrificial surfaces. Erosion here is a manufacturing stage. Wind abrades a layer, abrasion exposes catalytic fibers, and those fibers trigger the growth beneath. Age is not failure but a tool the landscape uses to machine itself.

Across very old districts, repeated wearing has produced shallow basins tens of kilometers wide. Since there is no water, they hold light instead. Their interiors are lined with millions of upright transparent blades that refract the bright sky downward. Looking into one is like looking into a lake, except that the apparent depths contain inverted factories and forests that do not exist above. At night—if night occurs—the stored radiance returns gradually, and the basins glow.

Night is uncertain here.

The sky dims on a schedule, but not everywhere at once. Darkness advances in geometric parcels: a square shadow, perfectly edged, settles over a region; farther away a long black band cuts from horizon to horizon; elsewhere daylight persists for months. The plants obey whichever darkness reaches them. Some fold. Some illuminate. Some become transparent, revealing their interior machinery as grids of moving green sparks.

Travelers eventually notice that the shadows do not always agree with the objects casting them. A tower may send its shadow east in the morning and straight upward at noon. Dense groves sometimes cast rectangular darkness onto empty air. On rare occasions, a person sees their own shadow continuing after they have stopped.

This is accepted as a property of mature landscapes.

Distance is similarly unreliable. Near things remain measurable; the body can trust ten paces, perhaps a hundred. Beyond that, the Prairie begins rearranging scale. A grove that appears a kilometer away may be microscopic structures repeated so densely they imitate a forest. A white tower on the horizon may actually surround the observer, its inner wall mistaken for sky. The landscape is vast not only in extent but in nesting: plains lie within leaves that lie within plains. Industrial districts are sometimes discovered inside seed husks, operating at full continental scale. No one agrees whether these are miniatures, folded spaces, or the original world seen from an incorrect side.

The ecology is correspondingly difficult to divide into individuals.

Most of the vegetative mass may be one organism, though different regions resist one another like competing species. Root systems conduct slow territorial wars deep in the structural decks. They reroute voltage, secrete inhibitors, counterfeit each other’s growth signals. On the surface this conflict appears as color: a copper stain moving across green fields; sudden rings of blue foliage; long borders where leaves stand edgewise, forming defensive palisades.

Pollination is performed mainly by the environment itself. Electric winds lift grains of metallic pollen. Conveyor vines exchange sealed gamete capsules at junctions. Towers broadcast genetic sequences as modulated light. Some flowers are pollinated by their own reflections in the luminous basins.

Animals are rare enough to seem accidental.

Small, pale things occasionally emerge from inspection apertures. They have too many hinged legs and bodies coated in soft felt, useful for collecting conductive dust. They may be native fauna or maintenance devices that have learned hunger. They move without calls, eating defective buds and snipping exposed fibers with mouthparts like ceramic shears.

Far out on the plain, there are reports of larger creatures: slow silhouettes crossing the horizon on stilted limbs. They resemble browsing animals until they stop beside a growth tower and connect to it with cables. Years later they detach and continue. Where they have stood, rings of unfamiliar flowers rise.

No birds occupy the sky. Now and then something passes high overhead, enormous and translucent, but it may be a detached layer of atmosphere rather than an animal.

The quiet is the most oppressive feature.

Such a place should roar. It contains planetary circulations, continuous construction, presses, pumps, harvest lines, electrical substations grown into orchards, roots forcing apart hardened decks. Yet almost every mechanism operates below hearing. The air carries only small sounds: the tick of resin curing; the dry brush of a million leaves adjusting; an occasional deep tone too low to hear directly, sensed in the teeth.

Speech does not travel well. Words are absorbed by velvety foliage and returned later in altered form. A shouted name may emerge days afterward from a flower several hundred kilometers away. Often it returns with one syllable replaced. For this reason, inhabitants—if the quiet figures seen tending distant structures are inhabitants—communicate by reflected light and pressure patterns tapped into the ground.

The Prairie listens through every surface.

Stand still long enough and nearby leaves turn toward you. Remain longer and shallow grooves form around your feet, not imprisoning but measuring. A thin, bright shoot may grow beside your hand and develop an object matched to your grip: a tool, a key, a fruit, or an anatomically accurate replica of a bone you have never broken.

The place manufactures responses before it understands questions.

Its governing rule seems to be that nothing inert may remain inert. Dropped metal acquires bark. Glass grows veins. Footprints become rows of specialized seedlings reproducing the pressure history of the step. Abandoned machinery is not overgrown but translated piece by piece into living equivalents. Even empty space is treated as unfinished material. Vines stretch across gaps, transparent membranes divide volumes, pollen maps currents too fine to see.

Yet the Prairie is not lush in the sense of abundance without discipline. It is dense, but ordered; exuberant, but machined. Every leaf has a tolerance. Every tendril participates in load distribution. Every forest is aligned to specifications whose authors may no longer exist. The greenery has inherited industry so completely that production has become its form of photosynthesis.

What it produces is less clear.

Seed vaults fill and empty. Vast pistons cycle under the plain. Completed structures are folded into buds and sent along tracks toward inaccessible horizons. Sometimes the entire landscape pauses: leaves flatten, current ceases, distant machines hold mid-stroke. In that perfect silence, a white line appears across the sky, as if a seam has opened in the world’s enclosure.

The line widens.

Beyond it is not space, nor another sky, but a brighter field of vegetation extending at right angles to reality. For a few moments its forests can be seen growing sideways, their roots sunk into daylight. Then the seam closes, the deep engines resume, and every flower on the continent rotates toward the place where it vanished.

Perhaps the Prairie is constructing an escape.

Perhaps it is the interior of something still growing.

Or perhaps construction, growth, and escape are merely three words for the same process here: the endless replacement of an impossible world with one that is almost, but never quite, able to contain itself.
