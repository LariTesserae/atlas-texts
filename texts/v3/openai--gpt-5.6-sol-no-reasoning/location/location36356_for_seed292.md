The range lies beneath the sea.

Its summits begin far below the shipping lanes, where daylight has thinned to a cold blue radiance, and rise for thousands of meters from the abyssal plain. They are not sharp mountains. Time has worked too thoroughly on them for that. Their flanks are rounded, terraced, and deeply furrowed, the remains of an ancient volcanic chain worn down by currents long after its fires went out. Valleys broad enough to hold cities pass between the peaks. Above them, the ocean continues upward like another range made entirely of moving water.

The highest summit almost reaches the surface. During storms, troughs expose its black crown for seconds at a time. Foam tears across it; then the next wave buries it again. Lower peaks bear forests.

These forests are rooted to stone but never still. Kelp rises from every shelf where light reaches, with trunks thick as a person’s thigh and fronds streaming fifty meters down-current. On the upper ridges it grows in continuous canopies. Seen from below, the canopy is a dark green ceiling fretted with bright windows, flexing under the passage of waves. Seen from within, it is a crowded vertical country: stalks, holdfasts, hanging blades, suspended silt, schools of fish, and ropes of bubbles all occupying the same volume.

The water is cool throughout the range. Even near the surface it carries the chill of deep upwelling. At the base of the mountains it is nearly black and only a little above freezing. The cold does not make the place quiet. It makes it dense. Nutrients lifted from the abyss feed plankton blooms around the summits, turning the clearer water greenish and drawing life from hundreds of kilometers away.

Fish move through the kelp in distinct layers. Small silver species gather among the outer fronds, flashing all at once when predators turn them. Rockfish hover close to the slopes. Eels inhabit cracks in the old basalt. Crabs travel across the bottom in busy, overlapping routes, picking through shell fragments and the rain of organic debris. Seals hunt the upper forests, folding themselves between the stalks with startling delicacy. Farther out, whales pass along the submerged chain, using it as a road through the open ocean.

Their calls travel down the valleys.

Sound is one of the strongest features here. Water carries every disturbance farther than seems reasonable. The surf above is a permanent, low concussion. Loose stones roll and knock in gullies. Kelp stalks rasp against rock. Shoals make a soft collective hiss as thousands of bodies turn. Shrimp crackle from crevices like burning brush. Pumps hammer in the distance. Chains strike metal. Propellers approach long before any vessel or machine can be seen.

The loudest places are the saddles between peaks, where tides are forced through narrow passes. There the whole sea accelerates. Kelp lies nearly horizontal. Sand lifts from the bottom in pale sheets. Cold jets climb the mountain walls, collide with descending water, and break into spinning columns crowded with leaves, fish, bubbles, and grit. The currents can reverse within minutes. A calm terrace becomes a rapid; a clear channel fills with mud; a mass of fish is swept over a ridge and scattered down the far side.

Yet the underlying land barely changes. That is the contradiction of the range: the water is violently alive, while the stone has arrived at a kind of final age.

Every exposed surface is worn. Lava cliffs have been reduced to long-backed hills and blunt escarpments. Caves have widened, collapsed, and become amphitheaters. The strongest rock remains as low ribs; the softer material has gone into sediment that fills the old craters and valley floors. Pebbles are polished, shells ground fine, wreckage rounded until it resembles natural stone. Nothing keeps an edge for long. Even recent fractures quickly acquire a skin of algae, tube worms, sponge, and lime.

Human structures are sparse and small against this geography.

On one upper saddle stands a maintenance station anchored to the basalt: a compact arrangement of steel chambers, intake pipes, cable drums, and ballast feet. It was built to service the turbines set in the tidal pass. Those turbines occupy the narrowest channels—large, slow rotors in protective cages, turning whenever the current runs strongly enough. Their movement is deliberate compared with the turbulence around them. Blades pass through green water; dynamos vibrate in sealed housings; electricity travels inland through armored cables laid along the mountain slopes.

The machinery does not dominate the range. Most of it cannot be seen from one installation to the next. There is a beacon fixed to the nearly surfacing summit, a pair of pressure-resistant workshops on a ledge below, and scattered sensor towers farther down. Old cable markers emerge from the kelp. A collapsed gantry rests in a ravine, overgrown so completely that conger eels shelter inside its beams and anemones cover its warning plates.

The station’s exterior lamps are practical rather than dramatic. Their white light makes limited rooms in the water—cones full of particles, fish scales, and drifting weed. Beyond them, ordinary daylight remains dominant on the upper slopes. Sun passes through the surface in moving bands. The forest alternately brightens and dims as waves bend the light. At depth, colors narrow: reds disappear first, then golds, until rock, metal, and flesh share shades of blue-green. The lower valleys receive only a broad dusk, interrupted by instrument lights and the cold flicker of animals.

There are no impossible creatures and no supernatural ruins. Everything here belongs to familiar processes carried to their full scale: gravity, pressure, hunger, tide, corrosion, growth. Its strangeness is only the strangeness of seeing an entire mountain region occupied by water.

Storms reshape the place without reshaping it permanently. When heavy weather crosses the surface, pressure pulses descend through the water column. The upper forest thrashes. Fronds tear loose and stream across the ridges in tangled fleets. Sand boils out of hollows. Animals crowd into the lee of boulders and machinery footings. Turbines brake or turn at dangerous speed; shutters close over the station intakes; warning tones beat through the pass. For hours, the range becomes a system of collisions.

Afterward, the water clears from the top downward. Torn kelp accumulates in deep gullies. Fish return to feeding lanes. Crews emerge from the station to cut vegetation from grilles and inspect the moorings. Seals investigate them. Crabs gather wherever the storm has exposed fresh sediment. Nothing is restored to exactly what it was, but the larger order reasserts itself.

Life here depends on instability. The same currents that rip organisms from the rock also bring oxygen and food. The same storms that destroy sections of forest open light for new growth. A bare patch of basalt acquires a bacterial film, then algae, then grazing snails and juvenile kelp. Within a season it becomes crowded. Within years it is indistinguishable from the old forest around it.

The deepest valleys tell a slower story. There, beneath the reach of the kelp, pale sponges and cold-water corals form thickets on the worn slopes. Basket stars cling to exposed points. Detritus settles through the darkness like fine snow but seldom lies undisturbed; bottom currents gather it into ripples and erase those ripples again. Occasional landslides descend from the mountain flanks in clouds of black sediment. Their sound arrives first: a long grinding that grows through the water, followed by the dense, advancing dark.

Below even these valleys lies the plain, flat and cold, extending beyond the range. The mountains seem enormous from there—an entire horizon rising into dimness—yet their age is visible in their silhouette. They are subdued giants. Their original height is gone. Their craters have softened; their cliffs have fallen; their substance has been carried away grain by grain and dispersed through the ocean.

Above, all is motion: waves, forests, animals, engines, weather.

Below, the old stone waits in the shape erosion has finally allowed it to keep.
