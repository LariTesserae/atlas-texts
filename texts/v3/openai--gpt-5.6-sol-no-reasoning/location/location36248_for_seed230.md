The town occupies a shallow saddle high in the hills, where two long ridges draw close without quite meeting. It is not large. From either gate, the opposite wall can be seen through the streets, and a person walking without haste can cross the whole settlement before a kettle boils. Yet nearly every part of the saddle has been built upon. Houses, retaining walls, stairs, courts, cisterns, workshops, and roofed passages fit together so closely that the town seems less constructed than carved from one broad piece of stone.

Beyond its boundaries the slopes are open and spare. Pale grass lies flat against gray soil. Low thorn shrubs stand far apart, each surrounded by bare ground and its own scatter of brittle leaves. Here and there, dark seams of rock emerge from the hillside, rounded and cracked by long weather. There are no forests nearby and few trees of any kind. A handful grow inside the walls: slow mountain pears in sheltered courtyards, their trunks twisted, their small leaves dulled by cold.

The stone of the town is the stone of the ridge—gray-brown, fine-grained, and easily split into blocks. Centuries of handling have softened it. Stair treads dip in their centers. Doorposts carry smooth places at shoulder height. Gutters have been worn into shallow channels that shine darkly when wet. Corners exposed to the weather are blunt rather than broken, and the outer wall has settled so completely into the slope that lichen crosses from natural rock to masonry without distinction.

Nothing stands very tall. Most buildings have one story, a few have two, and all have heavy, nearly flat roofs laid with timber, brush, clay, and flagstone. From above, the town resembles a field of overlapping gray shelves. Chimneys are short to keep them out of the wind. Windows are narrow, closed with wooden shutters or stretched hide, and placed inward toward courts whenever possible. The streets follow the contour of the saddle in two slow curves, connected by steep steps and alleys barely wide enough for two laden people to pass.

Construction is everywhere, but machinery is not. Blocks are shaped with iron chisels that have been sharpened until little remains of their original length. Beams are cut with hand saws and dragged uphill by people and small, broad-backed donkeys. Doors turn on wooden pins. Roof stones are raised with rope, tripod frames, and patient labor. The town’s sophistication lies not in its tools but in accumulation: each wall improves upon an older wall, each drain joins one laid by forgotten hands, each courtyard has been repaired so many times that no single generation can be said to have made it.

Water governs the plan.

Rain comes irregularly, usually as fine cold showers that drift across the ridge for an hour and vanish. There is too little for a stream, and the surrounding ground absorbs almost none of it. Every roof therefore inclines by a degree or two toward a stone gutter. Every gutter feeds a covered channel. The channels meet beneath the streets and empty into cisterns sunk deep into the saddle’s rock.

After rain, the town changes color but scarcely changes sound. Walls deepen from ash-gray to brown. Steps become glossy. Beads of water form under lintels and along the rims of clay jars. The air smells of damp mineral dust, wool, woodsmoke, and the faint green scent of moss awakened in the drains. There is no rushing runoff, only the intermittent tapping of drops into grates and the hollow, widely spaced plunk of water entering chambers below.

The central cistern lies under the largest court. A circular well mouth, waist-high and polished by generations of hands, marks its presence. The reservoir beneath is much wider than the opening suggests: a pillared room cut into bedrock, cool and black, with a waterline that rises and falls only slightly over the years. No one descends except for repairs. Buckets return with clear, cold water that tastes faintly of stone and smoke from the roofs.

Small pockets of moisture gather elsewhere. Ferns no longer than a finger grow behind the public bathhouse. Green film lines one shaded drain. In the north wall, condensation collects in a natural fissure and feeds a basin where animals drink. These wet places are treasured without being ornamented. No fountains waste water into the air. No planted gardens spread over open ground. Herbs are kept in cracked jars: thyme, bitterleaf, a little onion, medicinal mint in the dampest courts. The pear trees are watered with wash water, one cup at a time.

The town wall encloses less for defense than for shelter. It is low and thick, following the saddle’s edge, turning the prevailing wind and holding a quieter climate within. At its outer foot the land falls away in shallow terraces, some natural, some built. Once they were cultivated more broadly. Now only a few narrow plots remain in use, planted with hardy barley and lentils. Their soil is held by stone borders and worked by short-handled hoes. The fields do not look lush even at their best. Their green is a thin veil over earth.

Erosion here has finished its dramatic work. Nothing appears freshly cut or unstable. Gullies are old and shallow. Loose stones have already found resting places. The ridges have worn down to patient forms, neither sharp nor soft, and every path seems permanent. Even the rain follows established grooves. When frost loosens a flake from a wall, it falls into a gap below as though the gap had waited for it.

Life is scarce but not absent. Small gray birds nest under roof slabs and fly out at dawn to search the slopes. Lizards appear on warm stones in the middle of the day, though the air remains cool. Moths gather near lamps, and pale cave crickets inhabit the cistern vaults. Donkeys are the most visible animals, kept in low stables near the south gate. Their bells are wrapped in cloth inside town, so that they announce themselves only by breath, hoof scrape, and the soft knock of loads against doorways.

Larger wild animals are known mostly by traces. A fox sometimes leaves prints beside the outer basin. Mountain goats are glimpsed on the western escarpment a few times each year, standing so still among the rocks that they are noticed only when they move. There are no constant animal cries. The place belongs chiefly to stone, people, and open air.

Most hours are quiet. Work produces brief, distinct sounds rather than a continuous clamor: a mason’s hammer striking three times, then stopping; grain grinding behind a wall; a loom’s wooden frame knocking at a steady pace; sandals on steps; a gate bar lowered at dusk. Voices carry easily, so people speak without shouting. Wind can be heard beyond the walls before it reaches the gates, but inside it becomes a low breath along roof edges.

Smoke rises from dung fires and small bundles of imported wood. In cold weather it hangs close over the roofs, blue and level, then disperses across the saddle. Nights are chilly in every season. Frost is common, snow less so. When snow does fall it comes thinly, outlining gutters and stair rims, and is swept into jars before the sun removes it.

There is a sense that the town has reached its proper size. It does not expand, because the saddle offers no more ground and because extending the waterworks would demand more labor than the population can spare. It does not shrink, because every empty house is eventually repaired and occupied by another branch of a family already there. Walls are dismantled only to rebuild walls. Roofs are renewed in the same lines. A collapsed stair is reset with its old stones in their old order whenever their shapes can be remembered.

The inhabitants measure the year, but change is subdued. Rain returns without transforming the slopes. Barley is planted and cut. Pears ripen small and hard. Children grow into clothes passed down from older children and learn the same paths through the covered passages. The town records births, deaths, water levels, and roof repairs on notched boards kept in a dry chamber near the well. Viewed across generations, even these records seem less like history than maintenance.

At midday, ordinary light fills the saddle. Nothing glows or broods. The stone shows its grain; the pale soil shows every footprint. Shadows gather cleanly beneath roof ledges and stairs. From the upper gate, one can see the entire place arranged below: roof planes, narrow courts, a curl of smoke, a figure carrying two buckets from the well. Beyond the wall, the high country extends in muted folds under an open sky.

The town is neither ruin nor monument. It is simply complete—weathered, inhabited, and held in balance by stonework, restraint, and a little water saved from passing clouds.
