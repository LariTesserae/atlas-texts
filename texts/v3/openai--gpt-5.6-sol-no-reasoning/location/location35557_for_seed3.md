The city occupies a mountain that has not finished becoming solid.

From the lower valleys it appears as a sparse belt of yellow windows and black gantries wrapped around an impossible height. There is no continuous slope beneath it. Instead, enormous shelves of dark stone hang one above another, separated by vertical intervals of mist. Bridges cross these intervals where the mountain has failed to provide ground. Elevator towers descend through cloud and continue below sight, their cables trembling faintly in the cold.

The uppermost districts are rarely visible. They withdraw into a dim ceiling where condensation gathers around radio masts, chimneys, and the undersides of cliffs. On clear nights, one may look up from a street and mistake the lights of higher neighborhoods for constellations. The error is understandable: some of those lights move with the stars rather than with the mountain.

The rock is young. It has risen too quickly to weather into familiar forms. There are few gullies, no deep-cut canyons, and little soil except where the inhabitants have made it. The exposed faces are blunt and unfinished: basalt still bearing broad glassy skins, pale mineral seams that end abruptly, folded strata soft enough to mark with a boot heel. Surveyors drive numbered stakes into land that did not exist on their previous visit. Foundations sometimes emerge from the ground before their buildings have been planned.

Erosion has only begun its work. Rain does not yet know where to go.

Water arrives intermittently from the cloud layer. It beads on ironwork, gathers beneath suspended roads, and trickles along seams in the stone. Every surface sweats. Condensers spread from rooftops like skeletal leaves, funneling droplets into cisterns. In the residential terraces, gutters cross one another in complicated hierarchies, because water will occasionally climb a channel instead of descending it. Old pipes are labeled not only with contents and pressure but with an arrow indicating the direction gravity was facing when they were installed.

There are no great rivers. There are temporary ones.

After rain, silver threads appear across the raw slopes and run for several hours through factory yards, stairwells, cable conduits, and planted courtyards. Some empty into reservoirs excavated within the mountain. Others reach the edge of a shelf and pour outward, thinning into rain before they touch anything below. By the next day only dark tracks remain, and colonies of translucent insects assemble along them to drink moisture from the stone.

The city has grown around these uncertain waters. Its oldest quarter stands on a broad ledge halfway up the mountain: foundries, worker barracks, markets roofed in corrugated metal, and tall masonry buildings with narrow windows. Above it, districts are threaded along switchback railways and cable lines. Below it hang machine halls bolted directly to cliff faces. Vast counterweights move inside open shafts. Freight platforms rise with ore, lumber, animals, and tanks of captured fog, disappearing into neighborhoods whose streets may lie a kilometer higher but only a few hundred meters away across empty air.

Though urban in extent, the city never feels crowded. Its structures are scattered among immense vacancies. A row of houses may end at a field of untouched black rock. A tram stop may serve only a greenhouse, a shrine, and a lift operator’s hut. Factories stand widely separated, each surrounded by cable yards and cooling towers, their furnaces glowing dull orange through the vapor. Whole mapped districts contain nothing but pylons waiting for bridges not yet built.

Construction is perpetual but unhurried. The mountain changes too slowly to be seen and too quickly to be ignored. Engineers design in decades, fastening each structure so it can be loosened and shifted when the underlying ledge grows, tilts, or develops another altitude. Buildings stand on screw-jacks taller than houses. Streets include flexible sections of timber and braided steel. Doors are rehung every few years. In public squares, brass plaques mark previous positions of the ground.

Machinery provides the city’s weather.

Compressors exhale warm fog. Ore crushers make the pavement shudder at measured intervals. Turbines hidden in the mountain turn on water, pressure differences, and perhaps the slow upward movement of the rock itself. Their bass note passes through walls more clearly than through air. Cable drums groan. Rail wheels shriek around severe curves. At shift changes, whistles call from one altitude to another until the echoes overlap and become a sustained, many-throated chord.

Yet the loudness is broad rather than frantic. Nothing here hurries. Pistons cycle once every several minutes. Freight cages may take half a day to cross the city vertically. A hammer in a distant shipyard strikes at such long intervals that travelers forget it, then start when the next blow arrives. Even landslips are slow: slabs separate from the mountain over months, leaning outward while crews dismantle whatever stands upon them.

Vegetation occupies the gentler surfaces and all places where moisture lingers. It is surprisingly rich, though never continuous. Gardens flare green around cisterns, drainage mouths, and steam vents. Mosses coat the shaded sides of retaining walls. Ferns fill abandoned rail cuts. Stunted firs grow from sacks of imported soil tethered to rooftops, their trunks bent not by wind but by local variations in downwardness.

Higher up, plants become stranger. Blue grasses root in condensation without touching stone. They form suspended meadows beneath bridge decks, hanging upside down in long, dripping mats. Pale vines follow electric cables and produce warm pods that pulse in time with the current. Orchard keepers cultivate slow-growing fruit trees in vertical cages; their fruit ripens on whichever side of the branch faces the oldest rock.

Animals are everywhere.

Flocks of black alpine birds nest in ventilation stacks and ride columns of heated air between districts. White goats move along factory roofs, licking mineral salt from chimneys. Long-bodied cats inhabit the cable tunnels, where they hunt blind rodents and moths with wings like scraps of mica. Around reservoirs gather frogs, beetles, foxes, and small antlered creatures descended from livestock but no longer willing to remain within fences.

The open air belongs to larger fauna. Broad-winged gliders circle the mountain, almost motionless, feeding on swarms of insects lofted from the wet terraces. Their shadows cross several streets at once. Some have natural bodies; others carry brass tags, harness scars, or obsolete signal lamps grown into their flesh. Nobody remembers whether they were bred, manufactured, or merely recruited.

Far below the bridges, immense shapes drift through the cloud breaks. They are seen only in portions: a ridged back passing beneath a freight line, a row of dim organs shining through vapor, something like a fin turning against the vertical air. Their migrations coincide with fluctuations in gravity. When they rise, loose tools roll uphill, reservoir surfaces tilt, and the city’s hanging gardens shed rain toward the sky.

This is accepted as part of the mountain’s ecology.

The stranger disturbances are less easily named. Distance behaves unreliably between ledges. Two towers may appear adjacent but require three days of travel between them; other places, visibly far apart, share a single doorway. Echoes occasionally arrive before their sounds. In certain alleys, snow falls upward from drains into the low clouds. A person standing at the eastern rail yard can see the western rail yard at midnight, but only as it will look twelve years later.

The municipality maintains an Office of Measurements to monitor these conditions. Its clocks are housed separately so that they cannot influence one another. Its maps are printed on thin metal sheets with hinged sections. Survey crews carry plumb lines, barometers, tuning forks, caged birds, and small bowls of water. Where all instruments disagree, they paint a white circle on the ground and build nothing there.

Animals often inhabit these circles. They seem less troubled by contradiction.

The deepest rule of the place is that the mountain is not rising into the world. The world is forming around the mountain.

This is why the geology looks embryonic, why streams have no established beds, and why horizons sometimes show pale scaffolds beyond the known ranges: incomplete valleys, provisional moons, ridges still transparent enough for stars to shine through. The city’s heavy industry is not merely extracting material. Its mills stabilize iron into lasting shapes. Its kilns teach clay to remain clay. Its engines turn at exact speeds to hold local time in agreement.

Every bridge, pipe, foundry, and apartment block adds weight to reality.

The inhabitants understand this partly as engineering and partly as civic duty. They build because inhabited things persist more reliably than empty ones. A lit room resists vanishing. A road traveled often enough acquires a dependable destination. Names are stamped into beams and repeated at public meetings. The dead are recorded in several places so that no single failure of geography can remove them.

At dusk—which is frequent, for the mountain’s upper mass blocks much of the sun—the city illuminates itself cautiously. Lamps glow behind fogged glass. Furnace mouths brighten. Signal beacons blink across the void in patient sequences. The cloud ceiling reflects these lights downward until the whole settlement seems submerged beneath dark water.

Moisture ticks from cables. Wings beat in the vapor. Somewhere high above, a train crosses a bridge that has not yet reached the opposite side.

Its lamps advance slowly through the dimness, laying down a route by traveling it.
