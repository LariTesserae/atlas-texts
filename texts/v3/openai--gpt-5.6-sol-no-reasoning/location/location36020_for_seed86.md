Beneath a limestone plain lies a cavern so broad that its far walls are lost in dimness. It is not deep. In places the roof hangs only a few body-lengths overhead; elsewhere it rises into low black domes. The floor extends for miles in shallow terraces, pale shelves, and broad fields of freshly broken stone. Everything is cool. Moisture beads steadily on the ceiling, gathers at the tips of mineral teeth, and falls.

The dripping never stops.

There are no large lakes and no powerful underground river. Water exists here in fragments: clear basins sunk between slabs, narrow runnels no wider than a hand, mud-dark hollows, and thin films creeping over the rock. A few shallow streams cross the cavern, vanish under rubble, and emerge farther on without apparent change in volume. Their channels are young, their edges still sharp. They have not yet had time to polish the floor into sweeping curves. Even the larger cuts resemble fresh wounds in the limestone—angular, pale, and interrupted by collapsed blocks.

Yet the place itself seems older than any measure of age. Nothing visibly advances. No channel deepens. No fallen slab completes its descent into gravel. The same drops strike the same pools at an unvarying pace, preserving the cavern in a permanent present.

The light comes from openings far away: narrow sinkholes, roof cracks, and shafts so long that daylight reaches the floor as a diluted gray. It has no sunrise color and no evening color. In the largest chambers it spreads just enough to separate stone from shadow. Elsewhere, pale mineral crusts and wet surfaces catch it and return a weak sheen. Pools appear brighter than the surrounding ground, like pieces of dull sky set into rock.

Vegetation survives chiefly beneath the shafts. Ferns form sparse circles around dripping stone. Moss grows in thin green-black margins along the runnels, and mats of liverwort cling to ledges that remain wet. Long, colorless roots descend through cracks in the roof, dangling over the cavern floor or reaching the pools. There are no forests below, no continuous turf. Each patch of growth stands apart, surrounded by open stone.

Animals occupy all the spaces the plants leave empty.

From a distance, dark areas that resemble gravel shift and separate into bodies. Cave crickets cover whole shelves, packed leg against leg. Beetles stream through fissures in shining files. Pale woodlice gather beneath slabs in layers. Moths rest on the ceiling in such numbers that the stone seems furred; when disturbed, they lift together, filling the chamber with soft collisions and a stormlike beating of wings.

Bats hang from the higher vaults. Their colonies stretch beyond the reach of the dim light, rank after rank folded into the roof. The cavern is never silent beneath them. There is constant chittering, scraping, squealing, and the dry rustle of membranes. Individuals drop into flight without warning and sweep low over the pools, drinking in brief touches. Their passage presses cool air against the skin.

Where streams spread into shallow sheets, eyeless fish occupy nearly every depression deep enough to cover their backs. Pale salamanders lie between submerged stones. Frogs crowd the mud around the shafts, their calls amplified until a basin the size of a room sounds like a marsh stretching to the horizon. Minute shrimp cloud the water. Leeches knot together under ledges. Blind crayfish defend narrow territories with raised claws, though there are so many of them that each territory is scarcely larger than the animal holding it.

Larger creatures move through the abundance. Snakes hunt along warm patches beneath roof openings. Foxes sometimes descend through gentle sinkholes and live for seasons on frogs, bats, and insects. In the broadest passages, herds of small, shaggy ungulates graze the scant ferns and lick mineral salts from the walls. Their hooves have worn no roads. They disperse after feeding, leaving only droppings, hair caught on stone, and a low animal odor mixed with limestone damp.

The loudness of the cavern is not caused by any single source. It is the sum of life: wings, calls, claws, splashing, mating cries, quarrels, feeding, and millions of small bodies shifting over rock. Even the quieter reaches carry a distant continuous roar, like rain heard from inside a shelter. At intervals a colony changes its posture all at once, and the sound swells through adjoining chambers without any visible event.

Nothing here has been built. There are no walls, hearths, bridges, carvings, or discarded tools. Some animals use objects in simple ways. Otterlike hunters crack shells against favored stones. Birds entering from the sinkholes probe cracks with trimmed twigs. Certain apes from the surface occasionally descend to collect blind crayfish, carrying sharpened sticks and flakes of stone, but they take these implements away again. They do not remain. The cavern accepts no settlement.

Its geography is open despite the roof. One chamber gives onto another through arches broad enough for daylight to fade gradually rather than end. Low ridges divide immense floors into drainage basins. Fields of angular debris stand like islands. In places, the ceiling has recently separated along clean fractures, exposing raw cream-colored rock above older gray surfaces. The fallen blocks below retain keen edges. Water has begun to mark them, but the marks never seem to deepen.

That is the cavern’s quiet rule: processes occur, but the landscape does not progress.

Water falls, yet the pools neither rise nor empty. Creatures feed, breed, and die, yet their numbers remain crushingly constant. Ferns uncurl beneath the shafts, though the green patches do not spread. Mud receives prints, but older prints do not blur, and somehow the floor never becomes crowded with them. Stalactites carry drops at their tips but do not lengthen. Fresh fractures remain fresh.

To cross the cavern is therefore to travel through variation without passing into change. There are drier districts where dust lies between pale boulders, wet districts full of shallow reflective water, and vast middling reaches ruled by condensation and animals. There are low ceilings alive with insects, high vaults black with bats, clear basins packed with blind swimmers, and empty stone shelves where sound arrives from miles away.

All of it persists in cool, dim equilibrium: a young cavern held permanently at the moment just after its opening, already discovered and overwhelmed by life, but not yet softened by water or altered by time.
