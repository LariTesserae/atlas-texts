At first it appears to be a drowned mountain range.

Warm black water occupies everything: the valleys, the passes, the former sky. Peaks rise through it without ever reaching an air that can be breathed. Their summits stand miles above the unseen seabed, yet remain submerged beneath a further depth that cannot be measured. There is no surface overhead—only a slow brightening, as though daylight has been strained through an ocean laid on top of another ocean.

The mountains are immensely old. Their stone has lost the sharp grammar of fracture. No cliffs remain, only long brows and shoulders polished into continuous curves, each ridge worn smooth as the back of a buried animal. Currents have traveled these slopes for so long that granite has developed grain like wood. Basalt lies in broad, soft-looking folds. Limestone has been dissolved and redeposited until whole escarpments resemble pale wax left near a flame.

Yet this is high country. Altitude persists underwater as a force distinct from depth. To ascend a slope is to enter thinner, lighter water, although no atmosphere waits above. Some summits possess their own weather: crowns of warm mist suspended within the liquid, drifting rain that falls sideways without forming bubbles, and banks of cloud dense enough to cast shadows through the sea. Lightning sometimes travels silently inside these submerged storms. It illuminates no bolt, only a sudden branching absence in which the water momentarily ceases to exist.

Between the mountains spread plateaus broad enough to make horizons. They support forests.

The trees are not quite trees. Their trunks are rooted in seams of mineral-rich stone and rise in crowded pillars, dark green, bronze, and bruised violet. Instead of bark they carry overlapping skins as fine as gills. Their crowns are composed of thousands of translucent vanes, all angled into the prevailing current. When the water turns, an entire forest slowly reverses itself. Light runs across it in dull bands as leaf after leaf pivots.

Some growths creep close to the rock: velvet films, thick mosslike mats, and pale branching gardens that calcify at the tips and dissolve at the roots, giving the impression that they are moving uphill over centuries. Others hang downward from nothing visible. Their root masses vanish into dark water above, while long ropes of foliage trail toward the peaks below. Upright and pendant forests mingle in the passes until orientation becomes uncertain. A traveler can no longer tell whether the mountain supports the vegetation or the vegetation suspends the mountain.

There are places where both seem true.

The water is densely occupied without seeming lively. Pollen, spores, seeds, mineral flakes, and nearly invisible threads fill every reach. They drift so closely that distant terrain looks painted rather than seen. Curtains of planktonic flowers open when the dimness deepens and close at the approach of light. Vast colonies of bead-sized organisms migrate along the contour lines, but each colony moves so slowly and coherently that it resembles a stain passing over the land.

Animal life is rare. The forests have few grazers and almost no visible predators. Now and then a thin creature crosses an opening: six fins folded around a transparent body, a single black organ turning inside it like a compass needle. Small eyeless things inhabit cracks in the oldest rock. They emerge only when the currents stop, test the water with fans of white cilia, and retreat before motion returns.

Farther out, over the abyssal valleys, there may be larger animals. Their presence is inferred from pressure. A whole mountainside will bow its vegetation at once, though the local water remains still. Sediment rises in enormous rings. Hours later, a low ridge appears in the distance where no ridge was before, then folds downward and is gone. Nothing calls. Nothing hunts within sight. Whatever crosses those valleys has lived long enough to make haste unnecessary.

Silence here is not the absence of activity. It is a property of the medium.

Branches flex without creaking. Stones fall without impact. Storms turn in soundless gyres. Close to a surface, one may hear a faint cellular ticking from the forests, or the dry whisper of sand being drawn through the pores of rock. Every few days a deep pulse passes through the range. It is felt in bone and stone before it is heard, if it is heard at all. The hanging forests contract. Loose particles align. The pulse repeats once, sometimes twice, from a direction that changes depending on which way one faces.

There are structures scattered across the higher plateaus, though it is difficult to say they were built.

They are low rings of fitted stone, small enough to cross in a few strokes. Their blocks have no tool marks. Some contain a central pillar; others hold only a patch of water colder than its surroundings. A few have been engulfed by roots, but the roots bend around their openings with an avoidance too consistent to be accidental. These circles occur thousands of miles apart in stone of different ages, always at precisely the same altitude—not the same depth, but the same degree of *highness*, a distinction the landscape enforces.

Near them, gravity loses its certainty.

Sand may settle along vertical faces. Shed leaves fall toward the nearest summit. A person swimming across a ring’s boundary would feel the mountain rotate beneath them until the plateau became a wall, then a ceiling, while the surrounding sea remained unchanged. Leaving from the opposite side does not always restore the former direction. This is how some pendant forests may have begun: ordinary woods stranded on an abandoned down.

The oldest plateaus are more unstable. Distance there has eroded along with stone. A grove that appears close may require days to reach; a remote ridge may touch the hand if approached backward. Two valleys can occupy the same depression without meeting. From one angle there is a forested basin. From another, there is a trench descending into blue-black depth. Both receive the same falling sediment, which vanishes at the shared boundary and arrives in each place complete.

Time has weathered unevenly as well. Certain slopes are fixed in an ancient instant. Clouds hang unmoving over them; leaves remain halfway through a turn. Yet passage through these still regions is possible. The swimmer moves, but does not disturb the water and leaves no wake. On emerging, spores that had settled on their skin may have grown into mature fans, while a broken twig carried in the hand is still in the act of splitting.

Elsewhere the landscape changes continuously. Currents peel mineral skins from one mountain and lay them over another. Forests uproot in coordinated waves, drift across valleys, and anchor to new slopes. Thermal rivers climb from fissures, warm enough to shimmer though there is no air. They bear dissolved metals that bloom into red and gold clouds, feeding sudden gardens. These gardens live for an hour, release black seeds, then collapse into transparent pulp. The seeds may remain dormant for ten thousand years.

The climate turns in long cycles. During the bright season, the upper water takes on a murky green radiance and the forests spread their vanes wide. Growth becomes dense enough to close the passes. Warm currents ascend from below, carrying the scent of iron, salt, and something like rain on soil, though no dry soil exists here.

Then comes the darker season. The overhead glow contracts into isolated patches, like distant holes in a roof. Vegetation folds close to trunks and stone. Clouds sink into the valleys and become heavy, granular water that pours downslope in slow avalanches. In this season the small animals disappear, the stone rings frost over with bubbles that never detach, and the unseen valley-crossers move nearer to the peaks.

There is no obvious edge to the drowned range. Travel in any direction leads to more highlands, more plateaus, more immense basins crowded with dim vegetation. Maps cannot hold it. Mountains shift position between observations, not rapidly, but with geological calm. A peak may stand to the east for a century and afterward have always stood west. The forests adapt without record of the change.

Nor is there proof that this place lies beneath an ocean. It may be that water is the fundamental substance here, and stone is what sometimes condenses from it. The mountains could be temporary—the durable phase of a liquid world dreaming of solidity. Their age does not disprove this. On the contrary, their smoothness suggests they have spent eternity approaching a final shape and have nearly reached it.

Nearly, but not quite.

Erosion continues. Forests migrate. Silent weather circles the summits. Rare bodies pass through the abyss, bending miles of growth in their wake. The old rings preserve their impossible altitude. Gravity changes its mind around them. Whole valleys overlap and separate.

Everything is active, but nothing is hurried.

In the dim warm water, the ancient landscape goes on settling into a form that reality can no longer entirely contain.
