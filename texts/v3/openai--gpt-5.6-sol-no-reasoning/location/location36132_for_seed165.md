At the highest rim of the world lies a valley with no visible way down.

It is not a narrow place, though the mountains around it rise so steeply that distance is difficult to judge. The floor extends for many miles beneath a ceiling of dim, unmoving cloud. Escarpments stand on every side: black shelves, pale scree, cliffs banded with old ice. Their summits disappear rather than recede, cut off at a common height as if the upper world has been erased. Nothing crosses that boundary. Snow cast upward by the wind vanishes there without falling back.

The cold is deep but not violent. It has completed its work. Stones do not split further; ice does not advance or withdraw. The whole basin has settled into a finished state, weathered to forms so old that they seem less carved than remembered.

Its slopes are furrowed by dry watercourses. Rounded stones lie fitted into channels, and overhangs bear scallops where ancient rain hollowed them. Yet the ancient rain is also present. Beneath certain ledges, drops hang in rows from the rock, silver and swollen, never falling. In the basin’s lowest pockets are pools no wider than houses, dark enough to resemble openings. Their surfaces carry permanent rings from impacts that do not occur. A hand placed in one would find moving water, cold currents winding around the fingers, though the surface remains rigid and unbroken.

Moisture gathers everywhere without traveling. Frost beads the shaded faces of boulders. Condensation clouds the inner sides of stone hollows. Thin snow occupies cracks but no exposed ground, as if every flake knew in advance where shelter would be. Along one wall, a waterfall descends from the cloud layer to a shelf of basalt. It neither freezes nor reaches the shelf. The column of water hangs white and turbulent in the air, always falling through the same distance. Its roar fills the valley.

That roar is the first great sound, but not the only one. There is a continuous thunder under the ground, too regular to be weather and too broad to have a source. Cliffs answer it in long bass notes. Scree slopes hiss without shifting. At intervals the suspended waterfall produces the unmistakable crash of striking stone, though the gap beneath it remains empty. Echoes travel strangely here. A sound returns before it is made, then again afterward, weaker but closer. The valley is empty enough that these noises should escape into distance. Instead they accumulate.

There is no wind. Straps, hair, loose fur, and suspended grit remain still. Nevertheless, the air bellows in the ears, and high above, stationary streamers of snow curve around invisible obstacles.

Vegetation occurs only in isolated concessions. Rust-colored lichen grows on the underside of rocks but not their upper faces. Small mats of black moss surround the pools, each mat a nearly perfect circle, crisp at the edge. Between them may be miles of naked mineral ground. In sheltered seams, translucent blades rise from grit—three or four together, no taller than a finger. They are cold and hard, with bubbles sealed inside them. Whether they are grass, ice, or the fossil of a plant cannot be determined by breaking one: every broken blade is found whole when examined again.

Nothing blooms. Nothing decays.

The few animals have adapted not to scarcity but to stillness. Pale, heavy-bodied birds stand on inaccessible shelves. They have narrow heads and wings too long for their bodies, yet they are never seen in flight. Their cries cross the basin frequently, sometimes from empty air. If watched for hours, a bird does not blink or turn; look away and it may be standing on another cliff miles distant.

Around the pools are prints like those of a small hoofed animal. They form short sequences—six steps, nine, sometimes thirteen—and stop in open ground. No sequence is ever fresh, but meltwater continually wells in the deepest impressions. On rare occasions a white shape can be seen far out on the valley floor. It is low to the ground and motionless. Approaching does not make it larger. Retreating does not make it smaller. Eventually a ridge or boulder passes in front of it, though neither the observer nor the obstruction seems to have moved.

There are scattered structures, if structures is the right word.

On a terrace above the largest pool stands a ring of upright slabs. The stones have been weathered smooth, and all lean outward at precisely the same angle. No tool marks remain. Their arrangement might be intentional, except that similar rings appear half-formed elsewhere: two pillars in a ravine, one slab on a summit, a curved alignment buried beneath ice. Perhaps the mountain naturally produces ruins.

Farther east is a low shelter built against a cliff, made from flat stones laid without mortar. It has a doorway but no interior. Passing through the opening brings one to the same side from which one entered, facing outward. Snow lies on its roof and nowhere near it. Within the doorway’s darkness, the roar of the waterfall ceases completely, replaced by dripping water and the soft breathing of something asleep. Nothing seen from outside occupies the space.

Beside the shelter rests a cairn. It casts a shadow toward the dimmest part of the sky. Each of its stones is common in isolation—slate, granite, porous volcanic rock—but no two come from the same mountain, and one is wet with seawater. The cairn cannot be dismantled. Remove the top stone and it is still on top; the removed stone also remains in the hand until attention shifts, after which the hand is empty.

These are not enchantments laid upon the valley. They are its ordinary physical laws.

Here, distance is reliable only sideways. Height repeats. A climber ascending a hundred paces finds the valley floor no farther below, though the surrounding peaks sink. Descending restores the peaks but not always the route. Shadows indicate neither a sun nor a direction; they point toward whichever pool is deepest. Water possesses motion without displacement. Noise has duration but no event. Living things can change position, but change itself is not observable.

Time is similarly settled. The light never brightens beyond a blue-gray dusk, and never fades into night. Clouds retain the same folds. The air maintains the smell of wet stone, old snow, and something mineral-sharp like blood on iron. Hunger arrives but does not increase. Wounds bleed but do not worsen. Sleep may happen, though waking brings no sense of interval. There are no seasons hidden behind this equilibrium. The valley is not waiting for thaw, storm, or dawn.

Its permanence is not peaceful. It is the permanence of a sound held too long, of a fall that cannot finish.

Across the open center, gravel flats spread between shallow ridges. They seem at first to be the simplest part of the basin: vacant, level, gray. But their pebbles are arranged by size in broad concentric arcs centered on nothing. Walking across an arc produces a change in the underground thunder. At one boundary it deepens; at the next it acquires a second pulse; farther in, the cliffs begin returning footsteps in reverse order. Near the absent center, every loud sound ceases except the waterfall, which becomes unbearably close.

There is nothing there—no monument, fissure, animal, or pool. Only a patch of bare ground perhaps twenty paces wide.

Rain hangs above it.

Thousands of drops occupy the air from knee height to the clouds, each perfectly still. They distort the landscape into tiny inverted valleys. Between them, the air is dry. Passing through leaves skin and clothing damp, although no drop moves or diminishes. Looking upward through this column reveals no sky, only the same bare circle seen from a tremendous height, with a dark figure standing at its center.

The figure copies the observer exactly but does not copy at the same moment. Sometimes it is behind. Sometimes it completes an action first.

Beyond the rain, the basin continues: more shelves, more empty channels, sparse lichens under stone, another dark pool cupped in old moraine. The terrain has variation but no progression. Nothing becomes more fertile near water or harsher with altitude. There are no borders between habitats because there are scarcely habitats at all. Everything is an isolated fact, separated from the next by long tracts of cold ground.

The valley remains loud. The waterfall falls without descending. The hidden thunder rolls without approaching. Birds call from ledges where they may never have lived. Water waits in the prints of absent animals. The dimness holds every surface with equal patience.

Nothing arrives.

Nothing leaves.

And despite the noise, the place preserves the overwhelming impression that it has already happened.
