Far below the open desert lies a city that no one built.

Its streets spread through a buried stratum of limestone, following the old joints of the rock. They are broad, level corridors with intersections, chambers, narrow side passages, and occasional plazas where the ceiling rises beyond sight. From any one crossing, several others are visible in succession, dim arch behind dim arch, giving the impression of a settlement extending for miles. Yet there are no worked stones, no cut marks, no doors. The architecture is the mature form of erosion: walls softened by immense age, corners rounded, floors worn almost flat. Pillars remain where the surrounding stone vanished long ago. Collapsed shelves resemble terraces and ruined balconies. Mineral bands run horizontally along the walls like courses of masonry.

Once, water made this place. It has been absent for ages.

The former channels are dry to their deepest cracks. Basins that might have held underground lakes are paved with pale salts and fine dust. The old flowstone has lost its gloss and become dull as bone. Nothing drips. Nothing runs. Even moisture carried down from the surface is intercepted by hundreds of feet of hot, porous rock and dispersed before it can reach the city. Here, coolness survives without damp: the stable chill of depth, stone, and isolation.

Life persists on other terms.

Across the plazas stand sparse groves of tall, colorless growths. They resemble plants in their patience and arrangement more than in their substance. Their trunks are pale fungal columns wrapped in dry, overlapping sheaths. From them extend fans of thin membranes, ash-gray on top and almost black beneath. These collect neither sunlight nor rain. They feed on mineral dust, animal breath, and the faint chemical seepage rising through faults in the floor. Their roots spread as brittle cords along the rock rather than entering it, enclosing patches of ground in wide, delicate nets.

Some groves are lush enough to form canopies. Beneath them grow low mats of velvet fungus, hard nodules like clustered seed heads, and branching mineral lichens that take centuries to cover a handspan. But the abundance is uneven. A chamber may be thick with pale fronds, then the next avenue lies empty for half a mile. The city alternates between inhabited districts and bare stone, with long intervals of darkness between islands of life.

The fauna is everywhere.

Small blind grazers move in dense herds beneath the fungal fans, clipping their edges with rasping mouths. Long-bodied burrowers sleep in salt beds and emerge in slow coils when the air cools. Moths without wings climb the columns to feed, their soft feet adhering to stone. Pale beetles cross the avenues in steady lines. In cracks overhead, vast colonies of leathery animals hang packed together, each no larger than a thumb. Larger hunters wait beside the routes used by the herds: low, broad creatures that resemble salamanders, though their skin is dry, finely scaled, and cool as the walls.

None of this life is quick unless frightened. The ecology runs at the pace permitted by a place with almost no new resources. Animals rest for days between meals. The fungal trees add a new layer to their trunks over decades. Herds migrate from district to district according to cycles so gradual that a human lifetime would mistake them for permanent populations. Birth seasons may be separated by years. Predators can remain still long enough to acquire a coating of dust.

The city should therefore be quiet.

It is not.

Every surface carries a tremendous, continuous roar.

It comes through the rock rather than through the air: a deep concussion generated far above, where desert winds cross a field of immense stone ridges. The ridges vibrate under the wind like organ pipes laid on their sides. Their resonance descends through the buried strata, gathering in the hollow city. Floors hum. Pillars throb. Fine salt jumps in shallow basins. The broadest chambers amplify the sound until it becomes almost physical, an unbroken thunder that presses against the chest and loosens dust from the ceiling.

There are neighborhoods of distinct noise. Narrow streets produce a hard, grinding drone. Domed chambers hold a lower note that can make vision tremble. At certain intersections, opposing vibrations cancel and leave small islands of relative silence, no wider than a few steps. Animals gather there to sleep and breed. The groves grow especially thick around them. These quiet places function as gardens, refuges, and public squares within the accidental plan of the city.

Elsewhere, creatures have adapted to the uproar. Most possess little or no hearing. They communicate by touch, scent, flashes of faint body light, or patterned pressure against the ground. Herd animals stamp messages into the floor. Predators read movement through their jawbones. Colonies in the ceiling coordinate by passing contractions from body to body, so that whole vaults ripple in the dark.

The light is equally indirect. There is no sun and almost no natural flame. The fungal membranes emit a weak gray-green sheen when disturbed by vibration, and because the vibration never ceases, the largest groves remain dimly luminous. Moving animals leave temporary brighter wakes through them. Mineral lichens give off an even fainter blue at their growing edges. Most chambers are not illuminated so much as relieved from total darkness: pillars appear as darker masses, avenues as low horizons, distant colonies as dusty constellations.

No single inhabitant understands the place as a city. There is no tool use here, no construction, no repair. Yet generations of animals deepen paths, clear intersections, maintain dung fields, spread spores, and enlarge the silent refuges by wearing loose stone away. The result has acquired order without intention. Herd routes behave like roads. Salt basins become nesting courts. Groves mark habitable quarters. Predator territories form invisible walls. The ceiling colonies occupy the high vaults like dense upper districts.

Everything looks settled into a final state, but that finality is deceptive. Change continues beneath perception. A pillar narrows by the width of a hair. A grove advances into a vacant chamber. A salt crust rises and buckles. A roof fractures along a line first opened before animal life existed. Every few thousand years, one of the great halls collapses. Its fall is inaudible within the greater roar, though the concussion rearranges migration routes for centuries.

The oldest streets terminate at smooth walls where ancient collapses sealed them. Behind some of those walls, the city continues: further chambers, further plazas, perhaps entire ecosystems cut off long enough to become unfamiliar. But there is nothing supernatural in their isolation. Stone closes. Populations divide. Time does the rest.

Above, the desert remains empty beneath a hard sky. Below, the cool buried city extends through the limestone, dry as a skull and loud as a storm—crowded with life, separated by great spaces, illuminated by the faint stress of living things, and still being shaped by forces so slow they resemble permanence.
