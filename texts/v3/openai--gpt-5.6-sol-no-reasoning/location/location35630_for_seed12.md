The city stands at the summit of a drowned mountain, though *stands* is not quite the word. It clings, nests, and accretes.

Far below lies an ocean floor that no inhabitant has seen. Between it and the city is a warm immensity of dark water. The mountain rises through this water for miles, shouldering upward until its youngest ridges enter a high, dim stratum where the sea grows thin and clouds of suspended air drift like pale jelly. Here, around several black volcanic pinnacles, the city has been assembled.

There is no surface.

Water continues overhead, deepening into an inverted sky. Light descends from somewhere beyond it as a diluted green-gray radiance, too weak to cast clean shadows. At long intervals the brightness swells, and the highest towers become visible as silhouettes: woven domes, stone fins, hanging neighborhoods, gardens anchored upside down beneath shelves of porous rock. Then the glow fades, leaving only the small domestic lights of oil cups and colonies of cultivated luminous mold.

The mountain is new. Its stone has not yet acquired the shapes of age.

Pillows of basalt bulge from the slopes like half-cooled loaves. Mineral chimneys rise in blunt clusters, scarcely hollowed, their surfaces dark and lustrous. There are few ravines, no broad valleys, no old beds carved by persistent currents. Instead the terrain consists of raw steps, fractures, steep faces, and terraces formed wherever fresh lava met the water and froze. Some ledges are still warm. Threads of gas escape from seams with a soft, intermittent trembling, gathering under roofs before being guided into skin bladders or ceramic bells.

Because erosion has hardly begun, every edge seems provisional. A cliff may be smooth as poured wax on one side and sharp as broken pottery on the other. Whole districts are founded on stone younger than their eldest inhabitants. Surveyors tap it with weighted bone rods, listening through their palms for concealed cavities and brittle cooling layers. Houses are moved when the mountain’s internal murmur changes.

The settlement occupies a broad crown of peaks and saddles, open enough that its distant quarters appear and disappear in the haze. From one high garden it is possible to see three or four inhabited ridges: each a thicket of posts, ropes, retaining nets, terraces, and irregular masonry. Beyond them, dim water extends without landmark.

Nothing is level for long. Streets spiral around rock horns or cross open gaps on suspended walkways. Some descend as shafts through the roofs of lower districts. Others are simply guide ropes stretched across spaces where the current is mild. Citizens pull themselves along hand over hand, carrying baskets tied close to the body. Public ways are marked by knots: one pattern for the kiln quarter, another for the gas fields, another for the gardens and burial galleries. In the dark, the city is read with the fingers.

The oldest structures are dry-laid from slabs of basalt, fitted without mortar and held in place by their own mass. Newer ones are woven from tough water-vines around frames of woodlike coral. Their walls admit the sea but interrupt the current, making calm interiors in which tools and food do not drift away. Roofs are often more substantial than floors. They catch falling sediment, support hanging crops, and trap pockets of gas used for breathing, cooking, and ceremony.

Every dwelling contains at least one air bell.

These bells hang from ceilings like enormous clay bowls turned upside down. Gas collected from volcanic vents is washed through beds of moss and mixed with air harvested from wandering bubbles. Beneath a bell, a person can place their face into the trembling silver boundary and breathe. Conversations occur there in shifts: one speaker rises into the bell while others wait below, watching the distortions of the mouth. Wealthier halls maintain chambers large enough for several heads at once. Their occupants appear grotesquely severed at the neck, speaking above the water while their bodies sway beneath.

Most work is done in silence.

Hammering is rare because blows travel too far through the mountain and water. Stone is split with wedges of swollen fiber. Wood-corals are bent while young and left to harden into beams. Shell, bone, obsidian, fired clay, and braided vine provide nearly every implement. Metal occurs only as soft nodules gathered around certain vents; it is beaten cold into hooks, needles, and small sacred mirrors. A metal blade is an heirloom. For ordinary cutting there are glassy volcanic flakes, keen enough to open skin with barely a touch.

Fire cannot live openly. Kilns are built in hot cracks where the mountain itself bakes clay. Cooks lower sealed pots into thermal wells. Smiths, such as they are, work inside gas pockets maintained beneath stone hoods, tending tiny flames with bladder bellows while apprentices scrape condensation from the walls. The craft is dangerous and surrounded by rules. No flame may be made above a sleeping district. No gas bell may be opened during the long dim season. No one strikes stone twice without waiting for the echo to leave.

Vegetation makes the city seem larger and more crowded than its population.

Warm water and mineral-rich seepage feed broad gardens of ribbon kelp, crimson fans, bladder vines, and mosses resembling velvet smoke. Some plants root in stone; others are tied to frames and cultivated as floating orchards. Their fronds overlap streets and cover abandoned walls. Leaves brush shoulders, close behind swimmers, and fill courtyards with patient motion. Thick ropes of vine span one tower to another, bearing clusters of translucent fruit with a peppery, mineral taste.

The upper terraces grow pale cup-plants that collect bubbles beneath their leaves. Gardeners harvest these carefully at dawn-brightening, guiding the bubbles into communal reservoirs. Lower down, where the volcanic warmth is stronger, black-leaved tubers thrust roots into mineral silt packed inside baskets. Their flesh is orange and faintly luminous when cut.

There are also groves that are not clearly plants.

They consist of branching limestone structures covered in soft membranes, and they grow according to sounds transmitted through the rock. Near quiet houses they spread in broad, delicate plates. Beside workshops they rise into short, thick antlers. When a family abandons a room, the grove gradually alters its form, smoothing over doorways and filling niches until the architecture seems to have grown rather than been built. People harvest only the outermost tips. Cutting near the trunk is believed to confuse the mountain.

Animals are scarce enough to be noticed individually.

Small silver fish sometimes pass through the outer wards in a single coordinated body, turning corners as if familiar with the streets. Blind crabs inhabit the thermal gutters and are protected because they react to changes in the vents hours before humans can feel them. A few slow, ray-shaped grazers visit the gardens, and children are sent to push them away with padded poles. They are never killed. Their migrations are too infrequent, and their broad dark forms are considered messages whose meaning has been forgotten.

Larger creatures remain conjectural. Now and then ropes tighten without visible cause. A distant lattice collapses. Something removes an entire orchard from its anchors and leaves no fragments. In the lowest inhabited caverns, people occasionally find vast, clear scales stuck to the warm stone, each as thin as a leaf and larger than a door.

The city does not hurry.

Currents are weak around the mountain crown, divided by ridges and by centuries of woven barriers. Sediment falls slowly. Gardens unfurl slowly. Stone cools over generations. Construction may remain unfinished for the lifetime of its designer: a row of pillars awaiting a shelf of rock that surveyors expect the mountain to produce, or a bridge grown from two coral frames that will not meet for another forty years.

Time is kept by changes in water.

The high dimness waxes and wanes. Mineral blooms turn the sea rust-red every seventh or eighth year. Warm upwellings arrive from the lower slopes, carrying black grit that settles on roofs and must be swept into planting beds. Whole civic calendars are based on the inflation of gas pockets inside particular caves. The greatest of these, the Lung Below, takes almost twelve years to fill. When it releases, the mountain exhales through a thousand fissures. The city vanishes into columns of bubbles, guide ropes snap taut, bells overflow, and every garden bends upward.

Then, for several days, breathing is easy everywhere.

The most unusual law of the place is that height does not mean nearness to air. It means nearness to pressure of another kind. Above the city, the water grows stranger rather than shallower. Loose objects fall sideways. Bubbles rise for a while and then slow, flattening into lenses that remain suspended. In the highest district, rain occurs underwater: cold droplets condense from a clear layer overhead and sink through the warmer sea, striking roofs with faint ticks.

That district is called the Weather.

Its inhabitants build long, narrow houses beneath the cold fall. Their walls are tiled with shells, and their cisterns collect the sinking freshwater before it dissolves. From their balconies they watch curtains of submarine rain advance across the peaks. The curtains obscure whole wards, turning lanterns into blurred ocher stars. Sometimes a storm hangs motionless between two towers for months.

The dead are taken beyond the last gardens to fresh basalt ledges. There they are wrapped in weighted mats and placed in shallow hollows. No tomb is closed. Mineral film gathers over the body; moss follows; then coral. In a few generations the deceased becomes a pale, branching mound attached to the mountain. Families visit by touch, tracing remembered outlines beneath the growth.

It is said that the mountain is assembling itself from these remains—that stone, city, garden, and ancestry are stages of one material.

From a distance, the settlement resembles a dark reef illuminated from within. Its towers do not pierce the sea; they are almost lost in it. Paths glow faintly where generations of hands have polished the guide ropes with oil. Air bells shine silver beneath roofs. Warm vents send wavering columns upward into the dimness. Nothing is still, but little moves decisively.

The city hangs on raw geology in an ocean without a shore, dense with human making and green life, yet surrounded by enormous vacancy. It is young enough that its rules are still being discovered. Each new crack may become a street. Each bubble may become a room. Each unfinished spur of basalt may, after patient examination, reveal what the mountain intended to build next.
