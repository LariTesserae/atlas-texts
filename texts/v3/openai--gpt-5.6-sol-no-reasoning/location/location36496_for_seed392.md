The plain is tiled to the horizon.

Not neatly. The slabs differ in size and color—cream, vermilion, soot-black, a green like old copper—but every one has been cut, fired, carried, and set by hand. Their edges are still sharp. Straw and thumbprints survive in the glaze. Between them run narrow joints packed with pale mortar, except where the joints have been deliberately left open to receive rain.

There is no soil beneath them.

Lift a tile, if one can be loosened, and there is another tile under it, laid face-down. Beneath that is a course of hollow bricks; beneath the bricks, according to the well-keepers, are painted rafters, then plaster chambers, then another plaza under an inverted white sky. No one has reached an unmade layer. “Ground” here means only the uppermost surface on which people have agreed to walk.

It is low country, broad and almost level, extending beyond any single settlement but not beyond comprehension. From the raised lip of a cistern, one can see most of it: scattered orchards; long roofless reservoirs; isolated kilns; clusters of reedwork houses; tiled causeways crossing gardens of shallow water. Nothing crowds anything else. Each habitation stands apart with a generous interval of glare, wet pavement, and luxuriant growth between it and the next. The place feels both inhabited and unfinished, as though its makers built an entire province but populated only every fifth space.

Heat rests over it without wavering. It is the bright, dense heat of a sun directly overhead, though the sun itself cannot always be found. There are no clouds, yet the air continually condenses. Beads of water form on walls, leaves, ropes, skin, and fired clay. They fatten, hang, and fall. Rain is remembered everywhere but never observed.

Water runs through the construction in hair-thin channels. It seeps from lion-headed spouts, weeps down stairs, and gathers in square basins lined with turquoise tesserae. It lies just below the paving, audible when one presses an ear to the warm slabs: not flowing, exactly, but present—a broad, suspended pressure. Here and there the tiles descend by shallow steps into marshes made from thousands of watertight bowls fitted lip to lip. Sedges root in baskets sunk inside them. Lotus leaves rise from glazed jars. Moss spreads across damp cloth stretched on wooden frames. Even the mud is manufactured: crushed brick, charcoal dust, chaff, dung, and water kneaded together in troughs.

The vegetation is lush but discontinuous. Green erupts in composed islands, each separated by open brightness.

Banana-like plants grow from immense ceramic urns, their leaves glossy and torn into motionless ribbons. Fig trees stand in octagonal planting pits, roots wrapped around rubble, their crowns wide enough to shade small courts. Vines climb lattices of bone-colored wood and cross empty spaces on braided ropes. Ferns occupy dripping arcades. There are reed beds, but every reed has been planted through a drilled hole in a submerged plate. Beyond the settlements rise low artificial hummocks, stacked from baskets of potsherds and faced with turf woven onto mats.

No seed falls where it has not fallen before.

This is one of the landscape’s laws. A fig that drops at noon hangs forever a finger’s width above the pavement. A yellow leaf remains halfway between branch and basin. Drops gather and descend, but each follows a fixed path and arrives at precisely the same place. Animals move, yet their movements repeat so faithfully that they are less like journeys than gestures embedded in the world.

At the eastern reservoir, seven long-legged birds lift together, circle once, and return to the same blue ledge. Their wingbeats make the loudest regular sound in the province. Small green lizards cross sunlit walls on separate routes, pausing at the same nail holes. Beetles polish the same arcs into fallen fruit. White crabs inhabit the drainage channels, emerging to inspect the joints and withdrawing when shadows reach marks painted on the stones. Bright fish fill the cisterns. They are abundant enough to turn the water bronze, but each keeps its appointed depth, and none can be caught twice—or perhaps none can be caught once. A hand closes around scales and comes up holding a cool, fish-shaped absence.

Human beings, where they appear, speak little. Noise does not carry correctly here. A hammer strike is crisp beside the hammer and inaudible two paces away. Conversation must be held face-to-face. Bells swing silently. Water makes almost no sound unless heard through tile. The dominant noises are close and small: insect mouthparts, the rasp of a stone blade, wet rope tightening, a bird settling its feathers, breath passing through a clay mask.

The settlements are wholly built but technologically modest. There are no engines, no hidden conduits of metal, no miraculous machinery behind the walls. Doors turn on stone pivots. Water is lifted in leather bags. Timber is shaped with flint adzes and copper blades hammered thin and frequently annealed in charcoal fires. Roofs are palm thatch over lashed frames, although the palms grow from constructed beds and the thatch never decays. Builders measure with knotted cords. Masons sight along bowls filled to their rims with water. Potters use slow wheels pushed by hand.

Nearly every object bears evidence of touch. Walls lean slightly from one course to the next. Finger furrows decorate cistern rims. Handles are polished where generations seem to have held them, though no generation has passed.

That contradiction is ordinary here. Everything is freshly made and immemorial.

Mortar remains pale and uneroded. Corners have not softened. Drain mouths retain the tiny burrs left by carving. Wooden stakes show axe facets sharp enough to catch skin. Yet vines possess trunks broader than houses. Orchard roots have split paving slabs without rounding the fractures. Shrines contain bowls darkened by centuries of oil. The inhabitants remember ancestors, but if asked when those ancestors died, they gesture toward rooms where the ancestors are still sitting.

Erosion has barely begun and never proceeds. Cracks occur as completed facts. A wall is either whole or has always been newly broken. At the northern edge of the plain stands a terrace whose facing stones have burst outward, exposing red fill and woven retaining baskets. Dust hangs among the falling blocks. One may walk beneath them. They will not descend further, though their shadows insist they are falling.

Elsewhere, a whole colonnade inclines toward a reservoir at an impossible angle. The pillars are reflected upright in the water. If approached by boat, it is the reflection that casts shade.

This failure of agreement between things becomes more pronounced in the open spaces. Distance does not remain neutral. A kiln that appears half a mile away may be reached in twenty steps, while a nearby fig tree can require an afternoon. Roads are laid in straight lines but bend when followed. Some courtyards are larger inside their boundary walls than the entire tiled province seen from above. A flight of three stairs descends past twelve landings. Wells open into sunlight.

Still, the place does not feel chaotic. Its impossibilities are stable, locally understood, and carefully accommodated. People leave offerings at corners where distance folds. They tie white cord around doors that open into the noon sky. Children are taught never to chase an animal beyond its final repeated movement. Gardeners prune plants only along existing cuts, because a new cut may reveal masonry, bird feathers, or another green interior landscape instead of sapwood.

The brightest region is the central expanse, called the Unroofed House.

It is a city-sized floor plan without walls: rooms marked by bands of black tile, doorways indicated by paired posts, hearths burning in open air. Cooking pots stand beneath the fierce brightness. Sleeping mats lie on platforms surrounded by shallow lotus water. There are workshops, courts, animal pens, altars, and broad ceremonial stairs leading up to nothing. Each “room” is separated from the next by long intervals of bare pavement. In one, women twist fiber into cord. In another, far off but perfectly visible, a man taps a flint core. In another, goats browse on a hanging garden whose supporting scaffold has no shadow.

The goats are numerous and silent. They bear spiral markings in their coats resembling maps of the drainage system. When one drinks, water rises in distant basins.

At the center of the Unroofed House is a round platform paved in mirror-bright shell. It is unbearably luminous. Upon it stands the only object no one admits to having made: a rough gray stone, waist-high, damp on its eastern face.

Yet even this stone may be ceramic. Along one side is a seam fine as a hair. Knocked gently, it gives a hollow note—one of the few sounds that travels across the plain. Every bird stops during that note. Every hanging drop becomes spherical. Every person, including those behind walls or asleep in shaded rooms, turns toward the platform.

Then the note ends.

The birds resume their circuits. Water completes its appointed falls. The heat remains at its exact pitch. Nothing grows taller, though everything is lush; nothing dries, though the light is absolute; nothing collapses, though much of the world is caught in the instant of collapse.

At the far margins, the paving rises slightly and curves upward. The tiles become smaller with distance, climbing what first appears to be haze. Trees planted there tilt inward. Water in the outer channels runs uphill. Travelers who continue long enough report seeing the whole province arching above them: orchards, reservoirs, roofs, and bright plazas inverted in the sky, all connected, as if the landscape were the inner surface of a vast constructed vessel.

No one has reached its rim. No one truly travels far enough to return with new knowledge. The same expeditions depart at dawn. The same figures diminish along the same white causeway. At evening they can still be seen, neither farther nor nearer, their packs high on their shoulders, accompanied by three yellow dogs.

The dogs sometimes look back.

Their eyes flash in the unwavering light. Across the immense tiled quiet, one of them opens its mouth as if to bark.

It never does.
