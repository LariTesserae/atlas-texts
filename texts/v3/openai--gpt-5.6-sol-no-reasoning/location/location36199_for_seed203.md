Beneath the surface lies a sky.

It begins several kilometers down, where the last seams of ordinary stone divide into root-filled fractures and then simply cease. Beyond them opens the Underwood: a hollow so immense that no eye can find its farther wall. Its ceiling is obscured by brightness rather than darkness—a continuous pale canopy of living tissue stretched across the roof of the world.

This canopy gives the cavern its day. Broad translucent leaves overlap like scales, drawing heat and mineral charge from the strata above. Their veins shine white-green. Where they have torn, fierce bars of blue light descend through the vault, illuminating drifting spores and touching isolated regions of the floor hundreds of kilometers below. The light never sets, but it changes constantly. Waves of contraction pass through the ceiling forest, opening and closing its leaves, so that whole provinces flash into brilliance and then fade to pearl-colored dusk.

The place is brutally cold. Light fills it without warming it.

There is very little ground in the usual sense. The Underwood is mostly volume: immeasurable open air crossed by rare living structures. Vegetation has occupied every stable surface, yet stable surfaces are so widely separated that the result feels at once completely overgrown and almost empty. There are no bare cliffs, no naked islands, no exposed shelves. Wherever matter persists, plants become its skin, its substance, and eventually its replacement.

From the luminous roof descend columns resembling roots, trunks, ropes, and waterfalls arrested in mid-plunge. Some are hair-thin and many kilometers long. Others widen into braided towers whose bases cannot be seen. They are separated by enormous tracts of clear space. A person standing on one branch may look toward the next forest and see it only as a dark green weather front on the horizon.

The largest plants do not grow from soil. They hang.

Their root systems fasten to the ceiling and lower forests into the void: inverted mountains of wood, each with ridges, valleys, terraces, and tangled foothills. Small growths colonize the great trunks—silver moss, glassy needles, fan-shaped leaves, pale vines stiff with frost—until the host plant becomes an ecosystem broad enough to make its own winds. Near the tips of these pendant worlds, roots divide again and spread outward into rafts. These rafts are the nearest thing the cavern has to continents.

They flex underfoot.

A raft may be a hundred kilometers across and only several meters thick, woven from living roots under tension. Its upper surface is cushioned by dark moss and low, densely folded plants that trap every scrap of moisture. Beneath it hangs a wilderness of tendrils, bladders, seed sacs, and exploratory filaments, all reaching down into emptiness. The edges are young and raw: bright shoots thrust outward in angular fans, unsupported except by their own rapid weaving. New land can form within days. It may remain for centuries, or tear loose before the week is over.

The world is never still enough to grow old.

Pressure changes travel through the cavern as slow, colossal storms. Winds rise without warning, driven by distant contractions of the ceiling canopy or by the collapse of some hanging forest too far away to see. They move through the Underwood unobstructed, gathering force across hundreds of kilometers of open space. When they strike a root continent, the whole structure bellies like a sail. Trunks groan. Groves lie flat. New branches whip into one another and immediately begin grafting where they touch. Entire landscapes fold, split, and recombine.

Thus the turbulence is creative as often as destructive. A storm can braid three separate forests into one. A broken root may spear a passing raft and anchor it. Seed clouds collide in midair, condense into fibrous knots, and unfurl delicate airborne thickets that live for a season without ever finding ground.

At other times the wind pulls a continent apart.

The rupture begins with a sound like cloth tearing in another room. A luminous crack races across the moss. Water wells briefly from severed vessels, freezing into blue ridges along the wound. Then the two sides drift away, still joined by taut vines and thin white roots that snap one by one. Creatures, dwellings, and groves are divided without warning. Within hours the exposed edges bristle with new growth, as if the land itself has forgotten the loss and is urgently attempting to bridge the gulf.

Water is precious, though dampness is everywhere. Most of it exists as condensation: a sheen on waxy leaves, frost in deep folds, beads trembling along the underside of roots. The plants are architectures for catching it. Leaves form cups within cups. Moss rises into soft chimneys that comb moisture from the wind. Hollow stems breathe cold air in and exhale it slightly colder, causing clear droplets to gather inside them.

Rain comes locally and often from below.

When warm sap rises through one of the great columns, vapor blooms around its crown. A cloud forms beneath a hanging forest, catches in its downward-pointing branches, and releases a shower into the void. The drops may fall for hours before striking anything. Seen from afar, these rains are shining threads descending through bright emptiness. Often they freeze during the fall and arrive as sparse, hard grains of ice.

On the larger rafts, water collects in plant-made basins. These are black, still pools held inside enormous curled leaves. No mineral bank borders them. Their shores are soft green lips that tighten in dry weather and relax when rain arrives. Some pools migrate as their supporting plants crawl toward better condensation currents. A settlement built beside water may wake to find its lake moving away at the pace of a walking person.

True rivers are rarer and stranger. They flow through the vascular channels of dead or sleeping trunks. Where the wood splits, cold water emerges, runs openly for a short distance over moss, and disappears into another living conduit. Such river segments may be only a few meters long, though the hidden current continues for thousands of kilometers. Travelers map them by pulse and taste rather than by visible course.

The plants grow quickly but with little disorder at the small scale. Nearly every form is sharp, fresh, and newly articulated. Frost preserves torn fibers before rot can soften them. New shoots emerge as clean green spears. Bark breaks into pale geometric plates. The land lacks rounded stones, old soil, and sedimentary gentleness. Even ancient structures are continually replaced from within, leaving crisp surfaces and raw seams.

Decay is not a broad process here. It occurs in secret.

Fungi live deep inside the wooden masses, where their faint warmth does not escape. They hollow chambers, digest dead tissue, and leave flexible ribs standing. Over time a trunk becomes less like a solid beam and more like a cathedral lattice. Wind passes through it. Sap crosses its gaps on bridges of new growth. Those chambers are among the few protected places where people can live.

The inhabitants build sparingly because the living world already provides walls, roads, vessels, cordage, and roofs. A dwelling may consist of a single incision persuaded to heal into the desired shape. Builders cut a slit in a young trunk with stone flakes or cold-hammered copper, brace it open with bone pegs, and return over several seasons to guide the scar. The tree thickens around the empty space, creating a dry chamber lined with smooth, pale wood.

Clusters of such rooms appear at rare intervals along major branches. From outside, a village looks like a series of knots connected by braided handrails. Doors are bark flaps. Ladders are trained vines. Bridges are living roots stitched together with fiber. Platforms hang beneath branches to avoid the worst winds, and their floors are woven loosely so that snow, spores, and dangerous pressure can pass through.

Nothing is square unless it is very old or very stubborn.

Metal is scarce. There is almost no exposed ore, and fire is difficult to sustain in the cold, oxygen-thin reaches of the open cavern. Tools are made from chipped mineral nodules harvested from root cores, from teeth shed by the rare animals, and from dense wood cured until it rings like ceramic. Small copper blades are heirlooms. They are not cast so much as beaten, reheated in fungal kilns, and beaten again.

Light needs no manufacture, but heat does. Villages cultivate warm rot in sealed pits. Slow fungi feed on pulp and produce enough heat to keep one communal chamber above freezing. People sleep around it in layered hammocks stuffed with dry floss. Flames are reserved for emergencies, rites, and toolmaking. An open fire can injure the supporting plant, consume precious air, or attract the few large animals that have learned what warmth means.

Paths are temporary. Colored cords mark safe branches, but growth swallows them. Guides read tension in wood with their bare feet. A deep vibration means the raft is flexing normally; a rapid trembling means a storm is coming; a silence too complete may mean that the branch has died and is already separating from its parent body.

The commonest form of travel is along the interior of hollow roots. Narrow canoes, made from folded bark, move through hidden waterways in total darkness while the bright world lies only a handspan beyond the wall. For long crossings, people use seed sails: light baskets suspended beneath broad dried membranes. They wait at the edge of a raft for a favorable current, cut loose, and drift across distances that may take weeks.

There are few animals, and most seem temporary beside the triumphant abundance of plants.

Small transparent crawlers graze films of frost from shaded stems. Winged things emerge during condensation weather, mate in the brief rain, and die before the pools close. Blind burrowers move through trunks, leaving tunnels that the plant promptly lines with bark. Their presence is known by faint tapping at night—or at what the settlements call night, when the ceiling canopy dims enough for sleep.

The largest animals are the sailbacks. They live in the open air and may go years without landing. Their bodies are narrow, their bones hollow, their wings broad enough to carry gardens of algae and tiny ferns. They feed by flying through spore clouds and filtering the air. From a distance they resemble detached leaves. When one settles on a raft, everything nearby becomes quiet. Its weight can alter the land’s balance. People approach only to collect molted membrane, never to hunt; a startled sailback taking flight can rip a village from its branch.

Sound travels strangely through the vast hollow. The open air is mostly silent despite its ceaseless movement. Winds are often too broad and slow to whistle. Rain vanishes beneath itself. Distant storms appear first as changes in pressure, a tightening in the lungs and ears.

Yet the vegetation carries sound over impossible distances. A blow against a trunk may return days later through a connected root system. Settlements communicate with wooden hammers, sending simple rhythms into major limbs. Messages cross provinces as muted pulses: storm, fracture, water, travelers, death. Sometimes an answer comes from a direction where no settlement is known.

No one agrees whether these replies are made by undiscovered people, by echoes wandering through newly joined forests, or by the plants themselves.

The Underwood has rules, though they are less like laws of nature than habits of a living place.

Nothing dead remains unsupported for long.

Every open surface will green.

Every wound becomes a door, a bridge, or a mouth.

Weight calls growth toward it. A suspended object left in contact with a tendril is slowly enclosed and incorporated. Lost tools appear years later inside fruiting bodies. The bones of the dead become white trellises within new branches. Abandoned houses seal themselves and continue outward as enormous woody cysts, still containing ladders, bedding, bowls, and the marks of hands.

Direction is unreliable because the land moves, but “up” and “down” have social meanings beyond gravity. Upward is toward brightness, cold, and the origin of roots. Downward is toward darkness, warmer currents, and the unknown depth where falling things disappear. No one has reached the bottom. Seed vessels lowered on cords return bruised, sprouting, or not at all.

During the greatest storms, the lower void answers the ceiling.

Clouds rise from beneath, black and dense, shot through with pale biological light. They climb against gravity in towering spirals. Loose seeds, broken dwellings, ice grains, insects, and whole sections of forest are carried upward. The ceiling canopy opens above them in vast concentric wounds, and for several dazzling hours the cavern becomes too bright to see.

Then new shapes descend.

Not creatures, exactly, and not weather. Fresh root masses unfold from the roof at impossible speed, branching as they fall. Curtains of leaves spread across hundreds of kilometers. Old continents are pinned together or pushed apart. New rivers begin within the raw wood. Rains form under unfamiliar forests. The map ceases to be true.

Afterward comes the deepest quiet.

Broken vegetation hangs in the radiant air. Drops of sap turn slowly to ice. Far-separated communities emerge from their chambers and strike the trunks beneath their homes, listening for survivors. One beat answers, then another, each softened by distance.

All around them, in brightness without warmth, the green world is already rebuilding itself.
