The city occupies a high basin where a young mountain range has opened around a shallow, rain-fed lake. The peaks are not tall enough for snow, but they rise abruptly: dark ribs of stone with sharp crests, scarcely softened by weather. Their broken faces show pale seams and fresh fractures. Water emerges everywhere along them, not in violent torrents but in silver threads, darkening the rock beneath springs and gathering in narrow channels cut by hand.

The climate is warm and continually damp. Clouds often rest level with the higher ridges, while the basin below remains clear enough for ordinary daylight. Rain seems always to have just passed. Roofs glisten; paving stones hold a muted shine; walls are dark for a hand’s breadth above every gutter. The air smells of wet clay, mineral water, and leaves warmed after rain.

There are no animals. No birds cross the basin, no insects trouble the pools, and no fish disturb the lake. This absence is not dramatic. It is simply one of the conditions of the place, as ordinary as the warmth or the altitude. Flowers, where they occur, do not wait for pollinators. Some spill seed directly into runoff; others propagate through creeping roots or small plantlets that detach in rain.

Vegetation grows generously near water but leaves broad areas open. Reed beds mark the lake edge in widely separated stands. Low ferns occupy cracks between terrace walls. Groves of smooth-barked trees gather around springs, their long leaves shedding drops at regular intervals, but there is little undergrowth beneath them. Between these wet enclaves lie expanses of bare stone and cropped-looking grass. The result is neither forest nor moor: it is a spacious mosaic of water, masonry, green ground, and exposed rock.

The settlement follows the basin’s contours in long, shallow tiers. From a distance it appears urban—whole districts of rectangular roofs, retaining walls, stairways, plazas, cisterns, and raised causeways—but it is never crowded. Buildings stand apart, with gardens or empty paved courts between them. Streets are broad relative to the modest houses. Even in the center, one can often see past several roofs to open water or a mountain face.

Construction is simple and patient. Walls are made from freshly split local stone laid without mortar, each piece fitted by repeated chipping. Roofs are timber frames covered with overlapping slabs of fired clay. Copper is rare and used chiefly for small blades, needles, hooks, and the cutting edges of certain tools; most work is done with stone hammers, wooden levers, fiber ropes, and ceramic vessels. There are no engines. Water is lifted by hand in counterweighted jars or redirected by gates of wood.

Yet the city’s waterworks are extensive. Channels descend from the springs, cross one another on low stone bridges, pass beneath streets, and spread into stepped reservoirs. Every district has a rectangular pool whose overflow feeds the terrace below. Nothing moves quickly. The channels are shallow, the gradients exact, and the flow seldom rises beyond a quiet sliding sound. At the lowest level, all excess water enters the lake through a fringe of narrow stone mouths.

The lake itself is broad and strangely undisturbed. With no animal life and little wind inside the surrounding ridges, its surface can remain smooth for hours. Springs entering from below make slow circles that are visible only as differences in reflected light. Pale mineral deposits form terraces at the northern shore, still crisp-edged and only beginning to acquire moss. Elsewhere, black mud lies beneath clear water, patterned by fallen leaves that decay very slowly.

The city’s inhabitants are sparse enough that whole terraces may appear deserted. A figure may be seen carrying a clay jar along a causeway, or a small group may work soundlessly at a wall, passing stones from hand to hand. Voices are kept low, less by law than by habit. There are no hoofbeats, barking dogs, market animals, buzzing flies, or dawn choruses to fill the intervals. The principal sounds are water on stone, sandals on wet paving, the knock of a mallet, and sometimes the dull scrape of a gate being drawn across a channel.

Nothing here seems to alter rapidly. Rain falls in fine, regular periods. The lake rises only to a particular line and no farther. Springs do not fail. Channels accumulate little silt, and the sharp surrounding slopes shed almost no debris despite their youth. Plants grow, but slowly and without seasonal collapse. Fallen leaves darken, soften, and become soil over many years.

Even human change is incremental. A house may gain a room; a terrace may be extended by a few courses of stone. Repairs resemble the original work so closely that age is difficult to read. There are old districts, but they are not ruins, and new districts, but they do not look raw. The whole settlement rests in a sustained middle state: inhabited without bustle, wet without flooding, green without being overgrown.

At evening the ridges cast long shadows across the basin. Light remains on the upper springs after the city has dimmed, turning their wet rock faces briefly white. Smoke rises from scattered cooking fires and collects in thin horizontal bands under the cooling air. Lamps are few—small bowls of plant oil in sheltered wall niches—and most streets go dark.

Then the city becomes almost entirely a pattern of quiet water. Reservoirs reflect squares of sky. Roof gutters continue to drip long after the last rain. In the unlit courtyards, leaves release the moisture they have held all day. The lake receives each sound without echo, and the fresh mountains stand around it, sharp, warm, and motionless.
