The city lies across the summit of an ancient plateau, though *lies* is not quite right. It grows there, rests there, remembers itself there.

From a distance, the plateau appears almost level: a long green elevation standing above folded cloud, its rim softened by such prolonged erosion that there are no cliffs left, only descending shelves and enormous slopes worn smooth as old tools. Narrow rivers once cut these heights. Their valleys remain—broad, shallow channels twisting across the upland—but the rivers themselves are intermittent. Water now arrives in rain, mist, and condensation, collecting briefly in leaf-lined cisterns before vanishing into roots.

The whole visible surface is vegetal. There is no bare soil, no exposed stone, and almost nothing that can be called an ordinary street.

Mats of small, dark leaves form the ground. They are tough enough to bear carts and crowds, though a heel left too long in one place slowly sinks as the mat rearranges its stems. Buildings rise from trunks planted in geometric grids. Their walls are lattices of living wood packed with felted moss; their roofs spread as overlapping canopies, silver beneath and green-black above. Bridges are braided branches held under tension. Towers are hollow trees whose inner rings have been persuaded apart, creating stairwells, chambers, lift shafts, and vertical ducts without killing the trunks.

It is an urban place, unmistakably: districts, avenues, foundries, warehouses, pumping stations, worker terraces, public halls. Yet every structure is grown. Material is never removed from life; it is redirected.

The city is sparse despite its extent. Great distances open between its neighborhoods. One may walk for half an hour through uninterrupted low greenery, hearing machinery ahead, before reaching a cluster of six inhabited towers around a factory grove. These open tracts are not parks. They are part of the city’s infrastructure: photosynthetic fields, root reservoirs, wind lanes, and dormant construction plots. Slim marker trees indicate buried conduits by the color of their bark. Pale hedges carry electrical charge through sap salted with cultivated minerals. When current moves heavily through them, their flowers open in sequence, a cold white pulse traveling across the plateau.

The factories are the loudest living things.

They stand in low basins where moisture collects. Vast wheel-shaped flowers turn in the wind, operating crankshafts grown from lignified vines. Seed mills shake day and night, sorting hard black kernels from resinous pulp. In the cable districts, tendrils are drawn through ceramic mouths and twisted into cords stronger than steel. Compressor trees inhale through crowns spread over entire squares and exhale into underground bladders. Their rhythm gives the city its pulse:

a long suction like a receding sea,

a wooden detonation,

then the rush of pressurized air through root-pipes.

There are turbines, looms, presses, dynamos, and rail engines, but few of them are assembled in a conventional sense. Their casings are gourds or shell-thick burls. Bearings form inside oil-rich fruits. Gears grow as opposing rings of teeth and are kept from healing together by bitter sap. Heat is dangerous here, so combustion is uncommon. Most industry runs on wind, stored tension, fermentation gas, and the slow hydraulic force of plants swelling with water.

Along the transit avenues, sleds move on polished rails of living root. They have no drivers. The rail grips their undersides with rows of backward-curling hairs and passes them forward in muscular waves. Freight crosses the city continuously: bundles of dry fiber, drums of latex, vats of luminous yeast, bricks of compressed bark, sleeping bulbs wrapped in damp cloth. Bells sound at junctions, though no mechanism strikes them. Each bell is a bellflower large enough to stand beneath, and it tolls when its roots sense an approaching load.

The air is cool and thin. Even at midday, breath shows faintly in shaded places. Rain seldom falls hard. More often a cloud catches on the plateau and dissolves among the towers, glazing every surface. Condensation gathers on the undersides of roofs and travels along vein-gutters into cistern pods. For an hour after dawn, the city drips everywhere. By noon, much of that water has been drunk.

Pockets of permanent water survive in the oldest depressions. They are dark, circular pools crowded by vertical reeds. Nothing ripples them except rain. The city treats them as both reservoirs and archives, because the reeds preserve patterns in their annual growth—weather, pollution, electrical demand, births, epidemics, structural failures. Engineers read a cut reed as others might read a ledger. Cutting one without authorization is among the few acts considered both theft and murder.

Animals are rare.

A few pale moths inhabit the warm machinery hollows. Small, long-legged birds sometimes descend out of the upper atmosphere and walk the roofs with bewildered caution, pecking at flowers that close before their beaks. There are translucent worms in the cisterns and minute mites among the moss paving. Nothing large grazes here. Nothing howls beyond the city. Pollination, seed dispersal, pruning, and decay are performed by the plants themselves or by human crews.

The absence of animals gives the plateau a peculiar stillness beneath its noise. There are no wings in the canopy, no hidden scrambling, no distant calls. Every unplanned movement belongs to wind.

Or nearly every movement.

The city is extremely old—older, its surveyors insist, than the plateau on which it stands. That claim should be impossible, yet the lower districts contain streets bent into the stone’s former contours, including valleys erased by hundreds of millions of years of erosion. Some roots descend into rock and emerge fossilized before they enter it. Excavations beneath the western pumping groves have uncovered mature buildings compressed into coal, aligned exactly beneath their living counterparts.

The prevailing explanation is not that the city survived the ages, but that it has grown backward through them.

Here, propagation is not confined to space. A cutting planted today may establish roots in last century’s soil. A building demolished for safety is sometimes discovered intact in an older district, younger than before and occupied by people who have not yet moved in. Gardeners carry calendars alongside pruning hooks. Construction plans specify not only location and height but tense.

This temporal rooting is strongest in the afternoon, when ordinary light falls across the open fields. Shadows then become unreliable. A tower may cast the shadow of the sapling it once was, while a newly planted arch throws the enormous silhouette of its final form. Workers use these shadows as blueprints. During periods of instability, several possible canopies darken the ground at once, stirring in winds that have not arrived.

There are rules for crossing such places:

Never step into a shadow whose tree is absent.

Never shelter beneath a branch that remembers being cut.

If blossom bells toll in reverse, remain where you are until they finish.

Do not follow a rail sled that carries your own tools.

Above all, when rain begins under a clear sky, do not drink from leaves. That water has condensed from another season and may teach the body the wrong age.

Most days, these rules feel ceremonial. The city functions. Workers enter the mills at first bell. Trains pulse along the rootways. Survey crews walk the plateau rim, measuring its almost imperceptible decline. Children race seed-husks down the drainage grooves. Market canopies unfold in the morning and close at dusk around unsold goods. Cool winds comb the industrial groves, carrying scents of resin, wet cellulose, ozone, fermenting fruit, and the mineral sharpness of high altitude.

Yet reality never holds perfectly.

An avenue sometimes extends farther than the plateau permits. From one intersection, the same district can be seen on two horizons. Travelers descend toward the eastern lowlands and return from the west without turning. In the northern fields, distance ripens like fruit: walk toward a tower when it is green and it remains far away; wait until its windows yellow, and one step will bring you to its door.

Even sound is affected. Machinery from tomorrow often arrives before dawn—presses slamming in factories not yet grown, rail bells announcing shipments no one has ordered. During condensation fogs, the noise becomes immense. Every industrial era of the city seems to operate at once. Wooden gears clack beneath electric humming; obsolete piston groves pound underground; future engines produce deep, seamless tones that make the leaves lie flat.

Then the fog lifts, leaving droplets trembling on miles of green architecture.

From the plateau rim, the city seems open and serene despite its commotion: neighborhoods scattered like islands, factory crowns turning, transit lines drawing deliberate curves through low vegetative fields. Beyond them, aged slopes descend toward a distant dry country. The horizon is broad. The daylight is plain. Clouds drag their shadows over a surface with no visible beginning and, perhaps, no present end.

Beneath that surface, roots occupy every crevice. They bind foundations, carry messages, archive rain, distribute power, and pass blindly from century to century. The inhabitants say the city has no central government because the city itself coordinates what must be done. Traffic shifts before storms. New dwellings bud where population will increase. Sick districts isolate themselves by dropping bridges. At times entire avenues flower simultaneously, releasing pollen that induces the same architectural dream in everyone who breathes it.

No one agrees whether the city serves its people or merely includes them.

At night, the industrial noise lessens but does not stop. The plateau remains dimly articulate: rails whispering under freight, trunks creaking as towers adjust their weight, air moving through compressor crowns, roots cracking old stone far below. There are few animal voices to soften it. The city speaks almost alone.

And under the cool stars, across the ancient, nearly leveled height, thousands of sparse lights move through the greenery—not lamps carried by citizens, but pale buds opening one after another along pathways that have not yet been built.
