The place is a shallow court built below the level of the surrounding ground, though there is no true ground anywhere in it.

Its floor is made from thousands of fitted clay plates, each about the breadth of a hand. They have been pressed with ridges so that rainwater passes from one to another in branching channels. The plates are old, rubbed smooth in their centers and furred darkly at the joints. Some have subsided; some tilt beneath the weight of water trapped underneath. Step on the wrong one and a nearby plate exhales a cold thread of mud through its seam.

Four walls enclose the court. They are not high—perhaps twice the reach of a person—and lean inward enough to make the gray sky seem smaller. Courses of baked brick alternate with layers of woven reed sealed in pitch. Time has rounded every corner. Water has eaten shallow cups in the brick faces, polished spillways under the drains, and long vertical grooves where generations of runoff have followed the same paths. Nothing is ruined. The erosion has become part of the construction, as deliberate-seeming as carving.

At one end stands a squat kiln-shaped chamber with no door, only a low opening edged in bone-colored ceramic. At the other is a raised platform beneath a patched canopy. Between them lies almost nothing.

That emptiness is striking. There are no houses, no tools left out, no piles of fuel. Three stoneware posts stand at wide intervals across the court, and a single rope of twisted rush runs from wall to wall above head height. Otherwise there is only wet floor, dim air, and room for animals.

They come in extraordinary numbers.

Small slate-backed creatures pour from the seams whenever the pressure beneath the tiles rises. They resemble frogs until they move, when they reveal six narrow legs and a stiff tail used like a punt pole. They crowd the branching gutters, clicking their mouthplates against the clay. Pale worms hang from the wall drains in wet tassels. Thumb-sized crabs occupy the erosion cups in the brickwork, defending each cavity with frantic taps. Dark moths roost beneath the reed layers of the walls, their wings sealed against the damp by waxy scales.

Above them, long-legged hunting birds pace the court.

The birds are too tall for the enclosure’s scale. Their backs nearly brush the rope, and they must fold their necks into severe angles to look beneath the platform. Their feet make hollow ceramic knocks. Every few moments one strikes down, and the tile-creatures scatter with a sound like dry seed shaken in a jar. Then the crabs begin hammering warnings through the walls. The vibrations travel from cavity to cavity, so that a bird’s step at one end of the court produces a rattling chorus at the other.

Thus the place is rarely quiet.

Water drips from the rope. Gutters gurgle. The walls click with crabs. The six-legged marshers clatter over the plates, and the hunting birds bark to one another in voices amplified by the inward-sloping masonry. When wind crosses the top of the enclosure, it catches the reed courses and makes them groan. Even the buried water has a voice: a deep, irregular booming underfoot, as though someone were striking the underside of the floor with a padded mallet.

Rain does not need to be falling for the court to remain wet. Moisture condenses on the cool glazed surfaces each night and runs downward until morning. Beneath the tiles lies a shallow cistern divided into many cells by clay partitions. No mortar holds these partitions together. Each was shaped by hand, dried, and fitted by pressure alone. Water travels slowly through them, filling one compartment and emptying another. The entire floor rises and settles by fractions of a finger, breathing under its inhabitants.

During heavier rain, this breathing becomes visible. Plates lift at their corners. Channels reverse direction. Jets rise through deliberate pinholes, no thicker than thread, and turn the court into a low field of fountains. The slate-backed animals climb onto the posts and stack themselves in shivering layers. Crabs abandon the walls for the canopy poles. The birds retreat into the kiln chamber, where they stand packed together, grumbling in the dark.

The rainwater follows no simple path out. It circulates.

Every gutter leads eventually to one of the walls, but the wall drains feed hidden conduits that return the water beneath the floor. The court is less a basin than a closed vessel open to the sky. Some water is lost to evaporation, some enters through rain, but most has passed through the place countless times. It carries fine clay, shed scales, droppings, eggs, insect husks, and a black mineral stain that no one has named. After centuries of circulation, the water is faintly viscous. It beads on skin and smells of iron, wet rope, and old pottery.

Vegetation survives only in a few appointed places. A seam of gray moss traces the lowest course of the north wall. Three rushes emerge from a ceramic collar near the kiln chamber, cut back so often that their stems are thick and blunt. In one cracked drain grows a tiny plant with translucent leaves. No soil supports any of them. Their roots enter the woven reed inside the masonry or reach through joints into the circulating cistern.

The animals pollinate the translucent plant obsessively.

At dusk, when the light above the walls turns from pewter to charcoal, the plant opens five clear bells. Condensation forms inside them. The moths wake and crowd so densely around the crack that the wall appears to smoke. Their wingbeats fill the enclosure with a steady roar. The hunting birds flatten themselves under the canopy. The tile-creatures raise their hard faces toward the sound and open their mouths to catch falling moth scales.

This is the court’s richest hour, though nearly nothing grows here.

No natural stone occurs in the enclosure. The “stoneware” posts are fired clay filled with shards. The pale threshold is ceramic mixed with powdered shell. The pitch came from reeds rendered in sealed pots. Even the moss is believed to have been introduced, though no one remembers by whom. The court’s makers possessed neither engines nor complex mechanisms. Their accomplishment came from repetition: hands pressing clay, hands weaving reeds, hands smoothing channels with river pebbles brought from somewhere beyond the walls.

Those makers are gone, or have withdrawn. Evidence of them remains in the scale of things: shallow thumbprints under the tile lips, tie holes in the posts, a series of low niches beneath the platform. The niches contain simple objects laid out in strict order—a wooden paddle, two chipped blades, a coil of fiber, a clay cup repaired with cord. None are disturbed by the animals. The birds will reach around them for prey but never touch them directly.

The court seems governed by such prohibitions.

Nothing living crosses the raised platform from east to west. Water will flow across it, moths will fly above it, but feet avoid it. Predators do not hunt inside the kiln chamber. The six-legged creatures do not enter the wall cups, even when pursued. Any egg laid on bare tile is carried before dawn to the same gutter intersection near the center, where eggs of every species accumulate in a damp, trembling mound. Most are eaten. Enough hatch.

Another rule reveals itself slowly: nothing leaves by climbing the walls.

Birds with fully functional wings launch upward, reach the wall’s rim, and turn back as if blown by a wind that cannot be felt below. Moths spiral near the opening but descend before crossing it. Even spiders cast their threads inward. The rope sags under successive generations of silk, egg cases, shed skins, and mineral deposits until it resembles the root of a tree suspended in the air.

Perhaps the court was built to hold these creatures. Perhaps the creatures have learned the court so completely that the distinction no longer matters.

Beyond the rim there is only dimness and the intermittent sound of water striking other hollow surfaces. At times, especially after rain, answering cries come from nearby: birds barking inside another enclosure, crabs transmitting alarms through another set of walls, another buried floor booming as its cisterns fill. The court may be one chamber in a much larger construction, a made marsh extending in a grid of small, empty rooms.

But from within, it is a whole landscape.

Its weather fits between four walls. Its watershed lies underfoot. Its migrations are measured in paces: wall to post, gutter to platform, kiln mouth to central seam. Its cliffs are courses of brick, its caves are drain holes, its forest is one hanging rope. Life is abundant not because the place is rich, but because every crevice has acquired a use and every recurring sound has become a signal.

Near morning the uproar diminishes. The moths fold themselves back into the walls. The tall birds emerge from shelter and shake beads of water from their narrow feathers. Condensation moves through the channels in silver threads. For a little while the court looks almost vacant again.

Then the floor gives a deep, resonant thud.

A tile lifts.

Cold water wells around its edge, and the entire small world comes running.
