The land lies low and hot beneath an unbroken ceiling of cloud. It is not quite a marsh and not quite a forest, but a broad, shallow basin where soil and water have only recently agreed to remain separate. Dark earth floats in firm-looking mats over buried mud. Pools occupy the hollows between them, warm enough to steam faintly, their surfaces dim as tarnished metal. Rain is not falling, yet everything behaves as though rain has just ceased: leaves drip, stones weep, and clear beads continually gather along roots and blades of grass.

Nothing here appears old enough to be ruined.

The basin’s ground is raw, but not violent. There are no gullies, no carved cliffs, no exposed histories in bands of stone. Rock emerges in smooth, dark swellings through the mud, rounded not by long erosion but by the softness of its birth. Some are still porous and warm. Thin mineral skins cover them—green-black, pearl-gray, rust-red—and water condenses in their pores, travels slowly down their flanks, and vanishes into the soil. The land seems newly set: a surface that has risen, cooled, and been immediately claimed.

That claim is nearly complete.

Vegetation fills every available interval without forming the tall architecture of an ancient forest. The tallest growths are broad-crowned plants with swollen, pale trunks, their bark slick and nearly seamless. They stand in loose groves across the basin, lifting enormous leaves into the low light. Between them crowd reed fans, thornless tangles, feathery ferns, low palms, bladdered vines, and mats of minute red plants that close over bare mud within hours—or seem as though they might, though no one ever sees them advance.

Roots are everywhere. They loop above the ground in wet cords, knot around stones, and descend into black water. Some spread into thin, breathing plates that tremble when stepped upon. Others rise vertically from the mud like blunt fingers. In the dimness they give the impression of a drowned woodland trying to inhale.

The heat has no source and no rhythm. There is no cooler morning hidden behind the clouds, no evening relief. Warmth occupies the basin evenly, held close to the ground by moisture and foliage. The air is heavy but not stagnant. It passes slowly among the leaves, too saturated to dry anything, carrying odors of crushed greenery, mineral water, fungal sweetness, warm scales, and rich mud. Every surface is at nearly the same temperature: bark, pool, stone, animal hide. To touch the landscape is to find no distinction between living warmth and the warmth of the world itself.

Beyond the near groves, the country remains open enough to reveal its extent. Long waterways, seldom wider than a fallen tree is tall, wander through fields of dense growth. They do not seem to flow. Their banks are soft but stable, their levels neither rising nor falling. Farther out, stands of pale trunks dissolve into veils of vapor. Low islands repeat across the basin, each crowded with the same leaves, the same rooting forms, the same dark stones sweating clear water. There are no mountains. The horizon is made from vegetation and haze.

Animals overwhelm the place.

At first they are heard rather than seen. The basin produces a single crowded sound from thousands of separate bodies: throbbing wingbeats, resonant clicks, bubbling calls, low croaks, dry rattles, the scrape of shell against root, the slap of something entering water. None of it builds or fades. The chorus seems fixed in volume, as if each cry is answered by another at precisely the distance required to preserve the whole.

Insects occupy the air in layers. Tiny silver flies drift just above the pools. Heavy, lacquered beetles move under the leaves, each bearing droplets across its back. Long-winged creatures hang between branches with such rapid motion that their bodies remain still while the air around them blurs. Mosquito-like feeders are abundant, but no swarm pursues for long; each animal appears bound to a narrow region of air, circling the same root, the same warm stone, the same flowering cup.

The pools contain even more life. Schools of translucent fish stand motionless under the surface, their internal organs faintly visible as dark knots. Round amphibians cling to submerged stems and pulse their throats without moving. Armored grazers comb green films from rock. Flat, many-legged things lie buried in silt with only their eyes exposed. Now and then a long body passes beneath the reflection of the clouds, too broad to be a fish and too smooth to be a reptile, bending through water that closes without ripples.

On land, small creatures fill the roots. Bright lizards rest in groups, touching flank to flank. Soft-shelled crawlers feed on fallen leaves. Long-legged birds pick through the shallows, their plumage dark and oily, their beaks opening and shutting with quiet precision. Above them, larger winged animals circle beneath the cloud deck. They never climb out of sight and never seem to land. Their shadows pass repeatedly over the same groves.

There are predators, but panic is rare. A narrow-jawed hunter lies among the reeds with its mouth open, heat and condensation shining along its teeth. Nearby, grazing animals crop the ends of water plants. Every so often one disappears in a convulsion of mud and leaves, yet the surrounding herd scarcely shifts. The predator returns to stillness; the gap closes as other bodies step forward. Death is common here, but disturbance is not.

That is the basin’s deepest strangeness: its abundance does not become change.

Leaves fall, but the canopy never thins. Fruit splits, is consumed, and remains equally plentiful. Pools are entered and crossed, but their surfaces soon recover exactly the same configurations of floating plants. Young animals are visible among adults, yet no creature seems to age. There are carcasses under the roots, softened by fungi and feeding mouths, but decay neither accumulates nor diminishes. The land is caught not in lifeless suspension but in perfect biological expenditure—a ceaseless feeding, breeding, hunting, rotting, and growing whose totals never alter.

Even the clouds hold their shape.

The same dim light presses through them everywhere: greenish over vegetation, pewter over water, dull red where it enters thin membranes and wings. There are no distinct shadows except directly beneath bodies. Nothing glitters for more than a moment. Color exists in saturation rather than brightness—the violet mouths of flowers, yellow fungal shelves, blue scales, crimson seed pulp—all vivid at close range and swallowed by gloom a few steps away.

There is no evidence that hands have ever been here. No cut stem, arranged stone, ash bed, path, or discarded object interrupts the growth. Animal tracks appear in the mud, but they overlap so densely that none forms a route. Passage is possible only as other creatures pass: along a root ridge, through warm shin-deep water, beneath leaves that close back into place.

The basin has laws, though no one has written them. Water may circulate, but it may not depart. Life may consume life, but abundance may not decline. Stone may be newly born, but it may not weather into age. Sound may be made, but silence may neither approach nor retreat. Everything occurs; nothing develops.

So the landscape remains forever at the instant after arrival.

Its rocks are still emerging. Its soil is still becoming soil. Its forests have only just covered the raw ground, though they are already impenetrable. The first generations of countless animals have filled every branch, pool, hollow, and warm pocket of air—and will go on filling them, without history, beneath a cloud ceiling that never breaks.

Across the low country, water continues to bead on the leaves.

It never finishes falling.
