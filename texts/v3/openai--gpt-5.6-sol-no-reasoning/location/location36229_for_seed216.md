At first it seems to be a forest seen at night.

Then the distances become apparent.

The nearest trunk is perhaps a day’s walk away. Beyond it, others rise at intervals of ten or twenty leagues: pale, columnar masses vanishing upward into low amber cloud. Between them hangs almost nothing. There is no ground in the ordinary sense, only a warm gulf crossed by roots, bridges, suspended marshes, and the broad green surfaces of leaves so large that weather develops upon them. Their edges are coastlines. Rain travels across them in dark bands. Water gathers in their veins and drains for months through frayed tips.

The whole world is built from plants, but none of it is wild.

Every stem has been bent, grafted, braided, hollowed, or persuaded into place. Every apparent cliff is a wall grown from interlocked root plates. Every ravine follows an old seam in the construction. What looks from afar like bark resolves, nearby, into terraces of living shingles: overlapping scales cut while soft and trained to curl outward, shedding water into channels below. Thorn-pegs hold ancient joints together. Tendons of vine, tightened over generations, brace the enormous spans.

No one knows who first made it. The present inhabitants maintain only portions of the design, and even those with tools scarcely more complex than hooked stone, bone awls, ceramic saws, and cords twisted from vascular fiber. Metal is rare and poorly regarded. It poisons the green substrate, leaving ulcers that can take centuries to close. A sharp flake of volcanic glass is safer. A patient hand is safer still.

The builders understood growth well enough to make masonry obsolete.

This is the High Fabric, an artificial continent lifted above an unseen lower world. It may be suspended from the colossal trunks, or the trunks may hang from it. Travelers disagree because vertical direction is not fully reliable here. Objects fall toward the nearest mass of living structure, though rain usually falls according to some older gravity. A dropped knife may drift sideways into a wall and remain there. A person sleeping too long beneath a major branch can wake to find that “down” has moved during the night.

Because of this, every settlement keeps plumb-vines. These slender plants grow toward the local direction of falling. They are planted in bowls near doors, and their daily inclination determines which stairways are safe. When all the plumb-vines coil into knots, nobody travels.

The air is warm, dim, and immensely deep. Light comes filtered through layer after layer of canopy until it has lost its source. There is no visible sun. Morning is merely a gradual greening of the mist; evening, a return to brown-black shadow. At certain hours a diffuse gold glow passes upward from the gulf, illuminating the undersides of leaves and throwing inverted shadows into the clouds. The inhabitants call this the Below-Dawn and shutter their homes against it. Creatures caught in its light sometimes cast shadows before they arrive.

Moisture is everywhere without quite becoming abundance. The atmosphere condenses on skin, ropes, leaf hairs, and shell roofs. Rock—where “rock” exists at all—weeps continuously. These stones are manufactured too: blocks of mineral hardened inside the tissues of old plants, grown around lattices of root and then cut free. They sweat from microscopic pores long after the plant that formed them has died.

Water gathers in isolated countries rather than continuous seas.

There are rain-basins miles across, shallow wetlands held in the cupped leaves of the upper canopy. Reeds grow there in dense, floating bands. Whole villages migrate around their margins as the host leaf slowly changes its angle. There are vertical rivers that creep down trunk grooves, dividing and rejoining across hundreds of miles. There are chains of clear globes suspended from silk vines, each drop as large as a house, held together by surface tension that is somehow stronger here than elsewhere. Fish live inside them. During dry weather the globes shrink, bringing their inhabitants close enough to see one another through curved walls; in the wet season they swell until adjacent waters touch and their fish populations mingle for a few hours.

Water seldom makes a loud sound. Rain sinks into layers of moss. Streams run through fibrous channels. Even waterfalls descend in long tassels, droplets passed quietly from hair to hair. The dominant sound is condensation: a minute ticking, irregular and widely spaced, as beads release from leaf tips into the dark.

Under that lies the strain of the world.

One must be still to hear it: fibers tightening, root cables easing against their sockets, the soft internal splitting of wood as a bridge grows another fraction of its length. From very far away sometimes comes a single low knock, too deep to echo. It may be a trunk settling. It may be something enormous moving between the trunks. Afterward, every hanging pool trembles.

For all its vegetation, the High Fabric feels empty. Its life is concentrated into surfaces only a few handspans thick, wrapped around volumes of vacant air. One may cross a moss meadow crowded with ten thousand species and then stand at its edge before an unobstructed gulf larger than a nation. A vine bridge may be the only solid object for miles. A lone branch may carry a forest along its upper side while beneath it hangs warm darkness with no bottom and no visible inhabitant.

The emptiness gives everything that does appear an unsettling clarity. A bird crossing the gulf can be watched for hours. A distant raft of leaves seems close enough to touch until weather forms between it and the observer. Voices do not carry well. Shouts are absorbed by fog and vegetation, so people communicate over distance by changing the color of trained flowers. White means safe passage. Blue means water. A rapid alternation of yellow and black means that local gravity is turning.

Fauna is abundant, though much of it is small and embedded in the living construction. Bark plains ripple with herds of translucent grazers. Six-legged browsing animals move through moss as slowly as livestock, cropping spores with circular mouths. Warm rain wakes clouds of winged larvae from sealed chambers in the leaves. They rise without buzzing and fill the air like pale ash, mating, laying eggs, and dying within an hour. Their bodies are gathered by carnivorous flowers before they touch the ground.

Larger creatures inhabit the open spans. Sailbacks unfold membranes between elongated ribs and glide from trunk to trunk, sometimes remaining airborne for days on the continent’s slow convective currents. Their calls are below the threshold of hearing, but one feels them as pressure behind the eyes. Hook-footed hunters hang beneath bridges and imitate the minute sound of dripping water. Small animals approach expecting a drink. The hunter closes like a fist.

In the marsh-leaves live long-legged amphibians with gardens growing on their backs. The gardens conceal them from predators and feed on minerals secreted through the skin. Older individuals carry shrubs and may become unable to move; they settle into the wetland and root there. Their descendants visit, browse from them, and shelter between their branches. Whether the rooted elders remain animals is a question debated by the tenders.

Far out in the gulf drift hollow beasts whose bodies are little more than warm gas enclosed by living membranes. Forests of moss, orchid, and parasitic vine cover their backs. They migrate so slowly that maps record them as islands. Occasionally one becomes snagged in the structural lattice, and the vegetation on its back grafts itself to the High Fabric. After centuries the animal dies, but by then the graft bears its weight. Many inhabited districts began this way.

Death seldom leaves open space. Plant life receives every body and incorporates it. Bone becomes trellis. Skin becomes soil. Names are carved into seed coats and planted in the walls of dwellings, so ancestry literally supports architecture. Houses thicken with generations. Doorways narrow. Families periodically peel away interior layers to keep their rooms habitable, saving the fragrant sheets as records.

Yet the vegetation does not feel crowded. It is too large for crowding. Its completeness is that of a skin stretched over infinity.

Erosion here is not ruin but maturity. Rain has rounded the oldest constructions into forms that appear inevitable. Stairways have become watersheds. Abandoned towers have softened into branching hills. Once-straight avenues sag between anchor trunks in immense catenaries, and travelers walking them descend for weeks before beginning to climb again. Channels worn by countless feet have become root-lined trenches, then streams, then long marshes. The world has had time to digest its own design.

Some structures persist with impossible precision. There are arches slender as grass blades that span a hundred miles and have weathered unbroken since before memory. There are empty plazas paved in hexagonal plates of wood, each plate exhaling once every seven years. There are ladders descending into open air whose lowest rungs are worn smooth by hands, though no destination can be seen beneath them.

The tenders say that every piece once had a use.

They live in small villages grown into knots where major roots intersect. Their craft is intimate rather than industrial. Children learn to stitch wounds in bark before they learn to speak. Gardeners redirect sap with clay clamps. Bridge-keepers crawl inside hollow cables and listen for beetles. Water-cutters coax new cisterns by placing bitter pastes on selected buds. No one commands the Fabric. They negotiate with patches of it.

Their greatest works require generations. To raise a house, one family plants a ring of pliant stems; another bends them inward twenty years later; grandchildren graft the roof shut. Doors are not installed but withheld. Windows are fed salt so they remain clear. A settlement’s shape is therefore an archive of old intentions, compromises, accidents, marriages, and disputes.

Fire is nearly forbidden. In the warm, oxygen-rich air, flame could travel through resin veins faster than a person can run. Cooking is done in fermentation pits or on heat-leaves, thick red organs that maintain a temperature just below boiling. Light comes from fungi trained into ceramic cages. These lamps are dim, green, and easily startled; loud speech causes them to close.

Perhaps this contributes to the quiet culture of the place. People speak close to one another. Tools are padded. Hunters use gestures. Ceremonies involve long periods of listening to the structure, interpreting creaks and sap pulses as advice from those whose bodies have entered the walls.

Weather originates inside the landscape. Warm vapor rises from lower marshes, encounters cold layers beneath the high leaves, and becomes cloud. The cloud adheres to the vegetation, crawling along branches like a pale animal. Where two canopy systems overlap, their weather meets: warm rain from one, dry pollen wind from another. Lightning sometimes passes silently through root networks instead of the air, making distant flowers open in a traveling line.

Storms are slow and architectural. Pressure accumulates over weeks. Leaves turn their undersides outward. Villagers loosen bridge lashings and drain suspended pools. Then the great trunks flex. Entire regions move by miles while remaining intact. Paths no longer connect to their former destinations; valleys close; new gaps open. Rain is squeezed from the moss in silver curtains. Afterward, the continent rests in a slightly different shape.

This is how maps become false.

At the highest known elevations, the living substrate thins into isolated crowns. The air there is hot despite the height, heated by a dark radiance with no visible source. Pools simmer gently among red mosses. The few creatures that live there are flat, eyeless, and mirrored underneath. Above them stretches what appears to be a starless sky.

But objects thrown upward eventually land from below.

At the lowest accessible reaches, root tips hang among permanent clouds. Water beads along them and falls into darkness, only to return days later as warm rain. Explorers have descended on braided ropes and reported passing the same distinctive branch several times, always from different directions. One expedition lowered a bell for three years. When retrieved, it was coated in fresh leaves and contained a village whose inhabitants insisted the bell had always been their valley.

Such incidents are not treated as supernatural. They are hazards, like rot or unstable footing.

Reality in the High Fabric has grain. It can split along old growth lines.

Two travelers may take the same bridge and arrive in different seasons. A cut made across certain rings opens not wood but distance: a knife-thin view into another canopy, perhaps near, perhaps extinct. Reflections in still pools occasionally continue moving after the reflected animal has gone. Predators exploit this, hunting the delayed image and causing wounds to appear later on the body.

Memory also roots. Repeated actions become easier for the world to perform. A path walked for centuries shortens. An abandoned path lengthens and may eventually contain more distance than the region around it. Rain accustomed to falling in one place continues there even after the pool beneath has been moved. In the oldest plazas, crowds can sometimes be felt passing through, though the paving remains empty. Their footsteps make no sound; nearby leaves bend as they go.

The tenders maintain rituals not from superstition but engineering necessity. Doors must be entered in alternating directions or they begin to lead only inward. Bridges are given unused names so they do not attach themselves to similarly named places. The dead are introduced to the walls slowly, over several days, to prevent the house from remembering them too strongly.

There are failures.

A district may begin repeating one afternoon. A marsh may forget depth, leaving fish suspended in a shining sheet. A trunk may develop an interior larger than the space outside it and pull neighboring structures into its hollow. When this happens, people do not attempt repair. They plant warning blossoms, cut the connecting vines, and allow the affected region to drift away.

From a distance these severed places can sometimes be seen wandering through the gulf: lit faintly from within, raining upward, carrying forests and empty villages. Some circle back centuries later, heavily weathered. Others return young.

Through all of it, life continues at a patient, perceptible pace. Moss advances over a sleeping mat. Browsers migrate with the ripening of fruit walls. Pools fill and drain. Root bridges tighten in the wet season and relax in the dry. Sailbacks cross enormous intervals. Predatory flowers close. Villagers climb out before the green morning to inspect the plumb-vines.

The place is never still, but almost nothing moves quickly.

Standing at the edge of one of the great leaf-marshes, a traveler might see warm fog peeling from black water. Beyond it, the leaf’s rim curves away like a coast, lined with forests that are themselves no more than fine hairs on its surface. Farther still is open air: brown, gold, and dimly green, spacious enough to contain several weather systems. A trunk rises through it at an angle that makes the eye ache. Bridges reach toward that trunk and stop halfway. Drifting animals pass like distant islands.

Close at hand, droplets tremble beneath every blade. Tiny grazers turn the moss. The wall behind the traveler breathes through its vents. Somewhere inside it, sap moves with the slow pressure of buried water.

Then, in the gulf, rain begins along a perfectly vertical line.

It does not spread. It hangs there, a gray column a hundred miles high and no wider than a road. Shapes descend within it—perhaps birds, perhaps leaves, perhaps buildings very far away. Nothing nearby reacts except the plumb-vines, which incline toward the rain as if listening.

There is no thunder.

After a while, the column bends.

And the whole green world, handmade and ancient, bends quietly with it.
