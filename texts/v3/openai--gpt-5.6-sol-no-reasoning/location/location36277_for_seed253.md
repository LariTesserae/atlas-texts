The forest begins several miles above the old sea floor.

Its roots do not reach downward. They hang.

From the underside of the drowned world rise immense pillars of living wood, each wider than a hill, climbing through black water toward a surface no creature here has seen. They are not quite trees. They have no trunks in the familiar sense, no crowns gathered around light. Each is a continuous structure of roots, chambers, ribs, and hollow vertical wells. Their growth has long since filled the ocean from abyss to cloudline, making the water itself seem like an interior: a cool, submerged architecture extending beyond sight.

Yet it is not crowded. The great plant-pillars stand leagues apart. Between them lie enormous reaches of open water, dark and cold, crossed only by isolated tendrils as fine as bridge cables. Distance remains intact. Emptiness is one of the forest’s principal materials.

Everything solid is made.

There is no exposed stone, no raw sediment, no untouched cliff. The mountain range beneath the flood has been covered so completely that its original contours can only be inferred. Long ago, roots dismantled the peaks grain by grain. Acids softened them; boring filaments entered their fractures; patient pressure reduced the oldest rock to dissolved salts. The plants then rebuilt those minerals into buttresses, terraces, vaults, and pale load-bearing bones. What resembles a canyon wall is compressed bark. What resembles shale is a stack of dead leaves lacquered together. What seems to be a gravel bed is made of uniformly shaped seed cases, each hollow and interlocked with the next.

The whole landscape has the gravity of construction without any sign of tools. No builder ever stood outside it. Growth was the only masonry.

At the highest reaches, where the drowned mountains approach the distant skin of the sea, the forest forms suspended plateaus. These are vast horizontal meshes of rootwood, buoyant bladders, and woven fronds, some broad enough to hold entire watersheds on their upper surfaces. Rain falls upon the ocean there—not through air, but through colder layers of water. Dense currents descend in gray vertical curtains, mineral-rich and faintly luminous, striking the plateaus and dividing into rivers that flow along the undersides.

Thus elevation still matters, even underwater.

There are cliffs with nothing beneath them but deeper water. There are ravines that open upward. Waterfalls move sideways where differences in salt and temperature give one body of water weight against another. A creature may swim over a summit and then discover that the summit continues downward for six miles as a trunk perforated by arches.

The highest pillars disappear into blackness above. The lowest descend into blackness below. Their full height has never been witnessed by any animal with eyes.

Light is scarce and local. There is no general illumination, no dawn, no moon cast across a horizon. Fungal-looking growths occur, but they are plants as well: little cups set into the bark, harboring colonies of luminous cells beneath translucent skins. They make muted green points, seldom close together. Along certain ridges, rows of them suggest windows in a distant habitation. Elsewhere, broad leaves flash a low blue when bent by a current, then become black again.

Most of the landscape is perceived by touch, pressure, and sound.

It is loud.

The pillars groan continuously under the burden of the water. Their fibers are tensioned across immense distances, and each carries a profound, nearly unbroken note. Some hum below hearing and are felt in the swim bladder, the jaw, the soft tissue around the heart. Others produce clean tones like horns sounded from beyond a range of mountains. Currents move through root tunnels with a sustained roar. Gas rises through vertical vessels in slow volleys, making the trunks knock from within. Millions of grazing mouths rasp the bark. Shells click. Fins drum against membranes. Vast animals call to one another across the open reaches, and their voices return hours later from remote walls.

Nothing, however, is occurring for the first time.

This world has reached an old equilibrium. The water moves, animals swim, leaves flex, and sound crosses the distances, but the pattern does not alter. Every current returns by an answering route. Every fallen piece of tissue is consumed and reincorporated. Every animal population oscillates within such narrow bounds that the forest’s deeper rhythms remain unchanged. No pillar has gained measurable height in ages. No arch has failed. No new ravine is forming.

Erosion persists, but only as circulation. A terrace is rasped away at precisely the rate its underside thickens. Mineral is removed from one rib and laid down along another. The forest has become both ruin and repair, so ancient that these are no longer separate states.

Its surfaces bear the appearance of immeasurable age. Wood has been polished by passing bodies until it resembles dark bronze. Leaf-layers are compressed into smooth, undulating pavements. Former branch junctions have widened into rotundas. Root tips that once searched for purchase have become blunt towers, their function forgotten by the organism but their mass retained. In places, the ancient plant has sealed its own empty vessels, leaving halls full of perfectly still water. The entrances are oval and regular, ringed by incremental growth lines too numerous to count.

Fauna are abundant without making the open water seem full. The distances are too great for that.

Long, pale grazers travel the pillars in vertical herds. Each has many small gripping limbs along one side and a curtain of fins along the other; from afar, they resemble scraps of fabric climbing a wall. Schools of glass-bodied fish occupy the root bridges, aligning themselves with the tension in the wood. Their bones shine dimly when the bridges sing.

In the water between pillars drift bell-shaped animals several times the size of houses. They move so slowly that colonies of small fish live within their folds. They feed on spores, shed cells, and the continual fine dust of plant tissue. Their pulses contribute a soft concussion to the surrounding roar.

There are also narrow hunters that inhabit sound. They wait motionless beside resonant hollows until an animal passes through the note produced there. Then they strike at the disturbance. Their eyes are vestigial silver pits. Their jaws are sensitive to pitch.

At the abyssal roots, broad armored things walk upside down along ceilings of bark. They cultivate no gardens and use no implements; they merely carry thickets of symbiotic fronds upon their backs. When one dies, it fastens itself to the substrate by reflex. Its shell is gradually engulfed, becoming a decorative boss in the forest’s architecture. Some walls are covered with thousands of these shapes, ranked like ancient shields.

There are no machines and no artifacts in the ordinary sense. Nevertheless, the place often appears intentional. Spiral ramps wind around the great pillars at consistent slopes. Openings occur in repeating sequences. Galleries converge upon chambers whose acoustics amplify the forest’s lowest songs. Bridges join distant trunks exactly where their movements would otherwise pull them apart.

These forms were not planned. They are the settled answers of living matter to gravity, current, pressure, and duration. Given enough ages, necessity has acquired a style.

The temperature is uniformly cool. There are no thermal vents, no warm shallows, no seasons. The water touching the upper plateaus differs only slightly from that in the lowest visible trenches. This lack of gradient is one of the rules holding the place in its final form. Heat does not drive storms. Cold does not sharpen with depth. The animals migrate, but not in response to weather; they travel inherited circuits through an environment that never surprises them.

A herd may spend three generations completing one loop around a pillar and arrive to find the same leaves bent at the same angles.

The plants themselves are so thoroughly intergrown that it is difficult to say whether the forest contains billions of organisms or one. Thin living cables cross the void, joining pillar to pillar. Spores circulate, but genetic difference has nearly vanished. A wound in one distant terrace causes pores to close on structures far beyond it. When a great grazer settles to sleep, the platform beneath it stiffens almost imperceptibly. The response is biological, slow, and exact.

There is no predatory intelligence in the forest, nor any mind shaping events from concealment. Its awareness, if awareness is the word, is architectural. It registers strain. It balances loads. It routes nutrients through darkness. It remembers pressure in the thickness of a wall.

To cross the region is to pass through a succession of enormous, dim spaces.

First come the outer galleries, where roots descend in sparse colonnades and silver animals gather around knots in the wood. Beyond them is an open basin hundreds of miles across, empty except for three black pillars. Their harmonic groaning never ceases. Farther on hangs the Broken Crown, a ring of branchlike arches around a central shaft. Nothing is actually broken there; the jagged forms are the fossilized edge of a growth front that ceased advancing before the oldest existing species emerged.

Below the Crown, the water grows no darker, because it was already dark.

There is the Long Ceiling, a root plateau so broad that a swimmer can forget open water exists. Its underside bears shallow grooves full of animals. The grooves converge into branching channels, and the channels carry cold rivers of denser water toward the Throat—a round opening leading upward through six vertical miles of living tissue. The current inside it is deafening. Shoals enter at prescribed intervals, vanish into the ascent, and return years later by narrow pores along the plateau’s rim.

Past the Long Ceiling stand the smallest structures: isolated stalks no thicker than towers. These produce the forest’s rare light. Their leaves are transparent, each containing a dim red thread. From a distance the stalks resemble sparse constellations arranged in three dimensions. Predators avoid them. Newly hatched animals gather there until their pressure-senses mature.

There is nowhere dry. Even within the sealed halls, moisture saturates every membrane. Some chambers contain water so motionless and pure that entering them is like crossing an invisible surface. Sound changes at the threshold. The forest’s roar becomes remote, reduced to a vibration in the walls. Pale filter-feeders hang from the ceiling, opening and closing too slowly for movement to be seen. Their shadows do not shift.

Time feels least reliable in those rooms. A visitor can sleep and wake with no environmental sign that anything has passed. No sediment settles. No light fades. No tide turns. The same animal may be hanging in the same posture when encountered years later.

The landscape is not dead or dormant. It is saturated with metabolism. Sap moves. Water circulates. Creatures feed, mate, flee, and are consumed. But all of this activity closes upon itself. Life here does not advance into new territory. It does not recover from catastrophe or await one. The forest has already grown through every available answer.

Even death follows established channels. Corpses descend only until they touch a receiving leaf. The leaf folds, slowly, and encloses them. Minerals pass into the pillar. Gas is vented through the singing wells. New animals hatch in distant chambers in matching measure. Nothing accumulates on the unseen bottom. Perhaps there is no bottom anymore—only older and denser architecture, descending through the erased mountains into pressures no swimmer can endure.

From far out in the open basin, the plant-pillars resemble vertical continents suspended in night. Their sparse green lamps reveal fragments: an arch miles high, the underside of a terrace, a herd moving like pale stitching across a wall. Everything else must be inferred from the continuous sound.

The sound says that the pillars remain under tension.

It says that water is passing through them.

It says that innumerable mouths are feeding.

It says, without urgency and without pause, that this place has endured so long it no longer distinguishes endurance from existence.
