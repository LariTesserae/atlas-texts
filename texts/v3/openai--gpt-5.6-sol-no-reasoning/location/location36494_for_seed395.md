The basin is so broad that its opposite rim is usually hidden by dust. From within, it seems less like a depression than a level world: warm, pale, and crowded all the way to the horizon.

There is no surface water. There are no rivers, ponds, marshes, or traces of rain-cut channels. The ground is newly made and scarcely weathered—a shallow accumulation of ash, salt, windblown mineral flour, and crumbled volcanic stone. In places, dark plates of hardened earth still fit together like pieces of one broken skin. Elsewhere the surface remains soft enough to take a footprint cleanly, preserving the spread toes of lizards, the doubled marks of hares, and the small round impressions of beetles. The land has not existed long enough to acquire deep valleys or mature soil. Its relief is modest: broad swells, low scarps, raw shelves of stone, and countless shallow hollows that have yet to become anything more definite.

Across this unfinished plain stands a city without builders.

Its streets are animal paths, packed hard by generations of feet. Its towers are living columns: pale-barked succulents, thorn trees, giant branching euphorbias, and bundled grasses growing shoulder to shoulder. Low walls of root and fallen stem enclose irregular courts. Dense colonies of mound-building insects raise blocks of ocher earth, some no higher than a knee, others tall enough to cast long afternoon shadows. Burrowing animals honeycomb the ground beneath them. Birds occupy the upper levels, lizards the sunlit facades, beetles the cool interior passages. Every available height, crevice, and patch of shade has a tenant.

From afar, the vegetation appears lush. It forms an almost unbroken cover, green-gray and olive beneath the ordinary blue of the sky. Up close, its abundance is austere. Leaves are small, waxed, folded, or replaced by green stems. Trunks hold moisture in fibrous tissue, sealed behind smooth bark. Thorny crowns interlock overhead, not into a cool forest canopy but into a porous roof that sifts the sunlight and lets it fall in narrow, warm shafts. Ground plants crowd the gaps: rosettes, cushion shrubs, wiry herbs, low mats of silver leaf. Many spend most of the year seeming half dormant, yet none surrender their space.

Water exists only inside living things.

Plants draw minute quantities of vapor from the air before dawn. Some open their pores only at night. Others feed on salts and gases rising through the porous young crust. Animals obtain what they need from sap, seeds, flesh, and waxy fruit. Nothing here expects a pool. Nothing migrates toward a river. The idea of open water has no visible place in the landscape.

The citylike order arises from this scarcity. Tall plants stand at regular distances because their roots contest invisible territories underground. Around each grows a ring of dependents: shade herbs, seed thieves, pollinators, burrowers, predators. These rings press against one another until the entire basin becomes a continuous fabric of neighborhoods. Thorn thickets form districts. Insect mounds make dense quarters of reddish spires. Open plazas appear where mineral fumes leak from fissures and keep roots away. Broad avenues follow belts of hard stone too shallow to dig through. There is no plan, but there is structure everywhere.

The basin is low and warm, protected from strong winds by distant uplands. Heat builds slowly through the day. By noon, the plain trembles in the light, and aromatic resins thicken the air with the smell of pepper, dry bark, warm dust, and crushed herbs. Yet it is rarely scorching. Pale ground reflects much of the sun, and the close-packed vegetation makes innumerable scraps of shade. At night the warmth stored in the young crust returns upward, keeping the roots active and the burrows mild.

Life is abundant but quiet.

Small antelope move through the thickets with patient, deliberate steps, turning their narrow heads sideways to pass between thorns. Dust-colored hares crouch beneath shrubs. Tortoises graze on fleshy pads and dropped fruit. Lizards climb the smooth columns to hunt flies in the sun. Beetles roll pellets of dry plant matter along the paths; ants travel in narrow, ceaseless files; pale termites repair the outer skins of their mounds grain by grain.

Predators are present everywhere without often being seen. Long-legged cats rest in elevated forks. Snakes lie along root ridges, matching the color of the soil. Small foxes listen beside burrow mouths at dusk. Raptors circle so high that their shadows sometimes cross the ground before the birds themselves can be found.

Despite all this activity, the basin seldom grows loud. Dry leaves do not rustle much. Most animals communicate by scent, posture, vibration, or low calls that carry only a short distance through the crowded stems. The prevailing sounds are close and slight: a claw on bark, seeds ticking from a split pod, the papery scrape of a tortoise beneath a shrub, insect jaws working inside a mound. Now and then a herd passes, and the earth gives a soft, prolonged murmur. Then stillness closes behind it.

The land itself remains active. New vents open as hairline cracks and exhale warm mineral air. Thin crusts rise overnight where salts crystallize beneath them. In one place the surface may buckle into a low ridge; in another, a hollow may sink as buried ash compacts. Plants are repeatedly tilted, half buried, or lifted with their root masses exposed. The vegetation responds at once. Roots brace raw ledges. Grasses trap dust. Insects mortar loose grains together with saliva and fiber. Burrowers mix the mineral layers before they can settle.

Thus erosion has barely begun, yet construction is constant. The terrain is being assembled by geological pressure from below and by life from above. There are no old cliffs, polished stones, deep gullies, or rounded hills. Every form still has sharp intentions. Cracks are clean. Scarps are fresh. The few exposed rocks look recently broken, their faces unstained by age.

Toward evening, the whole basin changes character. Low sunlight enters beneath the thorn crowns and turns trunks, mounds, and seed stalks into long ranks of amber architecture. Paths become glowing corridors. The distant haze divides the plain into receding layers, each apparently more crowded than the last. Flocks rise briefly from the upper branches, reorganize, and settle a short distance away. Night-blooming plants loosen tightly folded petals. Their pale flowers collect moths without fragrance traveling far.

After sunset, warmth continues to breathe from the ground. Beetles and small mammals take over the avenues. Flowers close behind departing pollinators. Burrow entrances open. Predators descend from their resting places. The city rearranges itself without illumination beyond starlight and the pale reflection of the salt-bearing soil.

By morning, there are new mounds, new tracks, new cracks, and perhaps a new ridge where none stood the day before. Nothing monumental is finished. Nothing is empty. Across a continental breadth, the living city occupies its raw foundation completely, building itself from dust while the ground is still deciding what shape to become.
