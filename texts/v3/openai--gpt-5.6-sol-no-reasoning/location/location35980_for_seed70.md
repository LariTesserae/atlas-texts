The sea begins high above the ground.

It does not lie in a basin or stretch toward a conventional horizon. It occupies the upper reaches of a young mountain range, held among the summits as though elevation itself were a vessel. Warm, green-blue water fills the passes and covers all but the highest ridges. Beneath its surface, steep valleys descend into luminous depth. Peaks emerge briefly as black islands before sloping back under, and farther away submerged ranges overlap in blue layers until distance absorbs them.

There is no visible shore from within the water. Only the suggestion of one overhead: a wavering plane of brightness, broad as weather.

The mountains are newly formed. Their faces retain sharp folds, fractures, and blades of dark stone, with little of the softening expected from an ancient sea. Long fins of basalt rise between valleys. Pale mineral seams divide cliffs as cleanly as fresh cracks in pottery. In places whole slopes consist of angular rubble, yet the stones do not tumble. Everything here seems subject to a peculiar patience. Gravity persists, currents exist, but both act with reluctance.

The water is warm even at depth. It carries no cold abyss beneath its sunlit layers, only gradual changes in color: jade near the upper forests, blue in the open passes, then an almost violet dusk inside the deepest ravines. The warmth gives the water a heavy, enveloping quality. It seems less like something flowing over the land than a second atmosphere into which the mountains have grown.

Vegetation occupies nearly every surface that can hold it.

Forests ascend underwater cliffs. Their trunks are flexible columns of dark green tissue, ribbed and faintly translucent, rooted in fissures too narrow for soil. Instead of branches, they divide into broad streaming fans. Some stand upright in the near-motionless water; others bridge gaps between adjacent rocks, making layered canopies through which light descends in softened shafts.

There are plants like immense ferns whose fronds curl inward when shadow passes over them, though almost nothing passes. Copper-colored moss spreads across exposed stone in dense cushions. Pale vines thread from summit to summit, not taut, but suspended in long catenaries through open water. Their leaves are small and numerous, each one turning independently toward the brightest portion of the surface. From a distance, the vines resemble flocks of green scales arranged along invisible bodies.

In sheltered clefts grow bulbous forests of clear tissue. Their swollen forms contain pockets of gas, silver at the top and amber below, produced so slowly that a single bubble may spend years gathering before it detaches. When one finally rises, it ascends through miles of forest and open pass, wobbling toward the light. Its departure is one of the landscape’s few events.

Nothing here grows quickly. The abundance is the work of immense duration, not fertility in haste. A frond may take a season to uncurl. Groves shift their orientation over centuries as the sun’s path changes. Dead growth does not rot so much as become transparent, gradually losing color until only a lacework remains fixed to the rock.

The water is extraordinarily clear, but dense with layers. Near objects have hard edges: serrated ridges, forests of ribbon-leaves, mineral shelves white as bone. Beyond them, suspended pollen forms golden veils. Farther still, enormous green masses become indistinct, giving the sense that the sea is full of neighboring worlds pressed close together. Open space exists, but never emptiness. Every vista is crossed by stems, ridges, drifting films, or distant slopes.

Animal life is rare enough to feel accidental.

Small black creatures occasionally cling beneath leaves. They have many fine legs and shells like wet seeds. They may remain in one place for months, grazing an almost invisible film. In the deepest gullies, long colorless fish are sometimes seen hanging head-up in the water, each alone. They possess no obvious fins. Their bodies bend only when necessary, and they move by a slow pulse along the spine, vanishing behind stone with hardly a disturbance.

There are reports—if a landscape could be said to report anything—of something much larger crossing between the outer peaks. No complete shape is ever revealed. At intervals, vegetation across an entire mountainside leans in the same direction. A low dimness moves beneath the canopy, followed minutes later by a pressure felt more than heard. Then the fronds straighten. The open blue is empty again.

Silence dominates. Water carries the small sounds it is given, but the place produces few. There is the delicate tearing of a root extending into a crack. The remote click of stone settling against stone. The dull, upward passage of a bubble through layered leaves. Sometimes a submerged cliff emits a single deep note as its young rock adjusts to the mountain’s weight. The vibration travels from ridge to ridge and lingers in the body of the sea long after it has fallen beneath hearing.

There are no paths, ruins, cut surfaces, or objects whose form suggests intention. Nothing has been cleared. Nothing has learned to expect arrival.

Above, daylight shifts through the high surface. It is ordinary sunlight altered by extraordinary placement. At noon it lays moving nets across the upper ridges, catching on black rock and green crowns. In the valleys it arrives as columns. Clouds passing over the sea are visible only as vast, gradual shadows, too broad to reveal their source. Night does not fall suddenly; blue withdraws from the ravines first, then from the passes, while the upper forests remain green for hours. Some plants store this light and release it weakly after dark, not as points of brilliance but as a residual haze, as though dusk has become entangled in their tissues.

The strangest feature is the surface itself. It remains level when seen nearby, yet at great distances it appears to climb with the mountains. Peaks that should break into air remain underwater no matter how high they rise. The sea seems to preserve a fixed depth above every part of the range, bending without becoming a wave. Where a summit grows, the water grows over it. Where a valley subsides, the sea deepens faithfully.

Thus there is no highest point and perhaps no bottom in the usual sense. Elevation belongs to the stone, but immersion belongs to the world.

The mountains continue their slow emergence beneath the water. Fresh ridges press upward by fractions. Cracks open and fill with roots. Mineral warmth seeps from narrow seams and gathers gardens around them—red mats, ivory fans, tiny bladders reflecting the daylight far overhead. Erosion has only begun. Water rounds the keenest edges, but so gradually that the rock still appears newly broken. The terrain is caught between birth and weathering, sharp beneath its immense soft covering.

Nothing rushes to complete it.

The forests hold their positions. The warm sea bears down without violence. Shadows cross from range to range. Once in a great while, a gas bulb releases from a hidden grove and rises through the silent green architecture, carrying with it a tremor of leaves. It reaches the bright ceiling at last and disappears into whatever lies beyond, while the high drowned country continues—dense, open, almost motionless—under its impossible ocean.
