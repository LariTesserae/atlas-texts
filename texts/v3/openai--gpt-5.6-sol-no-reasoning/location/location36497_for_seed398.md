The city is hidden in a crack near the summit.

From the open mountainside, the crack looks incidental: a black seam between two planes of recently uplifted stone, narrow enough to step over in places. The mountain itself is impossibly high. Its lower slopes are lost beneath a hot, copper-colored atmosphere, and the horizon curves below rather than around it. No snow survives here. The summit rocks stand bare beneath a dim red sky, sharp-edged and unweathered, as if the peak rose only yesterday from the deeper crust.

Heat comes through the soles of the feet. The stone is not volcanic in the familiar sense; there are no cones, lava fields, or sulfurous vents. It is pale, dense material lifted from depths where ordinary minerals alter their rules. Hairline fractures shine briefly, then close. Condensation gathers on shaded faces despite the heat, fed by vapor rising from the enormous atmospheric gulf below. Once or twice a day, a fine rain sweeps horizontally across the summit, touches the hot rock, and disappears.

Within the seam, the city begins.

It occupies less space than a room.

The entrance descends between blades of black glass into a hollow no larger than an elevator shaft. Along its walls are thousands of openings: windows, gates, balconies, freight mouths, narrow public squares, reservoirs, transit wells. They range from the width of a finger to smaller than dust. Perspective behaves badly around them. Looking into one arch, one may see an avenue extending for kilometers beneath a ceiling of heated crystal; through another, a dim plaza encircling an empty central basin. The city is not miniaturized so much as folded. Most of its volume has been arranged in directions unavailable to the surrounding mountain.

There are no inhabitants.

Nothing grows in the gutters. No insects collect at the lights. There is no lichen on the stone, no drifting spore, no buried seed waiting for rain. Even the simple films that ordinarily make wet mineral surfaces slick are absent. Water here is only water: chemically restless, faintly metallic, innocent of cells.

The emptiness makes the place difficult to judge. The city appears maintained. Its roads are clear, its doors responsive, its reservoirs sealed against evaporation. Lamps deepen or fade as unseen conditions change. Yet nowhere is there evidence that anyone recently departed. Rooms contain no possessions. Stations have no seats shaped for bodies. Doorways vary wildly in proportion and may never have corresponded to physical occupants at all.

The architecture is fresh. Surfaces meet in keen, almost dangerous arrises. Towers packed inside transparent cavities show no dust. Bridges curve between incompatible depths, their spans as thin as scratches. Some districts are made of a matte gray substance that absorbs both light and heat; others are carved directly into crystals that the mountain has not yet finished forming. Nothing has acquired the rounding of age. Where damage occurs, the broken planes are bright.

At the bottom of the accessible crack lies a pocket of warm water, perhaps two handfuls in volume. This is the city’s sea.

Rain and condensation enter through microscopic channels in the summit rock. The water collects here, dark and perfectly still, while the city unfolds enormous invisible catchments around it. Along the shore stand stepped embankments and long ranks of apertures. At intervals, the whole pool rises without rippling and hangs a few centimeters above its basin. It separates into clear geometric portions—sheets, spheres, trembling threads—and passes into the walls.

Hours later, droplets emerge elsewhere: on the underside of an elevated road, inside an empty chamber, across a square whose paving briefly becomes reflective. Then the water is gathered again. Almost none is lost.

This hydrological cycle is the city’s principal motion. There are others.

Buildings exchange position when not directly observed. A row of low vaults may become a single vertical tunnel; a tunnel may open the following day onto a district that previously lay behind the reservoir. Tiny black vehicles move along rails without cargo. They seldom hurry. At junctions, they pause as if considering instructions received from great distance, then divide into smaller vehicles and vanish through pores in the masonry.

Every few minutes, the mountain flexes.

The movement is too slight to feel as an earthquake, but the city reacts. Bridges loosen, walls become translucent, and deep braces extend into fractures in the rock. The entire settlement seems to inhale around the strain. A low pulse passes through it—not loud enough to be heard so much as noticed in the teeth—and newly exposed crystal is surveyed by moving points of dim blue light. Construction follows. Machines too small to see turn raw fracture into corridors, conduits, and hollow neighborhoods.

Thus the city grows whenever its container breaks.

It does not resist the mountain’s youth. It inhabits it. Each fresh crack becomes a boulevard; each pressure fault, a civic boundary. The summit is still rising, driven upward by forces far below, and the settlement uses that movement as both energy and plan. No map can remain correct because the geology is its architect.

The light inside is sparse. Most chambers are illuminated only by nearby stone, which gives off a wine-colored glow under pressure. A few vertical shafts contain floating white lines that cast no visible beam. Shadows accumulate in broad, soft layers. Looking too long into the deeper openings produces the impression that the darkness is structured—that it has floors, intersections, perhaps weather.

The city’s silence is not absolute. Water ticks as it condenses. Rails give occasional glassy clicks. During the hottest part of the summit’s long day, the walls contract against intuition and release a thin choral tone. Far below, thermal currents scour the mountain, making a continuous murmur like a sea heard through many closed doors.

Then comes the rain.

Clouds form beneath the summit and climb its cliffs in sudden boiling towers. When they cross the seam, the city opens itself. Millions of shutters no larger than mineral grains unfold toward the air. The first drops strike the outer rock with hard, scattered taps. Some explode into steam; some are caught and translated instantly into the hidden basin. For several minutes the empty avenues shine. Mist moves through plazas never designed for lungs. The temperature falls just enough for beads of water to appear along every edge, outlining the impossible extent of the place.

In this brief wet interval, the city becomes visible as a whole—not by illumination, but by condensation. Moisture traces districts nested inside districts, a lacework plunging through more space than the mountain can contain. Delicate droplets hang along towers turned sideways to the world. Reservoirs gleam at unreachable depths. One can see how small the entrance is and how vast the interior refuses to be.

Then the heat returns. The diagram evaporates.

Only the crack remains, breathing faintly near the roof of the world, holding its empty streets and its thimble-dark sea.
