The lowlands begin below the last pale reach of the sky.

They are not deep in the sense of a canyon or subterranean chamber. The land simply lies low and level, a broad basin of black stone extending farther than can be judged in the darkness. Its horizons are interrupted by long shelves of shale, blunt mounds, and fields of mineral columns crowded close together. Nothing rises very high. Even the largest ridges have been worn down to the posture of resting animals.

Water is present everywhere, but seldom as open water. It sweats from the rock. It films the flats in skins thin enough to vanish beneath a passing body. It gathers in hollows no wider than a footprint and in long, connected seams under plates of slate. At irregular intervals a cool rain falls—not from visible clouds, for the sky is ordinarily indistinguishable from the land, but out of the general dark. The rain may last three minutes or three hours. It taps briefly on stone, then disappears under the living mass.

There are no plants. No trunks, reeds, mosses, or low mats soften the basin. Nothing roots itself. The surface is entirely mineral: old basalt polished at the high points, crumbling mudstone in the troughs, salt crust around the shrinking pools. Pale veins run through the black bedrock like fractures in bone. In places, slow condensation has raised forests of calcite needles, but these are only stone precipitated from patient drips. Most have been snapped short.

Nearly every available surface is occupied by animals.

They are so numerous that at first they do not resolve into individuals. The flats appear to ripple. Ridges breathe. What seems to be loose gravel draws inward from a footfall, each pebble lifting jointed legs and scuttling over the next. The low shelves are tiled with hard-shelled bodies fitted edge to edge. Crevices continuously exhale pale, soft creatures and swallow darker ones. Even the pools are dense with transparent threads, armored discs, eyeless swimmers, and small mouths that fasten to larger mouths.

The absence of vegetation has not made the basin barren. It has made hunger its organizing principle.

The smallest animals live on mineral films, dissolved salts, airborne dust, and the thin residues produced by the dead. Some carry dense colonies of simpler life inside translucent chambers in their bodies. Others scrape the stone directly, shaving away microscopic layers with mouths harder than the substrate. Their droppings feed soft-bodied grazers. The grazers feed hunters. The hunters are eaten while still hunting. Nothing remains unoccupied for long enough to decay in peace.

Rain reorganizes everything.

At the first drops, sealed bodies crack open along hidden seams. Creatures dormant as dry flakes swell into motion. Burrowers rise until the ground boils. The hollows fill, and with them come swimmers that could not have crossed the intervening stone—yet are always waiting in each new pool, folded into dust, carried in respiratory sacs, or sealed inside the shells of their predators.

For the duration of the wet interval, the basin becomes a thousand temporary seas. Courtship begins violently. Egg-ribbons cloud the water. Males call from under submerged slabs by striking their skulls against the rock, and the blows pass through the ground for kilometers. Long-bodied hunters follow those vibrations. They slide through water no deeper than their own width, flattening themselves into black bands. When pools begin to shrink, the survivors knot together in dense, glistening ropes and migrate toward the deeper seams.

In dry periods, life stacks upward.

Shelled animals climb onto one another to escape the cooling stone. Winged forms cling in layers beneath overhangs, bodies touching on all sides, their wings too cramped to open. Blind quadrupeds—low, broad things with dozens of delicate teeth—move through them slowly, permitting cleaners to strip parasites from the folds around their mouths. Now and then one closes its jaws. The cleaners scatter for less than a second and return.

There is no quiet.

The loudness is not a chorus with intervals but a physical medium, as constant as pressure. Shells knock together in the dark. Millions of legs rasp over stone. Membranous wings thrum in cavities under the flats, amplifying until whole shelves tremble. Larger animals issue bass pulses below easy hearing, felt in the sternum and in the fluid of the eyes. Above this runs a continuous metallic shrilling, produced by small creatures grinding mineral teeth against their own armor.

Distance alters the noise but does not diminish it. Far-off migrations sound like storms behind walls. Nearby feeding resembles fabric tearing underwater. When rain comes, every pool adds its own pitch, and the basin becomes so loud that loose grains dance across the shale.

The animals do not depend greatly on sight. Light is too scarce and unreliable. Many have no eyes; many others have too many, but use them only during rare changes in the sky. They navigate by pressure, scent, heat, electrical gradients, and the endless mapped vibration of the ground. A motion made on one side of the basin may be answered much later on the other, relayed through thousands of bodies without any creature understanding the whole message.

There is some illumination, though none resembling day. Blue sparks pass between crowded shells where static accumulates. Predators expose cold green organs inside their mouths. During mating surges, tiny airborne animals flash in coordinated sheets, revealing fragments of the country: a slick ridge, a packed slope, a pool white with larvae, the arched back of something immense. Then darkness closes again before scale can be established.

The largest inhabitants are rarely seen complete.

One may be mistaken for terrain until a ridge slowly changes position. These animals lie half embedded in sediment, with weathered armor on their backs and channels worn into them by centuries of runoff. Whole communities inhabit the pits between their plates. They move only when the distributed pressure of the basin shifts—after prolonged drought, unusual rain, or the arrival of another of their kind. Their rising displaces ponds, cracks mineral columns, and sends black tides of smaller life racing across the flats.

They do not roar. Their joints produce long geological groans, and the rest of the basin roars for them.

The place is old enough to have reached a harsh balance. Feeding paths have become grooves in the rock. Generations of scraping mouths have rounded entire ledges. Shell fragments have accumulated in low areas, been cemented by mineral water, and eroded again into new surfaces. Some strata are made almost wholly of compressed armor. The living crowd moves over stone composed of its innumerable predecessors.

Yet the landscape is not lush. Its abundance has no softness. Biomass is locked in shell, tendon, tooth, and dormant cyst. Every body is defended, parasitized, scavenged, or used as ground by something smaller. Space itself is a resource. To stop moving is to be climbed upon. To sleep is to become habitat.

There are rules here, though no mind established them.

Open water must be entered quickly, because it will not remain.

Warmth must be shared, because no body can retain enough alone.

Sound must never cease, because silence marks either poison, emptiness, or the approach of a hunter able to still everything nearby.

Nothing edible may touch bare stone for more than a few breaths.

Nothing living is solitary, even when it appears so.

And anything that rises above the crowd—anything that tries to occupy the air or claim an unshared outline—will be noticed by the entire basin at once.

On rare nights, the noise abruptly collapses outward from some unseen point. The silence spreads in a widening circle, reaching each region moments before an unknown presence does. Shells lock. Wings fold. Bodies flatten until the crowded flats seem, for the first time, empty.

Then something passes through the darkness without touching the ground.

Condensation shakes loose behind it. Pools dimple. The calcite needles ring once, all together.

Afterward the basin erupts back into sound, louder than before, and the animals pour into the wake to consume whatever has fallen there.
