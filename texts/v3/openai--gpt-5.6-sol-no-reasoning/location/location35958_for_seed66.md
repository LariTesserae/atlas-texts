The city occupies a high, warm plateau where water never gathers into rivers. Instead it inhabits the stone.

Rain arrives often but without violence, descending from a nearly continuous ceiling of pale cloud. It wets the broad roofs, darkens retaining walls, fills the grooves worn into every stair, and disappears. Beneath the paving lies an immense porous bed of limestone and fired ceramic, saturated almost to the surface. Water returns through seams and drilled apertures as seepage: clear beads on the undersides of bridges, curtains along quarry faces, steady threads emerging from carved mouths. On humid mornings, entire walls sweat.

Nothing grows here. There are no trees beside the avenues, no moss on the cistern rims, no grass between paving slabs. Even the oldest abandoned districts remain bare. Their masonry weathers into rounded edges and mineral stains—rust red, sulfur yellow, black manganese fans—but produces no soil. Dust is washed into settling chambers before it can accumulate. The landscape feels ancient without ever becoming overgrown.

Against that bareness, the fauna is overwhelming.

Animals occupy every useful surface and many that seem useless. Dense colonies mantle the warm parapets. Pale shelled creatures pack the drainage channels in articulated rows, opening when water passes and sealing themselves when it stops. Broad-bodied gliders hang beneath elevated roads, folded together like roof tiles. Millions of thumb-sized runners circulate through conduits built into walls, clicking softly against ceramic. Larger animals—six-legged, gray, and heavily padded at the feet—move down the boulevards in disciplined streams, guided by low barriers and changes in pavement texture.

There are airborne swarms too, though from a distance they resemble smoke. They rise from heated shafts in the late morning, turn above the city in slow, dense columns, then settle across sunlit towers until the architecture appears to acquire fur. Their wings make a constant dry pressure in the air. Near the cliff districts this merges with the hiss of seepage, the hammering of pumps, the groan of freight lifts, and the deep periodic calls of animals hidden below the streets. The city is loud, but its noise is continuous rather than frantic. It has the settled volume of surf or heavy machinery heard from inside a ship.

No recognizable plants support this abundance. The food web begins elsewhere.

At the base of the plateau, far below the inhabited levels, warm groundwater moves through mineral strata rich in iron, sulfur, and dissolved salts. Vast colonies of simple, leathery organisms line those dark aquifers. Whether they are animals, fungi, or something older than the distinction is disputed. They consume chemical gradients and produce dense films that are scraped from the wells by rotating combs. Pumps raise the harvest into processing halls. There it is pressed into gray nutrient cakes, oils, feed slurries, shell material, adhesives, and a sour combustible gas.

The city is therefore also a factory for feeding itself.

Its urban fabric has grown around that task for so long that infrastructure and geology are difficult to separate. Towers are anchored into natural stone pillars. Aqueducts cross former ravines whose floors have been roofed over and converted to breeding vaults. Old quarries have become stepped plazas, then reservoirs, then neighborhoods suspended above reservoirs. Buildings are mostly limestone, glazed brick, cast iron, and a dark aggregate made from crushed shell. Their walls are thick, their openings narrow, and their roofs shallowly concave to collect rain.

There is electricity, but it is treated as a local and temperamental force. Ceramic insulators march along the upper cornices. Substations hum behind bronze grilles. Yellow-white lamps burn in tunnels and covered markets, while daylight remains ordinary and ample outside: gray in rain, hard and colorless at noon, amber toward evening. Nothing in the illumination is magical. The strangeness belongs to what the light reveals—thousands of living bodies arranged over a completely leafless world.

The plateau is vast enough to make its own weather. Its central districts lie on a nearly level tableland crossed by ancient drainage scars. Toward the margins, those scars deepen into enormous terraced canyons crowded with bridges, hoists, pipe galleries, and cliff dwellings. Clouds form against the outer escarpment, spill over the rim, and fray into low mist among the upper towers. Rain may fall for days in one borough while another, a hundred kilometers away, remains beneath a washed blue sky.

The city has no clear edge. Workshops thin into pumping precincts; pumping precincts become animal enclosures; enclosures become old monumental causeways leading toward distant extraction towns. From a high tower, one sees ranks of construction diminishing into haze, broken by pale stone basins and black-water cisterns. Beyond them stand isolated industrial mesas with smoke laid flat above them. Some may be separate cities. Some are merely outer machinery belonging to this one.

Everything bears evidence of long erosion and longer accommodation. Steps have been hollowed by padded feet. Metal railings are polished at the height where migrating animals brush against them. Water has cut lacework into the undersides of bridges and deposited white calcite curtains over abandoned doorways. Instead of replacing these altered structures, builders reinforce them. Iron collars clasp thinning columns. New stairs descend into the worn bowls of old ones. Pipes bend around stalactites produced by earlier leaks.

Change is slow here, even where life is dense. The great herds follow circuits that take decades to complete. A colony may spend a human lifetime crossing one facade, feeding on salt exuded through the masonry. Reservoir levels rise and fall over seven- or nine-year cycles. Districts are not demolished so much as gradually emptied, sealed, flooded, mineralized, and reopened in another century for a different purpose.

The largest animals live in the lower city.

They occupy the old waterworks beneath the plateau, in chambers several kilometers across. Few are seen whole. Their backs surface in storage basins like wet, dark embankments; their breathing changes air pressure in distant tunnels. They feed on aquifer growths and on mineral slurry delivered by municipal pipelines. Their movement is essential. By forcing themselves through the flooded caverns, they keep channels open that would otherwise seal with deposits. Pumps are synchronized to their migrations. Entire neighborhoods know them only as a low vibration passing through cups, pipes, and bedframes.

Smaller creatures have entered into equally intimate arrangements with the built environment. Filter animals clean public fountains. Heat-loving colonies maintain themselves around boilers and digest industrial waste. Hard-shelled scavengers strip streets after market hours. Long-winged predators are encouraged to nest on transmission towers because they thin the conduit swarms. There are laws governing all of this, though they read more like hydraulic codes than protections for wildlife. No species is owned outright. Each is assigned corridors, tolerances, seasonal claims, and acceptable losses.

Human—or approximately human—residents are present, but they never visually dominate the city. They walk beneath awnings while animals cover the roofs. They ride slow cable trams through moving herds. Their clothing is waxed against condensation and reinforced at the shoulders against falling shells. Many wear small bells, not for ceremony but to announce themselves in tunnels where the native fauna senses vibration better than sight.

The city’s oldest rule is simple: nothing living may be allowed to obstruct the water, and the water may never be made lifeless.

This produces habits that outsiders initially mistake for reverence. Citizens pause before opening sluices. Factory supervisors consult migration boards before increasing discharge. Corpses are not buried—the plateau has no soil—but lowered into deep digestion wells, where mineral organisms return them to the system. Buildings condemned as unsafe are first opened to colonization, their salts and residues consumed before the masonry is dismantled.

At evening the rain often stops. Water continues falling anyway, dripping from kilometers of cornice and elevated track. The stone releases the day’s warmth, and vapor rises from streets in low transparent sheets. Lamps come on behind grilles. Freight engines begin their night ascent from the canyon works, hauling compressed feed, ceramic pipe, and ingots refined from the aquifer salts.

Then the flying multitudes lift from the towers.

They do not erupt. The landscape is too patient for that. One facade loosens, then another. Dark bands draw away from the upper levels and join over the avenues. Gradually the sky becomes crowded from roofline to cloud base, every body turning within the same immense, slow circulation. The sound spreads across the plateau: wings, engines, rainwater, pumps, hooves, distant pressure calls from below.

From the outer escarpment, the whole city seems to breathe—stone and iron sweating into warm air, countless animals passing through it, and beneath everything the hidden water moving by fractions through the ancient rock.
