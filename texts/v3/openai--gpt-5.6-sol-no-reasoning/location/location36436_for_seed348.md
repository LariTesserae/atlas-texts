The town occupies a shallow basin no more than a few streets across. From the surrounding chalk flats it looks unfinished: a scatter of brick sheds, iron gantries, squat chimneys, and tanks whose roofs barely rise above the basin rim. Nothing green softens it. The ground between structures is gray aggregate packed hard by generations of traffic, though no vehicle now moves. Rails enter from the east, divide among the buildings, and stop at iron buffers furred with orange rust.

The basin is cool and damp without being wet. Rain comes occasionally, straight and fine, feeding narrow gutters and shallow sumps. For long intervals there is only condensation. It gathers on pipe joints, on the undersides of catwalks, inside the bell mouths of idle machinery. Drops hang there with improbable patience. Beneath the lowest sheds, black water occupies inspection pits to the depth of a hand. A canal crosses one edge of town, contained between high stone walls, but its current is so slow that the surface resembles smoked glass.

There are no plants—not weeds in the mortar, not algae in the canal, not lichen on the roofs. The materials age nakedly. Brick rounds under weather. Zinc pales. Timber splits into hard gray fibers. Stone steps have been hollowed by vanished feet. Even the rust seems dry and mineral, advancing to a certain boundary and then stopping. The whole place has achieved the mature erosion of something that has stood for centuries and expects to stand for centuries more.

Its industry is intact.

Shafts turn in the long mill. Flywheels revolve behind wired windows. Belts run through slots in walls, entering rooms and emerging again without visible loads. In the foundry, overhead chains travel in a circuit, each hook empty. Pumps work beneath the streets, delivering condensed water from one sealed cistern to another. The power station emits a steady blue-white glare through its clerestory, and the cables above the alleys tremble under an unvarying current.

No smoke leaves the chimneys. No raw material arrives. No product departs.

The machines neither accelerate nor wear down. Their sound is the town’s climate: hammering, rotary thunder, the periodic iron concussion of presses, the scrape of chains, the sustained electrical note of transformers. Sound rebounds from the low basin walls and remains trapped there. Conversation would be possible only by touch, but there is no one with whom to speak.

There are, however, animals.

They cover nearly every sheltered horizontal surface.

Pale moths pack the interiors of warehouses from floor to rafters, their folded wings tiled like scales. Long-legged beetles occupy the rail beds in dense, orderly files. Transparent crabs fill the canal and the underground drains, standing shoulder to shoulder in the black water. Roofs sag beneath colonies of gray, seal-shaped creatures with lidless eyes and six small gripping feet. Birds—or things assembled according to the broad idea of birds—perch along every cable. Their bodies are narrow and metallic-looking, their feathers reduced to translucent vanes. There may be millions of them in the basin.

Yet none of the fauna moves.

This is not death. Small chests remain expanded as if halfway through a breath. Compound eyes shine wetly. A moth suspended above a boiler hangs with its wings at the top of a downstroke. One of the canal crabs holds a droplet between its raised pincers, and the droplet neither falls nor evaporates. Mouths remain open. Muscles are taut. The animals possess weight, warmth, odor, and every other evidence of life, but no succession of moments.

Only their voices continue.

The sound cannot always be separated from the machinery. The roof-creatures produce a deep communal barking exactly synchronized with the compressors. The cable birds give out piercing notes that ride on the electrical hum. From the drains comes a continuous clicking, too rapid to belong to the motionless crabs. The moth warehouses roar softly, like distant furnaces. Each individual seems arrested, but its call has become a permanent feature of the air: not repeated, but prolonged.

The result is overwhelming. There is scarcely a place to put a hand without touching a cool shell, folded wing, staring face, or bristled flank. They fill doorways while leaving narrow paths through the streets. They mantle boilers but avoid gauges. They cluster on unused platforms yet leave every moving belt exactly clear. The town’s sparse arrangement of buildings contrasts with this impossible biological density: open yards, broad alleys, empty loading courts—and at every edge, life accumulated in banks.

Nothing feeds. Nothing breeds. Nothing decays. Predator and prey may be found inches apart, equally poised. A white, many-jointed animal stands with its teeth against the neck of a smaller one beneath the freight office awning. Neither bite nor escape ever concludes. Their skins are damp with condensation.

There is no vegetation because vegetation requires increments: germination, leaning toward light, the exchange of one state for another. The fauna here obeys a different rule. Animals arrived—or were manufactured, or condensed out of the noise—in complete forms, and complete forms do not alter. The machines, likewise, do not perform cycles despite their turning. Every revolution is apparently the same revolution, indivisible and eternal. Follow a marked tooth around a gear and it never truly leaves its first position, though the eye insists on seeing it pass.

The town has weather, but not variation. The rain is always occasional. Puddles are always recently formed and nearly evaporated. The sky is a level sheet of ordinary silver daylight, neither brightening nor darkening. Looking away from a wet wall and back again, one finds its droplets in precisely the same places. The canal is permanently on the verge of carrying a scrap of black foam beneath a bridge.

A central square lies between the mill, the pumping house, and a roofless municipal building. Its paving stones have been worn into shallow bowls. Here the animals thin out, leaving an open rectangle around a cast-iron monument.

The monument is not a person. It depicts a valve.

The valve stands three times human height, rendered with civic solemnity: handwheel, stem, bonnet, flanges. Bolted to its pedestal is a bronze plaque polished by erosion rather than touch. The inscription consists of a single instruction:

**DO NOT COMPLETE THE TURN.**

One spoke of the handwheel points downward. It appears to have been bent slightly, as if immense pressure were being applied to it. Around the pedestal, thousands of small animals face inward. Their mouths are open in the same unending cry.

Beneath all the engines, all the calls, all the metallic reverberation, another sound can sometimes be distinguished—not quieter, exactly, but deeper. It is the sound of pressure held behind a barrier. It comes from below the basin, from under the canal and the foundations, where old brick conduits descend into cool mineral darkness.

The town may be a factory built to hold that pressure. It may be a city caught at the instant its final operation began. It may be the outer casing of a machine whose moving parts are the animals themselves.

Whatever its purpose, it has reached equilibrium.

Nothing arrives on the rails. Nothing leaves by the canal. Rain does not fill the cisterns, and pumping does not empty them. The beasts do not multiply, though their abundance feels one heartbeat away from becoming infinite. Every surface has been weathered into permanence. Every mechanism works without producing a future.

At the basin rim, the noise stops with startling precision. One step outward, and the flats are silent. One step inward, and the industrial thunder closes over the head.

From there the entire town can be seen at once: modest in extent, low-built, colorless, damp, and crowded beyond reason. The motionless animals shine among the factories. The factories labor without result. Above them, the ordinary gray light remains fixed.

Nothing in the basin is waiting.

Waiting would imply that something might happen.
