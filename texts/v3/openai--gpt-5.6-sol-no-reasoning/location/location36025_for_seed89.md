At the summit of a warm, eroded plateau, the mountain opens into a narrow cleft.

It is not a cave exactly. The roof is absent, and a thin strip of sky lies far overhead, but the walls lean inward so steeply that daylight seldom reaches the floor. From rim to bottom the cleft is scarcely wider than a grove, and one could walk its full length before becoming tired. Yet within those small boundaries everything is crowded: ledges over ledges, fissures within fissures, hanging nests, sleeping bodies, roots pressed into cracks, wings folded against wings.

The stone is old basalt altered by weather into rounded ribs and deep vertical fluting. Its original fractures have long since softened. Warm rain, arriving briefly and violently, has polished the walls black and hollowed thousands of shallow bowls. The cleft appears less broken than digested, as if the mountain has been slowly worked upon by a patient mouth.

Water is never plentiful, but dampness persists. Condensation gathers on the shaded rock before dawn. It threads downward in beads, pauses in minute depressions, and feeds a chain of dark basins no larger than footprints. After rain, these overflow one into another. For a few hours a clear stream runs along the floor; by afternoon it is gone beneath the gravel, leaving smooth wet stones and a mineral smell like iron, salt, and bruised leaves.

Because the plateau stands above much of the surrounding atmosphere, the cleft is warm without being bright. Heat is held in the rock and released through the night. The air near the floor feels close and animal-rich, while cooler wind moves above, audible but rarely felt. Occasionally a gust descends in a single heavy stroke, stirring loose feathers and carrying the smell of the outer heights: sunburnt stone, distant rain, and the thin cold of altitude.

Very little grows in the open. Pale tuft-grasses occupy the upper rim, their blades worn short by wind and grazing mouths. Lower down, where condensation is reliable, there are black cushions of moss, small waxy ferns, rust-colored lichens, and a few wiry shrubs no taller than a hand. Their roots travel astonishing distances through the cracks. Some emerge several body-lengths from the plant that produced them, pale and jointed, taking moisture from places where no leaf could survive.

The plants seem sparse only because animals cover them.

At dusk, the walls begin to move.

Small cliff-bats pour from seams too narrow to admit a finger. They emerge not in a clean flock but in layers: first the broad-winged adults, then clouds of smaller insect-eaters, then pale juveniles that flutter close to the stone. Needle-winged birds rise from the ledges at the same time, crossing through the bats in opposing spirals. Moths detach from the lichen. Beetles climb from the damp gravel. Long-legged cave crickets gather around the basins until the water’s edge appears fringed with trembling wire.

The density is greatest where the cleft narrows. There, fruit-sized frogs cling to nearly vertical rock, living on the nightly condensation. Their skin is charcoal gray by day and faintly blue when wet. Above them roost leathery gliders with blunt faces and hooked toes. Tiny amber lizards hunt among their droppings. Blind silver worms inhabit the gravel below. Nothing has much room, and almost every surface is simultaneously shelter, hunting ground, nursery, and midden.

Larger creatures arrive by difficult routes. Sure-footed, shaggy ungulates descend from the rim along invisible paths to lick mineral deposits from the wall. Their hooves have worn shallow crescents into the stone over many generations. Short-maned cats sometimes follow, though they do not stay long; the cleft is too full of alarm calls, falling grit, and attacking wings for comfortable stalking. A dark, heavy serpent lives near the lowest basin, digesting for days beneath a shelf while birds quarrel inches above its back.

Even at midday the place is loud. Water ticks steadily into the basins. Insects rasp from cracks. Young birds beg, adults scold, frogs click, bats mutter, hooves knock against stone, and wings make continual soft collisions with the air. These sounds pile upon one another until they become less like individual calls than the voice of the cleft itself. At dusk the voice swells enormously. Conversation would be useless then. The confined air shakes with chirring, shrieks, croaks, wingbeats, and the dry hiss of loose dust falling from the walls.

Nothing here changes quickly, despite the frenzy.

The animals move fast, but the life of the place follows slow turns. Colonies occupy the same ledges for centuries. Paths deepen by fractions. Guano accumulates in sheltered chambers, then hardens into dark mineral shelves. Roots widen cracks; rains smooth their edges; lichen returns. A basin may take generations to become a finger-width deeper. Nests are rebuilt from the remains of older nests until some ledges seem naturally made of straw, mud, bone, eggshell, and compacted fur.

No structure has been raised, and no object lies here that was deliberately shaped. Yet signs of rudimentary intelligence occur among the inhabitants. Certain dark birds use trimmed thorns to draw grubs from wet crevices. The gliders crack hard-shelled insects between selected stones and return to the same anvils night after night. At one basin, small primates sometimes descend in a nervous group carrying folded leaves as cups. They abandon the leaves when they climb away, and within hours beetles pull them apart. The mountain permits tools, but not possessions. Anything left unattended becomes food, bedding, or soil.

The cleft has one unusual law. Sound travels downward more readily than upward.

A pebble dropped from the rim can be heard distinctly at every ledge below, each impact repeated and magnified. But the thunder of the colonies scarcely escapes the opening. A traveler crossing the plateau may stand a few paces from the fissure and hear only a faint vibration underfoot. Leaning over the edge, however, reveals an enclosed world roaring in darkness.

The animals appear to understand this. Alarm begins high and falls through the cleft in a rapid cascade. Courtship begins low, where calls remain contained. Predators descending from above announce themselves by every scrape. Predators climbing from below are hidden by the noise.

Rain also arrives as sound before it arrives as water. Drops strike the upper stone, and their impacts descend through the canyon like approaching footsteps. The colonies quiet by degrees. Wings fold. Heads turn upward. For several breaths the cleft becomes almost still.

Then warm water comes streaming down every groove. Basins fill, frogs spread over the walls, roots darken, insects rise in a living haze, and thousands of mouths drink at once. The rain may last only minutes. Its passage is brief, but its moisture remains for days in cracks, bodies, eggs, moss, and cool stone.

Long after the sky clears, the mountain continues to drip.
