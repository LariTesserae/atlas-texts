Beneath the dead crust of the continent lies a city grown inside a root.

It is not a single root in the ordinary sense, but a buried vascular plain: a pale, continental organism whose oldest trunks run horizontally through the mineral dark. They are broad enough to contain districts. Their crossings swell into chambers several kilometers wide, while finer branches taper away into unexplored stone. The city occupies one such junction, an open country under the earth.

There is almost no water here. No streams polish the tunnels, no condensation pearls on the walls. The air is cool and desiccating, carrying the faint alkaline sting of dust and cut sap. Life uses other solvents: clear terpenes, slow oils, mineral-rich latexes that remain liquid in the cold. These move through translucent vessels beneath the root’s surface, glowing gold-green when the tissue thins. To drink, animals pierce a capillary and lap resin. To wash, the inhabitants use soft powders, absorbent moss-felt, and volatile botanical spirits that vanish cleanly into the air.

The central chamber is so wide that its ceiling resembles a low, luminous sky. It gives off an even ivory radiance—not bright, not dim, approximately the light of an overcast afternoon. The illumination comes from millions of leaf-discs embedded overhead. They have never seen a sun. Instead, they feed on heat leaking upward from the deeper rock and on currents induced in the conductive veins of the root. Each disc opens and closes on a municipal rhythm, so the chamber has days without dawns: light simply gathers, remains, and withdraws.

Across the floor stand the sparse towers of the city.

They are grown rather than assembled. Straight column-trees rise from measured plots, hollowed while young by collars of brass and ceramic. Floors form as internal shelves; windows are induced scars, sealed with amber membranes. Bridges are braided lianas hardened under tension. Warehouses bulge like seedpods along freight avenues. In the industrial wards, ribbed factory trunks lie on their sides, hundreds of meters long, their internal chambers divided into presses, fermenters, dry mills, and heat exchanges.

The settlement is urban in organization but oddly uncrowded in appearance. Buildings gather in small knots separated by broad, bare intervals of root-skin. Streets cross these pale expanses as grooves burned into living tissue. Rail lines are paired vines sheathed in metal, carrying low platforms whose wheels grip rather than roll. From a distance, each neighborhood looks like an island of chimneys and branchwork in an empty cream-colored plain.

The emptiness is deliberate. The organism beneath the city must breathe.

Every open square is a stomatal reserve, a place where the substrate may split without destroying a district. Every avenue follows a line along which new vascular tissue is expected to emerge. Surveyors continually revise maps because the ground is still becoming itself. Ridges slowly lift over months. Soft faults open as narrow red seams, exposing layers that have not yet hardened into bark. Knuckled hills appear where internal vessels branch. In some places the floor remains embryonic: warm, velvet-skinned, faintly pulsing, unable to support weight. These zones are marked with black stakes and left untouched until they mature.

There has been little erosion because almost nothing has had time to wear down. Shapes are blunt and unfinished. Escarpments end in tender lips. Cavities retain the smooth geometry of cell growth rather than the raggedness of collapse. Dust accumulates in corners but seldom travels far; there is no weather in the common sense, only slow pressure changes through the organism. At intervals, a cool exhalation passes across the chamber, smelling of pepper, wax, and stone. It lifts pale flakes from the plazas, then dies.

The city’s industries serve both itself and its host.

Great pistons massage clogged vascular trunks. Needle towers inject catalysts into sluggish sap. Crews in insulated masks descend through service wounds to remove mineral plaques. Refineries separate raw latex into fuel, plastics, medicine, lacquer, thread, and food. Dry turbines turn in the larger airways, harvesting the root’s periodic breaths. None of the machines are allowed to vibrate continuously. Constant rhythm causes the surrounding tissue to grow toward the source and engulf it, so factories operate in irregular pulses: seven minutes of grinding, a pause, three heavy strokes, silence, then a brief electrical whine.

This gives the city its peculiar quiet. It is active everywhere, but rarely noisy in any one place.

One hears the tick of cooling pipes, the dry whisper of conveyor belts, and an occasional deep wooden report as some distant chamber adds a structural ring. Trains arrive almost soundlessly on their gripping vines. Footsteps are softened by fibrous pavement. Conversation stays low because loud, repeated sounds attract the listening tendrils.

These tendrils are among the city’s less manageable inhabitants. Fine as hair when they emerge, they seek oscillation and grow rapidly around motors, throats, hinges, and nesting animals. Most are harmless: pale sensory organs tasting the chamber’s movement. Left alone, however, they weave dense acoustic knots that damp an entire district. Municipal cutters prune them at night, storing the sensitive fibers for microphones and seismic instruments.

Animal life is plentiful, though much of it is small.

Resin moths drift through the lit hours, broad-winged and nearly transparent. Long-legged bark hares cross the open plazas in sudden groups, their mouths adapted to scrape nourishing films without piercing the root. Clutches of ceramic-shelled beetles live near the factories and feed on metallic salts. Tube birds nest in ventilation crowns; they have no true feathers, only layered vanes like dried petals, and their calls are so soft they resemble breath passing over bottles.

Larger creatures keep to the unfinished margins. Pale browsing animals walk on six padded limbs, clipping fungal-free growths and carrying gardens of seed mites along their backs. Their principal predator is the splice-cat, a low, jointed animal that can flatten itself into vascular seams. It hunts by interrupting the faint electrical fields of its prey. When one passes beneath a street, lamps dim in sequence above it.

Nothing here decays wetly. Dead matter dries, delaminates, and is taken apart by rasping insects or by colonies of threadlike plants. There are no fungi. Their ecological work is performed by black filament-vines that invade a carcass, sort its useful compounds, and raise tiny red flags when the residue is safe for collection. Even the scavengers are botanical.

At the city’s outskirts, architecture and wilderness become difficult to distinguish. Apartment trunks put out unauthorized branches. Orchard vaults break through retaining walls. Old cranes root themselves after years of contact with active tissue, their booms flowering into cable-fruit. Abandoned vehicles are not rusting; they are being translated, gradually and selectively, into wood, resin, ceramic, and leaf.

The citizens regard this not as ruin but as annexation.

Their laws reflect the fact that the city stands within another life. No excavation may sever a vessel thicker than a person’s arm. No district may place more than a third of its mass on dormant tissue. Fires are forbidden except inside double-walled kilns, for flame sends panic contractions through the entire root plain. Most importantly, no one may claim permanent ownership of a structure. A building belongs first to the organism that grew it, then to those who maintain its shape, and only provisionally to those who inhabit it.

Far beyond the settled chamber, the rootways continue.

Some lead to cold nurseries where new districts are being coaxed from bare tissue. Others enter mineral caverns whose ceilings have only recently been reached by exploratory shoots. Survey lights there reveal isolated groves standing in immense darkness: scaffolds around a budding station, a pump house inside a seed-shell, three lamps along a freight spur that ends abruptly at raw stone.

The deepest routes descend toward the source of the root, though “down” becomes uncertain as trunks curve and fold. Workers speak of an axial forest below, a place where the great buried organism is still differentiating—where streets, organs, and wilderness exist only as possibilities in colorless pulp. Sometimes pressure waves rise from that depth. The whole city lifts by a fraction. Bridges tighten. Sap lamps brighten. Animals freeze and listen.

Then a crack travels across the empty plain, slow as a season.

Its edges do not crumble. They swell. A new avenue opens between them, glossy and untouched, extending toward some quarter that did not exist the day before. Survey crews arrive with stakes. Rail gardeners bring coils of dormant vine. Builders test the young surface with padded hammers.

For a while there is the restrained bustle of a city moving into fresh space.

Then the machinery stops, all at once, and the underground afternoon becomes quiet enough to hear the world growing.
