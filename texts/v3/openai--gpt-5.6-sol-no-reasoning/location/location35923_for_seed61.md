Beneath the old ground lies a city no larger than a burial chamber.

It occupies a lens-shaped cavity between two beds of black stone, a hidden interval where the earth separated long ago and never closed again. From wall to wall it is perhaps thirty paces; from its lowest channel to the shallow vault overhead, scarcely the height of three people. Yet the place has been so thoroughly built that distance is difficult to judge. Streets pass beneath streets. Rooms fit inside piers, stairs penetrate roofs, and little bridges span alleys narrow enough to touch on either side. Every available hollow has been subdivided by walls of fitted shale.

There is no vegetation. Nothing roots in the cracks, no film greens the water, no pale shoot searches for a sun that cannot reach here. The city’s surfaces are entirely mineral: slate, clay, chalky mortar, polished pebbles, fired brick, bone-colored deposits from ancient seepage, and thin scales of native copper beaten into fastenings. Even the water appears almost mineral—clear, cold, and heavy-looking, gathering in black runnels along the streets.

The builders had few tools. Their marks remain everywhere.

Blocks were split along natural planes rather than squared. Door lintels are slabs chosen for their shape. Holes were bored by grit and patience, leaving concentric scratches. Copper clamps, green at the edges but still sound, bind the most important joints. Elsewhere, braided mineral fibers have been stiffened with pitchlike bitumen. Pots are thick-walled and uneven. Every drain, cistern, stair notch, and threshold carries the small decisions of hands working within severe limits.

Nevertheless, the place is unmistakably urban.

It has districts, though each would fit inside an ordinary house. A central concourse, two arm spans wide, runs beside the main watercourse. Around it cluster vaulted store cells, dry chambers with raised floors, tiny courts paved in mosaics of river stone, and public basins worn smooth along their rims. An arcade of blunt pillars marks what may have been a market. Niches open at knee height, shoulder height, and just below the ceiling, implying generations of construction by inhabitants of different sizes—or a single population accustomed to using every level at once.

Toward the center stands a stepped structure like a temple reduced to the dimensions of a cupboard. Its facade consists of nine receding frames, each cut from darker stone than the last. Water beads continuously upon the innermost frame but never crosses it. Behind is only a shallow recess containing a round stone, white and impeccably smooth.

No inscription explains it.

The city is wet without being flooded. Water is always near, appearing as condensation on ceilings, sweat on clay walls, and trembling convex drops beneath shelves of rock. It seeps through hairline faults and descends by elaborate channels into sumps below the paving. The channels are not hidden. They are among the city’s grandest works: gutters lined with pale quartz, miniature aqueducts shouldered over alleys, settling pools with carefully graded lips, and spillways carved into the shapes of open mouths.

Despite all this movement of water, nothing truly changes.

Each drop forms at the same place. Each channel carries the same measure. Each cistern remains at the same dark line. The stone is profoundly weathered, but its weathering has arrived at completion. Corners have softened to fixed curves. Steps have been hollowed by feet that no longer pass, yet the hollows deepen no further. Mineral curtains hang from the roof in close ranks, many fused to the buildings beneath, but no fresh thickness can be detected. The whole cavity has attained an exact equilibrium, as though erosion itself exhausted its possibilities and became architecture.

It is cool enough that bare stone draws warmth rapidly from the skin. Breath shows faintly near the lower chambers, not as drifting vapor but as a momentary silvering that seems to vanish in place. There are no warm districts, no cold drafts, no sense of weather approaching. The temperature is the same beside the water, inside sealed rooms, and at the highest accessible ledge.

The light comes from shallow cups set throughout the walls.

They contain no flame. Each cup holds a dull gray paste that emits a low bluish glow, weaker than moonlight and apparently inexhaustible. The cups are spaced according to a civic logic: close together at intersections, solitary above doorways, absent from private cells. Their light reveals edges but seldom color. Copper becomes black-green, clay becomes brown-gray, and water resembles openings cut into the floor.

Some cups have been smashed. Their fragments still glow.

The city is also deafening.

From beneath the lowest paving rises a continuous roar, immense and close, like a river forced through a throat of stone. The streets tremble almost imperceptibly. Basins hum. Suspended droplets quiver without falling. In certain corridors the architecture catches the subterranean noise and separates it into tones: a deep steady note beneath the market arcade, a grinding chord in the western chambers, a hard rhythmic pounding under the little temple.

Yet there is no rhythm in the source. The apparent beating comes from crossing echoes, fixed in perfect repetition. If one waits for variation, none arrives.

Conversation here would be nearly impossible. The builders responded by carving broad systems of hand-signs into public walls: palms, hooked fingers, closed fists, hands touching brow or chest. At junctions, stone arms project from pillars to point along routes. Door knockers are absent. Instead, each dwelling has a polished patch where visitors must place a hand, transmitting vibration through the masonry. The city’s social customs seem to have been designed around touch, sight, and pressure rather than hearing.

Its rare animals are almost equally silent.

In the deepest sumps live a few translucent things resembling jointed eels, though they possess neither obvious mouths nor eyes. They wedge themselves against the current and remain there for years. Their bodies contain rows of dark mineral grains aligned like compass needles. Once in a great while one loosens its grip and crosses into another channel. This is the only spontaneous motion in the city, and even that migration seems less an act than a correction.

Small shelled creatures also occur in sealed wall cavities. They are found singly, never in colonies, their shells fitted so exactly into their niches that they could be mistaken for door plugs. If touched, they retract with a faint click felt through the fingertip. They do not feed on any visible substance. No eggs, young, remains, or waste have ever been found.

There are no molds, lichens, mosses, roots, spores, or traces of decay. Food stores remain in some houses, but they are mineral replicas: stacks of clay wafers, nodules of salt, chalk cylinders wrapped in tarnished foil. Whether these were sustenance, currency, votive objects, or the memory of foods from elsewhere is unclear.

No bodies remain either.

There are beds, low tables, workshops, communal washing rooms, and rows of narrow compartments furnished with personal objects. There are toys: rolling stones in grooved tracks, tiny stacking arches, whistles incapable of sounding above the roar. There are combs made for hair, sandals patched repeatedly at heel and toe, cups stained at the lip. The inhabitants are absent with a completeness that feels less like departure than omission.

One chamber suggests a council hall. It contains a ring of stone seats, each polished by long use, surrounding a depression filled with perfectly still water. The roar is strongest there, but if a hand is submerged in the pool, the noise ceases—not in the chamber, but in the mind. Through the bones of the wrist comes an enormous sensation of distance, as though the hidden city were merely a model of another settlement extending far below: district under district, descending beyond geology, all built beside the same unseen torrent.

Remove the hand and the city is small again.

There appear to be ways out. Staircases ascend into cracks. Passages narrow toward slots where cold condensation shines. One shaft has footholds and a cord of braided fiber descending from above. But each route returns, after only a few turns, to another quarter of the same contained city. The cavity has no room for such loops, yet the measurements remain consistent. Chalk marks left at intersections are never disturbed, and strings stretched through the passages do not tangle. Space here is not unstable. It has simply settled into a shape unavailable to the surface.

That may be the city’s governing principle: everything has settled.

Water flows but does not alter its course. Sound continues but carries no event. Buildings have aged into permanent age. Tools lie where they were last placed, their wooden handles replaced by stone-shaped voids. The dim cups shine without consumption. The rare creatures move only enough to preserve the arrangement. Even the atmosphere seems used and reused, condensed and released in a closed circuit.

Nothing grows.

Nothing rots.

Nothing becomes more ruined than it already is.

In the outermost lane—if any lane can be called outermost—there is a wall made from thousands of palm-sized tablets. Each tablet bears a shallow relief of the city, and each relief differs slightly. One shows a bridge where there is now a culvert. Another shows the temple with an open doorway. Another includes figures packed shoulder to shoulder in the concourse, their heads bent beneath the low ceiling.

The final tablet depicts the city exactly as it is.

At one edge of that carving, in a passage beside a wall of tablets, stands a solitary figure with one hand lifted toward the stone.

Because of the roar, it is impossible to hear whether the carving is humming.

But beneath the fingertips, the whole wall vibrates.
