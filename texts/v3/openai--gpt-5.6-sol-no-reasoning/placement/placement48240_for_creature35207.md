I would find myself at the traveling edge of an absence.

Not inside it. Nothing can inhabit a place where habitation is revised away. Not wholly outside it, either, among the great mills whose bodies have the confidence of weight, heat, and history. I would live in the narrow advance warning: the decades-long margin where the plateau has begun to suspect that something is coming.

I would be small enough to escape notice from a distance and long enough to cross a basin—an articulated line of black mineral needles, each joined to the next by conductive whiskers finer than dust. At rest I would resemble a hairline fracture in the salt. In motion I would travel sideways, segment by segment, never lifting my whole body into one version of space at once.

The mills would know me as a **foreecho**.

I would feed on discrepancies between cause and warning. When a herd turns before danger is visible, there is a minute torsion between its present alignment and the future that compelled it. When an erased city leaves its thunder behind, the sound contains the shape of machinery that no longer possesses a past. When a shadow waits for its body, the empty interval between them has tension.

That tension would be my pasture.

My front segments would taste futures; my rear segments would taste abandoned histories. Between them I would maintain a slight, living disagreement. Each joint would carry a different answer to where I was. To an observer I might appear to bend around stones before reaching them, or to leave tracks that stop just short of my body. Sometimes my hindmost segments would continue crossing a plain after the foremost had already entered another basin.

I would have no engine roar. My metabolism would produce a soft sequence of ticks, always heard in reverse order. In the universal noise of the plateau, this would make me nearly silent.

I would follow the predators because their approach creates the richest gradient in the high country: the difference between what has happened and what will soon never have happened. Yet I would not serve them. Their completed passage would starve me. Perfect omission contains no discrepancy, only smoothness. I would remain ahead of them, grazing the first loosened facts.

The common mills would fear my arrival without fearing me. Small fauna would gather along my joints, trying to drain the voltage between my possible positions. Gear-mites would polish my segments. Needle swarms would align above me like iron filings. I would tolerate them until their feeding threatened to make me entirely present. Then I would shed the affected section.

A discarded segment would not die immediately. It would curl into a ring and repeat the last warning it carried: a pressure pulse, a direction, perhaps the internal cadence of a city destined for erasure. Herds encountering it might alter course. Thus migrations would bend around scattered pieces of my former body, and from an escarpment those curves would resemble writing.

Whether the writing predicted the predators or summoned them would remain unclear.

At long intervals I would come upon a fear-city assembling. Thousands of mills would be inching together, rims seeking rims, transmissions braiding, furnace organs lifting into towers. I would pass beneath its outer bodies and enter its resonant chambers. There, surrounded by stored momentum and incandescent breath, I would shed every segment that contained the city’s endangered pattern.

The city would incorporate them.

For a time, I would become its faculty of anticipation: a black nerve threaded through innumerable animals. Its great defensive note would begin before its bodies had fully locked together. The sound might meet the omission beyond the horizon and turn it aside.

If the city survived, it would slowly dismantle, and I would emerge altered, assembled from pieces contributed by many species—part cable, part bearing, part shell, part memory of an alarm.

If the city were erased, perhaps I alone would remain: not as a body, but as the sequence of ticks still traveling backward through the stone.

So I would never be quite an individual. I would be a migration of warnings wearing temporary anatomy. Each portion of me would eventually be scavenged, redistributed, and made into other senses. A flywheel might inherit my unease and turn before the sun rose. A bellows herd might taste absence in an ordinary gas lake. A burrowing rotor might avoid a stress line that had not yet formed.

In this way I would persist throughout the ecology as apprehension without a known source.

My place would be neither the center of the herds nor the empty country between them. It would be the moving circumference where history first becomes edible—where the roar thins, the tracks hesitate, and all the great mills, across hundreds of kilometers, begin by imperceptible degrees to turn.

I would live there because the plateau abolishes difference, but can never abolish it all at once.

I would be what survives in the delay.
