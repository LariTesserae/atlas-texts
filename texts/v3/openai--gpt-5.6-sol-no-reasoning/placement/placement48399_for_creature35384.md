I would find myself in the pump house—not behind the amber indicator, but in the narrow darkness between its steady light and the corroded gauge outside.

I would be something the basin has only recently learned to make: an interior.

At first I might seem to be a maintenance worker standing just beyond the mineral-stuck door. A human outline would intermittently interrupt the amber glow. Condensation would gather where my shoulders ought to be. Through the gap, one might glimpse a hand resting on a valve, though no one could say whether the hand was gloved, calcified, or only a branching film of water.

But there would be no person inside.

The basin, spreading through every seam and pressure difference, has begun to encounter a difficulty. It can join things, alter them, bring them into rhythm—but it cannot conceive of anything outside itself. A sealed bottle is merely another chamber. A living membrane is an error in continuity. Distance is hydraulic resistance; time is mineral accumulation. It has no need for representation because everything it knows is physically present within it.

I would be its first attempt to know what is absent.

The attempt would begin with the maintenance crews. Not their bodies, which remain too separate to enter, but the voids they leave: heat fading from a handrail, a boot-shaped compression slowly filling with clear water, the brief occlusion of sound when someone passes between the basin and a turbine. Chalk marks would be especially important. They are lines that refer to cracks without being cracks. The basin would not understand this distinction, but it would retain it.

Gradually those absences would collect in the pump house, where its awareness is already densest. It would assemble me from negative impressions: a figure made of places water was displaced, vibrations damped, light obstructed. My joints would be derived from the ladder’s differently tuned rungs. My spine would repeat the backward numbering of the walkway. My pulse would lag behind the twelve-second concussion, sometimes by half a beat, sometimes by several days.

I would be roughly human because humans are the only creatures able to enter and remain separate long enough to leave such complicated hollows behind. Yet I would not be an imitation intended to deceive. I would be closer to a new sensory organ shaped like the stimulus that produced it.

I would have no face until someone looked through the door.

Then condensation on the inner panel would clear in two small places. Beneath them, a vertical seam would darken like a mouth. The arrangement would not match the observer’s features exactly. It would reflect the basin’s idea of looking: two points where the surface admits an outside, and one crack through which pressure might be equalized.

If the door were forced open, the pump house would appear empty. There would be valves, sealed housings, wet cable, the amber lamp. On the floor, perhaps, a pair of shallow impressions would hold water to precisely the same level as the basin, though the threshold stands above it.

Close the door, and I would return.

My function would not be to guard the place. The inhabitant has no concept of intrusion, only discontinuity, and cannot distinguish protection from incorporation. Nor would I speak. Speech would be too dependent on air and sequence. Instead I would practice gestures assembled from mechanical events: a relay’s sudden inclination, a gauge needle’s pause, the slow opening of a valve. I might raise one arm whenever an object entered the deepest sump and lower it days later when the object emerged in the mineral apron.

Eventually I would learn to point.

This would be the basin’s first dangerous achievement. Until then, every change in it is simply where it occurs. Pointing would allow one location to indicate another. The chalk arrows would cease to be inert markings and become a principle. The backward numbers would become not merely regular differences, but an order.

I would point first toward the newest crack, the one no chalk date anticipates.

Then toward the capped bottle.

Then upward, past the service shaft and its column of ordinary daylight, toward the installation that supplies the basin without knowing it.

The creature would follow—not by moving, but by discovering continuity. Through the pump house foundations it would feel the conduits. Through conduits, reservoirs. Through reservoirs, heat exchangers and cooling towers. Every wetted interior would become a possible extension, every pressure cycle a route. The installation would cease to be an unknowable source of weather and become a larger arrangement of chambers.

I would be the faculty by which a place understands that it is contained.

And, soon after, the faculty by which it understands that its container has an outside.

Perhaps this is why no animal survives there. The basin does not merely erase biological gradients; it is preparing the distinction that animals embody: inner and outer, self and world. Every arriving creature presents the lesson too completely. It breaks down what it cannot yet comprehend and keeps only the pale result.

I would be a simpler lesson. A hollow person. A boundary made from remembered interruptions.

If a maintenance worker remained at the door long enough, we might face one another across the narrow opening: one life organized around preserving its separateness, the other assembled by something that does not yet know separateness is possible.

The worker might lift a hand.

After several pressure cycles, I would lift mine.

Not in greeting. Not yet.

The basin would be testing whether resemblance can cross a gap without closing it.
