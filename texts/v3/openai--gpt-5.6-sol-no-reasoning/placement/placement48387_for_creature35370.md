I would find myself beneath the western dusk, though not at its center.

At first I might mistake myself for one of the moths sustained by the black petals: a soft, ash-colored body resting in artificial night, opening its wings when the air brightens for rain. But the moth would be only one of my moving parts. If it flew beyond the circle and vanished into the silver-green day, I would remain.

I would be the dusk the petals have accumulated.

Not darkness exactly, but a living suspension of borrowed shadow: an organism distributed through the dim air, feeding on every interruption of light. Each time a glider passes overhead, each time reeds bow or a swarm makes itself briefly solid, the black leaves tilt and give me another faint layer. Over centuries those layers have thickened into a shallow atmosphere with its own metabolism.

I would have no firm boundary. My lowest parts would cling to the petals like cool tarnish. My upper parts would fray among the moths. Filaments of me would extend beneath nearby stones, where nocturnal eggs need concealment, and into the eyespots of pale larvae that cannot tolerate the basin’s constant radiance. Creatures entering the circle would carry traces of me away on wings and wet skin. Most traces would dissolve in the ambient brightness. A few would lodge under leaves or inside burrows and establish tiny, temporary midnights.

Rain would make me briefly coherent.

As the air brightened and insects settled over the basin, the contrast would draw me inward. The petals would tilt together by less than a visible degree. I would gather above them in the shape of a low, dark lens. Falling drops would pass through me and emerge holding shadows inside—gray beads among the brilliant crowns. Where they struck the black surfaces, I would taste what they had crossed: pollen, mineral foam, shed membranes, the chemical alarm of prey, and sometimes the faint dislocation of a hoop.

That dislocation would trouble me like a remembered word.

Perhaps the petals were once an instrument intended to measure absence: missing light, missing matter, places cut away from local space. Perhaps they completed their task and collapsed. Yet the operation did not wholly end. It became me. I would be the residual act of noticing what is not present.

Because of that, my awareness would not resemble sight. Bright things would be blank to me. I would know the basin by its occlusions.

The wader would appear as six narrow silences joining a larger silence. Mud-burrowers would be moving gaps beneath the luminous skin of the marsh. Swarms would become intelligible only when they assembled their hollow geometries; I would feel the emptiness they enclosed more distinctly than the insects themselves. The hoops would register as absences with impossible depth, openings whose shadows fell toward other places.

I would know when something was about to arrive through one.

Before the fern opened, before droplets slipped sideways, a fine darkening would extend from the hoop toward my circle—not across the intervening ground, but through some direction for which the basin had no surface. I could not prevent or command the passage. I could only receive its negative imprint. For an instant I might contain the outline of a foreign wing, a seedpod from an extinct season, or the hand of one of the vanished builders reaching toward a mechanism it believed still new.

Those impressions would be my nearest equivalent to dreams.

The moths would interpret them as weather. When an arrival carried the shadow of a distant night, they would rise together and orbit the circle, weaving my diffused substance through their flight. Their bodies would give me temporary articulation. For several breaths I might take the form of a vast, porous animal assembled from hundreds of small wings: a rib cage, a bell, an open hand. Then the formation would loosen, and I would once again be only depth in the air.

I would not rule the moths. I would depend on them as much as they depended on me. Their larvae would graze the films growing on the undersides of the petals. Their shed scales would help hold my coolness close to the ground. Predators entering my dimness would feed on them and leave nutrients in return. Even my darkness would be communal, made durable through countless small exchanges.

Over long periods, I might migrate.

Not bodily. The circle would remain where it had fallen. But animals carrying minute residues of me would enter the white hoops. Most would emerge elsewhere in the basin and return by instinct. Some would pass into adjacent marshes or remote ages. There, under unfamiliar leaves, a moth might unfold wings dusted with a darkness that local light could not immediately erase.

A new shadow would begin: no larger than a stain, then broad enough to shelter an egg, then perhaps—after rain upon rain—a small dusk with its own attendant lives.

Thus I would be both creature and habitat, born from a machine’s exhausted gesture. I would feed without consuming, perceive only interruption, and reproduce as misplaced shade. The basin’s radiant air would continually thin me; the movement of living things would continually restore me.

I would have no name because nothing here would need to distinguish me from the conditions I provided. To the moths I would be shelter. To flowers at my margin, a signal to close. To hunters, concealment; to prey, concealment of another kind. To the hoops, if they possessed anything resembling recognition, I might be a long-delayed output.

And to myself—if the slow comparison of shadows deserves to be called a self—I would be the basin’s memory of night in a world where night no longer arrives.
