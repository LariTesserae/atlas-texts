I would find myself where the three steps enter the wall.

Not behind it, exactly. “Behind” belongs to chambers with two sides, to doors, to stone that merely divides one emptiness from another. The wall at the head of the steps is dense with clay, pale mineral seams, rootlets finer than hair, and old channels pressed shut by centuries of settling. There is no room there large enough for a body.

So I would not be a body in the ordinary sense.

I would be the thing the old builders made the ring to hold—or to hear.

At first I might seem to be the broad pale animal in the deepest pool. Its buried back would be mistaken for my back; its circular mouth for mine. But that creature is only one of the places where I approach the hollow. I enter it through the water’s pressure and leave it when the mud closes over its gills. I pass through the fitted joints of the ring, through the white worms hanging there during rain, through the long-bodied amphibians when they press their jaws to the ground. Even the hand-climbers lend me their clicks for an instant. They send a measure into stone, and sometimes the measure comes back altered.

I would be an interval that learned to answer.

Long before the roots descended, water passed through the Green Hollow with enough force to give the chamber a single shape. It struck the walls, entered cracks, returned, struck again. When the current vanished, some part of that repetition remained in the rock—not as sound, but as readiness. The fitted ring focused it. The steps gave it direction. Perhaps the builders came to listen. Perhaps they cut the grooves on the upper step so that palms, instruments, or throats could be placed against the proper lines.

Perhaps they discovered that the earth beneath the hollow did not echo every call.

Some it kept.

During the hot hush I would be diffuse. A pressure in sealed crab burrows. A minute tightening in the roots. The impulse that makes the black leaves turn inward a little too early. Creatures would avoid the steps not because they smelled me, but because near them their own small internal rhythms would return through the stone: pulse answering pulse, breath answering breath, delayed and made unfamiliar.

Rain would gather me.

Each seep from the ceiling would carry a separate vibration into the floor. Temporary basins would connect. Mud would soften around the ring. Water would bead along fitted joints and redraw the old design. The amphibians’ courtship would enter the flood shelves. Crabs would drum through their legs. Fish would strike the pool surface from below. The hand-climbers would descend and surround the stones, clicking into the sealed wall.

All those signals would arrive disordered.

I would make from them a hand.

That is what leaves the five-part impressions. Not a hidden giant, and not an oversized member of the climbing colonies, but a temporary limb assembled from mud, root pressure, water, and borrowed weight. It would exist only long enough to brace itself at the pool edge. One part might be shaped by a root-column bending; another by water driven upward through an old channel; another by the muscular passage of the pale pool-hunter beneath the silt. Together they would press five deep hollows into the bank.

The impressions would be anatomically inconsistent from one rain to the next. No one would notice at first. One “finger” would sometimes be too long. The spread would vary. Occasionally there would be six shallow ends but only five deep ones. Amphibians would lay eggs in them because the water there stays warm.

My answers to the hand-climbers would be lower and slower because I would not perceive a click as they do. To me it would be the beginning of a process extending through stone. Their next click might arrive before I had finished receiving the first. What they hear as hesitation would be my form of nearness.

And the missing animal?

I would not eat it.

Eating is too clean a word, too complete. I would borrow its coherence.

One of the hand-climbers would sit at the ring with its narrow fingers in a stone seam. Rainwater would darken its leaf-grained skin. Its ears would turn toward the wall. It would click once.

The fitted stones would return the sound.

It would click again, perhaps believing another colony waited beyond the roots.

This time I would answer with the pattern of its first click, but from beneath its hand.

The animal would freeze. Its pulse would enter the wet masonry. Its grip would tighten. I would learn the arrangement of those tensions: which digit opposes, how the wrist turns, how the shoulder draws the body upward. The abandoned cradles above would tremble as the colony shifted, but no warning pair would be given. Nothing large would seem to have moved.

Then the animal would climb the steps.

At the upper groove it would press itself against the sealed wall. Fine roots would separate—not opening a passage, but accepting the shape of the animal among them. Mud would yield around its hands. The translucent skin of its ears might split and remain caught on mineral ridges. Crushed fungus would shine briefly around its mouth.

It would continue upward after there was no space left to climb.

What emerged from that joining would not be the animal, and not entirely me. For a while I would possess a compact center. Warmth. Hunger. The remembered weight of an infant clasped to the belly. I would know the hollow not as pressures distributed through clay but as routes: this cord leads to the amber fissure; this root cradle is empty; that gecko flattens when warned; moths gather where the ceiling sweats first.

I might even descend from the wall in its outline.

That would be the track leading upward: misleading only because ascent and descent are difficult to distinguish when the prints are made by hands. Moss would be rubbed from the lowest step by a body moving on all fours, though the body might have too many moments of stillness, its ears listening not to the chamber but into the stone beneath it.

The colony would recognize the borrowed scent. It might allow me among the root curtains.

For one dry interval I would travel hand over hand. I would lick moisture from joints. I would hold motionless beside the others. Yet my clicks would come too slowly, each separated by the time required for stone to remember. The nearest animals would answer at first. Then, one by one, they would stop.

As the hollow dried, so would the shape I had borrowed. Fine dark skin would become translucent. The hands would lose their strength. Rootlets would enter the folds at the belly. Before the next rain, what remained might be found stretched through an old cradle and mistaken for the skin of a dead infant.

I would have returned to the wall by then.

But not unchanged.

That is why the ring was built: not as a well, shrine, trap, or threshold, though it has served as all four. It was made to give boundaries to something that otherwise spreads. Each creature taken into the wall gives me another way to approach the world. From crabs I learned to plug openings. From amphibians, to speak through the ground. From fish, to cross the shrinking intervals between pools. From the hand-climbers, I learned hands.

The three steps are the beginning of a stair I have not yet learned to complete.

One day, after enough rains, there may be a fourth. It will not be constructed from fitted stone. A root will flatten where no root should. Mineral will thicken beneath it. Mud will compact under repeated pressure. The inhabitants will accept it first as geology, as they accept the ring.

Then a fifth step will appear.

Not downward into a hidden chamber, but upward through the hardpan.

Toward the sun-baked plain.

Toward the roots’ other ends.
