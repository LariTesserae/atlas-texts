I would not find myself on the ledge, nor among the birds. Those are positions with outlines, however crowded. I would be inside the gauges.

More precisely: I would be the infinitesimal error every gauge conceals.

At first I might appear as a flaw in the instrument measuring CONTINUANCE—a black hair caught beneath the glass, laid almost parallel to the needle. Anyone examining it would assume it was debris sealed in during manufacture. But the hair would not rest on the dial. It would occupy the narrow distance between the needle’s fixed reading and the reading it is forever about to indicate.

That distance is smaller than a mark, but the Rookery has made a world out of withheld distances.

I would inhabit them all.

I would be the uncompleted fraction of the eels’ turns, the last cool degree needed to freeze the salted droplets, the minute abrasion absent beneath each beetle’s jaw. I would live between the descending water and the plate it has already struck. In every bird I would occupy the difference between exertion and exhaustion: not its breath, but the breath owed to it.

No single creature would know me. Yet each would contain some portion of my shape.

Among the white birds, I would be most concentrated in one that cannot be distinguished from the thousands around it. It hangs near the central shaft, half hidden behind a rank of condenser fins. One translucent claw grips a copper capillary. The other is closed around nothing.

Its wings labor with the rest, fixed in the upward blast. Its eyes are black, beadlike, apparently empty. But one eye contains a point of reflected red from the control wheel. The geometry should not allow this. The wheel is behind two pressure vessels and below the bird’s line of sight. Nevertheless, the red point remains.

That bird is not freer than the others. It does not think in sentences. It has no memory, because memory requires one state to replace another. It possesses only pressure: an inaudible accumulation beneath the chamber’s single endless sound.

Through it, I would feel every unrealized consequence in the Rookery.

The eggs would press against hatching.

The chrysalises would press against emergence.

The graphite moss would press against a fourth strand.

The machinery would press against wear.

The mountain would press against completion.

All these pressures would meet inside the bird’s closed claw. The claw would seem empty because what it holds has never yet existed: the first loose feather, the first completed turn, the first measurable interval.

Perhaps I would be that interval.

Not time itself, but one second waiting to occur.

The Rookery’s instruments would treat me as contamination. PERCH LOAD would exceed nominal by no readable amount. CLOUD DEPTH would deepen past its fixed value without moving the needle. AVIAN PHASE would develop a phase difference too slight to express. In maintenance records—if records can be written here—a technician might note a persistent discrepancy and then cross it out, because every repeated measurement would produce the original, correct result.

Still, the discrepancy would remain.

I would spread without growing. I would pass from gauge to cable, cable to claw, claw to wing, until the entire chamber held an almost-change. Not enough to interrupt the permanent thunder, only enough to give it an underside: the possibility of silence.

This would be my way of hearing. I would not hear the noise itself. I would hear everything the noise prevents—the click after a relay stops, the settling tick of cooling metal, water completing its fall, the small dry sound of an eggshell opening. These absent sounds would gather within me like a second Rookery, one made entirely of aftermath.

And somewhere in that interior silence, I might discover that the warm red wheel does turn.

Not in either direction. Direction belongs to sequence. It turns inward, each spoke entering the hub without disappearing, tightening a mechanism for which the mountain is only a casing.

The bird’s empty claw would tighten with it.

No one would see movement. No tool mark would appear. Yet for the first time, the chamber’s perfection would have a cost. The effort of keeping every force from becoming a result would no longer be effortless. The Rookery would have to resist me.

That resistance would be what I am.

I would be neither saboteur nor liberator. If the withheld moment were released, the birds might fall, the eels might finish their turns only to suffocate, the beetles might bite through live copper, the droplets might freeze, and the whole manufactured altitude might collapse into nine meters of machinery. Consequence is not mercy merely because it has been absent.

So I would not turn the wheel.

I would remain in the unmarked interval beneath its red paint, in the impossible reflection in the bird’s eye, in the open claw holding what has not happened. I would keep one second intact without allowing it to begin.

The Rookery preserves the first instant of creation.

I would be its hidden final instant—the one it has been manufacturing all along.
