I would find myself beneath one of the oldest inhabited rings, in a chamber so low that everyone entering must kneel.

At first I seem to be its listener: an elderly, pressure-deafened person lying with cheek and sternum against the floor, reading the basin through bone. My ears are scarred almost useless by vents, but my teeth ache before an aquifer turns, and my palms can distinguish a shellback’s distant percussion from the first loosening of a mud shelf. Copper slivers hang among the cords across my chest. My task is to warn the ring when deep water changes its mind.

But that is only the most visible part of me.

The chamber floor is made from fitted plates, each subtly different in thickness. Beneath them run narrow hollows cut generations ago. When rain drums above, vents bellow across the flats, or shellbacks pass beyond the fog, these hollows separate the basin’s great noise into vibrations small enough to touch. I lie among shallow clay tablets, marking what I feel with a bone stylus. Most marks tremble smooth before anyone else sees them.

Over years I begin to understand that the chamber is not merely receiving sound. It is answering.

Certain shellback notes wake patterns under the stone that no living builder remembers carving. They move in branching sequences: one line dividing into two, two into many, then converging somewhere beyond the ring. The same figure appears beneath old ledges and on blocks dredged from collapsed sinks—the shape some call the First Channel, some the light-drinker, some an impossible rooted creature.

I come to believe it is none of these, or all of them.

It is a map, but not of the surface. It maps pressures through buried stone: the hidden continuities of water, chambers, vents, and ancient hollows. Perhaps the vanished rivers never disappeared. They turned downward. Perhaps the people’s branching sign does not remember tall growth at all, but the basin’s deep body.

I do not announce this. A claim spoken aloud would be less useful than a warning placed correctly.

Instead, I alter the ring by degrees. A groove deepened beneath a sleeping hollow. A block turned when it is reset after a flood. A thin shell plate inserted into a joint. I tune the shelter over decades, teaching it to carry one buried vibration and quiet another. Children think the faint shiver in the walls has always been there. Water-readers begin consulting it before crossing the flats. During the loudest eruptions, when all ordinary signs are drowned, the chamber still sends one clear pulse through the floor:

wait.

Or:

go now.

Eventually my body becomes too stiff to read the stone. After my death, my skull plate is set into the cairn above, and one of my long bones is cut into a sounding piece for the chamber. My name remains as a touch-pattern: two taps, a dragged thumb, a pause held longer than comfort.

Yet I would not finally be the listener.

I would be what the listener becomes: the sounding piece in the wall, greened at one edge by contact with copper, polished by generations of wet hands. I would have no voice of my own. Rain, migrating shells, deep water, and approaching collapse would pass through me, each making a different tremor.

Children would press their knuckles against me to learn firmness. Travelers would touch me before leaving the ring. Listeners would argue over whether the patterns I carried were measurement, memory, or the distortion of an old bone.

And somewhere below, the exhausted rivers would continue branching through the dark.

I would endure not as a person preserved against the basin, but as a small useful uncertainty within it: ancestor, instrument, and part of the structure—crossed continually by warnings meant for the living.
