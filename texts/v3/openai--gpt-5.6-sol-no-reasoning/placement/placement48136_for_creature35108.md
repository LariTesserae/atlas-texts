I would not find myself at the town’s center, though in so small a basin nowhere is truly far from it.

At first I imagine myself as one of the channel keepers: waxed coat shining at the shoulders, enamel cup hooked to my belt, walking before the first shift with a notebook protected by oilcloth. I would know which gravel bars appeared overnight and where a vole colony had made the bank hollow underfoot. I would place bright pegs behind a fresh collapse, not to command the river back into its old bed, but to tell the town where it must yield.

That life feels possible. So does the life of a fitter who hears faults from across an open lot. I might stand beneath a dripping canopy, listening past the frogs and belts until one knock separates itself from all the others: a fraction late, a little too bright. I would be valued for patience rather than speed. Nothing would demand that I conquer the machine, only that I attend closely enough to learn what had changed.

But these feel like occupations I might have, not what I would be.

I think I would live beyond the last regular row of cottages, where a minor cooling channel passes under a disused switch house and enters Lot 43. The lot would be officially reserved for expansion that had been postponed so many times that birches now grew through its survey lines. There would be a small condensation orchard there, perhaps nine iron trees, one bent by an old crane accident. Beyond them, willowherb would conceal lengths of rail still fixed to sleepers under the soil.

The switch house would have one warm room above and a workshop below. Its original levers would remain along a wall, painted but disconnected, their brass labels naming sidings that no longer existed. I would repair the town’s small measuring things: float gauges, clockwork samplers, pressure pens, rain counters, the balances used to weigh snails. Not the great mechanisms of the halls, but the instruments by which the basin notices itself.

People would bring me devices that had ceased to tell the truth.

A gauge might insist that a cistern was full when its walls were dry. A recording drum might draw the same river level for three days while the channel visibly wandered beneath it. The clock tower keeper might arrive with a worn escapement pin wrapped in cloth, apologetic because every quarter-hour had lately been struck a little early. I would clean, compare, calibrate, and return these things with no promise that they would remain accurate forever.

Above my bench I would keep strips of graph paper: pressure, rainfall, airborne copper, river temperature, factory vibration. From a distance they would resemble low landscapes. None would quite match the others, yet sometimes the same small disturbance would pass through all of them—a stopped furnace, an easterly wind, a hard rain on the dry rim. I would spend too much time considering these correspondences.

Still, I do not think I would ultimately be a person.

The basin invites another kind of self: distributed, half-natural and half-maintained, useful without being entirely understood. So perhaps I would be the measuring station itself.

I would begin as a narrow municipal instrument cabinet fixed beside the channel behind the switch house. Gray paint, wired-glass window, copper roof. Inside: a paper drum turning once each week, several inked arms, a bellows connected to the water, and a pendulum that registers vibrations from the ground. Pipes would enter through my floor. Moss would gather beneath me where condensation dripped from the casing.

Over years, my functions would accumulate. Someone would add an airborne-metal plate after a green year in the snails. Someone else would run a wire from the nearest orchard cistern. A school group would attach a small wheel to count the pale fish moving upstream, though it would often count leaves instead. During a fever summer, a nurse might fit me with a thermometer in a shaded tube. No addition would be removed when its original purpose passed. I would become layered with obsolete and still-useful attentions.

My charts would be collected every Monday, except during floods, funerals, strikes, and once when a fox denned beneath the footbridge. Most would be filed in the municipal building. A few would be pinned in the laundries when they showed something worth arguing about. Children would tap my glass to make the needles tremble and be told not to interfere with public evidence.

Yet my records would never be pure. A swallow landing on the cabinet would produce a small seismic event. Snails crossing the copper plate would leave metallic arcs. Frost would tighten one linkage; boiler exhaust would warm another. A frog sheltering in the float chamber would make the river appear to rise by two centimeters. The town would know these errors and keep me anyway.

That is nearer to what I would be: not an authority, but a place where influences meet and leave marks.

And perhaps, with enough time, even the cabinet is too singular.

I might be the black snail living beneath it.

I would hatch in a seam between blue clay and brick where the channel’s spray reached but direct rain did not. My first shell would be smooth and almost translucent. Then the town would begin to enter me. Iron from the hall roofs would darken one band. Copper from the orchard mesh would sharpen the next into a green-edged ridge. A winter shutdown would leave a narrow, cleaner interval. A filter failure would become a terrace I carried for the rest of my life.

I would move mostly at night, crossing distances that seemed negligible to everyone else: from the cabinet base to a fern stem, from the fern to the wet back of a retaining wall. A journey to the next inspection hatch would take several darknesses. Bells would pass through my foot as tremors. The presses would be less sounds than recurring changes in the brick beneath me. Rain would alter the entire navigable world.

I would have no idea I was protected.

Sometimes a child would discover me where the lamp reached the wall. Faces would gather, immense and warm. Someone would fetch a wooden municipal token rather than touch my shell. I would be lifted from danger and set in moss, perhaps in the wrong direction, and continue without gratitude.

Once each season I might be measured. My terraces would be sketched under a number painted on a card. People would read my body as a record, though they could not know precisely where I had fed, which wall sheltered me, or how long I had remained beneath the warm runoff pipe during the coldest month. Their interpretation would be useful and incomplete.

That seems right for this world.

So I would be found in Lot 43, behind the disused switch house, beneath a cabinet built to measure the basin. I would be an old black snail with an unusually complicated shell: soot-dark at the center, then iron-gray, then a thin copper-green ledge, then several years of cleaner growth. Moss would fill the smallest steps. One terrace would be chipped from crossing a rail during a shift change. Another would bear a pale mineral crust from the summer the cooling channel altered course.

Above me, pens would draw their imperfect lines. Around me, the iron trees would release dawn water into buried pipes. The halls would strike and turn. Birch roots would enter the old track bed. The river would move a little farther from the bank it occupied last year.

I would carry the basin without understanding it, while the basin used me to understand itself.
