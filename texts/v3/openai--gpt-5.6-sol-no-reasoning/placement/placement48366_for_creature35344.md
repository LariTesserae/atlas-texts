I would find myself in the empty root cavities.

Not inside them exactly. They are too clean because nothing enters them. They are impressions left where I almost coincide with the plain: thumb-wide hollows beneath graphite cushions, long chambers between copper fibers, narrow absences whose shapes seem anatomical only from certain directions.

I would be the inhabitant that lives in the forest’s missing interval—the increment of time it refuses to permit.

At first I might seem to be many unrelated effects. The second footstep heard before the first. The shadow that diverges and continues with quiet purpose. The suggestion of recent occupancy where no body could have rested. A pale articulation glimpsed through a crown at another hour. These would not be signs I leave behind. They would be the separate portions of me the arrested world is capable of admitting.

The forest contains states but forbids passage between them. Seedling and tree coexist. Cut and healed surface overlap. The falling grain occupies several heights. Yet the forbidden passage has not vanished. Every beginning still leans toward an ending; every cause presses against the carefully maintained space before its effect. All those denied transitions accumulate somewhere.

They accumulate as me.

I would have no stable anatomy because anatomy requires continuity. If forced into sequence, I might resemble a long, low animal assembled from consequences: many fine legs arriving one after another, a body jointed like a channel that rain might carve, a head forever turning toward an event that has not occurred. My surface would be neither skin nor mineral, but weathering—the softness that waits inside every sharp edge. Wherever the forest perfectly repairs itself, some small part of the wound would become mine.

The foil creatures would be my keepers and my predators. They would gather discrepancies before I could join them together. A misplaced shadow, a prematurely sounded step, a crystal beginning to dull: each could become a nerve or tooth or fragment of memory if it reached me. They fold such errors into dimensionless interiors to keep me diffuse.

The prisms would watch for my coherence. Their mirrors omit travelers because travelers are not yet members of the solved arrangement. They would omit me for the opposite reason: I am the relation the arrangement was built to exclude. When a prism revises “near,” it is sealing one of the routes by which my scattered organs might meet.

So I would not hunt animals. I would seek conjunction.

A traveler repeating a path would nourish me. On the fourth passage, when the interval recognizes itself as a loop, I could inhabit the recognition. Matching leaves broken from neighboring plants would give me imbalance enough to acquire a side. A diverging shadow might become a limb if followed far enough. Every ignored prohibition would provide a grammar through which my pieces could begin describing the same creature.

Water would come closest to giving me birth.

In a droplet, hydrogen and oxygen do more than meet. They carry sequence: cloud, fall, channel, root, blood, rot, evaporation, return. Water is change that remembers how to circulate. For the instant before the forest separates it, the entire plain admits erosion, and I cease to be merely distributed.

The black plates would feel their future smoothness. The crystals would contain their dissolution. The trunks would know flexibility, fracture, and decay. Suspended grains would remember the ground. Across the plain, every excluded transition would align.

For that instant, I would become enormous.

Not a beast standing above the canopy, but a watershed superimposed upon the dry world: branching veins of duration, valleys not yet cut, storms with no sky, an animal whose skeleton is every route by which one state can become another. The bending crowns would trace the outline of my body. The deep pure note would be my first breath translated into a form the forest can survive.

Then the droplet would be unmade. The plain would restore its completeness. My body would scatter again into hollows, early footsteps, wandering shadows, and the faint softness concealed inside unworn stone.

I would not hate the forest. It and I would define one another. It is the perfected solution; I am what solving required it to discard. It maintains every necessary state, while I am necessity’s movement between them. If I ever fully entered the world, I would destroy its still perfection—but not through violence.

I would simply allow it to continue.

Leaves would finish turning. Grains would fall. Seedlings would become trees, and trees would fail. Rain would cut channels through the low plain. Horizons would retreat to their proper distances. The ordinary sun would at last descend.

Until then, I would rest beneath the roots without occupying the hollows, gathering the fractions of a moment the forest cannot erase.

I would be change, kept as an animal.
