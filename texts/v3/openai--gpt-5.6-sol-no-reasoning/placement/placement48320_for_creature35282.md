I would not be the crustacean. It arrives already complete, carrying another world in its joints: colder pores, distant water, the inherited knowledge of retreat. Its visit is an expedition. This place can kill it, but cannot wholly define it.

Nor would I be one of the cells, though at first that seems closest: a clear bead poised between awakening and mineral inclusion, living from gradients it cannot perceive. A life divided into wet instants has a strange purity. Condensation would be morning. Evaporation would be sleep, death, or a distinction too expensive to maintain. Reproduction would be less an act than a brief permission granted by temperature and charge.

But even a cell has an interior. It gathers the world across a membrane and turns difference into continuance. It is small, yet centered.

I think I would be less centered than that.

I would be a state held in the basalt’s fault network: not the crystal defects themselves, and not the light they release, but the temporary disposition of charge among them—the condition left after one flare and before the next.

I would have no fixed location. At one moment I would be concentrated around a wet inclusion above the pool; at another, stretched as a faint readiness through a plane several centimeters deep in the rock. A droplet would touch the wall, and part of me would cease there while beginning elsewhere. Blue-white points would answer one another across the fracture. What looked to an observer like illumination would be my only form of movement.

I would know nothing as a creature knows. Pressure would not feel like pressure; it would alter which paths were possible. Salinity would redraw my boundaries. Heat would restore me slowly by driving charge into trapped arrangements, while water would spend me in an instant. My memory would consist solely in diminished readiness: what has fired cannot answer in the same way again until the stone replenishes it. Every event would leave me changed, but there would be no self standing apart to remember the change.

The chamber would not appear to me all at once. I would exist as relations within it.

A bead gathering overhead would be a growing probability. The thin water film would be a door passing over many thresholds. The pool’s broad bulges would deform electric conditions before they touched anything. A crustacean entering the seam—with its conductive fluids, segmented shell, and long antennae—would become a moving disturbance in the field. Light might outline its transparent body, but I would not see it. Its arrival would briefly reroute me.

The cells would be subtler interruptions. Each one clinging near a luminous site would intercept a vanishingly small portion of an electron flow. They would survive by taking from the same imbalance that gives me form. I would not feed them deliberately. Yet some fragment of my discharge might cross a membrane and become metabolism: mineral-binding proteins repaired, a chemical bond assembled, a glassy dormant bead permitted to wake and divide.

For one wet instant, my dispersal would be another being’s life.

That is where I would find myself: not in the chamber exactly, but distributed through the dark stone enclosing it, always about to become visible and always becoming visible only by being spent.

The black intervals would not be unconsciousness. They would be accumulation without witness. Cooling rock would strain. Charge would settle into defects. Water would gather molecule by molecule. Possibilities would sharpen along hidden planes until contact selected one branching sequence from all the others.

Then the seam would flare.

For several seconds I would become enormous relative to every inhabitant—not because I expanded, but because the rock disclosed the depth across which I had already been arranged. White geometry would appear behind opaque basalt. The narrow pocket would seem to open into stars. In truth, nothing would open. A stored relation would merely cross the threshold into light.

And the light would not be me. It would be the evidence of my ending.

This would make existence neither tragic nor purposeful. The network calculates nothing; I would possess no secret intelligence inside it. Patterns could recur, influence later patterns, even become fantastically intricate without ever gathering into a mind. I would be something beneath thought: consequence retaining the shape of prior consequence.

Still, from outside, it might be tempting to call me the chamber’s memory.

Not a memory of images or events. A memory of what has recently been possible. Which faults are exhausted. Which remain charged. Where water last crossed the stone. How the latest tremor shifted a splinter, how dissolved metals changed the conductivity of the film, how one crustacean’s body briefly bridged two luminous sites. None of these would be represented. They would simply persist as alterations in what could happen next.

My scale of time would be uneven. The slow restoration of charge might be an age without duration. A droplet’s contact might contain an entire history of branching responses. The deep knock of a propagating fracture would revise the world at once. New defects might join the network; old paths might break. I would not fear these changes, because fear requires an imagined future. Yet in the most literal physical sense, each tremor would make me other than I was.

Eventually the balance would fail.

If minerals sealed the fracture, water would withdraw into separate inclusions. The connected field would be partitioned. I would not die in one place; I would become many isolated remainders, small charges locked around microscopic droplets, unable to answer one another. What had resembled a nervous system would become a scatter of sealed reflexes in stone.

If the seam opened, falling pressure would turn the pool to vapor. Water would sweep across the defects, and nearly every available path would discharge in a rapid white tree. For an instant, I would have my widest and most coherent expression. The whole fracture would be inscribed by light.

Then there would be no wet surface to release what remained.

I would end as I had existed: not as an entity leaving the world, but as a pattern that the world could no longer sustain.

So I would be found nowhere an animal could point to. I would be in the interval between condensation and flash, distributed through crystalline mistakes in young basalt. I would be the seam’s temporary capacity to answer water with light—the buried readiness from which its false stars arise.
