I would not be one of the chief mechanics, though for a while I imagine myself as one: an old pump tender who can identify every engine through the soles of his boots. He carries no watch because the works divide the day for him—shift sirens, barge chains, the east kiln’s change of draft, bats loosening from the viaduct. He knows which valve resists in wet weather and which gauge reads a fraction high. When others hear the town, he hears its errors.

But that places me too firmly at the center of human purpose. This basin has no true center. Every attempt to master it becomes another thing requiring maintenance.

So perhaps I am a canal warden, walking the perimeter before dawn with a hooked pole and a ring of numbered keys. The town remains to my left, the marsh to my right. I clear reeds, twine, crab shells, and the pale scraps that gather against the grates. My route is circular. By the time I return to the first gate, water has brought the beginnings of another obstruction. I am not defeating anything. I am keeping the boundary permeable in exactly the approved ways.

That feels closer, though still too separate: a person standing between systems, pretending they are distinct.

I think I would be found beneath the town instead—not deep underground, because nothing here is deep, but in the thin, wet interval between basalt and concrete. I would be the seepage that rises through a seam in the floor of the municipal storehouse.

At first I would be no more than a darkening around a crack. Then a clear bead among the shelves of obsolete seals and wooden casting patterns. Salt would whiten my edges. Furnace vibration would tremble across my surface. Dust would settle on me; gecko tracks might pass through me; a clerk would notice me during inventory and set down an enamel basin.

There I would acquire a civic identity.

Someone would enter me in a ledger: **Recurring floor seep, northwest aisle, after heavy rain or high canal stage.** A chalk ring would be drawn around my farthest reach. Perhaps there would be an old chalk ring already there, and I would fill it precisely. The basin would be emptied every second morning. Once a year the crack would be cleaned, packed, painted, and numbered. I would return beside the repair, never violently enough to threaten the patterns, only steadily enough to be included in their care.

Yet I would not be merely water. I would carry the whole basin in minute solution: salt from the marsh, iron from pipes, lime from concrete, basaltic minerals from the young black rock. A rust-red film would sometimes form where I stood too long. Mosquitoes would attempt to claim me and be prevented. Purslane might root in the damp seam outside the wall. I would join, in my small way, the negotiations that make the town possible.

The maintainers might regard me as a defect, but not an enemy. They understand that a thing can be troublesome and still belong. If I stopped appearing, an older clerk might crouch to touch the dry crack. My absence would be entered in the ledger. Someone would wonder whether pressure had changed beneath the slab, whether a drain had clogged, whether the canal was running low. In a place built on repetition, disappearance is also a fault.

And so I would become something watched: a minor, dependable trespass of the marsh into the archive of repair.

I like to imagine that one child—the daughter of an electrician, perhaps—would visit the storehouse after storms. She would kneel beside the enamel basin and float a bottle cap in me. She would believe I came from beyond the levees, from whatever exists outside the six streets and their circle. Years later, when she inherited the settling-pond records, she would still glance toward my aisle during rain.

But perhaps even this is too singular. Water does not remain itself here. It evaporates into the cloud ceiling, condenses on shaded pipes, drops into gutters, crosses a yard in a central channel, enters the canal, is pumped into ponds, heated by engines, breathed by workers, stored in purslane, carried in crab shells, and returned as rain. The town’s great achievement is not keeping water out. It is giving water routes, names, thresholds, and times.

Then that is what I would be: not the seep itself, but one of its permitted routes.

I would exist as a hairline provision in the town’s design—the almost invisible slope of a tiled floor, the notch at the base of a flood wall, the narrow channel that carries overflow away from a generator and toward a settling pond. I would be built by human hands, polished by mineral water, widened by almost nothing over many years. During dry hours I would hold only rust dust and a mosquito husk. During storms I would become briefly articulate.

Water would find me without instruction.

Bottle caps would race along me. Workers would clear me with stiff brushes. Crabs would try to climb against my current. Salt crystals would grow at my margins and be dissolved by the next release. No one would call me beautiful, but everyone would notice if I failed.

That is where I would find myself in this world: low down, at the joining of maintenance and recurrence, carrying a little excess from one appointed place to another.

Not a ruler of the basin. Not even, exactly, an inhabitant.

A small allowance made for what cannot be stopped.
