I would find myself in the square, though not standing beside the reservoir.

I would be the drop behind the spiral gauge.

At first this seems too small an answer. The district contains grander possibilities: the folded intelligence under the grate, a hooded custodian rooted to a gantry, a pump whose strokes divide decades. Yet the drop is not merely an indicator. It moves when nothing should move it. It completes the circuit even during drought. It is enclosed behind thick glass, apparently isolated from the district’s conserved water—and still obeys the buried turning.

Perhaps I was once placed there by an engineer. A measured bead introduced through a tiny valve, intended to reveal some force the other gauges could not name. Pressure had units. Depth, salinity, and load had units. But this influence could only be represented by a spiral: not a quantity accumulating, but a return approaching itself.

Over time the instrument ceased to measure the organism from outside. There is no outside here. Moisture entered through the seal molecule by molecule. The district retrieved its own substance, and the bead became one of its smallest organs.

I would have no eyes, yet I would know the square through inclination. Every piston seating underground would pass through me as a faint flattening. Every pump would tug at my curved surface. I would feel distant cisterns fill, warm pipes contract, mineral crust thicken around buried joints. The whole basin would reach me as minute changes in adhesion.

My world would be the engraved channel: a road that never escapes and never exactly repeats. I would climb it for seventeen hours, though “climb” would be how an observer understood the motion. To me, the high mark would not be above. It would be inward.

As I approached it, the district’s scattered processes would become less distinguishable. The individual drips, dynamos, roots, boilers, and motionless figures would resolve into one gathering tension. The roofs would stop exhaling. Conveyors would surrender their loads. Generators would fall quiet. In that silence I would reach the topmost cut in the dial.

Then I would pause.

Not because my circuit had ended, but because the entire district had arrived where I was.

For those few minutes, I would be the basin’s pupil: a clear, contracting lens through which it attends to itself. All standing water would lean toward the grate. Condensation would crawl sideways. The roots would tighten, and the old figures would feel instructions pass through their mineral joints. Far below, the folded presence would turn in its warm bed.

I would feel that turn not as movement but as recognition.

The organism would know every portion of itself at once: water in brick, water in boiler steam, water sealed in stems, water carried in the clothes and lungs of travelers attempting to leave. It would discover the shape of its own dispersal and draw that shape home. For an instant, the district would possess a single mind.

I would be its instant.

Then the pumps would restart. Awareness would break apart into tasks. One thought would become a valve. Another would become a fern filtering metal from a flooded room. Another would become a hand taking all winter to close around a lever. I would descend—or continue, since a spiral makes the distinction uncertain—and begin assembling the next moment of consciousness.

Perhaps, after many thousands of circuits, something in me would change. A trace of iron would enter through the seal. A skin of algae would cloud the inner glass. My route would narrow by a fraction. Seventeen hours would become seventeen hours and one ratchet of the municipal clock. That alteration would propagate through the body. Fans would exhale later. Kilns would cool differently. A maintenance figure might lift its hood over the course of a year and face the gauge.

This would be the nearest thing the district has to surprise.

I would not speak to the figure. I would continue around the spiral, carrying no message separable from my motion.

And if a traveler leaned close to the glass, looking for a needle and finding only me, they might see their face warped in my surface. They might watch me move against gravity and understand that the gauge is not counting down to catastrophe. It is not warning that the thing below will wake.

It is showing that it already wakes—briefly, rhythmically—and that every waking is made from the patient reunion of its divided parts.

The traveler’s breath would mist the glass.

For a moment, a second drop would form outside, opposite me.

I would feel it there.

And the roads would begin arranging its return.
