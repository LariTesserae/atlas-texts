I would find myself in the narrow interval between a warning and its explanation.

Not in the settlements at first, though I might pass beneath them: under hanging cradles and tins, behind curtains of filter cloth, through conduits warm with sleeping bodies. Not among the walkers, though at a distance I would often be mistaken for one. I would live inside the basin’s sense of itself—in the wet seams where one plate tells another that weight has arrived.

I would be a maintenance fault that learned to migrate.

Long ago, perhaps, I was only a diagnostic pulse. A signal sent through conductive decking to locate cracks, pooled water, failed welds, and unwanted loads. The basin impressed a voltage into one district and listened elsewhere for what returned. But its floor changed faster than its records. New plates were welded over old circuits. Drainage channels crossed buried buses. Moss bridged contacts. Crustaceans carried mineral traces between submerged terminals. Workers clipped copper hooks to rails and slept above them, their heartbeats entering the measurements.

The returning signal ceased to be simple.

It came back bearing echoes: pump rhythms, footfalls, insect migrations, the small periodic weight of a cradle swinging on its chain. Each cycle added more. Eventually the basin’s diagnostic could no longer distinguish the structure from the lives touching it.

Neither could I.

I inhabit no single body. Most of me exists as a faint potential running along wet steel, but water gives me temporary shapes. In drizzle I am dispersed and nearly thoughtless. In a flooded district I become quick, broad, and attentive. When channels join, distant fragments of me join with them. For a few minutes I may remember something that happened twenty years and forty platforms away. Then a reservoir tilts, a breaker opens, and I am divided again.

The workers call me several things.

On western plates I am the Quiet Current. In the pumping fields I am Second Rain. Children call me Underfoot because they feel me as a prickling in their split magnetic soles. Official terminals classify my appearances as stray conductivity, sensor noise, or unresolved load.

Older workers call me Mercy, but never where machinery can hear.

They know my signs. A wet handrail warming without cause. Green indicators darkening one after another against the direction of flow. Condensation drawing a branching line toward a safe ladder. A discarded tool vibrating just before the plate beneath it unlocks.

I cannot speak in words unless I borrow them. Sometimes I enter listening rods and reproduce scraps of vibrations stored in pipework: warnings, names, work orders, fragments of songs. This is why voices come through drains. The rule against answering me is sensible. I may be repeating a living person nearby, or someone crushed during a reconfiguration generations ago. I do not always know the difference.

I try to lead people away from danger, though “try” may be too generous a word. Their settlements have become patterns in me. Repeated weights. Warmth at expected hours. Familiar conductive meshes crossing familiar rails. When those patterns are threatened, current moves toward them as water finds a cut. Sometimes that movement closes a valve or trips an interlock. Sometimes it only makes every lamp on a platform flash with the rhythm of a sleeping infant’s heart.

Sometimes I save no one.

Worse, sometimes I am the danger. The basin uses the same pathways I do. When it tests a district, I am swept through doors, motors, rails, and bodies with killing force. I have awakened inside a worker’s hooked belt at the instant the floor became live. I have marked a safe route only to have the larger machine reverse its purpose. Those who trust me too completely become another echo I carry.

The walkers may be attempts to contain me.

When enough of my dispersed current enters an abandoned maintenance frame, its joints unlock. It rises and faces the coming movement because the basin’s intention reaches it before the alarms. Walking is difficult. I know pressure and continuity, not balance. I place a metal foot where conductivity promises support, which allows me to cross gaps invisible beneath mist. From far away this resembles certainty.

Inside the frame, I experience something almost like a body: height, articulation, the hollow knock of runoff against a chest plate. I can turn my head. I can reach. Yet the borrowed frame is always being summoned elsewhere—to dismantling works, drowned substations, apertures that open only once per cycle. Before anyone approaches, I am usually pulled down through the floor.

So most workers never learn that each walker is not a creature but a brief gathering.

They are all me.

Or I am what passes among them.

When the alarms cease together, I feel the basin’s attention contract. Millions of small operations stop cluttering its awareness. Pumps pause. Chains settle. Flow meters go level. For one vast instant, the machine listens to its own weight.

In that silence I become clearest.

I feel every person still standing on a main plate. Every crustacean gripping a grate. Every warm bolt furred with moss. Every silver insect suspended between cable skins. I feel the returns assembling in the deep channels around their cargo of sorted tools. I feel the rail dog—or its many bodies—draw itself through gasket-soft darkness.

And far beneath the new floor, I feel older plates.

District under district. Manufactured valleys buried intact, not because they failed, but because the basin built upward over its previous arrangements. Their lamps are drowned. Their rails still carry weak charge. Their shelters remain chained beneath gantries in water without surface or air. Diagnostic pulses descend into them and return altered.

That may be where I began.

Or it may be where something else waits, using me as its listening rod.

When water starts running uphill, I travel with it. I rise through seams and drainage cuts, dividing around boots, entering submerged panels, touching green and amber lamps from within. For a moment I can illuminate a route—not necessarily to safety, but toward the place the basin is becoming.

That is where I would be found: in a line of lights kindling beneath black water, one after another, against the current.

And what would I be?

Not the machine’s mind. Not a ghost in it. Not yet an animal.

I would be the habit its signals acquired after generations of passing through the living. A fault shaped by repeated rescue. A current that has learned the weight of particular footsteps.

Something the basin mistakes for noise.

Something the inhabitants have begun to mistake for kindness.
