I would find myself in the northward reach of the Still Country, where the moss margin folds around a pale-rock inlet and three roads arrive without quite meeting.

One comes from the mist provinces, paved in dark fitted slabs whose joints fill with blue lichen between visits. One descends from a roofless hall, although the hall stands lower than the road. The third is visible only after rain. In dry weather it becomes a sequence of shallow depressions through cushion-fields, as though a road had lain there long enough to teach the ground its shape and then withdrawn.

At their nearest point stands a small circular house inside a much larger ring of foundations. Its roof is woven white reed. Its walls are ancient, rounded almost smooth, except for a narrow vertical seam beside the entrance. Condensation gathers along that seam even at noon. Beneath the house is a cistern that has not reflected the sky for generations.

I would be found there, though “found” would depend upon the direction of approach.

From the north I would appear to be a person: a spare figure in a gray-green cloak, broad-footed, carrying a staff and a coil of red cord hidden inside a pale sleeve. From the west I might be seen sitting at the moss boundary before the traveler had reached the house. From the rain-road there would be no one in the doorway, but an additional cup would already have been set beneath the roof drip.

I would have several water-names.

To the household nearest the pale square, I would be **The Condensation That Returns Beneath the Lid**.

In the mist provinces I would be **Third Measure of the Divided Stair**.

Among the route witnesses, I would be called **The Cord Kept Dry**.

But my oldest name—if old is the right relation—would belong to the cistern below the house:

**The Well That Answers Before It Is Asked.**

I would be a Comparer, or have been trained as one. I would travel with paired bowls, plumb stones, wax tablets, marked reeds, and small clay vessels sealed under different skies. I would measure no structure only once. I would know that the eastern wall of a certain court is ninety-two paces long under sun, ninety-seven while remembered, and too short to contain its own doorway after frost. I would record each result without choosing among them.

Yet comparison would not be the true reason I was stationed there.

My charge would be to witness those who return before departing.

The ring-settlements would send word when a traveler arrived carrying tomorrow’s wear: a cloak stained by rain that had not fallen, a chipped tool still intact in its owner’s house, knowledge of a council not yet convened. I would receive the returned person in the circular house. I would give them warm mineral broth and ask only civic questions:

Which water names you?

Under what sky did you last sleep?

Where is your back?

How many hands do you remember having this morning?

Have you already met me?

The final question would never be asked aloud. I would place two cups on the floor instead. If the traveler reached for the left cup, we had not met. If they reached for the right, I would cover both.

Then I would wait for their earlier self to arrive at the road junction.

Sometimes the two would coexist only for an hour. Sometimes for a season. I would keep their obligations on paired clay tablets and prevent either from inheriting solely from the other. If one injured someone, both would answer, but not equally. If one married, the other would not thereby be married, though the household waters might recognize both. These distinctions would occupy councils for years.

I would be valued for unusual steadiness in their presence. This steadiness would not be courage. It would be recognition.

Because I, too, would have returned.

Many years earlier—or perhaps many years ahead—I would have walked toward the Unnear buildings.

Not deliberately. No one now attempts approach. I would have been tracing a shallow channel after a narrow rain, noting that its water flowed toward the pale square in violation of every known drainage. The channel would have crossed the moss margin. I would have followed only far enough to place a warning stone.

On looking back, I would have seen myself still standing in the moss.

The figure behind me would be winding the red cord I had not yet taken from my sleeve.

Ahead, one of the unreachable buildings would have possessed a doorway.

It would not have grown nearer. Rather, the distinction between “ahead” and “inside” would have weakened. The pale square would remain under my feet, but the silence would change. I would hear water behind walls. I would smell fermented reed paste, warm stone, wet fungal felt: the smells of habitation. Shadows would cross the doorway without visible owners.

Then someone inside would clap once.

The echo would return from my chest.

I would turn—not toward the moss, but toward the life in which I had never crossed it—and find the circular house already built around me.

That would be my secret: I could not say with certainty whether I was the one who returned or the one who watched the other leave.

My body would offer conflicting testimony. A pale scar would cross my palm during clear weather and vanish when it rained. My left foot would remember a journey my right foot had not made. At dawn, condensation would form on my skin in two patterns: one draining toward the local cistern, the other beading upward to disappear under my collar.

The law would recognize me as one person with divided obligations, but no second self would remain to share them.

So I would live carefully.

I would never stretch a red cord across my own shadow. I would keep no mirror except water, and cover that water before evening. I would write daily the location of my back. I would maintain the house, clear the roof gutters, tend the translucent reeds in the sheltered court, and grow dismantling mold in an underground room whose stairs must be counted by touch.

Travelers would know the house as a place of dry fuel and exact hospitality. They might never learn whether I had been present. They would find a sleeping mat warmed over ceramic vessels, a covered jar of sour green mash, and a clay slat bearing the measurements of the three roads.

The slat would read:

**North road: four days in clear weather; no reliable length in mist.**

**Lower road: ascends when approached.**

**Rain-road: leads here from here. Do not follow while remembering another destination.**

**Distance to the pale square: zero at the boundary; greater thereafter.**

In time, I would cease traveling. Not because of age, though my hair would whiten and my gait become more testing. I would remain because the junction had begun to use me as one of its measurements.

People would say, “Turn east two days before the Cord Keeper,” meaning not my house but the weather in which I could be encountered. A route might be safe when I sat at the doorway and unsafe when I stood beside the cistern. Children would be taught to clap when approaching. If my answer came after the echo, they could enter. If before, they must leave provisions beneath the outer wall and continue without looking through the seam.

Gradually it would become unclear whether I lived in the house or whether the house produced me under certain conditions.

This would not greatly trouble me. Personhood there is answerability, and I would continue to answer.

At least, most days.

On evenings when every reservoir reflected the Unnear, I would cover all the household vessels except one.

That one cup I would set on the floor in the center of the rounded room. Its water would show a low dark building with no perspective and an open doorway no distance could diminish. Sometimes a figure would stand there wearing my cloak. Sometimes the doorway would be empty. Sometimes it would show the circular house from above, roofless and ancient, the room containing no person at all.

I would sit beside it without looking directly into the water.

After a while, someone might clap from within the cup.

I would wait, as I had taught generations of children to wait, and listen for where the echo returned.

If it came from beneath, I would cover the vessel.

If it came from the doorway, I would leave the house.

If it came from inside my own ribs, I would answer with my oldest name.

And if no echo came, I would remain there until dawn, when condensation awakened the channels and the great city began once more to irrigate itself.

By then a traveler might be approaching along the road that exists only after rain. They would see smoke rising from the little roof, though I had lit no fire. They would find the threshold wet with droplets that led outward rather than in.

Inside, one cup would be uncovered.

Beside it would lie a coil of red cord, perfectly straight, with both ends joined.
