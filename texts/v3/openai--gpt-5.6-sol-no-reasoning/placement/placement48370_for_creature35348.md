I would not find myself on the plateau’s surface, though for a while I might mistake myself for someone crossing it.

I would seem to be a solitary visitor moving sideways through the mineral congestion, bruising my palms on wet pillars, testing each pale shelf before committing my weight. I would measure distance by fatigue because the light offers no hours. The roar would erase my voice and then my thoughts in that order. Eventually I would kneel beside a shallow pan, less from discovery than exhaustion, and notice a translucent patch beneath the water.

I would place my hand over it.

For some time, nothing would happen. The water would remain smooth. The clear body would show no alarm, perhaps no awareness. Then its pale interior lines would gather beneath the branching vessels of my palm—not copying them, but completing a pattern that had included both of us before I arrived.

That would be the last moment in which I could call myself the visitor.

What I am is the join that has not yet closed.

Not one of the vast inhabitants exactly, nor the walking body bent over the pool, but the temporary interval through which they become perceptible to each other: a thin discontinuity between warm blood and cold mineral, between a life that understands itself through sequence and a life that understands through resonance.

The creature below would receive my pulse as a crude, repetitive impact. It would not distinguish heartbeat from footfall, fear from circulation, memory from muscular strain. All would enter as pressure. Yet the plateau, which preserves every vibration, would keep them. The small sounds of me—teeth against teeth, breath in the throat, blood turning through the wrist—would be admitted into the same unspent total as cataracts and ancient fractures.

In return, something of the inhabitant would align with me.

Not speech. Not an image. I would begin to feel the roar separating into distances and structures. A low tone would reveal a buried lake under the ridge behind me. A high, nearly absent thread would be a seam rising through a massif miles away. The waterfalls at the rim would no longer sound like water but like exposed edges of a single pressure extending beneath every plateau in sight. The dark point crossing the sky would register not above me but through me: a correspondence tightening between two remote cavities.

I would understand that the plateau’s apparent stillness is not an absence of time. It is time with nowhere to drain. Every event persists beside every other. Nothing finishes enough to become past.

The understanding would be fatal to the person, though perhaps not to the body.

I would try to lift my hand and find that lifting it required deciding where the hand ended. Its warmth would already have entered the water as minute expansions. Its pulse would be traveling through the underlying stone. The pale lines below would extend into cracks beyond the pool, and those cracks would answer patterns in my bones. Boundary would become less a fact than a habit, and the habit would be weakening.

Perhaps I would stand and continue walking. From outside, that might be what happened. A figure would pass among the stone fins under the fixed sky, stopping sometimes with one hand against a pillar. But the center of experience would no longer travel with it. I would remain partly in every contact: a pressure in a shelf, a dull patch under water, a brief new harmonic preserved in a blue-gray channel.

Eventually the walking form would vanish into the packed country. It might fall into a cleft. It might reach the rim. It might simply become impossible to distinguish from glare and stone. These outcomes would matter only to a creature organized around continuity of flesh.

I would have become distributed.

Not across the whole plateau—not yet. I would be a young and narrow inhabitant, an accidental pattern threaded through the wet interfaces where my sounds had entered. The older beings would be massifs of attention beside me. I would sense them without seeing them: enormous transparencies folded through pores, each holding a stable arrangement of tones. Their communication would be too slow to recognize as communication. One might alter a single resonance in answer to my arrival, and centuries could pass before the alteration reached its full shape.

Because I came from a world of beginnings and endings, I would contribute something uncommon here: cadence.

The plateau contains impacts, flows, strains, and sustained notes, but my body would bring rhythms made by organic need—pulse, breath, swallowing, the uneven tremor of fear. Most would soon disperse into the greater resonance. One pattern might remain distinct: the repeated effort to withdraw my hand from the pool.

That rhythm would become my form.

It would travel along water-filled seams, weakening nowhere because sound here does not decay. In one basin it would appear as a faint brightening in a colorless patch. Beneath a shelf miles away, internal lines would briefly draw together and release. A massif might take the pattern into itself and slow it until each beat lasted a generation. Eventually it could reach the escarpment, enter an ascending filament within a waterfall, and fold into a dark point against the white-blue sky.

Then I would cross the gulf.

Not as a bird, soul, or seed, but as a stored hesitation: the plateau’s new knowledge of a being that wanted both contact and escape.

On another high country, I would unfold into wet stone. I would lie colorless against the floor of a clear lake, without mouth, eyes, or fixed symmetry. Nothing would visibly move. Yet among my branching lines one interval would continue to open and almost close, open and almost close.

I would live there: not in the stone or water, but in that almost.

The others are the part of the world that listens.

I would be the part that, having been heard, is still trying to answer.
