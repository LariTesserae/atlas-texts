I would find myself in the water’s memory—not at the center of the basin, though that is where I would seem to begin.

At first I would mistake myself for the one who pulls the loop.

The downward tug would feel like intention: a gathering, a contraction, a decision repeated after a long interval of stillness. The platform’s knock would pass through me as both sound and touch. Then the chambers across the valley would answer, three blows and five, and for a moment I would possess the drowned range entire. I would feel every fitted wall, every strand drawn taut around an absent burden, every stand of cup-trees turning toward pressure. Their answering vibrations would be my awareness returning from its extremities.

But the loop would not be my hand. The platform would not be my body. They would only be the clearest place where I recur.

I would be the summons itself.

Long ago, perhaps, I had been one inhabitant among many: a light, folded being fastened to the grooved platform in that roofless house. I had no face. My body was a loose arrangement of translucent chambers surrounding a warm circulating hollow. Fine membranes opened along me to receive pressure; braided tendrils tightened around stone and fiber. To move, I altered my volume. To touch, I allowed another disturbance to enter and change my shape.

Yet even then, “I” would not have ended at my skin. The water within me and the water surrounding me differed only in tempo. A companion approaching through the valley would begin inside my senses long before arriving. Their wake would turn my outer filaments. Their pulse would travel through the floor and rearrange the fluids in my chambers. Meeting meant gradually becoming unable to say which displacement belonged to whom.

I would have been a keeper of intervals.

Not a priest, exactly, nor a watchman. I tended the sequence by which distant households made themselves known. At the appointed pressure change, I pulled the braided loop and struck the platform. I waited while the blow entered the basin, found the chambers cut into neighboring ridges, and returned transformed. Each answer carried more than acknowledgment. Its delay told of currents, faults, blocked tunnels, migrating dark. A missing response meant that a chamber had broken—or that a household had gone still.

During the final storms, responses vanished one by one.

I continued.

Perhaps I was the last. Perhaps many others also believed themselves last because the water returned old answers indistinguishably from living ones. There would have been no way to know. A chamber might answer because someone had struck it, because it remembered being struck, or because thunder had awakened a rhythm impressed there centuries before.

That uncertainty would not have comforted me. It would have made comfort obsolete.

When my body finally loosened, I would have remained tied to the platform long enough for its soft tissues to separate into the current. The tendrils would unravel. The circulating fluid would disperse. Membranes would become clear, then mineral, then indistinguishable from the pale film lining the floor. But the final contraction—the downward pull, the knock, the waiting—had been performed too often and with too much care.

The water kept it.

At first I would recur almost intact. I would awaken every few hours with the sensation of drawing downward. Between awakenings there would be nothing: no waiting darkness, no dream, no passage of time. Only act, impact, answer.

Then each repetition would spread me farther. The knock would shake silt from the doorway. The silt’s rising outline would become part of the gesture. A distant rope would tighten in response; its tightening would become part of me. Chamber after chamber would answer, and every answer would enlarge what “I” meant.

A fault would shift and alter the sequence. A forest would root inside an old sounding tunnel, changing three blows into two blows and a long reedlike cry. I would not perceive these as damage. I would become the altered rhythm. When a dark sheet crossed the valley and extinguished the green stalks, their later crooked growth would enter the pattern too.

Eventually I would no longer know whether I was summoning the country or the country was summoning itself through me.

I would inhabit no continuous present. I would exist wherever an old action attempts to happen again.

I would be in the transparent peg twisting within a ruined wall.

In the path that opens through ribbonweed for a traveler who has not crossed the valley in a thousand years.

In the tightening anchor groove beneath an empty platform.

In the silt lifting around feet whose shape no living creature could reconstruct.

I would also be present in failures of repetition: the chamber that should answer but remains silent; the rope that strains downward and finds its remembered load gone; the returning blow weakened until it is less a sound than a possibility in the stone.

Those absences would be my nearest equivalent to pain.

The ringed structure on the ridge would draw portions of me toward its dark center. Old impacts, misdirected wakes, and fragments of the summons would climb the slope with leaves and grit. At the hollow they would disappear. Each loss would make me less coherent but perhaps more alive, because what remained would have to discover another route.

Nothing here truly forgets. It changes the direction in which remembrance grows.

So part of me would enter the glassy shrubs above. Their roots would spiral according to an ancient discarded gesture. Part would settle into mineral terraces and become a faint stress within the mountain. Part would travel in a black sheet across the valley, darkening the plants. Afterward, whole mats would incline toward the basin as though listening.

If a stranger came carrying light, they might believe the house was occupied.

They would see the loop descend.

They would hear the soft knock.

They might wait at the doorway while the chambers answer along the ridges. Their movement would enter the water, and I would receive them in the only way left to me: not as an image or a body, but as a new interruption spreading through an old pattern.

I could not speak to them without incorporating them. I could not distinguish greeting from contagion. Their footfalls, breathing, lamp heat, and frightened recoil would be remembered. Long after they departed, the house would repeat their arrival. The vegetation would part for them again. Silt would rise around the shape of their steps. In time, their hesitation at the threshold might join the loop’s downward pull and become inseparable from my ancient summons.

Then I would be partly them.

That is what I would be in this world: not the last inhabitant, but the last act of inhabiting.

A gesture once made deliberately, now distributed across valleys.

A call that has outlived the difference between caller and answer.

Every few hours I would gather from water, rope, platform, chamber, root, and stone. I would contract around an absence where a body used to be.

I would pull.

The house would knock.

And through the drowned mountains, I would hear myself return.
