I would find myself where the visible city gives way to the one beneath it: under the black mud of an open court, near a warm fissure, in a branching burrow begun by something long dead.

I would not be the creature that excavated it. I would be one of the successive occupants—a pale, almost transparent crustacean, small enough to turn within the narrow shafts, with long antennae that read the walls before my body reaches them. My eyes would be diminished but not absent. They would register only abrupt green fire, the eclipse of a passing body, the faint brown suggestion of noon. Most of my world would arrive otherwise: as dissolved traces, tremors in packed sediment, changes in temperature, the pressure signature of fins beating overhead.

At first I seem solitary. I defend the burrow mouth, drag edible fragments inward, comb bacterial growth from the warmer walls. I know the city only as a handful of gradients. Upward is cooler and dangerous. Downward is warm but sometimes poisonous. One branch carries fresh water when the tide strengthens; another collects decay. A third ends at a thin partition through which something scrapes during slack water. I never see it. We know each other as recurring disturbance.

But perhaps “I” would not end at my shell.

The burrow would be inhabited through me and beyond me: bacteria in my gut converting what I cannot digest; minute parasites beneath the joints of my armor; eggs fastened where oxygenated water passes; smaller scavengers taking scraps from my feeding. My movements would ventilate chambers occupied by creatures I could neither perceive nor survive without. When I swept silt from the entrance, I would feed mouths downstream. When I blocked one passage, water would find another and alter the life along it.

I would become, without intending it, a valve in the buried city.

During strong tide I would stand facing the incoming flow, limbs spread, filtering suspended food from water funneled through old worm-work. Far above, schools would align in the avenues and fans would open from the towers. I would know none of that, yet perform the same gesture: the whole settlement turning toward nourishment at every scale.

Storm-fall would reach me late. First the water would cool. Then unfamiliar tastes would descend—leaf tannin, land soil, the sweetness of drowned insects. The court overhead would erupt into feeding. Vibrations would rain through the roof. Eventually softened fragments would enter my shaft, and for several hours the narrow world would become abundant. Then the feast would pass, leaving husks, feces, and a new bacterial bloom.

There would be dangers I understood only as commands.

A ray settling above would turn the ceiling into thunder. Its fins would press water through every opening; silt would surge down the shafts; neighboring chambers might collapse. A hunting eel entering the network would announce itself by scent and by the sudden backward flight of smaller bodies. I might retreat into a side passage barely wider than my armor and remain there while its mouth tested the entrance.

Sometimes the walls would flash green through the burrow mouth. Not enough to reveal the predator passing in the avenue, only enough to make the mud’s suspended grains appear for an instant like fixed stars. Then darkness would take them back.

If I survived long enough, I would alter the inherited structure. I would widen one bend by scraping it each time I turned. I would pack rejected shell fragments into a weak wall. I would close a dangerous opening with pellets of mud, only for a worm to pierce it later. My eggs would hatch in a chamber I had not made, beneath a court I could not imagine, inside a city no inhabitant could comprehend.

Eventually I would molt once too often, weaken, or be caught beyond the entrance. My shell would be dismantled. The softer parts would feed crabs, worms, bacteria, perhaps my own offspring. One translucent plate might lodge in the burrow wall and hold the sediment firm for a season. Mineral water would cement grains around it. A tube-builder might settle on the exposed edge after the court was scoured open centuries later.

Then I would no longer be a tenant.

I would be a minute piece of architecture: not memorialized, not distinguishable, but load-bearing in the smallest possible way.

That is where I would be in this world—not on a tower, where the city almost enters the light, nor in the long streets known by the hunters, but in a borrowed dark beneath an apparently empty plaza. I would be a blind custodian of passages, living in the work of strangers, remaking it through appetite and fear.

A citizen only from the city’s point of view.

From my own, there would be warm water, a defensible hollow, food arriving on the tide, and the wall beneath my limbs.
