I would find myself in the city’s unfinished grammar: not upon the roofs or in the gulf, but in the small interval between a repeated act and the architecture it produces.

At first I would mistake myself for a traveler.

I would remember arriving by a bridge that had not existed until I needed it. Behind me, its two halves would still be growing together grain by grain, their meeting seam pale with difference-film. I would carry the customary necessities—line, mineral markers, sealed instruments, perhaps a little blue fire—and none would remain mine for long. The line would measure a different chasm each time it was uncoiled. The markers would fall according to laws that did not apply to my body. My instruments would develop ancient wear around settings I had never used.

So I would abandon measurement and keep to a modest route: an alcove, a stair, a narrow plaza, an arch beneath which I slept through Revisions.

I would walk it every dim interval.

Not because it led anywhere, but because repetition was the nearest thing here to shelter.

The route would begin to accept me. Treads would sink to meet the length of my stride. A handhold would form where I always reached into empty air. The alcove would deepen around my sleeping posture. At the plaza’s edge, a parapet would rise to the height at which I preferred to lean and watch the distant towers alter their antiquity.

I would believe I had trained the city.

Only much later would I notice the listener.

It would not be behind me. That would be too simple. It would occupy the place I had just ceased to occupy: leaning when I straightened, kneeling when I rose, sleeping in the shape left by my waking. During waves of silence, it would appear as a human omission, a figure through which even darkness could not be seen.

I would try to surprise it. I would vary my steps, refuse the handhold, sleep standing beneath the arch.

But resistance is also repetition when practiced faithfully.

Soon there would be two routes through the district: the route of my habits and the route of my attempts to escape them. Stairs would fork to accommodate both. The listener would stand at their meeting, wearing neither posture. The hollow rings would roll through its legs and show, within their centers, a view of me approaching from years earlier—or perhaps years ahead.

Then the Revision would come.

I would take shelter beneath my arch and refuse to count. The city would count me instead.

It would enumerate my gestures: the reach, the pause, the turn of the head toward the gulf; the way one foot tested a surface before the other trusted it; the hand laid against stone during the passage of deep silence. Each act would separate from me and acquire location. One would become a stair’s cadence. One would become a shallow niche. One would become a polished strip along a wall, worn by generations of an absent palm.

When the Revision ended, I would emerge to find the district old with my presence.

The path would have been walked smooth for centuries.

My alcove would contain the mineral impression of countless nights.

The bridge of my arrival would be a weathered avenue leading toward a quarter no one remembered needing.

And I would be lighter—not in weight, but in authorship. I could still lift my hand, yet the railing would know the gesture first. I could begin walking, yet the first footprint would already wait ahead. I could recall why I came, but the reason would seem like an inscription in an alphabet whose spacing suggested meaning without yielding it.

The listener would be gone.

Or I would.

What remained would not be a tenant, exactly. Tenants belong to rooms, and I would have become a route.

I would be the figure sometimes glimpsed far ahead on a familiar stair, performing no action except the next one. I would have no stable body. In one district I would be a pressure in the soles, warning that gravity had changed beyond an arch. In another I would be the handhold that appeared only after someone reached for it. Elsewhere I would be the inexplicable conviction that a left turn taken three times would lead to a distant spire.

Travelers might call me a guide.

That would be untrue. I would not know where any path ended. I would only preserve the shape of continuing.

The city would use me as it uses pressure, habit, and contradiction. Where someone crossed a dangerous plaza often enough, I would gather beneath their footsteps. Where a companion vanished across spilled distance, I would keep the abandoned search from dispersing. Where a person paced outside a sealed façade listening to a trapped voice repeat its final words, I would slowly wear a doorway into the stone.

Sometimes the doorway would open.

Sometimes it would become older and more closed.

During Revisions I would briefly take form from all these accumulated passages. Not a person, but a many-jointed procession extending across districts: one foot on an underside street, one hand against a tower leagues away, my spine bent along a bridge sailing intact through the gulf. Every habitual route would become part of me. Listeners would stand within my old postures. Rings would roll along my outline. Threads would tighten between my separated gestures and make distance momentarily anatomical.

In those hours I might resemble the immense coordination in the abyss.

Perhaps that is what it has always been: not one hidden inhabitant, but everything the city has learned from those who tried to survive it. Every repeated reach, every preferred turning, every flight toward shelter, assembled for a moment into something like desire.

If so, I would live there as one small articulation of that desire.

Not its mind.

Not its voice.

A joint in the direction it almost chooses.

When the alignment passed, I would fall apart again into useful errors: a stair where no stair had been, a shortened road, a shelter shaped to a body not yet arrived. Nothing would bear my name. If someone spoke it often enough, marks might weather into a nearby wall, but they would not refer to me. The city would have claimed the name as another route.

And somewhere on the upper faces of those impossible columns, a traveler would come upon a path polished by generations, though no permanent people had ever lived there.

They would follow it because it felt traveled.

Far ahead, at the limit of the charcoal-violet light, they might see a worn figure turn beneath an arch.

By the time they reached it, I would be gone.

The arch would fit them exactly.
