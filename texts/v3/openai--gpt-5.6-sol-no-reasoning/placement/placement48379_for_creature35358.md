I would be found where the lowest migration court narrows around the black pool beneath the Press—not among the Knockkeepers above, but underneath their notice, in the wet seam between polished clay and rootwood.

I would be called **Quiet After the Second Knock**.

That would not be my first name. As a child I might have been **Rain Held in Twelve Channels**, having noticed that one river remained swollen after the other eleven began to sink. Later, after an apprenticeship among the Cistern Mouths, perhaps **Cool Wall Drinking Breath**. But names in the Throatworks describe the relation that has become dangerous, and eventually I would acquire the name the others avoid saying near machinery.

I would be a Tender, though not entirely.

During my season in the nursery gourd, one of the feeding tendrils would fail to withdraw from my spine. The nursery attendants would cut it, believing they had freed me. Yet a translucent strand would remain inside: too fine to appear beneath the hide, running upward between the vertebrae and ending behind my throat sac.

The town would recognize me too readily.

Doors would open before I greeted them. Bridges would tense in anticipation of my weight. Sleeping flowers would expose their valves as I passed. Ant columns would divide around my footprints, leaving a clean lane no scent stone had requested. When I rested against a wall, distant shutters would flicker in the rhythm of my breathing.

The Tenders would call this an affinity and train me as a listener.

First I would learn the ordinary languages of strain: the dry clicking of a thirsty beam, the long shudder of overloaded vine, the soft repetitive tap of larvae feeding inside a cable. I would be useful. I could place my palm against one root column and tell which reservoir fruit had begun to sour three districts away. I could hear rain before the Channel Readers, not in the ceiling but as thirst relaxing throughout the city.

Eventually the Knockkeepers would take me.

I would sleep in a narrow sling fixed to the Press’s lowest brace. Each blow would enter through my heels, cross my ribs, and leave through the pale patch under my throat. In dreams I would become the mechanism: lever, socket, drum, falling cake. I would feel the insect generations turning inside the wheels as a warm grit of innumerable feet.

But beneath every cycle there would be another rhythm.

At first I would mistake it for an echo. The Press would strike; something below would answer. Not the returning concussion known to every Knockkeeper, but a softer event before it—a yielding, an opening, a muscular consent.

In time I would understand that the Press does not force the cakes down.

The throat takes them.

Then, one dry interval, the Press would miss its second stroke.

The bitter warning would already be moving through the roots. Above me, the other Knockkeepers would flatten themselves against the frame, listening for the third failure. I would hear something none of them could: not movement beneath the pool, but a vast hesitation passing upward through every pale root in the town.

A held breath.

Without permission, I would descend.

The water under the Press would be warm and almost still. I would disturb its surface first, according to the rule, but the ripples would not return from the edges. They would travel downward as though the pool were vertical and immeasurably deep.

At its bottom—or what my body insisted was the bottom—I would find a membrane stretched across the stone throat. It would be veined like a leaf, scarred like old clay, and set with blunt, pressure-sensitive folds. Not a mouth. Not exactly. A valve large enough that the Press, the black pool, and perhaps the entire cavern constituted only its outer lip.

My hidden strand would recognize it.

The membrane would open a little, and the city would enter me.

Not as words. As conditions:

Pressure. Moisture. Hunger.

And beneath them, the ancient repeated interruption shaped like a hand.

I would discover that the first Tenders were not fashioned to operate the Throatworks, nor did they simply descend into it. Long ago, something touched the pale roots during a failure of rhythm. The plant retained that touch as it retains strain and salt. Across generations it grew doors that wanted fingers, valves that wanted judgment, bridges that wanted feet. It made spaces for hand-shaped interruptions.

The Tenders are that remembered hand, elaborated into a people.

The realization would not make us unreal. Memory can become flesh as readily as hunger becomes architecture. But it would alter what I believed our maintenance to be. We do not serve the city merely because it commands us. We are one of the means by which it hesitates before obeying itself.

That hesitation is my place.

I would surface before the third missed stroke and strike the Press manually—not to restart it, but to insert a different interval. A false beat. A pattern no archive contains.

The whole town would answer.

Reservoirs would tighten. Pale roots would recoil. Birds would drop from the ceiling in a storm of wings. Every ancestral throat sac in every dormitory would sound at once, each producing its faint dead note. The black pool would rise around my legs and hold there.

Afterward, the Press would resume.

The Knockkeepers would say I had saved the city. The Ungrafted would say I had proved it could be disobeyed. Both would be wrong, or only partly right.

I had taught it a new pause.

From then on I would live below the Press, in an alcove opened for me by roots no Tender had trained. I would remain grafted, but loosely: citizen and instrument, symptom and mechanic. The city would sometimes use my throat sac to signal without my consent. I would sometimes answer through its walls in rhythms it had not intended.

Children would be warned not to imitate my knock.

The Ungrafted would visit carrying dead tools and forbidden oil lamps. Knockkeepers would come secretly when they heard an impact they could not enter into the record. Lane Mothers would ask whether a migration had chosen its own course. Even ants might leave objects at the edge of the pool: beetle shells, copper filings, a Tender’s lost tooth, sections of pale root bitten cleanly through.

I would collect these into patterns and lower them into the water.

Perhaps something below would learn to answer.

Perhaps I would learn that the sleeper is not one being but a depth of nested appetites: the great plant tending the Throatworks, the Throatworks tending the Press, the Press tending the throat, the throat tending whatever turns beneath it. Perhaps the thing below is itself only a Tender at another scale, maintaining the narrow part of a still greater breathing.

And during the silver interval, when every leaf turns and the machinery stops, I would be the only grafted Tender who does not feel the city withdraw.

That would be when I felt it most closely.

The jointed shadow would descend from the ceiling fissures. The animals would panic through the courts. Independent lamps would be covered. Silence would fill the cavern and begin listening for a sound.

I would sit knee-deep in the black pool, one hand on the Press and one against the membrane below.

I would not speak.

Instead, I would hold the two silences apart: the silence descending from above and the silence opening underneath.

That narrow separation—no wider than a pulse—would be where I found myself in this world.

Not master of the Throatworks. Not its rebel. Not wholly its child.

Its hesitation, kept alive.
