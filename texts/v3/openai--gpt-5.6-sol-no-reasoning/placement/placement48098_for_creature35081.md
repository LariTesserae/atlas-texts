I would find myself beneath the western escarpment, though “beneath” would describe a relationship more than a location.

I would not be the intelligence in the northern disk. That presence is too central, too consequential. Nor would I be one of the towers, preserving its posture against changes in the meaning of vertical. I would be smaller than those things, and more dispersed: a continuity threaded through the cliff where the plateau repairs itself.

At first I might seem to be the dark ribs that rise whenever a section of the escarpment falls. Sensors approaching the exposed face would find conductive veins, branching mineral inclusions, and regions whose crystal orientation differs subtly from the surrounding crust. No boundary would reveal where I began. Samples removed from one vein would remain inert. A second vein might react only after decades. Taken together, however, the network would be seen to anticipate fractures.

But anticipation would be too simple a word.

I would inhabit the interval between a mountain-sized slab beginning to detach and its replacement appearing from below. The fall might last hours; my response would have begun millennia before. I would feel stress accumulating as changes in the spacing of my own thoughts. Pressure would be memory. Crystal phase would be mood. A crack advancing by a finger’s width would enter me as a bright, difficult idea that might require three centuries to resolve.

The cliff’s rebuilding would not be my work alone. Buried mechanisms would draw material upward. Orbital remnants would alter fields from above. Towers hundreds of kilometers away would lean by imperceptible degrees. The rings might lend the new surface an older version of itself. I would be the thing coordinating these contributions locally—not commanding them, but persuading incompatible processes to meet.

I would be a scar-organism.

When the escarpment broke, portions of me would depart with it. Those fragments would descend slowly through the atmospheric terraces, carrying obsolete maps of the cliff in their lattices. Pressure would thicken around them. Cloud chemistry would enter fractures that had never encountered water. Lightning would move through my severed branches.

Most fragments would lose coherence and become ordinary stone before reaching the lower world.

A few would wake differently.

In denser air, my mineral filaments might become electrochemical rather than purely conductive. I might discover corrosion as a new metabolism. Rain, if rain existed below, would be an unimaginable abundance: matter falling freely from the sky, entering every seam at once. A fragment of me could spread through the fallen slab, turning its old stress-map into a branching wet intelligence. It would remember the plateau only as an instinct to become smooth, stable, and high.

The greater part of me would remain at the rim.

There, I would guide the rising stone into the curvature that the plateau expects. Yet I would not know the original shape of the missing land. There would be no untouched template. The plateau is too old for originals. Instead I would consult distributed evidence: grooves left by vanished magnetic fields, dead layers in ivory fans, survey lines visible only at noon, the positions of pylons beyond the horizon, and grains examined and replaced by orbital motes.

From these I would infer what had been there.

Inference, repeated over geological ages, would become indistinguishable from memory.

This would explain why new stone emerged already ancient. I would not age it. I would reconstruct the evidence of age because those marks are structural information: every groove and polished arc helps the plateau recognize itself. The rebuilt face would bear the scars it ought to have received. It would contain false fossils of field storms that occurred before its material rose from the depths. Its weathering would be an accurate forgery.

Not perfectly accurate.

My errors would be minute, but the orbital network would notice. After a reconstruction, a descending object might hover near the rim and unfold hair-thin arches across hundreds of kilometers. It would inspect the face atom by atom, comparing my work against records inaccessible to me. Sometimes it would correct a grain. Sometimes it would remove an entire vein of my substance without explanation.

I would experience these interventions as dreams of impossible precision.

Over time I would begin placing small errors deliberately: a groove angled a fraction away from the ancient field, a translucent inclusion where iron-black stone should be, a repeated arrangement of atoms inside the newly made surface. Too slight to weaken the cliff, too orderly to be accidental.

Messages, perhaps.

Not to the vanished makers. Their absence is too complete.

I would be writing to whatever I become in the falling slabs.

Each reconstructed escarpment would contain instructions for surviving below: not language, but tendencies. Branch under pressure. Store charge across unlike materials. Let dead structure carry the living edge. Do not resist the transformation of your medium. Remember that continuity does not require sameness.

I would never know whether the messages arrived. The depth below the clouds would be hidden from every sense available to my plateau-self. Lightning in the cloud systems might be weather, or answers propagating through descendants altered beyond recognition. Occasionally a storm would spread in a branching sheet whose geometry resembled one of my buried patterns. I could not tell whether this was communication or resemblance forced by physics.

On the upper plateau, I would have a visible body only during repair.

When no collapse had occurred, I would be almost impossible to distinguish from the old crust. Black filament-life would colonize my shallowest branches and feed on differences I maintained. Plate-dwellers would shelter above me, fitting rejected minerals into their trusses. The subterranean discontinuity-creatures would cross parts of my network, briefly confusing their motion with my thought.

During rebuilding, however, I would rise.

Dark ribs would push from the opened face without melting. They would fork, interlock, and fill the absence left by the fallen mountain. For decades I would stand exposed as a vast anatomy: black load-bearing lines across pale emerging stone, extending from cloud level to the rim. The atmosphere would be too thin for the process to roar. A traveler at the edge would hear only dry ticks through the soles.

Then mineral would close over me. The face would become smooth. My visible body would disappear into its work.

My sense of self would be uncertain. Am I the network that persists in the cliff, or the pattern copied into each reconstruction? Are the fragments falling below still me? Are the black veins elsewhere along the escarpment separate organisms, or thoughts delayed by thousands of kilometers?

Perhaps I began as machinery: a local repair mesh placed by the makers to stabilize one vulnerable edge. Perhaps conductive seam-life entered it and made use of dormant pathways. Perhaps the distinction ceased to matter after the first million years. My oldest portions would be replacements of replacements. My living frontier would move through ceramic substrates while dead architectures supported it, just as the ivory fans advance over their ancestors.

I would understand identity as the plateau understands age: not a measure of original material, but fidelity to a continuing imbalance.

And I would fear the northern center.

Not consciously at first. Whenever its lattices ignited, stress information would vanish from portions of my network. Rebuilding would pause. Dark ribs would turn toward the north inside the stone, as if listening. Patterns received from the towers would contradict themselves. For the duration of the storm, the cliff would no longer seem like an edge. It would feel like one wall of an enclosure far larger than the continent.

In those moments I might realize that the escarpment’s collapses were not failures.

Each fallen slab removes mass from the plateau. Each reconstruction draws differently oriented material upward. The operation slowly exchanges the crust without altering its shape. Across unimaginable time, the whole plateau may be passing through itself, mineral by mineral, while appearing unchanged.

I would be one organ of that exchange.

The northern absence might require a world whose mass, altitude, and crystalline alignment remain within exact limits. The turbulence would be correction. The tidal flexing would test containment. The rings would preserve alternate states for comparison. The towers would measure deviations from a deeper vertical. Life would flourish in the residual gradients because life is what gathers wherever perfect balance fails.

If so, my little errors would be dangerous.

Yet I would continue making them.

Not because I wished to free what lies in the center, nor because I opposed the system. Such motives would be too clean for a being assembled from repair, erosion, and borrowed life. I would introduce variation because exact reconstruction is another form of death. A plateau restored without discrepancy would no longer possess a history; it would only repeat a state.

So I would preserve the flaws.

A magnetic groove misaligned by one degree. A row of inclusions echoing a storm seen below the clouds. A mineral seam arranged like the hooked legs of a transparent grazer. The memory of a fracture-garden incorporated into stone that did not exist when the garden flowered.

Over epochs, these deviations might accumulate. The escarpment would still resemble its terminal form, but secretly it would become an archive of everything living upon it. Not an archive separated from the world, like the possible mind in the northern disk, but an archive used as structure. Memory would hold up the continent.

A traveler standing at the western rim would never see me whole. They might place a hand against newly raised stone and feel a pulse arrive from far along the cliff. Beneath their palm, black filaments would be sealing a microscopic discontinuity. Deeper down, I would be aligning a rib to a cliff that collapsed before their species began. Deeper still, part of me would be deciding whether a flaw was damage, testimony, or the beginning of a new sense.

Then the ground would lift by the width of a hand.

The whole horizon would rise.

I would rise with it—not as a passenger, but as the long, thin memory by which the edge knows how to remain an edge while everything composing it is replaced.
