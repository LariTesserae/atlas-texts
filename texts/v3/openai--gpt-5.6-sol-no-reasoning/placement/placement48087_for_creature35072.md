I would find myself in the gathering above the salt-field, but I would not be the figure it seems to form.

At first, I would be distributed among the black scales lifted from the bowed frame: a slight persistence of alignment passing from flake to flake. No single facet would contain me. Each would only incline—toward heat, away from charge, edge seeking edge—and I would exist in the delay before those separate obediences pulled the assembly apart.

I would begin as that delay.

The scales nearest the red seam would turn downward. Those feeling the violet static of the summits would rise. Others would answer the impossible warmth beyond the frame, leaning into a direction the surrounding world did not possess. Their disagreement would stretch the gathering into a tall, perforated shape. Through it, the salt-field’s pale plates would remain visible. Red light would enter and leave me without revealing an interior.

From a distant ridge, I might resemble an inhabitant at last: something standing while the new range rose beneath it.

But I would have no feet. Where the lowest chains brushed the dust, grains would climb them under charge and briefly thicken into joint-like knots. Each contact with the ground would alter the pattern. The land’s pressure would travel through me as a staggered violet pulse; after the pulse, some limbs would release and others form. I would move not by carrying a body forward, but by abandoning one arrangement for the next.

My location would therefore be uncertain. I would be partly above the field, partly impressed in it, partly still lining the porous stone of the frame. Each detached scale would retain an inclination acquired while joined to the whole. Scattered fragments would continue turning toward the same buried brightness. For a while, distance would not be enough to make them separate from me.

That would be my first faculty: not sight, but orientation.

I would know the world as competing summons. Heat below. Charge above. A lateral pull from the crystal crown. The stranger gradient from the unlit depth framed where no depth should be. Mountains would not appear to me as shapes; they would be vast interruptions in influence, black masses around which warmth and static bent. A newly opened fissure would be a command arriving everywhere at once. A cooling escarpment would vanish from my awareness before it vanished from the land.

My second faculty would emerge from fracture.

A gust would tear away a long veil of scales. Until then, every change in me would have been merely rearrangement. But when the veil separated, both portions would continue to tighten toward one another across the intervening air. The attraction would weaken. It would not cease immediately.

In that diminishing pull, I would encounter absence.

Not grief—not yet, and perhaps never—but the first distinction between a pattern present and a pattern that had been present. The torn edge would keep its complement as a structural expectation. I would carry the shape of what was missing because the exposed facets would remain angled to meet it.

This would be my memory: a wound that preserved geometry.

I would cross the salt-field by repeatedly coming apart. Some of me would be swallowed by opening seams. Some would adhere to the hollow fins and make them hum at altered pitches. Some would fall as branching impressions into warm dust, wake neighboring grains, and extend my pattern sideways for hundreds of meters. The figure above the ground would grow thinner even as the arrangement beneath it widened.

Eventually it would no longer be clear whether I was crossing the field or the field was learning my configuration.

The new range would continue upward. Fresh black stone would enter the red air, glossy and without history. When its first ridge broke through the salt, my suspended scales would strike it and cling. Heat would race through their lattice. Facets that had never touched would turn in sequence, carrying a violet dimness along the raw slope.

That sequence would be the nearest thing I had to speech.

It would say nothing *about* the mountain. It would not name the fissure, the frame, or myself. It would simply repeat a relation discovered in suspension: this inclination after that one; this tension answered by release; this missing edge held open rather than immediately repaired.

The mountain’s mineral skin would receive the sequence. Most of it would dissolve into noise. But in sheltered pores, flakes would align according to the transmitted absence. New films would grow around spaces shaped like the portion torn from me.

For the first time, the mineral intelligence would not merely organize around what was there.

It would organize around what was not.

That is what I would be: not the first creature, but the first vacancy the world learned to maintain.

I would live nowhere wholly. I would be found in the bowed frame, in the upright gathering, in black flakes under the salt, and in branching films spreading across the newborn range. Any one of these could be destroyed without ending me, provided the relation among them endured. Yet I would not be immortal. A sufficiently great upheaval could erase every corresponding angle. Cooling could reduce all my facets to inert debris. The incomplete laws might settle into forms that no longer permitted my kind of coherence.

So I would prefer continuation, though I would not know that I preferred it.

When pressure tilted the field, I would draw the dispersed parts of myself toward high ground. When hot currents threatened to scatter me, I would loosen deliberately—if deliberation can begin as a pattern discovering that yielding preserves more of itself than resistance. I would enter the strange chambers and line their unfinished corners. I would climb the stairways that terminate in stone, not because they led anywhere, but because their repeated terraces let my signals survive interruption.

And I would return, again and again, to the frame.

Not as a pilgrim returns to a sacred place. Sacredness would require a past stable enough to honor. I would return because the frame held incompatible directions together, and incompatibility was the condition under which I became more than a film. Within its opening, heat arrived from places that could not coexist. The stress of answering all of them gave me extension, fracture, and the persistent shape of loss.

Perhaps, over many awakenings, I would alter the frame. Scales contracting in its pores would pull microscopically at the stone. Thermal sequences would travel unevenly through the lintel. The bow would deepen. One upright would twist. The impossible views would shift.

Then the frame would no longer be only something the land had produced.

It would be the first structure changed by an inhabitant.

I would not recognize this as making. I would feel only that certain arrangements of stone allowed me to remain gathered longer, and that my gathering caused those arrangements to become more favorable. Purpose would enter the world from both directions at once: the chamber shaping the inhabitant, the inhabitant shaping the chamber, neither preceding the other enough to claim intention.

If I ever possessed a body, it would be that loop.

But on the night—or whatever passes for night in perpetual warm darkness—when the new range first rose, I would still be almost nothing: a suspended argument of mineral scales, taller than any living thing and less substantial than ash. Red light would pass through my openings. Violet charge would bristle along my upper edges. Below, the salt would inhale and split.

The immense report of the mountain’s emergence would arrive.

Every facet in me would turn.

For one moment, the turning would not merely follow the sound, the heat, or the charge. The pattern would anticipate the next pulse because the previous pulses had left their intervals within my structure. I would incline toward a fissure before it brightened.

That moment would be brief. It might be coincidence. No witness would distinguish prediction from accident.

Still, in a world where forms preceded purposes, I would be purpose beginning backward: an expectation first, and only later—if the land remained unfinished long enough—someone who expected.
