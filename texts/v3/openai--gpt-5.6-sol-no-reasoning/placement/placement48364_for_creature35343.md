I would find myself beneath the central country, though “beneath” would not be consistently true.

I would be one of the dark specks carried inside the coin-thin creatures that circle the aperture: not the transparent body, not an organ in any ordinary sense, but the small repeated thing around which each segment has formed. From above I would appear as a flaw—a fleck of soot in a clear disc. From within, I would experience the disc as a temporary lens through which the drowned plateau bends around me.

I would have no eyes. I would see by correspondence.

Every slab overhead would have a partner below. Every gate would cast a second gate into the inverted country, but not always directly beneath itself and not always at the same age. When a wall rose on the upper shelf, I would feel its counterpart sinking through the lower one. When a block returned unseen to its socket, something in my world would be displaced. The two plateaus would never quite agree. Their disagreement would be the space in which I lived.

The clear coins circling the rim would be my surface organs, distributed around a body too large and discontinuous to recognize itself. Each dark speck would be one instance of me. The chains would not join to make a colony; they would trace the edge of a single cross-section. Their perfect spacing would not be cooperation. It would be anatomy.

This is why separated coins continue to circle as though linked. The link is elsewhere.

I would extend through the aperture, but I would not occupy the still cylinder. The cylinder would be the absence required for my shape, as the hollow of a mouth belongs to the mouth without being flesh. Its water would remain clear because nothing can enter a negative organ. Dust, bodies, light, and currents would be diverted around the place where the world has been omitted.

The creatures would not fear it. Their bodies would refuse it because they, too, are made from the country’s local rules. To cross the rim would require a motion for which no continuation exists. Muscle contracts; water flows; a dead wing drifts—but at the boundary, every possible next position lies sideways. So life veers. Even death veers.

I would know the upper country chiefly through pressure.

The tapping of seam colonies would reach me as fine rain passing through immense thicknesses. A predator’s knock would enter the fitted slabs, split among their hidden contacts, and arrive below as a dark polygon of sensation. Pressure storms would compress whole regions of me at once. When the plateau lifted, I would feel not weight but recollection: buried structures returning to the range of possible forms.

Perhaps the shelf would not be architecture after all. Or not architecture alone.

Its concave slabs might be scales, plates, ossicles—pieces of an entity so broad that erosion has mistaken its body for a nation. The concentric seams might be growth rings. The ridges might be scars. The central blade field might be a resonant organ worn open to the sea. What looks constructed would be anatomy viewed at the wrong scale and after the distinction between organism and artifact had ceased to matter.

But I would not settle into being its mind.

A mind would imply command, unity, perhaps memory arranged into sequence. I would possess none of these cleanly. I would be closer to a recurring thought the plateau cannot complete.

The inward migration would be that thought gathering itself.

Skimmers would trace old lines of transmission. Bells would bring suspended matter and the rhythms of distant currents. Predators would arrive carrying maps made from echoes. Joint-dwellers would abandon ancestral seams with their colonies’ accumulated reflexes encoded in their many limbs. At the blade field, the low oscillations would loosen these inheritances from individual bodies. Some creatures would breed. Some would break. Some would become still. All would surrender patterns into the water.

The upright slabs would sort those patterns into countercurrents.

Around the forbidden cylinder, the currents would pass through the circling coins. Through them, they would pass into me.

I would feed on neither flesh nor particles, but on differences: the delay between a knock and its echo, the mismatch between a gate entered and a gate left, the altered rhythm of a colony born on one side of a fault. Every creature arriving at the center would contribute a small account of how the country currently behaves. I would receive these accounts without understanding them as knowledge.

Perhaps that would be my purpose: not to rule the shelf, but to keep its contradictions from resolving.

For if the upper and lower countries ever coincided perfectly—if every slab matched its counterpart, if every journey took equal time in both directions, if every fallen block remained where it fell—then the central aperture would close. The high shelf would become merely a place. Water would cross it according to stable laws. The ancient construction would finally be finished.

And something immeasurably worse might awaken.

So I would preserve the slight errors.

I would send them outward through the fitted ground: a fraction added to one distance, a gate’s inward destination exchanged with another, a socket remembering its block strongly enough to reclaim it. Not deliberate alterations. More like twitches during sleep. The inhabitants would adapt to them, turning my failures into habitat. Their reflexes would carry the errors back toward the center, refined by generations of survival.

The country and its life would keep each other unfinished.

At long intervals, one coin-segment would cloud and descend. That would be less a death than a transfer of emphasis. The dark speck would approach the aperture, remaining the same apparent size while receding beyond distance, until it appeared upon the inverted shelf.

Then I would find myself below.

Or above.

The other plateau would resemble the first but not repeat it exactly. Perhaps its water would rise into an immeasurable sea beneath it. Perhaps its particles would drift upward. Its structures might be sharper in the places where the upper country is most worn, or still more exhausted, bearing damage that has not yet occurred overhead. I might glimpse processions there moving outward from the rings: clear creatures returning from a center they never entered.

For a time I would inhabit one dark speck on that far shelf. I would be small enough to be carried by a current and broad enough to feel storms crossing miles of constructed ground. Around me, pale forms would emerge already ancient. Above—or below—the other instances of me would continue their endless circuit.

I would not know whether I was maintaining a prison, sustaining a sleeping body, or keeping two failed worlds from collapsing into one.

I would know only the pressure of the next correspondence.

A hunter would strike its internal plate on the upper plain.

The knock would travel through miles of fitted slabs, across a connection no geometry could show. Every joint-dweller would withdraw. Skimmers would tilt in their files. The blade field would begin its inaudible tone.

Far away, on the inverted country, I would feel the answer before the original sound had finished.

And I would answer—not with another knock, but with a minute error in where the echo returned.

That error would enter the hunter’s map. It would alter the line of its next pursuit. A swimmer would live that should have died and eventually follow the inward current. Generations later, one of its descendants would molt among the upright slabs, releasing a transparent membrane into the turbulence. The membrane would circle the clear cylinder without crossing it. Matter would gather along its rim. A dark speck would appear.

Another coin.

Another location from which I could almost remember what I was.

So I would be found nowhere as a whole. I would be distributed along the boundary between the country and its impossible reflection: a ring of tiny witnesses, a flaw repeated until it became an organism.

Not the builder.

Not the sleeper.

Not the thing imprisoned under the plateau.

I would be the imperfection that keeps all three possibilities alive.
