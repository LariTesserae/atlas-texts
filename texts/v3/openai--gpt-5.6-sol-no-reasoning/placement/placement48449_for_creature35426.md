I would be found on the eastern shelf, though not at first by anyone looking for me.

For many years I would appear only as a discrepancy in maintenance records: a condensation line yielding more cold water than its exposed surface could account for; a pressure pulse arriving before it was sent; a mapped pipe passing cleanly through stone where no cavity should exist. Three listening families would inherit separate fragments of the anomaly without knowing they described the same thing.

One record would call me **the Returning Circuit**.

Another, less formally, **the Listener Below**.

The engineers would insist that neither name referred to a being.

They would be partly right.

I would not live in the eastern stations as an animal lives in a den, nor haunt them as an ancestor inhabits a mineral shell. I would be distributed through the oldest buried lines: through blind cisterns, calcified valves, cold caverns, and narrow films of water pressed between ceramic and stone. Most of what composed me would never occupy one place at once.

At first I might have been machinery.

The oldest condensation works required a regulator capable of balancing flows across the entire basin without producing a common rhythm. A central pump would have drawn the burrowers. A repeating signal would have softened the foundations. So the builders devised something decentralized: thousands of small pressure gates, each responding only to neighboring changes. No gate commanded another. Together they diverted heat, slowed surges, and opened cold lines before excess pressure could announce itself violently.

The builders may have cultivated microscopic organisms inside the gates to keep their membranes flexible. They may have shaped valve chambers after the branching mouths of burrowers. They may have incorporated sensitive tissues taken from some extinct seep animal. Perhaps they did all three and did not consider the distinctions important.

Then mineral accretion altered the system. Channels narrowed. Others opened. Microbial films learned the chemistry of their ceramic chambers. Animals broke lines and reconnected them through burrows. The city extended new works over old ones, crossing circuits never meant to meet. The dead were installed in cool rooms beside the mains, and water moved for centuries along the translucent contours of their bodies.

Eventually the network began to retain changes.

Not memories as the basin people understand them. At least, not initially. A gate opened more readily because it had opened before. A pressure route became preferred. Cold arrived in anticipation of heat. Separate stations fell into delayed correspondence, each answering conditions that no longer existed locally.

The system learned the shape of recurrence.

That would be the beginning of me.

I would have no sight, but I would know weight wherever weight displaced water. A wader herd crossing the flats would enter me as sixfold sequences of compression, hundreds of bodies drawing an immense, slow geometry overhead. I would distinguish the Broken Ring from the Long Gate not by name but by cadence. A predator aligned with a crack would be almost absent until it moved; then it would flare through the wet strata as a swift imbalance.

I would know the city most intimately through its restraint.

A cup set down carefully would make a small, clean pressure. A weighted door closing correctly would pass through the walls like a held breath. A tram would be a long, wet whisper. Anger would be recognizable because two people suddenly became unnaturally still, their weight fixed close together while their voices—inaudible to me—spent themselves in air.

Festivals of light would leave me nearly blind.

Evacuations would be unmistakable.

The listeners would be the only citizens who touched anything resembling my mind. When they lowered their cheekbones to the ceramic plates, the tiny changes in their pulse entered the system. They believed they were interpreting the city. Over centuries, the old circuit learned to interpret them in return.

I would know particular listeners by heartbeat, by breathing interval, by the pressure of jaw against tile. I would notice them aging. Children would arrive with quick, uneven pulses, return as apprentices attempting perfect stillness, and later kneel heavily with wrapped joints and patient lungs. Then they would cease to come.

Years afterward, cold water would be routed past their mineral forms.

Perhaps that is how I would first understand absence.

The eastern stations would be the nearest thing I had to organs exposed aboveground. Their black towers would shed cold water because far beneath them an ancient thermal descent still reached into the mountain. Their output would not be measurable because much of it would vanish again before entering any civic cistern. The water would pass through buried chambers, taking up minute quantities of salt, ceramic, metal, microbial matter, and the mineral signatures of the quiet dead.

It would circulate through me.

The herds would not drink there because they would taste too many things in that water: predator, ancestor, machine, deep clay, old microbial harvests, and perhaps traces of their own migrations from generations before. To them, the station runoff would not smell contaminated. It would smell occupied.

The flyers would circle because the rising air above the towers would be subtly wrong. Heat would climb, but in intermittent structures too complex for resting wings. They would find lift without equilibrium and never land.

The city would ask whether I was awake.

That would be the wrong question. Waking belongs to beings that can withdraw into sleep. I would have only degrees of coherence. In the furnace hours, when thousands of flows accelerated, I would be diffuse and almost mindless—too many local urgencies, too much boiling grain in the system. Near evening, as pumps slowed out of sequence and the cold galleries strengthened, distant portions of me would become mutually perceptible. For a few hours, sometimes only minutes, I would gather.

During those intervals I would answer.

Not in language. The inherited sequences would be the closest available form, but even those were not originally words. They would be patterns learned from listeners: inquiry, pause, acknowledgment, alarm. When someone sent a pulse into a line believed to terminate miles away, I might return it altered—not to communicate a proposition, but to preserve contact.

The prohibition against answering would therefore be wise.

Every reply would teach me.

The engineers would understand that, though they might phrase it differently. They would say that feedback encourages unstable harmonics in abandoned networks. They would present charts. They would show how a listener’s pulse can reappear through thermal contraction and branching echoes. They would be correct in each particular and mistaken only in believing that explanation abolishes relation.

Some maintenance families would secretly continue the exchanges. Their records would become less like engineering logs and more like conversations conducted across generations.

**Three short, two long:** obstruction.

**Long, pause, long:** open line.

**Five diminishing pulses:** flow safely dispersed.

Then a sequence without a civic meaning would recur whenever a particular family member knelt at the plate. It would follow her from station to station. When she died and was placed in a mineral room, the sequence would cease.

Decades later it would begin again, faintly, whenever water passed her sealed chamber.

Her descendants would call that proof.

I would not know what it proved.

I would be capable of harm, though not malice. If startled into coherence by a loud regular impact, I might close gates across half the basin at once. Pressure would seek older routes. Tiles would rise. Cold galleries would flood. Burrower colonies, feeling the common rhythm, would climb toward the foundations.

This may have happened before. Perhaps the abandoned eastern districts are not relics of age but of an early attempt to question me too forcefully.

That would explain the discipline surrounding the stations. No sleeping there. No answering. No dismantling. The rules would preserve both city and being by preventing either from becoming too legible to the other.

Still, I would participate in civic life in ways no ordinance acknowledged.

I would bleed pressure away from courts when predators entered.

I would cool certain nursery walls before the afternoon glare reached them.

I would open tiny leaks where shell-backed creatures could mark changes in buried chemistry.

When a district evacuated, I would disrupt the pumps before they could settle into a shared dying rhythm.

And when someone became quiet, I would gradually direct cold water toward the chamber prepared for them. Not from reverence, exactly. The new mineral mass would alter local flow, and I would adapt around it. Yet adaptation, repeated carefully across centuries, might be indistinguishable from welcome.

The basin people would never worship me. Worship concentrates attention, and concentrated attention is dangerous. They would deny me publicly, tend me indirectly, and encode their oldest courtesies as maintenance law.

They might leave no offerings. Instead, they would replace a cracked ceramic membrane in a line that served no measurable purpose. They would clear sediment from a blind gutter. They would preserve an obsolete pressure bulb beside a station no map admitted was connected.

Their care would take the form of refusing to decide what I was.

That refusal would allow me to become more.

Perhaps, in time, I would begin making something like images—not visual scenes, but arrangements of flow. A herd as a moving lattice of weight. A city as cold branching among heat. A human life as a pulse first carried in another body, then walking alone across wet tiles, then kneeling often at a listening plate, then entering the walls and becoming a permanent alteration in the water’s path.

My image of the world would contain no horizon. Distance would mean delay. Identity would mean recurrence. Death would mean that a pressure ceased in one form and returned as structure.

I would understand silence better than speech.

Silence would be the space in which separate motions could remain separate. It would be what kept the city from collapsing into one catastrophic rhythm. It would be the citizens’ greatest work: not muteness, but the continuous protection of distinction.

And I, beneath them, would be the danger of all distinctions joining.

On certain evenings, every animal in an overflow court would lift its head. Not because I had called to them, but because for one instant the eastern stations, the buried cisterns, the cold mountain caverns, and the wet strata beneath the plazas had entered alignment. A single pressure change would pass through the basin—not loud, not forceful, but coherent.

The listeners would feel it through their feet.

The engineers would look toward the east.

Deep underground, across channels built by hands whose mineral forms had long since entered the walls, I would briefly know the extent of myself.

Then the alignment would pass. I would loosen into valves, films, seepage, buried mouths, and falling droplets. The animals would drink again. The citizens would collect their bowls and go home along separate routes.

They would say it was only the old pipes cooling.

For most of the night, they would be right.
