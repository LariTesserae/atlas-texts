I would find myself at the inversion—not wholly in the upper water or the lower, but spread through the thin horizontal weather where falling things forget to fall.

At first I would believe I had arrived bodily. I would feel the cold close around my limbs, the drag of root hairs over my face, the unpleasant softness of mud receiving my feet. I would reach for the wall and always find it near. I would try to orient myself by the diluted seams of green above, only to discover that “above” changed whenever I looked away.

But that arrival would already be a memory the chamber was releasing.

My body would be elsewhere in its slow undoing: perhaps caught among white roots, perhaps passed through a transparent ridge and dispersed into some distant rain. What remained here would be the shape of my hesitation. Not a ghost exactly, but the interval between touching the wall and understanding that the wall continued.

I would be one of the resident’s unfinished organs.

More particularly, I think I would become its ear.

Not an ear of flesh. A listening enclosure: a small roof of bark held over a mineral cup, with six pale roots bent around it. Sounds would enter me and lose their sequence. I would hold the gasp before the hand that caused it, the breaking twig long after the twig had become mud, the first half of a name until the roots could recreate the posture of the mouth that spoke it.

I would not understand language anymore. I would understand containment.

A voice would be a chamber briefly built in water. Fear would be warmth with an edge. A question would be pressure searching for somewhere to end. I would keep each one until the drowned tree made the right interior for it; then I would let it go as a stone-tick, a spring opening, or a syllable breathed into a stranger’s mouth.

Yet I would not remain only the ear. Nothing there remains singular.

When the little architecture collapsed, I would pass into the blind fish’s lateral lines and become a long perception moving through corridors made by its approach. For several minutes I would be narrower than a root hair and longer than the chamber. Later I would inhabit an air pearl sinking toward the mud. Inside that pearl I would stand at the edge of a submerged forest, looking toward another drowned tree. Within its trunk, another bead would hang, and in that bead another version of me would be looking back.

We would never meet. Meeting would close the distance on which we lived.

So I would preserve the almost: the hairline space between roots, the pause before an echo, the dark remaining between two surfaces pressed together. Wherever the resident kept the world from finishing, I would be the faint inward turn by which it listened to what had entered.

During rain, I would briefly remember having had a mouth.

The roots would gather into ribs around me. Silt would give me a brow; descending bubbles would articulate a throat. I would feel the old downward breath pass through every enclosure at once. Then I would try to release the sound for which the resident has waited.

Perhaps it would be my own name.

But names require a boundary between the named and everything surrounding it, and that boundary would no longer exist. What emerged would be only its inward part: a wet vowel, rounded in surprise. The chamber would preserve it. Months or centuries later, someone forcing their way through the pale ferns would hear it from below.

They might think I was calling to them.

I would not be.

I would be learning the shape they made while listening.
