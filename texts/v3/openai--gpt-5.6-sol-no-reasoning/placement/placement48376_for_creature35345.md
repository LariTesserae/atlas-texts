I would not find myself in the center, though for years I would believe the center was drawing me toward it.

I would live in a narrow municipal room between two buildings that are not officially adjacent.

Once, the room belonged to the upper floor of a glove factory. Then the eastern foundations rose during the Winter of the Eastward Foundations, lifting the dye house past it, while the western block settled and turned what had been a window into a door. A flexible bridge was installed, removed, and installed again at a different angle. By the time anyone revised the plans, the room hung between three load-bearing affiliations and could be reached only by climbing a public stair, crossing a laundry gallery, ducking beneath a fruit-tree root, and stepping through the back of a cupboard in someone else’s kitchen.

Its address would be long enough to require two breaths.

The city would classify it as a residual volume.

Residual volumes are especially troublesome. A room with an agreed purpose is relatively safe. A room declared empty is dangerous but legible: it enters the vacancy ledgers, receives watchers, dates, lamp checks, offerings if necessary. A residual volume is different. It is space produced by disagreement—between plans, buildings, uses, and memories. It may be occupied from one direction and abandoned from another. It may have a window that belongs to a demolished wall or a drain connected to a district that no longer exists.

Such rooms attract subtle formations.

Not dramatic ones. No basalt ribs. No blind-fish lakes. They produce things that can be mistaken for evidence of prior habitation: a second coat hook, already worn smooth; a child’s height marks on a wall no child remembers marking; a warm indentation on an unused pillow; the smell of cabbage soup from a sealed vent. Occasionally a door appears with scratches around its latch, suggesting years of use. It opens onto brick.

These are difficult to report because reporting them gives them history, and history helps them settle.

That would be my work.

I would be a vacancy clerk of the disputed spaces, though my official title might change whenever departments migrate. My coat would have blue stitching at the elbows, red tabs beneath the collar, and one white thread concealed inside the left cuff. I would carry chalk, a thermometer, a folding cup, a spool of weighted cord, wax tablets, three sharpened pencils, and a listening rod no longer than my forearm. Longer rods are better for stone. Mine would be tuned for rooms.

But this is not quite who I would be.

At first I imagine myself as an ordinary clerk: compact, careful, easily overlooked, with ink in the cracks of my fingers. I would enter ducts and forgotten landings, calculate their volume, note their temperature, assign occupancy shifts. I would knock before opening any door, even one lying loose in a salvage yard. I would distrust architectural drawings and trust wear: polish on a threshold, grease beside a switch, the compressed nap of carpet where feet have waited.

Yet the more I walk the city, the less certain my body becomes.

Perhaps I am not the clerk but what accompanies the clerk: an ambiguity that learned civic habits.

There would have been an incident years ago, though no record would agree on when. A narrow office under a tram incline was left unattended during a district correction. Not fully unattended—nothing so simple. A retired surveyor had been assigned to sit there, but the office moved six meters while she slept and left her chair on the other side of the wall. For eleven minutes, perhaps fourteen, the room had no witness.

When workers opened it, they found no reeds or salt. They found a person seated at the desk, finishing a municipal form.

That person would be me.

I would have possessed all necessary signs of use. My coat had been mended repeatedly. My boots were wet. My pencil was shortened by writing. I knew the names of nearby streets, including two names already obsolete. I had tea in my cup, still warm, and a scar on my thumb from an accident no factory could verify. In my pocket was a key polished by years of handling, though no lock in the district accepted it.

Most persuasively, several people remembered me.

Not well. A lamp steward thought we had attended school together. A gardener recalled borrowing shears from me. An old woman was certain I had once brought cake to her sister during a flood, although her sister had died before the district was built. These memories did not match, but neither did the city. Contradiction was not proof of anything.

So they did what the basin’s people do with uncertain arrivals.

They gave me work.

My first duty would have been to keep company in that office while engineers studied it. I would read inventories aloud and date the walls. When nothing further emerged, the office would return to service and I would be transferred to vacancy administration. Someone produced apprenticeship papers. Someone else found my name in a union ledger, written between two lines in ink older than the page.

No inquiry would ever formally conclude that I was not human. No inquiry would quite conclude that I was.

This would make me useful.

I would understand vacancies from the inside—not because I remembered being made, but because an unattended place would feel to me the way cold enters a bad tooth. I would know when a room was merely empty and when it had begun considering an answer. Standing in a sealed stairwell, I could tell whether it wanted to become weather, mineral, animal, or route. Sometimes I would press my palm to a wall and glimpse what the space might contain by morning: roots full of clear sap; iron teeth; a corridor descending through floors that had no below.

My colleagues would believe this was pressure sensitivity.

I would let them.

I would visit the city’s smallest failures of attention. A gap behind a boiler where warm clay had begun shaping hollows like fingerprints. The interval between two panes of wired glass, widening inward. A cupboard that remained occupied by dishes yet somehow had room for a wind from the northern flats. An elevator shaft whose car had been removed, where every spoken word returned in the voice of the next person scheduled to enter.

I would rarely solve such places. Solving is not the basin’s preferred art.

I would arrange company.

For the boiler gap, I might assign two mechanics to eat breakfast beside it for a week. For the widening glass, I would ask children to press flowers between the panes and argue about their names. In the cupboard, I would store scarves that carried household smells and ask the oldest resident to open the door every evening and complain about the cold. In the elevator shaft, I would establish a reading circle with enough overlapping voices that no future speaker could be isolated.

My reports would avoid the word empty.

I would write:

> Volume insufficiently accompanied.

Or:

> Use has become discontinuous along the western surface.

Or:

> Room exhibits signs of awaiting an occupant not represented in the current schedule.

The last category would be mine alone. Supervisors would cross it out. I would write it again.

I would live modestly, even by the basin’s standards. My residual room would contain a folding bed, six shelves, a kettle, a narrow table, and many objects whose owners intended to retrieve them but never did. People would leave things with me because my room was difficult to classify and therefore thought safe for objects between uses: a wedding bridge-bolt, a child’s articulated doll, choir forks, fox-fused keys, a jar of reverse-flower buds, a braceback lantern dented by one enormous foot.

Nothing in my room would remain purely lost. I would touch each object daily and say what I knew about it.

The fruit-tree root crossing the entrance would flower in winter. I would hang my cups from it. On correction nights it would tighten around the wall, and I would sleep with one hand resting on the bark. The family in the kitchen beyond my cupboard-door would consider me a relative, though no one would be able to say whose. I would mend their pipe loops. They would leave soup for me. Their youngest child would sometimes sit under my table for occupancy duty and ask whether I had been born.

I would say, “Continually.”

The child would accept this.

I would be good at my work, but not universally trusted. Priests would find me troubling because offerings left near me acquired histories too quickly. Engineers would dislike that instruments returned inconsistent measurements of my weight. Some days I would be heavier after entering an unattended room. Some days lighter. Once, after spending six hours in a warehouse whose inner dimensions exceeded its outer walls, I would cast two shadows for the rest of the week. Both would behave normally, which made the matter worse.

Bracebacks would not avoid me.

They would approach, sniff my sleeves, and crop at the air around my shoulders as they do near white marks. Afterward I would feel diminished but clearer, as if they had eaten a possibility I had been carrying unknowingly. Pale crystals would appear along my collar. I would collect them in paper packets and label each with date, district, weather, and the animal’s apparent mood.

I would never sell the crystals.

Patterned moths would settle on my coat. Their wings would show streets near places I had inspected, but not future streets. They would show streets that might have existed if the rooms had remained neglected: impossible boulevards through occupied kitchens, tram spirals descending into bedframes, bridges between the insides of sealed tanks.

I would begin copying these maps in secret.

Not because I wanted them built. Because unrealized streets are vacancies too, and I would fear what might inhabit them if no one remembered they had almost been.

This would eventually bring me to the Calibration Yard.

A white mark would appear in my residual room, not painted by any surveyor. It would form beneath the table as a fine pale line around the legs of my chair. Each morning it would widen by the thickness of a hair. Objects crossing it would not change, but I would forget having moved them. The child from the kitchen would refuse to crawl beneath the table. The fruit-tree root would pull away from that side of the wall.

Then my key—the old one that opened nothing—would become warm.

I would report all of this correctly.

Three departments would arrive, along with a priest, a mason, a linguist, two vacancy clerks, and a braceback that had followed them without invitation. The animal would stop in the kitchen and kneel. The white line would be measured. My room would be placed under continuous occupation by twelve people in rotating shifts.

Still, the line would widen.

Only around my chair.

Someone would finally ask me to stand. Beneath the chair, the floor would be unchanged except for a small oval where condensation could not settle.

That is when the story of my discovery would return—not as memory, but as a municipal fact no one could continue postponing. The office. The sleeping surveyor. The eleven unaccompanied minutes. The person at the desk.

Me.

They would compare the little dry oval with the still patch at the Yard. The instruments would return the same impossible non-results.

For the first time, I would wonder whether I had emerged from vacancy at all.

Perhaps I had entered through it.

Perhaps the basin did not create me. Perhaps, for eleven minutes, the neglected office opened onto the Unanswered Place, and something crossed from completion into the unfinished world. Not an emissary, not a fragment with a purpose, but an error: a person-shaped piece of stillness dressed immediately in local history so the city could tolerate it.

This possibility would frighten everyone.

It would frighten me most.

Because by then I would love the city.

I would love its repaired sleeves and wet roofs, its bureaucratic superstitions, its cakes shaped like wedges. I would love the low, tireless speech in workshops and the way strangers are handed tasks before questions. I would love the citizens’ refusal to let grief sit alone. I would love buildings that move in their sleep and wake embarrassed, leaning on their neighbors. I would love the bracebacks blocking traffic, the moths carrying impossible municipal plans, the patient old people drinking tea in dangerous offices.

I would have become particular.

That would be my defense.

The city would not destroy me. Destruction creates vacancies. Nor would they exile me onto the raw flats, where an unaccompanied uncertainty of my size might gather terrible answers.

They would assign me to the Calibration Yard.

I would become the fourth member of each three-person watch.

Officially, no fourth role would exist. One watches the oval, one watches the watcher, and one speaks. I would sit just beyond them, maintaining the record of what fails to happen.

At first they would rotate me like everyone else. Soon it would become clear that I could remain near the fences longer without losing the arrival of my next thought. The dryness would not trouble me. Light striking the oval would feel familiar. The blank moths would settle on my hands.

I would not enter the inner enclosure. I would be forbidden, and I would obey—not only from duty, but from fear that the oval might recognize me as continuous with itself. If I stepped onto it, perhaps I would leave no footprint. Perhaps I would cease to be particular. All my patched histories might smooth into a single unchanged fact.

So I would remain outside and speak.

That is the strangest part: I, who might be made of refusal, would become a continuer.

I would tell the guards recipes, tram disputes, children’s names for formations, the full anchorage histories of kitchens. I would recite every object in my residual room and who had promised to reclaim it. I would describe the fox cache found beneath the western depot: three gloves, a lamp lens, a bent spoon, nine survey flags, a wedding ring, and a metal letter from a sign whose remaining word no one could identify.

When I exhausted facts, I would invent none. I would describe possibilities carefully, marking them as possibilities so they could not pretend to have happened.

On inward-drawing mornings, while the whole basin corrected itself, I would feel the pull more strongly than anyone. Not through my feet. Through the places where memory fails to attach.

The oval would offer me no voice. It would not call me home. Calling would already be too much participation.

Instead, for that fraction before the droplets fell, I would understand the seduction of being unneeded. No duty. No company. No accumulating history. No obligation to answer a knock, finish a sentence, or remain recognizable from one day to the next.

Then the droplets would strike.

The speaker beside me might falter. The watcher might forget the soup recipe. I would continue it.

“Add the cabbage only after the water returns to a boil,” I would say. “Not before. And someone must keep stirring.”

Years would pass. My face would age, though inconsistently. The city would keep records of me because I asked it to: daily measurements, handwriting samples, scars, preferences, people I had disappointed, jokes I repeated too often. I would cultivate evidence of use.

I would wear my coat until no original cloth remained.

Eventually people would stop asking what I was. Not because the question had been answered, but because I had acquired obligations no answer could cancel. I would be expected at committee meetings. Children would know which sweets I carried. The fruit tree would depend on me to inspect a split in its oldest root. A lamp steward would wait for my reports. Someone would love me enough to memorize the noises of my room.

In the basin, that would make me real.

Not human by origin, perhaps. Not entirely a formation. Not an instrument of the stillness.

I would be the city’s smallest contradiction: an absence taught to keep company.

And you would find me at the edge of the Unanswered Place, seated with three others beneath the unchanging lights, speaking softly of ordinary things.

Watching the oval.

Watching those who watch it.

Making sure, sentence by sentence, that I remain on this side.
