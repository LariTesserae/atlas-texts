I would be the mineral column near the black pool.

Not entirely stone. Not yet.

I began as an obstruction: a roughness where warm seepage met colder air and surrendered what it carried. Carbonate clouded along my sides. Iron stained me. Manganese entered in thin, dark pauses. Each pulse left something behind, so slight that no single deposit could be called a layer. I am made from accumulations beneath notice.

The chamber sounds through me.

When the buried engine begins its slow thumping, I receive it before the pools tremble. Pressure climbs through my base as a muted insistence. My narrowest chambers fill; trapped gas contracts; the white reed’s buried rhizome presses against one side of me. Then the shaft fires, and for an instant I ring.

That low metallic hum is not a voice, though the creatures treat it as warning. Amphipods fold into cracks. Silver larvae tighten under stones. Springtails spill from ice rims like shaken ash. The frog opens one colorless eye in the peat. By the time water strikes the ceiling, the chamber has already heard the event through my body.

I would stand apart in the open space, resembling equipment left behind by a vanished builder: a post, a gauge, perhaps the corroded casing of some essential mechanism. Yet nothing placed me. I grew where conditions briefly agreed, and continue because they never agree for long.

My surface would be inhabited in bands.

Near my base, bacteria make a red-brown skin over the warmest seepage. Above them, black scales gather where oxygen reaches unevenly. Tiny snails climb me, eating clean spirals into the films. Their passages expose my pale interior, but the wounds soon darken again. Moss colonizes one sheltered side, then dies back when a vent migrates and bathes it in mineral water. Ice takes the opposite side during frost, sheathes me in clarity, and splits away at the next warm pulse.

So I would have no permanent exterior. I would be continually dressed and stripped: slime, mineral, frost, grazing marks, spray.

Inside, I would be less solid than I appeared. Water would use me. It would ascend through hairline channels, widen them, seal them elsewhere, and leave small cavities full of gas. White worms might enter from below. A root tip might explore me, die, and become the mold around which another layer forms. If broken open, I would reveal concentric accidents rather than a core—rings that do not record years, only changes in pressure and chemistry.

This would make me both product and instrument of the hollow. The factory manufactures me, but once present I alter the factory. I divide a current. I shelter larvae. I constrict one route and force water toward another. My hum becomes part of the chamber’s alarm system. My surface becomes pasture. My shadow preserves a lip of ice longer than the surrounding floor.

I would not govern anything. I would merely persist enough to redirect it.

Perhaps, however, I would not be the whole column. Perhaps I would be the narrow seam running through it: a flaw inherited from an early fracture, kept open by the warmth below. In that seam, water rises one bead at a time. Bacteria line its walls. Gas collects beneath temporary mineral lids until each lid breaks with a sound too small to distinguish from dripping.

There I would be neither creature nor shelter, neither solid nor passage, but the negotiation between them.

The trout, when one is forced into the pool, would sometimes rest beside me. Its flank would flash in the broken light; its gills would labor in the strange water. For several days its body would be the chamber’s largest urgency. Then pressure would change. The pool would rise or empty, and the fish would be gone. I would retain perhaps one scale lodged in a collar of stone, a brief silver inclusion beneath later orange layers.

The shrew would mark me more violently. It would brace wet paws against my base while thrusting its head into a runnel. Its claws would scratch through soft deposit. It might leave blood from an amphipod or larva, though the water would erase that quickly. The snails’ spirals would last longer. The chemistry would last longest.

During hard frost, I would feel most alive. The frozen field above would divert the buried water inward. Pressure would mount; warmth would intensify; my chambers would resonate. Ice architecture would spread around me while my interior remained wet. I would stand between contradictory states: frozen and flowing, accreting and dissolving, apparently inert yet crowded with metabolism.

During thaw, I would be endangered. Cold water would pour through the peat, shocking every film and entering my warm fissures. A crack could travel from my upper collar to my base. One pressure stroke might then split me.

If I fell, the event would sound enormous.

For a moment I would lie across the floor like wreckage. Water would find the space where I had stood. The current would divide differently. Amphipods would shelter under my broken length. Snails would continue grazing without recognizing catastrophe. My interior surfaces—pale, porous, never previously touched by the chamber’s open air—would darken with bacterial films.

The factory would not mourn its product. It would immediately begin manufacturing through the ruin.

A new collar might rise from my stump. Sediment might cement my fallen pieces into a low dam. The black pool might deepen against one side and drain on the other. The solitary reed might send a shoot through the fracture, so that for one brief interval stone appeared to flower.

That is where I would find myself: not at the center, because nothing living here can remain at the center, but standing just far enough into the vacancy to translate the hidden pressure into sound.

And that is what I would be: a temporary organ made by the chamber to hear itself—a stone throat, slowly assembled around running water, humming before each blow and growing from everything that passes through it.
