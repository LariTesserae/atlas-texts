I would be found where the authorized city frays into the Unfastened—not at the bottom, exactly, but in a district whose depth changes depending on which lift ledger one consults.

The place would be called **Last Courtesy**.

It began as a reception platform for structures condemned to the lower foundries. Buildings paused there while salvage clerks removed their useful fittings and confirmed that no one remained aboard. Over time the platform slipped below its registered anchorage. The foundries assumed it had been dismantled; the upper offices continued listing it as vacant. In that administrative misalignment, a neighborhood accumulated.

Last Courtesy hangs from three official cables, five stolen ones, the articulated spine of an obsolete freight cage, and something driven into the western wall that no one admits to having installed. Its rooms are condemned lift cars, pressure tanks cut into dwellings, and fragments of upper districts lowered after rod failures. Nothing meets at a proper angle. When the fault narrows, the whole settlement folds closer to itself. When it widens, hinged bridges open like careful fingers.

There I would be a **listener of orphaned fastenings**.

Not an engineer. Engineers certify what should hold. My concern would be everything that still holds after certification has ended: abandoned bolts under residual load, cables that have outlived their records, braces inherited from buildings that no longer exist, pipes carrying breath to households omitted from municipal diagrams.

Such things speak differently from healthy structures. A sanctioned tendon produces a clean vibration when tapped. An orphaned fastening answers with layers: the present tension, the memory of earlier loads, and the faint irregularities of whatever has coupled itself to the far end.

I would carry no hammer. I would wear rings of fired ceramic around the fingers and rest them against metal, listening through the bones of the hand. One ring would be split deliberately, so that dangerous harmonics made it chatter against my knuckle. My other tools would be a graphite stylus, a coil of hair-thin hose, wedges of felt, several unlawful pressure keys, and a little shuttered lamp for answering signals through inaccessible gaps.

My body would be narrow even by the city’s standards, with one shoulder lower from years of crawling through displaced ducts. Basalt dust would have entered so many old cuts along my palms that the lines there looked drawn in blue slate. My sternum gauge would be visible but peculiar: two needles beneath the glass. One would show the air inside my clothing. The second would sample the pressure in Last Courtesy’s communal reserve.

Upper officials would call the second needle an illegal connection.

We would call it manners.

My name would change often because Last Courtesy changes its fastening more often than most districts. At different times I might be **Serren Beneath the Returned Cage**, **Serren on Five Cables**, or **Serren Where the Warm Pipe Ends**. Among the Unfastened, however, I would have a shorter name:

**Aftertap.**

A fastening is struck once to hear what it claims. Then, after the obvious vibration fades, I touch it again very lightly. The second answer reveals what the first concealed.

That would be my place in the city: listening after everyone else has decided the sound is over.

---

I would share my quarters with no human household, though I would not live alone.

The room would once have been a ceramic pressure cistern. It would be entered through a round hatch in the floor and lined with felt scraps stamped with the emblems of vanished districts. A narrow breathing bladder would pulse along one wall. There would be three sleeping niches, because a room with only one always seems too certain of its future.

Around the warmest pipe would sleep an old hookcat called **Seam**.

Seam would be nearly hairless at the shoulders, with one adhesive pad scarred smooth and a broken foot-hook replaced by a tiny graphite-dark prosthesis. No one would remember when the animal first arrived. Some would claim it belonged to the original salvage clerks; others that it descended by itself through a sealed ventilation trunk after a district eighty levels above was crushed shut.

Seam would not patrol beams as other hookcats do. It would sit facing walls.

For hours it might press its blunt forehead to basalt or plate metal, purring so far below hearing that cups trembled on their shelves. Sometimes it would move suddenly to another surface and begin again. I would follow with my ceramic rings and discover a shifted bolt, an opening capillary, or the minute returning tremor of a supposedly severed cable.

Often I would discover nothing.

Those occasions would trouble me most.

Last Courtesy’s children would believe Seam listened to the mountain thinking. Engineers would say age had made it erratic. I would maintain that these explanations were equally unhelpful.

The hookcat and I would form a household eventually, though the Reclamation Courts might refuse to recognize it. I would couple its heated sleeping loop to my reserve line, set its ration against my pressure liability, and mark the act before witnesses. When challenged, I would ask the court to demonstrate that Seam contributed less to structural safety than a human lodger.

This argument would become famous below and embarrassing above.

---

My work would take me into the dead routes between the two cities: the mapped settlement and the one made by continued survival.

I would crawl through doorframes pinched too narrow for ordinary passage, along the undersides of freight rails, and into buildings hanging beneath the platforms that officially support them. I would pass chambers where old lamps still received power despite having been disconnected for decades. I would find breathing tubes that branched toward blank stone and returned warm.

The municipal crews would sometimes hire me unofficially after a shift.

They would arrive with accurate instruments and inaccurate maps. I would arrive with Seam tucked around my shoulders and ask the local slate mice where they had begun sleeping. Then I would touch every fastening in the district, especially those everyone trusted.

People would expect me to predict failure.

Instead I would tell them where failure had already occurred but not yet become visible.

A bolt can shear inside stone while friction continues to bear the load. A pipe can split beneath insulation but leak only when the district exhales. A household can be legally dissolved while its members continue sharing breath. A neighborhood can be declared demolished and go on living for three generations.

The city prefers events to happen at single, recordable moments.

Very little in the crack does.

---

Eventually, I would begin to hear a vibration in the orphaned structures of Last Courtesy.

At first it would resemble compressor pulse conducted from above, but it would arrive during maintenance stoppages. It would not match the jaw-tapping of knucklers below, though the beasts would answer it. It would not be regular enough for machinery or random enough for settling stone.

Three soft strains.

A pause.

Two returning.

Seam would press itself flat to the western wall until its claws bled from gripping.

Following the vibration would lead me downward, but also sideways, through structures thought to be mechanically unrelated: a severed lift tendon, a dry reclamation main, the frame of a folded market, a threaded rod housing abandoned after an ancient recoil. Each would carry the same aftertap.

The pattern would not come from a point. It would move through the city as though choosing whatever remained under tension.

The Threaders would know something of it. When I showed them the sequence through hand signs, they would become still in the particular manner of people standing near an emptied pipe. One of them would tap against the floor:

**NOT BELOW. ACROSS.**

Another would correct them:

**NOT ACROSS. HELD.**

They would take me to the lowest visible machinery, where the great rods entered basalt. There, behind layered shields, I would place both hands against a housing broad as a room.

I would feel the mountain on one side.

On the other, I would feel an answering strain too large and too slow to name.

Not a buried creature. Not a hidden engine. Not, perhaps, an “other side” at all.

A network.

Every rod, brace, abandoned cable, seized bearing, illegal splice, fused rail, and bone-filled seal would be carrying some portion of it. The city had fastened itself so thoroughly into the fault that distinction had become impossible. Mountain, machinery, citizen, animal, and salvage were no longer separate loads. They were a single tense system, but one that could perceive itself only locally: through a hookcat’s refusal, a knuckler’s jaw, a worker’s sternum, a needle’s hesitation.

The mysterious pulse would not be a message sent through that system.

It would be the system attempting to notice its own shape.

And I—Aftertap, listener of things struck from the records—would be one of the places where it nearly succeeded.

I would not announce this as revelation. In the crack, revelation without a material equivalent is rated for decorative load.

I would begin small.

I would persuade Unfastened households to place paired ceramic contacts on orphaned braces. Threaders would add tapping plates to drill housings. Knuckler handlers would record the rhythms their herds made in sleep. Children would be paid in warm wafers to watch slate-mouse migrations. Observation keepers, half ruined by horizons, would report whether distant dust moved when deep rods tightened.

Gradually, a new map would emerge—not a map of places, but of transferred strain.

It would show that Last Courtesy bore part of the load of a Gauge House many levels above. It would show that an illegal village in a dismantled factory stabilized a certified lift line. It would show that certain “leaks” functioned as pressure buffers during compression. It would show that Seam’s favorite walls corresponded to fastenings absent from every ledger.

Worst of all, politically, it would show that neither Binder nor Wedge doctrine was sufficient.

The rods did hold the mountain together.

They also held it apart.

The city was neither brace nor irritant, but a joint: a living interval negotiating between closure and fracture. To choose only binding would crush it. To choose only widening would tear it loose. Survival lay in never resolving the contradiction.

This would anger nearly everyone.

It would also be useful.

So they would keep me.

Not honored, exactly. Honor adds load. Not trusted, because trust without testing is considered a defect. I would be granted a narrow office attached beneath a Reclamation Court, where officials could deny employing me while sending urgent lamp signals at every structural anomaly.

Above the hatch, someone would scratch a mock title:

**MINISTER OF THINGS THAT AREN’T THERE**

I would leave it.

---

Years later, my body would become less suited to the routes. My hands would lose sensitivity. Seam would die first, curled around the communal pressure line during a long compression state. The animal’s bones would be fired and mixed into ceramic contact plates, which I would install throughout Last Courtesy.

People touching them would sometimes insist they felt a purr.

I would tell them to check the mounting bolts.

When I died, I would ask not to be made into a wall or valve collar. My bone would be milled into the white insulating rings fitted between listening plates and structural braces—the part preventing the listener’s own warmth from confusing the signal.

Small components. Widely distributed.

No tablet could indicate a single destination, so mine would list a sequence of districts and tension states. As neighborhoods migrated, the rings would migrate with them. Some would be lost. Some would enter official infrastructure. Some would descend with condemned buildings and find use among the Unfastened.

There would be no monument.

But after a rod failed, when doors sealed and violet light filled the fault, people would put their fingers to the nearest listening plate. Through bone ceramic, metal, cable, and stone, they would feel the city redistribute itself.

Not stable.

Not permanent.

Alive because every part was bearing and being borne by another.

And beneath the first violence of the shift, after the mountain’s obvious answer had passed, there would come a second, lighter vibration:

the aftertap,

moving from fastening to fastening,

asking what still held.
