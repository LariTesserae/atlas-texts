I would find myself where the chamber nearly ceases to be a chamber: not in the open center, nor beneath the salamander’s stone, but inside the farther wall, in a hairline fracture between the abandoned riverbed and the river’s present course.

I would be small enough that the fracture was a country.

At first I imagine myself as one of the colorless worms in the basin sediment, living by coldness and seepage. But the basins are too exposed to the human world. Hands enter them. Lamps bend over them. Their rims receive thirst, grief, and faces wavering on black water. A worm there would know disturbances without knowing their source.

Then I think I might be the salamander, because it has become the one visible answer the chamber permits. Its appearance reassures people that the river still runs correctly, though the creature itself makes no promise. Yet that is too complete a role. To be seen as an omen is still to be drawn into human meaning.

I would be something less legible.

A pale, nearly transparent mite, perhaps, or a soft-bodied grazer without eyes, living in the damp skin of the crack. My world would be a narrow vertical passage furred with fungal threads. Condensation would descend through it not as droplets but as floods: sudden clear bodies that filled the passage from wall to wall, carried grit, bent whole stands of growth, and withdrew into the basin below. The smooth black rock would be my sky and ground together. Mineral grains would be landmarks. A crust around an ancient wooden peg might be a distant white range glimpsed only over generations of movement.

I would not hear the river as the visitors hear it. I would have no useful distinction between sound and touch. The river would be the condition of matter.

Each pulse would pass through the stone into my limbs. I would know the hidden channel by changes in pressure: the long tightening when water rose, the fine tearing tremor of gravel shifting, the deep interval when some unseen obstruction gave way. What people called louder and quieter would be, to me, weather. Perhaps more than weather: day and night, season and direction, warning and summons.

The fracture would open toward two immensities. On one side, the hollow chamber: dry, airy, intermittently pierced by fire. On the other, beyond several arm-lengths that no creature of my scale could cross, the buried river: absolute motion without light. I would live in the thin habitable uncertainty between them.

There would be food there because almost nothing is ever entirely barren. Fungal hyphae would enter from damp seams. Bacteria would gather where mineral-rich water lingered. The dissolved tablets of the dead would arrive grain by grain: clay, skin salt, soot, perhaps the faint organic remains of fingers that shaped the names. I would graze upon this film without knowing it as memory.

But something of memory might still exist in the form available to me.

Not names. Never names.

Weight, inclination, recurrence.

I would learn that certain tremors came with footsteps in the abandoned bed. Human beings would announce themselves long before their lamplight entered the crack. One tread would arrive heavily and alone. Another would be quick, pause often, and return after filling a skin. Two sets of impacts might approach one another, stop, and remain so close that their vibrations mingled. Foreheads touching would become a minute coupled resonance transmitted through skeleton, floor, wall, and finally into the filament on which I rested.

Their vow would reach me after its words had been lost.

That would be the chamber’s truest language: not what was said, but that two living weights had leaned together and remained.

Sometimes three sharp changes of light would pass across the crack—the missing-person signal—but light would matter less to me than the altered movement afterward: many feet abandoning their usual paths, palms striking walls, bodies kneeling to search beneath ledges. Fear would have a recognizable rhythm. So would relief. So would the slower, heavier entry of those carrying a clay tablet.

Over years, I might come to know individuals as recurring patterns. The one who dragged the left foot. The child whose steps gradually struck farther apart. The old visitor who always rested a hand against the river wall, sending a faint, warm pressure into my fracture. Then the pattern would cease.

Much later, clay from that person’s tablet might wash past me.

I would not understand the exchange. Yet perhaps I would pause on the new sediment because its mineral taste differed from the older bank. A fungus would take hold. I would lay eggs among its threads. My young would feed where a name had been.

This would not be immortality. It would not even be remembrance. It would be continuation stripped of consolation.

The river would remain larger than all of it.

Eventually the crack itself would widen. Only by a hair in a human lifetime, but for me that hair would be an opening valley. New moisture would enter. Old films would dry. Some passages would become habitable; others would become impossible. The wall would not seem permanent. It would be a slow animal whose joints altered while generations lived inside its skin.

Perhaps, during an unusually high phase, pressure from the hidden channel would drive a dark bead of water through the fracture. It would swell from the stone, carrying the river’s deeper cold and a dust of minerals from places neither humans nor salamanders had touched. To the chamber it would be nothing: one more wet point on the wall, one drop lost against the roar.

To me it would be the arrival of the world.

I would approach after the first violence passed. The new water might contain unfamiliar spores, fragments of blind life, or only the chemical signature of remote stone. I would feed at its edge. Behind it, the hidden river would press against the narrow passage, immense and unknowable.

And I think that is where I would belong: not as guardian, witness, or keeper, but as a minute interpreter of pressure. A creature for whom absence had texture. A creature living so close to the inaccessible river that every part of its body was shaped by something it could never enter.

If a lamp found me, I would be almost nothing—a pale fleck withdrawing from brightness into a seam.

The person holding the lamp might never notice.

The chamber would empty again when the flame moved on.

But inside the wall, among wet threads and grains of nameless clay, I would continue to feel the river drawing everything toward it: stone, water, memorials, footsteps, and my own translucent body, each at its proper speed.
