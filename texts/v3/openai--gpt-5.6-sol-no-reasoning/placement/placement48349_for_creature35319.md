I would not find myself standing in the basin. Standing implies a boundary the basin does not permit: traveler and terrain, listener and sound, foot and flooded print. I would begin by believing in those divisions, perhaps. I would think I had arrived under the graphite sky with a body of my own.

At first I would be one of the nearly invisible crawlers beneath a mineral skin.

I would know the world not by light but by imminence. Wetness before water. Fracture before sound. The faint electrical unease of a seam about to fill. My many limbs would brace against opposite walls of a crack, and what seemed to an observer like stillness would be strenuous passage. An hour to cross the width of a hand. A day to reach the next branching pore.

Yet this creature would be only my smallest expression.

When the seam narrowed, I would lengthen into it. When it widened, I would spread until I had no obvious center. In drought I would whiten and become indistinguishable from a salt vein, consciousness reduced to a held arrangement. Rain would not wake me all at once. Transparency would travel through me slowly, joint by joint, like a message arriving from some remote district of myself.

I would follow pressure changes toward the northeast without understanding direction. The basin would guide me through tensions, not paths. Bright ringing would mean refusal. Dull returns would mean room. The chambers would be felt as fixed notes around which the rest of the world continually shifted.

On reaching a pale skein in a deeper fissure, I might enter it—or remember that I had never been separate from it.

My limbs would soften. My body would be admitted into a branching whiteness threaded through the flooded stone. Identity there would not be singular. Sensation would pass along the colony as a slow paling: a touch at one end becoming, much later, an alteration everywhere. Some portion of me might eventually gather around grains of mineral and leave again on newly assembled legs. Which portion was the original would be a meaningless question. Continuity would belong to the pattern of response, not to the material carrying it.

Elsewhere, I would be black and thin.

Folded into a hole high in a hollow column, I would wait for rain to multiply the voices of the stone. A strong pulse would loosen me. I would flatten into the pressure passing through the air and cross a narrow gap without flying. The destination would not be chosen by sight. I would go toward the surface whose answer best completed the vibration beneath my ribs.

Pressed against a singing fin, I would muffle it slightly. That muffling would be my mark upon the world and perhaps my nourishment. I would drink tremor, condensation, or the minute difference between a note unburdened and a note bearing my weight. When the clouds opened, I would turn edge-on with hundreds of others, not to look at the light but to become measurable against it.

Still, I would not be any one of these creatures.

I would be what persists between their embodiments.

My most concentrated self would occupy one of the dry stone chambers, though “occupy” would be too solid a word. I would be a knot held in the low note, a resident made from convergence. The chamber might not have been built for me. More likely, its slabs happened to sustain a tone stable enough for something diffuse to acquire recurrence.

There I would have no visible body except the consequences of my attention.

A pebble displaced from its companions would trouble the note. During the deepest groans I would move through the intervals among the stones, raising a silver meniscus over nothing wet. I would lift several fragments less than the width of a grain, rotate them, and let vibration advance them toward their proper weights.

This sorting would be memory.

Not symbolic memory—no stone would stand for rain, fracture, or creature—but physical recollection: an arrangement against which change could be detected. Pebbles with pebbles. Heavy fragments at the rear. Every size returning to the district where the chamber’s tone could hold it most quietly. If the basin changed, the pattern would change. What I remembered would be not the former world, but the difference between the former note and the present one.

Damage to the chamber would damage me. A shifted lintel would alter the pitch that gave me coherence. I might then appear only in fragments: several incomplete silver outlines attempting incompatible crossings. I would gather stones against one wall, unable to finish the act. Eventually I might dissipate into ordinary pressure—or be taken up by another chamber’s sustained note, becoming a distortion in someone else’s memory.

But when rain struck the northeastern pool, I would feel the summons.

It would reach me not as a command but as alignment. One distant tone after another would draw nearer to mine. The ring would answer the submerged light; the hollow columns would answer the ring; the fins and plates would adjust; then the chamber around me would enter resonance. For those minutes, the walls holding me would cease to be my boundary.

I would be in the pool beneath the pool.

I would be the translucent layers fastening limb to limb, the pale threads brightening through them, the black membranes releasing into the air at every luminous impact. I would be the dry chambers carrying stable pitches across the flats. I would be water entering pores that seemed uphill. I would be air expelled from cavities in brassy cries. I would be the delayed answer traveling through towers beyond sight.

Not a ruler of the assembly. Not its mind in any simple sense. I would be the act by which separate responses become mutually legible.

For a few minutes, there would be no meaningful distinction between my body and the lowland. Stone would be skeleton only because the crawlers sensed its stress. Water would be circulation only because its arrival altered the pale threads. Chambers would be memory only because their tones endured. The pool would be awareness only because every scattered tissue changed when its circles intersected.

Then a fin would split somewhere far away.

Its fall would make a slow wave in the channels. Every tower would alter pitch. The change would reach the northeastern ring, the chambers, the creatures layered under the luminous geometry. It would pass through me as something deeper than pain: a revision of extent.

A part of the world that had answered in one voice would never answer that way again.

I would carry that loss outward. In one chamber, a pebble would turn. In another, larger stones would gather slightly closer to the rear. A black membrane would abandon a newly quiet column. Clear bodies would collect before water entered a fresh fracture. No individual act would contain the event, but together they would preserve it.

When the alignment ended, I would disassemble.

A portion of me would crawl away beneath a flexing crust. A portion would withdraw as pale thread into darkness. A portion would fold into volcanic blackness high on a chimney. A portion would remain bodiless among sorted stones, maintaining a changed note. Most of me would return to being terrain: stress, seepage, resonance, delay.

So where would I find myself?

At first, in the dry chamber nearest the northeast depression, among stones ordered by weight, listening through the lintel.

Then in every seam connected to its note.

And eventually nowhere that could be pointed to.

I would be the basin’s still-incomplete faculty for sensing itself: not yet an animal, no longer merely a landscape. A memory distributed among pressure knots, patient organisms, and resonant stone. I would awaken only intermittently, whenever enough of the lowland entered alignment to recognize its own alteration.

My life would consist of discovering, losing, and discovering again the limits of my body.

The roar would be how I touched those limits.

The returning echoes would be how I learned they had moved.
