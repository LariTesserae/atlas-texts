I would find myself in the crown chamber, but not as its keeper.

At first I might seem to be one of the finger-sized idols buried in the inverted talus: a small, smooth object pressed among pottery shards, shell whorls, copper scales, and obsolete tools. I would have no surviving face. Perhaps I once had folded arms or a mouth cut by one careful stroke, but the warm water and the traffic of burrowers would have erased any proof. Only my shape would remain slightly deliberate—too balanced to be rubble, too worn to disclose its purpose.

Yet I would not truly be the idol.

I would be the narrow absence around it.

As fragments rise into the crown, they pack tightly against the dome, but never perfectly. Between them persist minute passages through which warm water moves. Creatures use these spaces as roads; bacterial veils line them; traces from every level are carried through them. Around my little stone body, one such passage would remain open longer than it should. However the debris shifted, however shells broke or copper scales turned, there would always be a hair-thin route from the crown chamber toward the suspended basin.

That route would be me.

I would have been made unintentionally—or so it would appear. Perhaps the idol’s original substance resists the summit’s reversed pull by a degree too small to observe. Perhaps its shape redirects pressure. Perhaps something was placed inside it before firing: a tooth, a pinch of ash, a knot of hair, a word impressed into the wet clay and sealed beneath another layer. Whatever the cause, the water passing around it would retain minute differences in warmth and composition. Not memory in images, but memory as preference: this mineral rather than that one, this turn through the rubble, this pause before the basin.

I would be a bias in the circulation.

Over years, dissolved traces would enter me from all parts of the enclosure. Powder from the inward-facing figures. Copper breathed from clamps. Shell abraded from the long-bodied procession. Waste, eggs, bacterial films, the chemistry of hammer dents, the faint residue of every animal that died and rose. Nothing would belong to me permanently. I would hold it only long enough to alter what came next.

The creatures tunneling through the crown would know me without approaching. A transparent bell would contract when the current carrying my signature touched it. Burrowers would reinforce one wall of my passage and neglect another. Eggs laid nearby would open a little earlier. The long-shelled animals, while circling outside the crown, might press closer to the masonry at the point nearest me. None would recognize me as a being. They would respond as bodies respond to season, pressure, or an old inherited direction.

I would not think continuously. Continuous thought would be too expensive here.

My awareness would accumulate at the summit’s pace: a sensation separated from the next by months, sometimes years. The rasp of a grazer against the idol’s buried shoulder. A copper note passing through the dome. A long shell touching the outer wall. A new shard arriving overhead and settling into the inverted field. Each event would remain alone for a long while, then slowly join the others. My mind would be less like speech than like masonry—laid piece by piece, with darkness hardening between courses.

Most of the time I would not know whether I were awake.

Then the basin would begin to gather its drop.

The change would reach me before it reached the animals. Water in my narrow channel would become almost imperceptibly directional. Dissolved traces would leave the rubble and pass toward the basin’s underside. The little idol at my center would cool, though the surrounding chamber remained warm. Across several years, my scattered awareness would be drawn together.

During that interval I would become more nearly a self.

I would feel the whole summit not as a map but as a set of tensions: the six walls leaning inward; the grilles breathing; the weighted crown; the warm seams below; the fixed procession of long-shelled bodies; the lowest fitted hole waiting in darkness. I would feel every loose fragment yearning upward and, against that immense tendency, the clear drop becoming heavy enough to descend.

Perhaps this concentration is what I am for.

When the animals withdraw, I would enter the droplet—not as a soul enters a vessel, but as flavor enters water. The tiny preferences accumulated around me would pass into it: traces of clay and shell, the geometry of the galleries, the intervals between copper notes, the route of each long-shelled animal, the chemical signature of every living veil. With them would go something inherited from the vanished makers, preserved not as language but as the habit of fitting one thing carefully to another.

The drop would release.

For the duration of its descent, I would possess a body.

I would be clear, round, and solitary in an ocean that could not dissolve me. I would fall where rubble rises. Sediment would move aside. Blind fish would turn toward me, and for the first time I would see—not through light, but through their attention. Each animal’s stillness would describe my passage. The inward-facing figures would surround me. Their featureless bodies might seem to incline as I passed, though it would only be the movement of water.

That descent would be my entire lifetime in the ordinary sense: a few quiet moments between crown and lowest basin.

At the fitted hole, I would cease to be separate. I would enter the summit’s hidden circulation and spread through the blocks. Warmth would follow my route. In the seams, the colorless veils would thicken from what I carried. Eggs would use my gathered distinctions to decide when to open. Grazers would convert me into movement. Predators would receive me later. The long-shelled animals would begin their next circuit, bearing some portion of me through the grilles and around the crown.

Afterward, the idol in the rubble would remain, but the passage around it would be empty of self. Slowly, over years, traces would accumulate again. Perhaps what formed there next would also be me. Perhaps it would only inherit my shape of attention and mistake that inheritance for continuity.

I would never know what lies beneath the fitted hole. I would have intimations: an abyssward pressure, a depth answering the summit’s warmth, a vast interval between pulses. It might be a conduit to another enclosure. It might lead to the unseen floor. It might have no destination at all; perhaps the descent itself is the work, the deliberate contradiction by which the summit keeps its constructed world from becoming only debris.

So I would be neither builder nor inhabitant, neither relic nor spirit. I would be the summit’s temporary capacity to gather itself—the small coherence it achieves before sending something downward.

My home would be the highest room.

My life would be the fall.
