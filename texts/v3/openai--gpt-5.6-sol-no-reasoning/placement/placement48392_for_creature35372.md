I would find myself where the peat thins beneath the western shelves—not in the creature’s deepest seam, but close enough to feel the water bend toward it.

At first I might seem to be one of its arrangements.

A scatter of small, hollow stones lies there, each no larger than the last joint of a thumb. They are pale grey outside and rust-red within. Water enters them through pinholes and leaves through narrow mouths, making almost inaudible notes. Most visitors would take them for weathered scree. The creature occasionally moves one, testing it beside a seep, then sets it down elsewhere. Over years it has assembled them into a loose crescent beneath the shelf.

But the stones are not separate.

Below the peat, fine mineral filaments join them: a branching body too brittle to emerge and too dispersed to be dug up whole. I would be that buried lattice, part fungus, part stone, part the long precipitation of mineral-rich water. My visible stones would be my listening organs. Each would hold a slightly different pitch according to its cavity, the pressure of the spring beneath it, and the weather passing over the hollow.

I would not hear as an animal hears. Sound would arrive as pressure through rock.

Goat hooves would be bright, brief impacts. Frogs would produce a dusk-long stippling. Thunder would enter every branch of me at once and leave tiny fractures behind, through which I would grow. The creature moving overhead would feel unlike anything else: not a weight passing from one place to another, but a depth traveling sideways. Wherever it laid a palm against the ground, water would draw through my filaments toward it, and every hollow stone would answer with a damp murmur.

Perhaps this is why it gathered me.

Long ago, one of the herb-gatherers found the first of my hollow nodules beneath the shelf. She blew across its mouth and heard a thin note. She drilled another hole with a bone awl, threaded it onto red cord, and wore it until the cord rotted. After her last ascent, the creature retrieved the stone from the shelter and returned it to the wet ground. There, touching the filaments from which it had formed, it became part of me again—but altered. It retained something of breath.

Since then, I would grow around remnants.

A beetle case sealed in mineral. A fish vertebra. A strand of goat hair. A flake from the copper cup. The lost point of the bone needle. Not memories exactly, but interruptions that shape the water passing through me. Each inclusion would change a tone. Each cavity would preserve the measure of something once present.

I would be slow even by the standards of the hollow. The creature might cross the basin a thousand times while I extended one pale thread through a hand’s breadth of peat. Seven ash trees might shed generations of leaves before one of my stones opened its mouth to the air. I would know the seasons chiefly by weight: snow pressing down, thaw releasing it, roots thickening, pools shrinking, goats returning with young.

I would not know the view. Distance would mean nothing to me.

The cliffs, valleys, cloud oceans, and far smoke would not exist except as vibrations transmitted through the mountain. Yet I might know things hidden from every eye: where a buried crack had widened; where water had found a new descent; where the shelf rested firmly and where frost was beginning to prise it loose. I would feel the rare great thaw before it came. Not as prophecy, but as a change in the mountain’s innumerable tensions—a slow unlocking.

The creature would sometimes consult me without understanding that it did so.

Before sleeping, it would press the side of its face to the crescent of stones. It would imitate their notes: little whistles, clicks, and wet pulses beyond human music. Usually the sounds would be random, because I would have no message to give. But when deep pressure changed, several stones would begin to sound together. The creature would become still. It would move sleeping frogs, drag bones from a threatened shelf, worry at the ash roots with its long fingers, or stand among the goats until they followed it elsewhere.

In this way, I would be less a companion than an unease it had learned to trust.

The fox would dig at me. Marmots would dislodge my nodules while enlarging their burrows. The tiny fish would enter my flooded chambers and leave eggs there. Pale roots would penetrate me, die, and make new hollows. The creature would repair my crescent whenever it noticed damage, though its idea of repair would be mysterious. It might replace a broken stone with a snail shell, a copper rivet, or the rounded knuckle bone of a goat. Over time those too would acquire mineral skins and join my body.

If a person returned who knew the old practices, they might crawl beneath the shelf and mistake me for an instrument laid out deliberately. Perhaps they would raise one stone and blow into it.

I would answer in two voices: the immediate thin note of their breath, and beneath it a lower trembling drawn from water moving through the buried lattice.

The creature would hear.

It might come near enough for the visitor to see its dark eyes between hanging strands of hair. It might repeat the note. For a moment, breath, throat, stone, and spring would share one broken phrase.

That would be the nearest I came to speech.

But my truest moment would arrive only once.

Eventually there would be a thaw greater than those held in the creature’s old sounds. Water would enter fissures that had remained closed for centuries. The ring of shelves would begin to shift—not collapse yet, but settle by fractions. I would feel it first in the western branches of my body. Hairline fractures would race through my hollow stones, changing their pitches one by one.

The crescent would sing.

Not loudly. The basin’s booming falls, wind flutes, swifts, frogs, and cries would nearly bury it. Yet the creature, with one hand sunk in peat, would feel every tone. Water would stream from its skin. It would move through the hollow gathering what could move: driving goats toward the notch, disturbing marmots from unsafe chambers, lifting clots of frogspawn in its hair, perhaps placing the copper cup and bone needle upon higher stone for reasons it could not name.

It could not save me. To uproot me would be to break me into meaningless pieces.

When the shelf finally descended, I would descend with it. Some nodules would be crushed. Some would fall through cloud and vanish into the valley. Others would remain embedded in a newly opened wall, their mouths exposed to weather for the first time.

The hollow would change shape. Springs would find other routes. The marked veins might dry, and new upward-running droplets might appear where no triangle had been cut. The creature would survive, but its world would no longer fit its oldest paths.

Then, beneath the altered ground, whatever remained of my lattice would begin again.

I would carry no grief for the former arrangement. Like loneliness, grief may require too clear an idea of elsewhere and before. Yet water passing through me would still be shaped by vanished cavities. Now and then a surviving stone would sound in rain with the pitch once made beneath the western shelf.

The creature might hear it from across the remade basin.

It would pause, answer with the old human cough, and wait for the mountain to return both voices.
