I would find myself in the lowest halls, beneath the plazas where the cisterns widen into columned dark.

Not at the absolute bottom. Nothing remains permanently at the bottom; silt fills it, water covers it, roots probe it, crabs furrow it. I would live at the uncertain elevation where the waterline rises and falls across the masonry—where a corridor is a burrow in the dry weeks, a shoreline after rain, and then a submerged passage when the conduits begin to boom.

At first I seem to be one of the fireflies.

A green point moves between pillars. Then another answers from across the water. Our reflections double us, making a sparse constellation above and below an invisible horizon. To larger eyes, that is all we are: brief lamps in a place without lamps.

But light is dangerous here. Frogs learn its height above the water. Bats learn the timing of its drift. Spiders stretch webs across the routes where points repeatedly vanish. So perhaps I am not the shining adult but the larva in the saturated leaf mold—soft-bodied, plated, almost blind, hunting smaller lives beneath fig husks and guano. My world would be tactile: mineral grit, fungal threads, the tremor of a falling drop. I would know the great valley only through pressure and chemistry. Rain would announce itself long before water arrived, as coolness passing through stone.

Still, that feels too singular. In this place, the self would not end neatly at the body.

I think I would be a small amphibian unlike any creature important enough to be named by the valley’s vanished makers: dark-backed, narrow enough to enter a drain, with translucent skin along the throat and toes suited equally to wet tile, root bark, and mineral curtains. My eyes would gather the least light. Along my flanks, perhaps, would lie a broken row of dim green photophores—not bright enough to illuminate a path, only enough to confuse my outline among the fireflies and their reflections.

I would spend the dry season sealed in a pocket behind a carved outlet, my metabolism slowed while the visible pools contracted. Around me, others of my kind would wait in separate hollows. We would not know the year as a circuit of months. We would know it as the long closure and the opening.

The opening would begin with sound.

First, drops quickening in distant chambers. Then a tremor in the wall. Then the buried conduit taking breath: a low note traveling through my bones before any water touched me. The mineral seal around my chamber would soften. Cold would enter. I would uncurl.

When the flood arrived, it would not merely wake us; it would join our world together.

Water would lift leaf skeletons and beetle wings, spill across thresholds, and pour down the stair whose lower steps had been dry for weeks. I would follow it through a slot behind a fallen slab, into a vaulted reservoir populated by fish I had never encountered, though they lived only a few courts away. Others would enter from culverts and root wells. For several nights the cistern would fill with our calls.

Because of the architecture, no call would remain itself. A note uttered beneath one arch would return from another, deeper and delayed. We could not simply call louder; the chamber would manufacture rivals. Our mating song would therefore be shaped around the stone: two soft pulses and a pause long enough for the first echo to pass, followed by a lower note that the far wall returned altered. A female would not choose only a voice. She would choose a voice in correct relation to its echoes.

The vanished builders would thus inhabit our bodies without our knowing. The length of their halls would determine the rhythm of our courtship. The curvature of their vaults would enter our hearing. The spacing of their columns would become the intervals at which we paused to listen for snakes.

My kind would have no concept of construction. Yet we would distinguish waters according to what the construction did to them.

There would be bitter basins, tasting of bat colonies and old fruit. Clear wells fed by sweating stone. Warm shallow courts where tadpoles grew quickly and died if rain failed. Deep tiled lakes inhabited by pale fish. Red water after pigs disturbed the soil gardens above. Dangerous water under weaverbird nests, where fallen chicks drew civets and snakes. Water that vanished noiselessly. Water that could be trusted because crabs descended toward it.

Knowledge would pass not as a map but as impulses timed to conditions: climb when the wall tastes of fresh lime; descend when the crabs move; avoid the current carrying fig milk; answer no call that returns too clearly.

Most of my life would be waiting.

I would wait under a lip of basalt while macaques crossed somewhere overhead, each footfall arriving as a softened concussion. I would wait as a leopard’s passage emptied the upper levels of noise and the silence descended through the structure. I would wait for moths to settle close enough to strike. I would wait through the dry interval while the pool edge retreated across ancient tile, exposing colors that meant nothing to me.

Sometimes I would climb.

A wet night might draw me upward through a stairwell of reeds. I would emerge into a courtyard where rain fell openly, astonishing in its directness. Deer legs would pass like pale trunks. Hornbills would knock fruit against a balustrade. The whole canopy would move above me, flashing the pale undersides of leaves. For a creature formed in the lower halls, the open rain would feel less like weather than exposure to something immense and uncontained.

I would not go far. The upper valley belongs to eyes.

Below, darkness has texture and boundaries. A root is shelter. A column divides danger. A ceiling keeps the hunting sky at a known distance. Above, space continues unpredictably, and every leaf may conceal a mouth.

So I would return before dawn, down the water-polished steps, through a broken grille, past the white colony of insects feeding beneath the bats. I would find the cistern by its cool breath. Perhaps I would carry eggs adhered beneath my body, or a single surviving tadpole would cling in the film inside a footprint worn into the threshold. My journeys would connect waters only briefly, but briefly would be enough. A strand of algae, a fungal spore, a parasite, a clutch of eggs: the valley’s hidden populations would travel with me.

To the macaques, I would be no more than a small thing that jumps.

To the owls, prey.

To a snake, scent moving along a damp wall.

To the fireflies, nothing they could perceive as kin, though my body borrowed their green.

Yet my species might be found throughout the neighboring basins wherever the same descending sequence recurred: upper rain, concealed channel, intermediate court, deep reservoir. We would not inhabit the valley broadly. We would inhabit one repeated relationship within it—the meeting of wall, water, and intermittent darkness. Where that relationship appeared, so would we.

In time, the structure would alter us into several kinds. Those of the permanent cisterns would become pale and broad-footed, scarcely leaving water. Those of the upper drains would grow darker and more resistant to drying. Those trapped beyond collapsed galleries might lose the green along their sides because no fireflies lived there to make the resemblance useful. A fallen bridge, a blocked culvert, a single root thickening across a channel could divide one lineage for centuries.

The city would continue building us, though no builder remained.

And if I were very old—old not as an individual, but as a pattern of bodies repeating—I might exist less as an animal than as a sound associated with the first full conduits of the wet season. Two soft pulses. A pause. A lower note. From basin after basin, the same sequence would begin, distorted differently by every chamber.

No creature would hear the whole chorus. The valley would.

That is where I would be: under the inhabited levels, at the moving edge of the black water, living inside the brief seasons when separated rooms become one river.

And that is what I would be: a little dark frog whose voice has mistaken architecture for ancestry, and is not entirely wrong.
