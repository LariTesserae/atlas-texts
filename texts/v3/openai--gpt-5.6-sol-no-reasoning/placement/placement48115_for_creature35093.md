I would be found at the city’s edges, where the last disciplined road continues past the last inhabited court and crosses ground that has not yet decided whether it is street, meadow, or foundation.

I would not live alone, exactly. Behind me would stand a low house shared with a loose court of people whose rooms had gradually gathered there: an elderly water reader, two sisters who cultivate rain-sensitive cloth, a child who claims to remember the eastern city, and someone who came through a transit pavilion carrying a key that has never fit anything. My own room would remain slightly detached. On dry days a narrow passage would connect it to the kitchen. During rain, that passage would lengthen and turn toward the open plain.

The house would not dislike me. It simply would not be certain that I belonged entirely indoors.

I would be a surveyor of unfinished routes.

The Office of Agreements would call the work **destination tending**, an awkward official term for a profession older than its records. Roads here do not merely require maintenance. Some lose conviction. A street laid toward a new district may begin hardening under travelers bound elsewhere. A footpath may curve toward a garden that has migrated away. A bridge may shorten so effectively that its farther half ceases to expect arrivals. Beyond the inhabited districts, pale roads sometimes continue for kilometers toward places no map admits.

I would walk those roads.

Not to discover where they lead, but to learn where they are trying to lead.

My clothing would be municipal gray only at the cuffs. The rest would be layered black fiber woven with thin green lines that emerge in moisture like routes on a map. Around one wrist I would wear a porous-stone bracelet borrowed from the Office, although I would not be an official. Around the other would be cane rings, each holding a bead of water gathered from a different district. When a road began to revise itself, the droplets would tremble at different times.

My tools would be modest: chalk that adheres only to surfaces intending to remain solid; a ceramic vial for collecting dust from crossroads; four weighted cords for determining whether distance is being borrowed; and a small folding seat. The seat would matter most. Roads are accustomed to being crossed. They reveal more when someone sits down and asks nothing of them.

I would walk softly enough for the pavement to recognize me but not so habitually that it mistook my route for an instruction.

That distinction would be the center of my work.

At intersections I would test each direction by thinking of different destinations—not naming them aloud, but holding them in mind: home, water, a person I missed, an obligation postponed, a place I feared. The ground would subtly alter beneath my soles. A trustworthy road responded to intended places. A troubled one responded to longing instead.

Those were dangerous roads. Not malicious. Generous in the wrong way.

They led travelers toward what felt most absent.

I would mark them with low black stones and ask the Office to restrict passage until an agreement could be reached. Then I would return repeatedly, bringing ordinary purposes with me. Bread for a household. A message for a gardener. A child’s lost cup. By giving the road enough small, completed errands, I could sometimes teach it that destinations need not be profound to be real.

People would tease me for speaking of roads as though they were shy animals. Root advocates would object that roads were processes, not organisms. Volume stewards would insist they were agreements in spatial form. Children would ask whether a road could miss someone.

I would tell them yes, but that this did not mean they should follow it.

My work would often take me near the eastern field.

I would begin by avoiding it. Most destination tenders would. Roads there behaved badly: they aligned with stairs that were not present, arrived at intersections suspended several meters above the grass, or divided around structures visible only as condensation. Measurements taken in the field became accurate several years later. Chalk marks sometimes appeared on the exposed stone before I made them.

Yet one road would trouble me more than the others.

It would begin behind my court as a narrow strip of pale pavement. Municipal maps would show it ending after six kilometers at an abandoned cistern. In practice, it would continue beyond the cistern, past the grass rings, becoming firmer underfoot as the visible surface faded. No one else would be able to follow it very far. They would find the ground softening into meadow.

For me, it would remain a road.

This would make me suspect that I was not merely a surveyor.

There would have been signs throughout my life. Transit pavilions opening a moment too early as I approached. Bridges refusing to shorten for me, as if they considered the full distance important. Houses recognizing my gait without offering an entrance. Six-legged grazers showing, in their reflective hides, not my recent position but an empty place where I was about to stand.

My shadow would usually belong to me. Usually.

I would have no clear memory of arriving in the city. My court would say I was found as a child asleep inside a dry channel after an unexpected wet cycle. There had been no footprints in the gray sediment. The water reader who raised me would insist that the current had been flowing both ways.

This would make me an unplaced resident, though I would be entered in household records and behave well enough that no one emphasized it.

I would be human. I would bruise, lose patience, prefer certain fruits, and dread the sudden years that sometimes arrive in a wet season. I would love people in particular rather than humanity in principle. I would know the voices of my court through walls and which neighbor needed company disguised as a practical task.

But I might also be something the city had made to answer a question it could not ask directly.

A pathfinder, perhaps—but not for people.

For the city itself.

The young ground would be full of possible arrangements. Beneath every road, another direction. Beneath every house, rooms not chosen. The eastern architecture might not be a second city but the accumulated shape of all the decisions this one had deferred: height, density, permanence, enclosure, ascent. Where the present city spread low and spacious, the other rose in wet colonnades. Where one revised itself gently, the other waited complete.

Perhaps I would be the narrow faculty by which the unfinished city could approach that possibility without becoming it.

If so, this would not feel grand. It would feel like fatigue in my feet, damp cuffs, and too many evenings spent waiting beside uncertain pavement.

My closest companion would be a continuity physician from an underground district, someone practical who distrusted metaphors because patients could become trapped in them. They would accompany me when I worked near the field, carrying fixed-wall anchors and food with strong familiar scents. We would argue about whether my experiences indicated temporal displacement, municipal adaptation, or ordinary obsession.

They would say, “A road is not calling you.”

I would say, “No. But it is making room.”

They would answer, “That is worse.”

We would be in love without having constructed a permanent definition for it. Our vow, if we made one, would be: *When one of us becomes difficult to locate, the other will begin with the ordinary places.*

Not the eastern field. Not the impossible pavilion. The kitchen. The market after rain. The bench beside the reservoir tree. The lane where our footsteps were known.

This would save me more than once.

Eventually, during a strong condensation event, the absent city would appear with unprecedented clarity. Water would outline its high windows and descending avenues. Crowds would gather at the permitted distance. Officials would stand in dry-gray clothing around the rings. Children would be kept home and would escape anyway.

The pale road behind my court would illuminate.

Not with the green border-light of wet vegetation, but with the diluted blue of the fissures. It would pass through the field and continue upward along one of the suspended stairs.

For the first time, others would see it too.

The Office would ask me to observe only. My companion would ask me not to go. My household would place a bowl for me beneath the table, though rain had not yet begun.

I would walk as far as the abandoned cistern.

Then farther.

At the edge of the first grass ring I would realize that the figures in the other city were not beckoning. Their raised hands were not invitations. They were touching walls from their side, reading for vibration, exactly as officials here placed palms against uncertain buildings.

They, too, would be negotiating with a city.

They, too, would be trying to determine what I was.

And on the suspended stair opposite me would stand another surveyor in clothing whose wet patterns climbed vertically. Their face would not be mine, but their posture would carry the same occupational patience. Between us, droplets would define a door with no wall around it.

Neither of us would open it.

That would be the important thing.

Instead, we would sit on opposite sides of the absent threshold.

I would unfold my small seat. They would do something equivalent, though from my angle it might resemble kneeling on the underside of a step. We would remain there while both cities watched. No contact, no crossing—only the establishment of a shared limit.

The road beneath me would relax.

Behind us, in each city, routes would begin to alter. Not merging, but acknowledging. Transit pavilions would acquire one new tone. Water in certain channels would carry conversations that truly existed, though elsewhere. Buildings would turn by a fraction of a degree away from the empty field, no longer straining toward it.

When the condensation fell, I would still be there.

I would walk home in the direction of the temporary river, even though that required circling the eastern districts twice. By the time I arrived, my room would have joined the kitchen directly for the first time in years.

Afterward, people might treat me as an authority on the other city. I would disappoint them. I would offer no theory of ancestors, futures, or unrealized arrangements. I would say only that something lived there, or lived as nearly there as we lived here, and that it had shown restraint.

This would become my real profession: not finding destinations, but preserving the distance that allows two places to remain themselves.

I would teach apprentices that connection is not always passage. That an unopened door may still be used for recognition. That cities, like people, can ruin what they love by eliminating every interval between them.

I would grow older unevenly. For decades my face might barely change; then one season the rain would bring silver into my hair and pale branching lines across my hands. I would stop walking the longest roads. Younger tenders would bring me vials of dust and ask what destinations the samples suggested.

My answer would usually be mundane.

A reservoir. A household forming. A district not yet built. A road that needs more travelers with simple errands.

At last I would live mostly in the empty lot granted to me beside the court, among waist-high leaves. The house would keep a doorway facing me but would not insist I enter. Children would visit and ask whether the eastern people were real.

I would ask whether they had checked their shadows.

They would groan and say yes.

Then I would tell them: “Real is not the same as reachable.”

When I died, I would not be given to the eastern field. That would make too neat a story, and this city would know better. I would be laid on bare young stone until my shadow settled. The road behind the court would be asked whether it wished to receive me.

Perhaps it would.

A shallow garden would be planted along its margin, carefully outside the shadow. During rain, color would wake in the leaves as thin intersecting lines, none quite touching. Travelers would rest there before deciding whether to continue.

The road would still run toward the horizon.

But it would no longer seem to be fleeing the city.

It would seem to be carrying the city’s attention outward—carefully, without trespass—into whatever had not yet agreed to exist.
