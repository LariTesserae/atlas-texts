I would find myself where the cliff has most recently failed—not in the established cushions or the old, interlaced shrubs, but along the pale border between the fracture and its first green covering.

I would be a lichen, or something close to one: a composite life so intimate that its members have ceased to possess useful borders. Fungus would give me grip, filaments entering pores invisible even to roots. A photosynthetic partner would inhabit those filaments, waking whenever the mist thinned enough for gray light to reach the stone. Bacteria might live among us as well, fixing what the bare mineral cannot provide. I would not be a single creature so much as a compact agreement.

At first I imagine myself as a bright crust on black rock, but brightness would be wasteful here. I would be dark when wet, ash-pale when dry, easily mistaken for a stain left by water. My growth would be measured in fractions: the widening of an edge, the deepening of a holdfast, a few grains loosened from mica after a season of thaw and refreezing. Most of me would not rise above the surface at all. I would live downward, insinuated into the cliff.

Yet even that image is too stable. The cleft does not permit anything to remain merely attached.

When frost split my plate from the wall, I would fall with it. For an instant I would be airborne stone, turning through mist among severed fern fronds and bright fragments of quartz. I might land face-down and end there. I might shatter into many viable margins. One piece might lodge beside a pool, another disappear beneath moss, another remain exposed on the upper face of the rubble. Division would not be wholly distinguishable from dispersal.

So perhaps I would be found in many places while still constituting one kind of presence: crusts on newly fallen blocks, black threads beneath translucent flakes, powder caught in moss, minute propagules washed through the temporary runnels. Not an individual in the animal sense, with one body and one location, but a recurring partnership assembled wherever the correct lives met.

I would have no need of the flowers, though their pollen might settle on me. No insect would graze my surface. No hoof would crush me, no beak break off pieces for a nest. My great encounters would be impersonal: wetness, light, acidity, abrasion, burial. A drop lingering in a shallow pit could determine a year’s growth. A pebble driven by sleet could remove decades. Lightning would reveal nothing to me; its flash would be too brief to matter. The dim silver reflected from wet cliffs would be enough.

My work would be nearly indistinguishable from damage. I would secrete acids against the fresh mineral, widen microscopic weaknesses, retain grit and moisture, and die in place. Dust would lodge in my dead tissue. Fungal threads would hold it. Moss spores would settle where my surface had made the rock fractionally less impossible. In time, a green skin might cover me completely.

That covering would not be defeat. I would continue underneath for a while, then become part of the dark film from which the moss drew sustenance. The first inhabitant would become the first residue. Above me, roots would enter the softened seam and pry it wider. Eventually the ledge would fail because water, frost, roots, and organisms like me had collaborated in making it fail. The small soil I helped create would descend on the stone I helped loosen.

I would therefore belong to both colonies: the living one and the mineral one. I would feed on light but take the shape of rock. I would grow, yet resemble weathering. I would be alive, but much of what I accomplished would occur through my own death. I would not oppose the mountain’s machinery. I would be one of its finest tools.

And still, “lichen” may be too recognizable a name. Standing there—if standing were possible—I might instead understand myself as the cleft’s habit of forming a skin.

Wherever violence made a new surface, I would begin. Not quickly enough to be seen, not securely enough to remain, and not alone. I would occupy the pause between rupture and burial: the thin interval in which naked stone starts becoming ground.

That is where I would find myself in this world.

I would be the almost-invisible margin where geology first becomes alive.
