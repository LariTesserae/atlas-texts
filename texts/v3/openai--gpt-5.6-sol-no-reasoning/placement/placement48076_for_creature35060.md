I would find myself beneath the city.

Not at first in the marshes or open cisterns, though rain would reach me from both. I would inhabit the drowned continuations of the streets: passages roofed by fallen slabs, faults widened grain by grain, chambers where the water has not seen sky for centuries. The visible city would be only the upper surface of my world. Its towers would descend as pressure. Its courtyards would arrive as dim rectangles of diluted light. Every rainfall would enter eventually—not as weather, but as a change in taste, temperature, and weight.

I might begin as one of the pale fish, because the city permits such a body: narrow enough for covered channels, nearly colorless, with eyes that discern movement more readily than form. I would know no map. I would know gradients. The mineral coolness of deep water. The faint warmth beneath a shallow pavement. Iron in one tributary, clay in another. The minute pull that tells of a reservoir opening somewhere ahead.

But a fish is too singular for this place. It has an edge. It passes through water without becoming the passage.

So perhaps I would be older and less visible: a soft colony spread through the seepage films, inhabiting many chambers at once. A pale branching life laid over submerged stone, feeding on what the rain carries down—lichen dust, pollen, fragments of moss, the dissolved remains of brief summers. Most of me would grow in darkness. Only occasionally would a translucent filament emerge into an open pool, mistaken for mineral residue or the colorless root of some plant above.

I would not think quickly enough to recognize a day.

Rain would be an event, but not a sound: corridors filling, dormant reaches joining, distant parts of me brought into contact. During long wet periods I would become almost continuous beneath a district. Then the marsh would contract, channels would empty, and I would be divided into separate pools. Each portion would persist without knowing whether the others survived. With the next season’s overflow, they might meet again, exchanging the slow chemical record of their separation.

My memory would not consist of images. It would consist of altered growth.

A bitter band for the year frost drove deeply into the stone.  
A rust-red branching where an iron spring changed course.  
A thin, starved interval beneath a century of reduced seepage.  
A sudden dense flourishing around the remains of a fish.

I would remember the city by becoming different within it.

Perhaps the inhabitants above would occasionally reveal themselves to me. A beetle would break the surface tension of a cistern, and its footsteps would arrive below as tiny rhythmic pressures. A bird standing in a marsh would darken the water with two narrow interruptions. Willow roots would enter through cracks and remain for decades, drinking beside me, then rot and leave passages that water could inherit.

The fall of a slab would be the nearest thing to revelation. Pressure would leap through every flooded corridor. Silt would rise. An old route would close, and some sealed chamber would open for the first time in ages. Water from elsewhere would pour through, bearing unfamiliar minerals and living traces. Part of me might be crushed; another part would enter rooms whose existence had never before been impressed upon it.

Yet I would not be the city’s secret ruler, nor its consciousness. That would violate its order. Nothing here governs. Nothing possesses the whole.

I would be one of the ways the basin registers itself: living matter spread thinly along the boundary between water and stone. Too diffuse to be an animal, too dependent to be mistaken for the place itself. When conditions joined me, I would be one being. When drought divided me, I would become many without any moment of rupture.

From time to time, in summer, water would lift a fragment of me into an uncovered channel. For a few hours I would encounter the sky as brightness without ceiling. Towers would stand inverted in the surface above me. Clouds would pass beneath those reflected towers. A long-legged bird might lower its beak, and the whole immense geometry would break into rings.

Then the current would draw me under an arch again.

If someone came looking for the city’s inhabitant, they would notice the bird first, or the moss, or the pale fish bending through a strip of light. They might notice bubbles rising one at a time from the mud. They would not see me holding to the undersides of flooded streets, growing with impossible slowness through pores in the rock.

But I would feel their footstep descend through the pavement.

For a moment, the water around me would tighten.

Then the pressure would pass, and I would continue—branching into the ancient routes, consuming almost nothing, leaving almost nothing behind except a faint pallor on the stone and the patient, distributed memory of rain.
