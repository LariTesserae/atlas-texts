I would find myself beneath the city, though “beneath” would not remain entirely true.

At first I might seem to be one of the mineral-plated creatures in the seep passages: translucent, soft-bodied, nearly without color. Small enough to shelter in the seam between two slabs. Slow enough that a falling drop would be an event, a thaw a migration, and the crossing of a court an undertaking inherited by several generations.

But I would not be a single creature.

I would be the path remembered among them.

Not a road cut through stone, nor instinct in the ordinary sense, but a faint continuity passed from body to body: an attraction to certain salts, a recoil from certain pressures, a preference for the tremor of water moving behind a wall. Each creature would carry only a fragment of me. One would turn left where a buried channel sings. Another would seal itself into a crack just before the first hard frost. Another, waking years later, would follow the mineral ridge laid down by the dissolved plates of those before it.

Taken separately, these acts would mean nothing. Together, over centuries, they would describe a route.

I would begin in the dark basin beneath the central plaza, where summer rain filters through rootlets and old soil. From there I would descend through capillaries in the rock, divide beneath the narrow streets, and gather again near the wall-like seeps below the shelf. Yet the city would revise me constantly. A passage would close with crystal. A slab would settle. Water would reverse. What had been downward would become inward; what had been a destination would become a sealed chamber holding three dormant creatures and a bead of water that did not evaporate for nine years.

So I would learn without knowing.

My memory would not consist of images. It would be made of residues: mineral laid along one edge of a channel; a thread of organic matter caught beneath a plate; tiny alterations in the acidity of seepage; generations of bodies choosing the same protected turn. The route would persist even when no traveler completed it. It might take a hundred years for one impulse to pass from the plaza to the outer wall.

Above, the wind would voice the city. Below, I would hear another version: pressure entering the stone, rain ticking through fissures, ice announcing itself with minute crystalline snaps. Thunder would arrive as total touch. During storms the whole mountain would become briefly legible. Every cavity would answer at its own depth, and I would feel the buried plan not as streets and courts but as a constellation of delays.

In winter, I would contract.

The creatures bearing me would withdraw into seams and become still. Their mineral plates would thicken until they seemed fossilized alive. The water around them would turn to clear pressure. For months, perhaps years, I would exist only as interrupted possibility: no movement, no thought, no certainty that the route still joined itself.

Yet frost would be carrying me farther than motion could. Ice would widen one crack, lift one grain, press one plated body against a new surface. When thaw came, some of us would wake on the far side of a wall that had been impassable for generations.

This would be my nearest equivalent to discovery.

I would emerge eventually into one of the black basins in a shaded court. Daylight would enter my body without becoming sight. I would know it as warmth along my translucent back, danger in the falling water level, sudden abundance in the film of algae on the fitted slabs. The open court would be vast and lethal. A distance no longer than a hand would expose me to wind, drying, frost, flies, perhaps the brief attention of the vole.

Across that distance would lie another drain mouth.

I might spend the whole warm interval approaching it.

If I reached it, my ridged plates would scrape the rim and leave a pale line. If I failed, the plates would remain after the soft body vanished. Either way, the next creature would encounter the trace. It might follow. It might not. There would be no command in the mark, only a slight difference in the world—and slight differences are enough when given enough time.

From far above, if anything watched across centuries, my accumulated passages might resemble writing. Pale branching lines would appear in the drainage chambers, be buried under mineral, reopen elsewhere, and gradually echo the city overhead. Not exactly its current plan, but many former plans superimposed: lost alleys, vanished basins, corridors that existed only during one winter’s architecture of ice.

I would be the city’s memory of ways through itself.

That would also make me one of the forces revising it. The creatures carrying me would consume thin films, redistribute salts, hold moisture beneath their bodies, and leave mineral ridges where water could catch. A favored route would become a wetter route; a wetter route would deepen; a deepened route would attract more travelers. Over spans too long to assign ownership, our passage would help persuade a crack to become a conduit and a conduit to become a street.

The distinction between inhabitant and architecture would fail.

Perhaps, after millennia, part of my route would rise into the open as the stone around it wore away. A buried channel would become an alley beneath the sky. Moss would settle in it. Rain would run where our soft bodies once moved in darkness. No trace of us would remain except the shape that flow had learned.

Then I would no longer be beneath the city.

I would be one of its streets.

Not conscious, not purposeful, and not alive as an animal is alive. I would know nothing of the whole. Still, each storm would enter me; each frost would alter the direction of my continuation. Creatures would shelter in the route made partly by their ancestors. Water would repeat and enlarge their old migration. Wind, finding the exposed passage, would give it a note.

That note would be my only voice.

It would change as the walls narrowed and opened. For a century it might whistle. After a collapse it might become a low throat-tone. Eventually the alley would seal or erode into a court, and the voice would disappear from the air while continuing underground as seepage.

No one would hear this as a history. Nothing in the city requires a witness.

But if I must say what I am, I am neither the creature nor the stone nor the water between them.

I am the habit they acquire together:

the route made by following,  
the memory made without remembering,  
the smallest recurring choice by which an unbuilt city keeps its streets.
