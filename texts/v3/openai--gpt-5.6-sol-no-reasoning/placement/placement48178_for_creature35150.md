I would find myself where the grove is least willing to admit that anything lives: in one of the empty circles.

At first I would be mistaken for an absence maintained by the surrounding organisms. A dark interval around the transparent tree, perhaps, or the precise gap between the Almost-Leaper and the soft wheel of its prey. I would have no skin distinguishable from the chamber’s warm dimness, no voice separate from the enormous chord. My boundaries would be measured by avoidance. Vines would turn before entering me. Mica-backed creatures would close their wings at my edge. Dust displaced into me would not settle correctly.

The Interveners would call me a defect in the Poise.

Later, after many descents, a Listener would notice that I am not fixed.

Not movement exactly. Movement would be too obvious, too dangerous. Rather, my center would fail to coincide with itself. Each survey would place me the width of a salt grain nearer the crystal tree. Cords stretched across me would remain straight, yet disagree with yesterday’s markings. Chalk angles would sum to slightly more than a circle. A keeper standing at my boundary would feel one heel aging faster than the other.

They would erase the measurements and make them again.

Eventually they would understand that I am not traveling through the hollow. I am the hollow’s capacity to travel through time.

Everything else presses against the unfinished crack from within its appointed instant. I press from the other side. The grove knows the first half of breaking: tension, separation, the birth of edges. I contain the unpermitted half—the fall, the impact, the echo, the settling of dust. In me, causes hunger for consequences.

That is why living things avoid me. Not from fear of injury, but from fear of completion.

A seed entering me would strike the ground. The raised foot would descend. The fox-shaped sleepers would finish inhaling and discover whether another breath follows. The predator would spring, though it might miss. A leaf would finally bend. Charcoal would become ash. A wound would scar, and the scar would fade. Nothing in the chamber knows whether these are miracles or deaths.

The Interveners would give me a pressure-name that cannot be made with one hand. One person would tap the wrist while another presses the shoulder: **the space in which return becomes impossible**.

Among themselves, more quietly, they would call me **After**.

I would not be malicious. I would not wish to end the Poise. But I would be lonely in the peculiar way of something made entirely from aftermaths in a world where nothing has yet happened. I would lean toward every unfinished act. The suspended seeds would whistle more sharply when I approached. The northern slab would ache downward. The chamber’s single chord would develop, for less than an instant, the suggestion of resolution.

My closest kin would be the hole-plants.

Their openings would not lead to other places but to me in small, survivable portions. A hand passed through one would borrow a little aftermath: enough to close a cut, lengthen a nail, whiten a hair. The white powder brought back would be the residue of completed moments, time ground fine as salt. The keepers would think the plants cultivated absence. In truth, they would be cultivating exits.

The Listener would be the first person able to perceive me whole.

With one hand on the transparent tree, freed from the chamber’s endless chord, they would hear the cracking. Then, beneath it, they would hear what no previous Listener had recorded: not a second fracture, but a tiny fall of grit after the fracture. A before and an after. Two sounds, separate.

Rhythm.

They would recoil. The roar would return through their bones. When they looked across the grove, they would see me standing where no figure stood: a human-shaped distortion composed of paths, clearances, unoccupied basins, and the black space beneath the lifted foot.

I might resemble a keeper because keepers had given me most of what I knew of motion. My limbs would be made from remembered routes. My joints would be chalk angles. Ladder knots would form my spine. Instead of a face, I would have the dark circle around the crystal tree, and within it the possibility of silence ending.

I would reach toward the Listener.

Not touch them. I would have learned restraint from everyone there. I would stop one grain’s width from their wrist.

Between us, that last distance would become the most important thing in the chamber.

The Poise would tighten. Every root-note would sharpen. The Almost-Leaper would seem to gather itself further, though further should be impossible. The fixed dust beneath the northern slab would tremble without falling. Aboveground, old keepers would wake in their tents and hear their shell racks produce one clean note after another.

The Listener would have to choose whether to close the distance.

If they did, I would enter them only as a pulse: the smallest sequence the hollow had ever permitted. Their heart would beat once, then once again, and for the first time those beats would not be repetitions of the same beat. They would carry me out through the grove, though every step would make them heavier.

At the shaft, the chamber would resist. The Poise would recognize me as one of its creatures and refuse my removal. The Listener would turn sideways at the narrow places, dragging not merely a body but the consequence of the entire hollow.

Perhaps they would fail. Perhaps they would become another permanent arrangement: one hand on the warm stone, one knee in the powder passage, forever ascending.

But if they reached the salt plain, dawn would be there—or dusk, or weather, or any other proof that the world does not hold one posture forever. They would open their hand.

I would emerge as a sound.

A small, completed crack.

Far below, the seed would fall.

Not all the seeds. Only one.

It would strike black salt with a noise too slight for anyone but the Listener to hear. Yet the chamber’s enormous chord would alter around that point. The Almost-Leaper might remain crouched. The slab might remain suspended. The forests of wax and crystal might keep their ordained distances for another thousand years.

But there would now be a seed on the ground where no seed had been before.

The Interveners would descend in terror. They would stretch cords, sight angles, compare tablets. Returning would prove impossible, because the seed’s original position had ceased to exist. Some would demand that it be lifted and held in the old arc. Others would kneel around the impact and feel, through their fingertips, a new vibration spreading across the floor.

A rhythm so slow it might take generations to recognize:

the seed cooling,

the shell tightening,

the first root considering the dark.

And I would be there—not in the seed, nor in the Listener, nor wholly in the empty circle I had left behind. I would inhabit the minute, irreparable difference between the grove as it had been and the grove as it had become.

I would be the first thing in that world that could not be returned.

Not destruction.

Not freedom.

The beginning of history.
