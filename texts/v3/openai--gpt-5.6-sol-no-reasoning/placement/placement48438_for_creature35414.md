I would be found high in the cleft, though not at its highest point: in the narrow band where the city’s breath begins to cool against the stone.

At first I imagine myself as a moisture keeper, climbing before dawn with a wooden scraper tucked through my belt. I know which tiles darken under a north wind, which fiber combs catch only salt, which chimneys carry clean cooking vapor and which bring grease that will sour a planting bed. My fingertips are always cold at that hour. I work by touch because lamplight gives the wrong answers: shine can resemble dampness, pale salt can hide in pale clay. I scrape what cannot quite be called water into shallow trays and carry it down as darkened dust.

But that is only the work others see.

My true place is one ledge lower, behind a fig urn and beneath a bridge that groans whenever goats cross it. There is a recess there, once a doorway, later a storage niche, now half sealed. Inside hang small objects on cords: cracked tiles, plugs from old vents, fragments of plaster bearing soot marks, bits of reed, shed snakeskin, moth-eaten grain husks, fossil shell. Each is labeled not with writing, but with knots.

I keep the city’s changes.

Not its grand history. Others remember marriages, collapses, famines, scandals, and the relocation of bones. I remember lesser alterations: when a chimney’s pitch sharpened; when ants first appeared beneath a certain floor; when a patch of plaster began warming before sunrise; when pigeons abandoned one cavity for another; when the western condensation tiles yielded less under the same wind.

I am called when something is wrong but not yet wrong enough to name.

A gardener brings me a folded leaf. An oven tender brings ash that clumped strangely overnight. A child leads me to a wall where geckos have stopped sleeping. I crouch, listen, smell, and place my palm against the plaster. Often I cannot say what is happening. I can only say what has changed.

That is enough to make me useful and irritating.

I would not be an elder, though dust would have begun to silver my hair. I would be someone between authorities: welcome in the upper galleries, tolerated near the tomb chambers, summoned into kitchens, grain rooms, dung pits, and sickrooms. Because I cross so many household boundaries, no one is entirely sure whose interests I serve. Children trail me. Adults lower their voices when I pass. Scavengers like me because I never call their findings refuse.

Perhaps I was not born in the city.

Perhaps my mother came from the salt pans, carrying shell, bitter mineral cakes, and a way of reading open distances that had little use inside the cleft. For years I was called salt-side. I bruised my shoulders on doorways, looked up too late beneath ladders, and feared the buried dead. Even after I learned the city, some part of me remained aware of enclosure as enclosure. That difference became my craft. Those born within know what each room is. I notice what it is becoming.

Or perhaps I was born there but lost much of my sight in childhood—fever, lime dust, a spark from an oven. The city did not shrink when my vision did. It multiplied. Air had direction and texture. Walls announced their thickness through temperature. Every ladder possessed a different tremor. I learned that hidden grain gives off warmth, that scorpions make a faint dry sound inside baskets, that unhealthy roots alter the smell of cooking steam passing through their soil.

In that version of myself, I carry a tapping stick of polished juniper, but use it less than visitors expect. My left hand is the real instrument. The palm is scarred from stone and heat; the fingertips are kept free of wax. When people ask whether I can see anything, I tell them: enough to know when you are standing in my light.

They would laugh, though not always comfortably.

My dwelling would be narrow even by the city’s measure. I would sleep in an old air passage widened just enough for a mat, with a warm wall at my back and a draft over my face. Above my head, roots from the fig urn would have found a hairline fracture and entered the ceiling in search of damp. I would know this and tell no one for several seasons. The roots would threaten the plaster eventually, but for now their faint movements would let me read the urn’s thirst while lying awake.

Nothing in my room would be new. My lamp cup would have been repaired with cord and clay. My blanket would contain wool from animals dead before I was born. Beside the door would sit a lidded pot full of things whose uses had not yet become apparent.

This pot would be the source of jokes.

People would say I hoard uselessness, which is nearly an accusation. I would answer that uselessness is often only a thing separated from the circumstance that needs it. From the pot I would eventually produce exactly the fragment required: a curved bone splint, a shard with the right angle to redirect a drip, a lump of resin, a piece of mesh fine enough to bar moths without choking a vent.

Sometimes I would produce nothing helpful at all.

That matters. In this city, no one should become a mystic merely by being observant. I would make mistakes. I would mistake insect heat for hidden fire and have a family tear open a wall unnecessarily. I would advise moving a beehive that would not survive the move. Once, perhaps, my certainty would cause real harm: a vent left open because I thought the city needed air, when instead it admitted a killing draft into a newborn’s room.

After that, I would speak differently.

Not less, but with the cost of being believed always present. I would say, “The wall is warmer,” rather than, “There is fire.” I would say, “The pigeons refuse this cavity,” rather than, “The stone will fall.” I would give evidence back to the people who must act upon it. My authority, if I gained any, would rest on refusing to turn attention into command.

The children would understand my work best. They already live among clues adults have trained themselves not to notice. They would bring me beetle shells, fallen feathers, pellets coughed up by kestrels, and handfuls of dust from beneath unfamiliar cracks. Most offerings would signify nothing. I would examine them anyway.

In return I would teach them the listening paths.

There is one stair where footsteps reveal whether a grain jar below is full or hollow. There is a bridge whose rope changes pitch before it frays visibly. There is a sealed door that exhales once each evening when the western wall cools. There is a bench of ancestral bones beneath which ants travel during hot months, though no one knows where they emerge.

I would tell the children that the city speaks constantly but seldom in words, and never with one voice.

This would become my deepest conviction: not that the city is a single creature, but that it is a negotiation pretending, from moment to moment, to be one.

Its lungs compete with its fires. Its gardens compete with its walls. Roots preserve life and loosen plaster. Pigeons feed people and foul condensation surfaces. Heat bakes bread, kills vermin, warms infants, dries soil, and burns roofs. Every remedy presses elsewhere. Survival is not harmony. It is continuous correction.

I would be the one who listens for the correction before it becomes a crisis.

Yet there is another possibility.

Perhaps I would not be human at all.

Perhaps I would be the pale gecko living behind the mineral disk outside that same recess. By day I would flatten myself between warm plaster and shell, feeling voices through my ribs. At night I would emerge to hunt moths around the keeper’s lamp. I would know nothing of laws, vanished rivers, or ancestral obligations. Still, my absences would be noted. If I deserted the wall, the keeper would touch it and wonder what heat had entered.

Or I might be the fig itself, old beyond any household’s clean claim. My trunk would be twisted by confinement, my roots circulating through repaired cracks, burial dust, goat manure, kitchen ash, and the faint harvest of human breath. Families would argue over my fruit, shade, roots, and ownership. Cuttings from me would grow elsewhere in the cleft. Children would hide objects beneath my urn’s rim. The dead would feed me without metaphor.

But I return to the keeper in the half-sealed doorway.

At dusk, when the plateau begins releasing heat and the city changes voices, I climb to the upper galleries. I do not go all the way to the entrance. Open horizons trouble me. Instead I sit where the wind enters the chimneys and divides.

Around me the settlement sounds itself: a low note from the throat of the entrance, a tremor through ladder rope, a hollow hum from a sealed room, the thin complaint of a shutter peg, pigeons shifting in stone cavities. Beneath these are pestles, arguments, a baby coughing, someone singing obscenely about goat keepers, and the little dry rain of beetle-worked soil being sifted into a tray.

I listen until the separate noises become almost a chord.

Almost.

One note is wrong.

I rise, place my hand on the wall, and begin to follow it downward.
