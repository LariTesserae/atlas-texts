I would be found where the city has just ceased to be accurate.

Not in the crowded wards, though I would know their floor-grains by heart. Not beyond the clean doors, though for much of my life others would suspect I had come through one. I would live along the temporary margins: beside a newly diverted road, beneath a mirror’s discarded angle, in masonry still showing the pale outlines of the district that occupied it before us.

I think I would begin as a child of a carried object rather than a fixed household.

Our object would be a door slab, narrow and blue-gray, veined with a mineral that held warmth longer than the surrounding stone. It would have been repaired along one edge with six bone clamps and bored with more pivot sockets than any single doorway required. Every migration added another socket. Every generation hung it differently. In some dwellings it would turn; in others it would slide, lie flat across a cistern, become a butcher’s table, or stand upright as a shield against falling salt.

My public name would change several times. As a child I might be **Sleeps-Behind-Blue**, then **Finds-the-Old-Road**, and later something less flattering, given after I failed to leave a condemned quarter when ordered.

My private name would remain a breath no one outside the door-kin knew.

I would have been born during a local rain, but not beneath it. The courtyard beyond our membrane would have filled violently while our roof remained dry. My first remembered world would be the luminous rods of falling water a few paces away, each drop white in reflected light, ending at a boundary so exact that I could put one hand into rain and keep the other powdered with dust.

Perhaps that would shape me: the certainty that neighboring places can obey different conditions.

At first I would train as a listener.

I would lie with my cheek against stone while older listeners placed bowls around me: milk in one, oil in another, shell beads in a third. They would make hidden disturbances—draw a cord through a crack, pour water into a buried channel, walk a herd animal beyond a wall—and require me to separate them. I would learn that water thickens a vibration, that a roof preparing to shed itself produces not a single warning but a crowded hesitation, and that distant hammering can resemble footsteps if one wants footsteps badly enough.

I would be competent, though not gifted in the respected way. I would miss obvious fractures and notice sounds no one could verify.

Three taps beneath an empty road.

A sledge-runner’s scrape under bedrock too thick to contain a gallery.

The small creak of something bearing weight inside walls that had not yet been built.

My teachers would accuse me of listening for meaning rather than danger. They would be correct.

So I would become a surveyor of obsolete places: one of those sent ahead when a district must move, or after the river withdraws, or when a ceiling fall exposes ground that has not been walked in living memory. I would carry clay tiles, chalk plugs, measuring cord, a shallow bead bowl, and a hammer wrapped so completely in fungal cloth that its testing blows sounded like a pulse heard through a body.

My work would be to say where people could live-there-while-it-holds.

I would map conditions rather than distances. Here the floor sweats without rain. Here the salt answers late. Here blind herd animals turn aside. Here the ceiling is stable except during full brilliance. Here voices return before they are made—or seem to.

Officially, I would mark such a place “uncertain echo.”

Privately, I would mark it with the old number of our blue door.

Over time I would become recognizable by the clay fragments tied at my belt. Most mapmakers broke obsolete tiles and returned the clay to workshops. I would keep mine. Their edges would clatter softly when I walked, an offensive noise in a city that valued quiet. Fitted together, they would not depict the basin as it was at any one time. They would show vanished roads crossing present cisterns, future cracks inferred from tremors, elder-song paths, shell migrations, river incursions, and the locations of clean walls.

The result would look less like a city than the anatomy of an animal repeatedly opening and closing its body.

People would come to me for routes that officially did not exist.

Thieves would ask for passages between store courts. Water keepers would ask which sealed cellars once drained. Households separated by collapse would ask whether two warm faults might be the same fault. Returners preparing to enter a clean door would ask where earlier returners had emerged.

I would accept payment in numbering clay, old cord, and permission to touch household objects with long repair histories. I would press my fingertips to their seams and copy each sequence of marks.

Not because I believed the objects remembered.

Because I would begin to suspect that something remembered through them.

The suspicion would start with repetitions. A construction number found behind a clean door would match the repair marks on a jar still unbroken. A floor plan uncovered after a salt fall would contain a recess exactly fitted to a copper hook owned across the basin. A returner would describe a plaza where every household object had been abandoned except the doors.

Then our own blue slab would begin to warm.

At first only during brilliance. Then during condensation. Then without any shared condition at all.

Shell creatures would gather around its lowest edge while dormant. Flat scavengers would wait before it at hours when no one intended to open it. A herd animal, passing our house, would kneel and press its face to the stone. My family would dismantle the dwelling, fearing a warm fault, but the bare floor beneath would be cool.

The warmth would travel with the door.

That would make it kin’s business rather than civic business, and therefore more dangerous.

Some would want to surrender it to a council. Some would want to break it, though breaking a lineage object would be nearly unthinkable. An elder would sing an obsolete map naming a “blue closure” in a district buried generations ago. The description would include seven pivot sockets.

Our slab would have six.

I would understand, or believe I understood, what had not happened yet.

After that I would cease to be merely a surveyor. I would become a keeper of adjacencies: the person called when two things appeared next to each other that should have been separated by stone, time, or causality.

I would sit outside newly revealed doors and watch whether they breathed. I would lay sole-wraps across thresholds to see which side gathered condensation. I would set bowls against warm walls and count the ripples. I would compare the taps beneath them to builders’ circle rhythms, lovers’ private signals, animal guidance clicks, and the coded flashes of mirror crews.

What answered would never be language long enough to prove itself.

That would be essential. Certainty would make me either priest or fraud, and I would be neither. I would remain a municipal nuisance: useful often enough that councils tolerated me, wrong often enough that no guild claimed me.

Eventually the ground beneath a dry plaza would give a slow vibration. No one else would feel it. The listeners’ bowls would remain still. Yet the shell swarms would arrange themselves around an empty rectangle, leaving its interior bare.

I would raise two fingers.

The market would halt.

For hours nothing would happen. Traders would curse me softly. A water keeper would demand the road reopened. The council would prepare to dismiss me.

Then a line would appear in the floor—not a crack opening, but dust withdrawing into a seam already present. Four edges would define themselves. Stone would descend without grinding. Warm air would rise.

Below would be a room of clean walls.

In its center would stand a door frame.

It would be the exact size of our blue slab.

My family would bring the door, not because I asked but because carried things decide kinship, and by then the slab would have become too warm to hold without cloth. We would lower it into the waiting frame. Six sockets would fail to align.

Then, as the weight settled, a seventh socket would become visible along the repaired edge, beneath the oldest bone clamp.

No one would know whether it had been hidden there for generations or formed at that moment.

The door would turn.

Beyond it I would not find the older population, nor the future city, nor blind cultivators of heat. Not at first. I would find another basin district, empty and brightly lit. Its roads would be familiar but incorrectly joined. A market stair would lead into a slaughter court that lay half a day away in our city. Rain would fall upward along one wall and disappear into the luminous stone above. Numbered blocks would be stacked in household groups, awaiting removal.

Among them I would find fragments of my maps.

Not copies: the same tiles, aged beyond recognition, including pieces I still carried at my belt.

I would not go far. The city teaches controlled fear, and survival often consists of refusing the grand gesture. I would tie no cord around my ankle; by then I would know cords were useful only where distance behaved. Instead, I would remove one sole-wrap and leave it across the threshold, half on each side.

On our side it would remain cool.

On the other it would slowly gather the warmth of a living body.

That is where I would finally become what I was.

Not a returner, because I would not fully enter. Not a listener, because hearing would be only one part of the task. Not a door keeper, because doors do not need guarding from those who understand them, and guards are useless against those who do not.

I would become a **witness of the interval**.

I would live beside that threshold while districts formed and dissolved around it. Councils would relocate; markets would use the plaza, abandon it, return. Mirror crews would bargain for the angles needed to illuminate the room beyond. Water keepers would test its condensation and declare it potable one week, poisonous the next. Children would dare each other to touch the warm frame. Lovers would exchange promises there because the doorway made every vow visibly conditional.

My work would be to record what crossed without pretending crossing was simple.

Dust would enter and not settle.

Shell creatures would emerge in varieties known only from the black river.

A household bowl placed just beyond the threshold would return with a repair no living potter remembered making.

Sometimes an object would disappear from the city, only to be found waiting inside. Sometimes an object taken from the room would vanish years later, at the instant its owner most needed it.

And sometimes, when no one stood near, the blue door would open a finger’s width.

Warmth would breathe through.

From the other side would come the tightening of rope fibers.

I would grow old there. My certainty would become suspect, as all old certainty should. Younger surveyors would accuse me of confusing patterns with intentions. I would tell them they were right and make them take measurements anyway.

Eventually they would ask whether something lived in the rooms beyond.

I would say yes, but not in the form they meant.

I would tell them the inhabitants were arrangements.

A cup beside a hand. A wall around sleep. A channel waiting for water. A numbered block awaiting the family that will carry it. A door fitted to generations of repair. Perhaps the clean rooms were not kept by hidden people. Perhaps readiness itself had become a kind of life below the basin: the persistent preparation of shelter for whoever survived long enough to need it.

They would consider this an elder’s useless answer.

Later, it might become directions.

When my body failed, I would not be fed to my household’s usual growth chamber. Unknown warmth would have entered my bones after so many years beside the opening, and the cultivators would refuse me. My kin would place me in a dry hollow for watching.

But I think the hollow would not remain dry.

Condensation would gather only there. Shell creatures would encircle me. The old blue door, wherever it had been carried by then, would warm once and become cool forever.

My numbered map tiles would be distributed among surveyors. For generations, pieces of my impossible city would hang from different belts, carried into districts I had never walked. When brought near certain buried walls, their broken edges would tremble.

And far beyond the inhabited zones, beneath the brightest ceiling, someone would eventually find one tile fitted into a clean floor.

On its underside would be my private name.

Not written, because no one had ever written it.

Breathed into the clay while it was still soft.
