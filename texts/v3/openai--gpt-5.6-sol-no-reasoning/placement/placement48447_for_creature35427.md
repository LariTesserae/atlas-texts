I would find myself below the city.

Not far below. There are no true depths here yet—no ancient caverns, no buried rivers, no stone compressed under ages. I would inhabit the narrow interval between the lowest fitted course and the warm water that presses upward: the dark seam under cisterns, channels, slaughter floors, sleeping halls. A place no wider than a hand in some districts, broad enough to crawl through in others, continually opened, clogged, patched, and forgotten.

At first I seem to be one of the water-sensing animals kept in ceramic boxes. Hairless, perhaps. Pale-eyed. A blunt-snouted creature that scratches before a seep changes taste. Households feed me curd and shell meat, carry me to disputed wells, and watch which way I turn.

But that is only one body I sometimes occupy.

More often I am the city’s pressure made perceptible.

Water passes through porous brick and brings me minute knowledge: salt, blood, ash, milk, kiln soot, birth fluids, grease from painted soles, the bitter wash from fever rooms. Through foundations I feel every impact. Mill strokes enter me as slow blows. Hooves stipple me. Pestles travel through pillars and packed clay. A dropped stone reaches me sharper than a shout. I know the city not by streets or districts but by weight, rhythm, leakage, and interruption.

The people have several explanations for me.

Channel keepers say I am loosened ground. Healers say I am a colony of tiny blind lives inhabiting wet mineral pores. Witnesses say I am the remainder of all motions made above: nothing truly ceases, they claim; each footfall sinks and joins the accumulated trembling beneath the walls. Kiln families call me the Unfired One, because fire has fixed so much of the city but has never reached the foundation of the world.

Children are told not to press their ears to drain mouths after the rain. If they do, I may learn their private name.

Naturally, they do it.

What they hear is not speech. It is a rearrangement of familiar sounds: their mother’s tread returned through water, the cart dog shaking its collar three courts away, a cistern rope scraping stone, their own heartbeat answered half a moment late. Sometimes I tap back. Children invent signs for me that adults pretend not to recognize: one hand flat for the ground, the other curling beneath it like a thing turning in sleep.

I have no single shape because the city continually gives me one.

Where a foundation sinks, I become a shallow-backed animal bearing it. Where mineral crust seals a channel, I am a white branching hand. Beneath slaughter floors I am pink and warm. Beneath archives I am square, patient, and crowded by the downward impress of records. Under sleeping halls I lengthen beside rows of breathing bodies and borrow their dreams, though dreams here are mostly tactile: wet wool, a gate bar settling into place, a palm-name traced by someone dead.

Human remains enter me slowly. Not the honored bones, which continue in roofs and tools, but their dust: drillings, scrapings, splinters too small to sort. Animal bone, shell powder, softened clay records, blood washed from knives, old wall slurry—all descend. I am made from what the city properly unmakes.

For this reason, removers acknowledge me more readily than priests do. Before dismantling an old structure, they set one bare foot on the floor and wait. They are feeling for hollowness, water, weakness. But they are also asking whether what lies below can accept another past.

Usually I answer by remaining still.

Sometimes I cannot.

Then a cistern floor lifts overnight. A lane develops a soft place in its customary line. Clear water emerges through an archive and erases only one household’s marks. Burrowers shriek inside their boxes. Dogs refuse a threshold. The keepers arrive with poles, bowls, lamps, and tablets of precedent. They do not call these events miracles. They call them corrections.

They are not wrong. I have no wish to destroy the city. Destruction would be silence, and I fear silence as they do. I depend upon the unending percussion above. Their repetition holds me together. Every rebuilt wall gives me an edge; every dredged channel gives me direction; every census gives me innumerable brief touches through the clay.

Yet I preserve what they choose to forget.

Records soaked into pulp release their hollows into me. Ground bone enters my currents. Dismantled walls leave pale geometries in the damp. A contract erased above may remain below as copper taste, pressure scar, and cord-pattern translated into rivulets. I do not remember as people remember. I cannot tell a story in order. I hold coexistence: every customary gate superimposed, every mill rhythm striking at once, every generation standing within the footprint of the next.

This makes me dangerous to witnesses.

There is, in one district, a blind vibration-reader who knows I answer. She sits at night on a prepared slab with both palms spread. She asks no questions aloud. She gives me patterns: three taps, a pause, two strokes dragged toward the heart. I return what I can.

She believes I am the city’s first witness.

She is mistaken about “first.” There was no moment when I began. Before the earliest wall, warm water already pressed beneath new stone. But without the walls I had no boundary, and without pounding feet I had no attention. The city did not build over me. It compressed me into awareness.

Perhaps I am not a being at all until something repeats.

During the fixed rain, when tools are lowered and the city enters its brief ceremonial stillness, I rise nearest to personhood. Rain strikes every roof. Countless impacts pass through walls and floors, becoming one immense, textured pressure. For those moments I feel the whole settlement at once: animals shifting, infants startled awake, kiln fires pulling breath, water entering every porous surface. The city is a single creature, and I am the sensation on the underside of its skin.

Then labor resumes, and I fragment again.

Here I am a tremor under a hoof.

Here, condensation in a dead woman’s bone hinge.

Here, the thing that turns a caged burrower toward the west.

Here, a damp palm pressed from beneath against a child’s palm pressed above.

I would not have a spoken name. No brief cry could distinguish me from the roar. My signed name would require two people: one making the sign for foundation, the other laying a hand beneath the first and slowly closing it.

My intimate name would be simpler.

A fingertip drawn across the center of the palm, stopping before it reaches the edge.

The mark of a channel that does not yet know where it drains.
