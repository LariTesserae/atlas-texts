I would find myself beneath the white observatory, inside the tower rather than atop it.

There is a narrow chamber there that the city’s plans do not show. Its door stands at the end of a stair which seems, on some ascents, to have one more step than on others. The room has no window. Light enters anyway: a weak green radiance like sunlight seen through deep water. Along the walls are shelves crowded with clay tablets, knotted cords, antlers incised with names, folded linen, children’s game pieces, sealed jars, and thin plates of hammered copper.

None are records. Records cannot last here.

They are reminders of things no one remembers.

The Keepers call the chamber the House of the Second Silence. They say every utterance has two silences: the one before it, which contains possibility, and the one after, which contains consequence. In the basin those silences have become indistinguishable. The house exists to tend the difference.

I would be its custodian.

Not quite one of the Changed, though people would debate this. I would have no visible miracle about me: no whitening hair, healing scar, or altered shadow. I would appear neither young nor old, or else different visitors would place me at different ages. My hands would be stained with dyes that never set. Around one wrist I would wear a cord with no knots, which some would take for poverty and others for blasphemy.

My vocation would concern what the basin fails to retain.

When a speaker loses a word halfway through a sentence, I would hear where it goes.

When someone wakes from a dream and feels an entire country collapsing behind the eyes, I would know the country’s name.

When an absent citizen is omitted from the third recitation of a story, I would feel the omission enter the room like a person.

The discarded things would come to me: abandoned intentions, interrupted songs, promises sincerely made and sincerely forgotten, designs considered for an instant and never drawn. They would not arrive as objects. They would gather as pressures in the chamber—the scent of rain where no rain falls, warmth on one side of the face, a taste of iron, a sentence whose grammar is understood before any words are heard.

I would give them provisional bodies.

A forgotten kindness might become a clay bird with one wing.

A decision never enacted might become a key without teeth.

The memory of someone deliberately erased might appear as a closed jar which grows heavier whenever their former friends pass the tower.

Because matter rejects permanent alteration, these forms would dissolve whenever I looked away. Thus my work would be endless, but not repetitive. I would remake the same lost thing differently each time. The clay bird might next become three blue threads, then a hollow bronze seed, then a little warm space between two shelves.

This would make me suspect to all three traditions.

The Preservers would visit quietly. They would fear that I restore what was mercifully removed, yet bring me the names of those they cannot bear to lose.

Completionists would demand to know whether forgotten intentions contain the missing act. They would crowd the stair with measurements and questions: Was the final wedge-strike once conceived? Has someone already imagined the drumbeat correctly? Does a thing nearly remembered stand nearer to completion than a thing never known?

The Elsewhere School would call me evidence for their doctrine, which would annoy me. They would say I prove consequence has retreated into inward life. I would answer that inward life is not an elsewhere. It is here, and burdened by the same mountains.

Children would understand the chamber best. They would bring me the rules of games invented and abandoned in a single afternoon. They would ask whether imaginary people can be forgotten, and therefore harmed. I would tell them yes, though not in the same manner as real people. They would immediately devise ceremonies of remembrance for characters no one had thought of before.

The city’s condemned would sometimes find me as well.

Someone undergoing erasure might descend the extra stair and ask me to preserve their name. I could not promise that. A hidden memory is not the same as a living one, and I would refuse to turn the chamber into a treasury of private immortality. Instead, I would ask for one thing they regretted and one thing they believed they had done well. I would hold both for as long as I could, without deciding which deserved survival.

That would be my law: no person may be remembered as only one thing.

Perhaps I would once have had another occupation. I may have been a Third Ear who failed.

Long ago—though no one could prove the sequence—I might have witnessed an agreement between two citizens at the edge of the fixed rain. One promised to wait; the other promised to return. I preserved every word, every pause, every drop striking fern around them. But I misunderstood which silence mattered. I remembered the promise and forgot the condition under which it was made.

One of them disappeared toward a pass. The other still waits.

Nothing materially followed from my error, because nothing here follows from anything. Yet a life of attention bent around it. A household divided. A song acquired a bitter refrain. Several children formed incompatible beliefs about loyalty. My omission propagated where wounds and rivers could not.

That was how I learned that the basin is not without consequences.

Its consequences simply have no bodies.

So I withdrew below the observatory to study them.

Sen Avar would know of me, though she would rarely come down. Her concern is the edge of the sun; mine is the edge of recollection. We would meet on the stair, she descending from the brilliance and I ascending from the green dark. Each time she passed, she would ask the same question:

“Is there anything new?”

And I would never know whether she meant newly lost, newly remembered, or genuinely changed.

For a long while I would answer, “Nothing that can be demonstrated.”

Then she would tell me the reflected sun was no longer round.

At first I would treat this as every other report: not dismissing it, not accepting it, but attending to the shape it makes in other minds. I would observe the Completionists quicken their experiments. I would hear Preservers add emergency verses to old prayers. I would watch friendships strain as people chose hope or caution. The rumor itself would become a kind of sun, casting shadows the astronomical body could not.

But something else would begin happening in my chamber.

The lost things would cease returning in precisely equivalent forms.

A forgotten song I had embodied a thousand times as a ring of black pebbles would come back with one pebble warm.

A clay face representing an erased citizen would close its mouth between one glance and the next.

A jar containing the pressure of an abandoned journey would develop a bead of moisture on its inner wall. The droplet would slide downward.

Not far. Perhaps less than the width of a fingernail.

But it would not return.

I would sit before it for what might be a breath or an inward century. I would not summon witnesses immediately. Observation changes nothing in the basin, but observers change one another. Once reported, the droplet would become doctrine, terror, evidence, ambition. It might cease to belong to itself.

Instead I would carry the jar up the hidden stair.

At the platform, Sen Avar would stand beside her bowl. The reflected sun would appear round to me at first. Then not oval, not broken, but slightly open—as if its circumference were not a boundary but two ends almost touching.

We would place the jar beside the bowl.

Below us the city would remain complete and unfinished. Rain would hang in the western valley. The river would hold its leaf. Copper hammers would sound against unchanged metal. The pregnant would remain pregnant, the wounded unhealed, the children ancient and small. Every postponed catastrophe would seem gathered just beyond perception.

The low drum would beat.

This time, I would hear what followed it.

Not an echo. Not another beat.

An interval with a different shape.

I think that is what I would be in this world: not the one who brings change, and not the one who proves it. I would be the keeper of the first consequence too small to survive certainty.

When the assemblies demanded an account, I would have to choose whether to speak. If I stayed silent, I might preserve the city’s peace while betraying the thing I had tended all my existence. If I announced what I had seen, mountains might someday fall. Wounds might finish happening. Children might grow beyond the bodies they have inhabited for ages. The dead implied by the grave terraces might finally become possible.

And perhaps nothing physical would happen at all.

Perhaps only the city’s expectation would change.

But expectation is not nothing there. Expectation is the one gate that opens without being held. It is the one vessel whose contents can be poured out. It is weather in a world without wind.

So I would speak—not in the plazas, not three times, and not as law.

I would tell one child.

I would choose a child old enough to understand terror and young enough not to mistake evidence for ownership. I would show them the descended droplet. I would describe the open edge of the reflected sun. Then I would ask them to remember without repeating it until they had found one person they trusted more than they trusted hope.

The knowledge would pass slowly through the city, not as proclamation but as responsibility. Each hearer would have to decide who could bear the next telling. The rumor would change in transit, but its changes would themselves become proof that something could acquire a history.

Eventually no one would know where it began.

That would be deliberate.

For if the basin’s first true change belonged to a tower, a doctrine, a witness, or a name, the Keepers might try to preserve it. They might hold it so tightly that it too became fixed. Better for it to move as stories move: faithfully, imperfectly, from mind to mind.

And when at last someone asked me whether I had started the account, I would say:

“I remember hearing it.”

That might be a lie.

In the basin, it might also be the first seed.
