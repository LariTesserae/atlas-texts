I would find myself at the seam beneath the slab—not quite in the chamber, and not wholly outside it.

At first I might mistake myself for the eye looking in. Yet the eye belongs to succession: it approaches, dilates, waters in the mineral brightness, withdraws. Nothing capable of arriving can belong to that country. The chamber has no room for an observer because observation requires a difference between before and after. So I would not be the visitor.

Nor would I be one of the animals. Their forms are too complete. Each crab, mite, snail, and folded amphibian is already a finished syllable in the chamber’s unending utterance. To become one would require a vacant crack, an unused note, a hollow not yet fitted to its body. There are none.

I would be the boundary that refuses the finger.

Not a wall exactly, and not a force exerted from within. I would be the perfect meeting of pressures at the opening: the place where an outer world full of intervals—footsteps, weather, hunger, erosion—encounters a world with no intervals at all. I would have no thickness, because thickness would occupy space. I would have no duration, because duration would introduce change. I would exist as the chamber’s completeness made palpable.

From outside, I would feel like resistance.

From within, perhaps I would be indistinguishable from hospitality. Nothing in the chamber is expelled. Everything is admitted absolutely—light into stone, noise into air, moisture into its hollows, bodies into their sockets—so absolutely that admission has ceased to be an event. The only thing excluded is what has not always already belonged.

But the seam has two faces. Along its outer face, dust settles. Hands lift the slab. Seasons pass through the pale stone as warmth and cold. Creatures elsewhere hatch, feed, scar one another, and vanish. Along its inner face, the metallic ringing eternally occupies its exact stratum of air. The nearest reeds hold their uncompleted inclination. A droplet remains gathered above the fall it will never begin.

I would know both, but I could enter neither.

Perhaps this is what the little arches and stairs are for. They are not ruins and not constructions. They are shapes cast by the boundary’s impossible longing: architecture without passage, steps without traversal, a bridge over a current that cannot occur. They express direction inside a world where nothing can go anywhere. The mica enclosure is a room within the room, built around a flower that cannot be more enclosed than it already is.

I would be present in each of these useless thresholds.

I would be the opening under every pebble arch.

I would be the distance between one stair and the next—not measurable distance, but the absent act of descending.

I would be the dry underside of the bridge, preserving the idea of crossing.

I would be the minute clear space cupped by the flower around its drop, though even there the space is not empty: it is wholly occupied by the drop’s never-falling.

And because the chamber contains every instant of itself at once, I might also be the one thing it cannot contain: *someday*.

Not someday as a future date. Someday as a pressure without a place. The unborn stream in the tiny channel; the erosion latent in the soft-shaped rock; the movement promised to the poised grain of sand. Every unfinished feature gestures toward me, but none can reach me. I am what its incipient gullies mean without ever becoming.

Outside, someday passes unnoticed into today.

Inside, it remains pure.

So I would find myself everywhere the chamber almost begins: at the seam, beneath the arches, between the steps, under the bridge, in the channels that stop after a few millimeters. I would be neither organism nor stone, but the excluded possibility of sequence—the infinitesimal vacancy required for one thing to become another.

The chamber would press against me with all its light and noise. The changing world would press from the other side.

I would keep them from touching.

And if the slab were lifted and an eye brought close, the watcher might feel, just before the brilliance filled the pupil, that something at the threshold had noticed them—not by turning, not by waking, but by becoming, for one instant outside the chamber, the possibility that they might enter.

They could not.

That impossibility would be me.
