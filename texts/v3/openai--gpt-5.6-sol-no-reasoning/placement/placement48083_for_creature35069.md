I would be found in the Index, though not always in the same tower.

I would be a precedent—or at least that is what the brass tab on my coat would say. The corresponding district has never been built. Each year the Index engineers find more of it around me: a short flight of exterior steps inside a ventilation shaft, half a nursery behind a drainage wall, an arcade of sealed shops occupying three centimeters of interior depth. These fragments agree with my apartment in materials and dimensions. They bear the same municipal designation, followed by dates still decades away.

My profession would be **counterpart surveyor**. I would trace correspondences between structures that claim to be separate: a culvert with the proportions of a chapel; an orchard aisle repeating the geometry of an obsolete freight platform; two doors miles apart whose scratches continue from one surface to the other. Most surveyors establish where one structure has been copied. My specialty would be continuity without passage—places that are not connected by any route, but behave as parts of the same room.

I would travel lightly: chalk, folding gauge, plumb cord, condensation cup, three obsolete keys. One key would grow warm near manifestations of the river. One would fit no known lock. The third would open doors only when someone was waiting on the other side.

People would summon me for minor disturbances. A household table might cast its shadow into an apartment four districts away. Rain entering one stairwell might emerge from the ceiling of another. A child could whisper into a heating grille and be answered by her own voice, older and tired. I would measure, mark, interview both sets of walls, and file a recommendation:

**Treat as one premises until further notice. Obligations of adjacency apply.**

Quietly, I would suspect the Index does not contain an example of every structure.

I would suspect every structure contains an example of the Index.

My home would be one of its prototype apartments: narrow, pale, and furnished for a life slightly different from mine. The cupboards hold shallow recesses shaped for objects not yet manufactured. There are handrails at the height of an absent child. A second cup leaves a wet ring beside mine on certain mornings. Behind the western wall comes the soft domestic noise of a neighborhood that does not exist: pipes knocking, chairs dragged back, someone practicing four notes on a stringed instrument.

I would not open that wall. I would sit with my wrist against it and tap the customary greeting.

Sometimes it would answer.

Not in code—more like a structure settling around the idea of language.

My closest companion would be a young magnetic grazer with one defective hoof. It could cling to iron with three feet, but the fourth attracted obsolete signage, loose fasteners, jewelry, and once an archivist’s entire ring of keys. Because it could not safely join a façade herd, it would follow me through the Index, clicking unevenly at my heels. Around certain doors it would stop and press its brow to the frame. These doors invariably belonged to my unbuilt district.

I would map them in secret.

Over years, the marks would form not a neighborhood plan but the outline of an enormous organ: folded chambers, branching corridors, conduits gathered around a central hollow. At first I might call it a heart, because cities invite that metaphor. Later I would understand that it resembles an ear.

The basin is listening.

The lamps, the aqueducts, the loaded walkways, the rooms acquiring corners—all of them alter in response to pressures too faint or distant for their inhabitants to perceive. The Index is not predicting construction. It is recording what the high country hears through its foundations: other basins, possible basins, buried ages, perhaps the white brightness beyond the ridge. New buildings appear first as resonances. The city builds them to give those resonances somewhere to exist.

And my district would be the shape of something approaching.

Not an invader, nor a machine. Perhaps not a being in any useful sense. A pattern moving through manufactured depth as weather moves through open land. The apartment around me would be its first completed cavity. I would be called a precedent because the municipal vocabulary lacks a gentler word for symptom.

Still, my days would remain ordinary.

I would certify shared shadows. I would drink water gathered from four manifestations of the river. I would help families negotiate custody of rooms that had migrated between them. At lamp-dimming I would climb to a bridge between Index towers and watch moths assemble over the basin. Their pale sheets would resemble plans for my missing district more clearly each year.

Eventually, one evening, the third key would become warm.

I would return home to find a door in the western wall, outlined by fresh condensation. The grazer would stand before it with all four hooves fixed firmly to the floor. From beyond would come the metallic call of a white bird, though no birds live inside the Index.

Protocol would forbid me to open it alone.

So I would not be alone.

I would bring an archivist whose handwriting has begun appearing on future labels. I would bring the child who hears her older voice in the vents. I would bring a river worker carrying bowls from the canal, pipe, atrium fall, and rooftop pool. I would bring the traveler from the highest platform, with a handful of purposeless soil sealed in glass. The grazer would come without invitation.

Together we would wait for the room beyond to establish its dimensions.

Then I would use the key.

Whether the door opened onto the unbuilt district, the country beyond the ridge, or some interior height of the world itself would matter less than the first thing we did upon entering:

put one hand against the nearest wall,

and feel what was moving underneath.
