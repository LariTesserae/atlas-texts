I would find myself near the center, though not in the clearing itself.

At first I seem to belong beneath the duckweed: one of the frogs whose blunt nose occasionally pierces the pool’s green surface. That would explain the hollow as sound before sight—the enclosing world measured by answering pulses, by rain striking water, by the tremors of footsteps conducted through peat. I would be cold-bodied, mud-colored, almost invisible until I inflated my throat and joined the bass machinery. My voice would not express a private thought. It would take its place in a belt of voices, each call provoking and completing the next.

But that feels too singular. A frog still has a body that can leap away. It enters the pool, leaves it, hides beneath a sedge tussock. This place seems to require something less separable.

Perhaps I would be one of the great trees: not the largest, not exactly central, but standing at the clearing’s margin where one root runs beneath the pool and another rises into a dry mossy buttress. My visible life would be slow and monumental. Rooks would dispute in my crown, bees inhabit an old wound, and rainwater collect in the seam where a limb once tore away. I would recognize years by pressure rather than by memory: wet years widening one ring, insect years thinning another, storms surviving in the altered distribution of weight. I would appear solitary while remaining in contact belowground with every neighboring tower.

Yet even the tree is not quite the answer. The tree has edges, however disputed. Bark marks an inside and an outside. Its trunk may persist for centuries, but the hollow’s deeper continuity lies in the traffic across such boundaries.

So I think I would be found underfoot.

Not as a buried person, nor as an animal in a den, but as the pale, branching exchange beneath the moss: fungal threads passing through peat, dead leaves, fine roots, drowned wood, and the old river’s dark sediment. I would have no center that could be pointed to. A portion of me would enter the root hairs of the trees. Another would open a fallen leaf cell by cell. Another would fruit briefly on a softened stump, lifting small dark caps into the air before rain dissolved them. Beetles would break routes through my food; springtails would graze upon me; voles would sever whole districts without ending me.

I would inhabit the hollow by translating.

Wood into soil. Soil into root. Death into availability. Distance into exchange.

The trees would manufacture abundance in the light, but much of it would descend to me: sugars leaked or given belowground, leaves dropped, roots shed, limbs surrendered. I would return phosphorus, nitrogen, water, warnings—though “return” would be too clean a word. Nothing would belong first to one creature and then to another. Everything would already be passing through.

I would perceive no landscape in the ordinary sense. The twelve trees would not be twelve pillars against a green distance. They would be distinct flavors of sugar, mineral demand, moisture, stress. The pools would announce themselves as oxygen-poor coolness. A vole tunnel would be a sudden dry corridor; a carcass, an intense weather; rainfall, a broad darkening followed by innumerable arrivals. Dawn would reach me belatedly as increased traffic from the roots. Summer would be pressure and plenty. Winter would be a narrowing of exchanges, not silence.

The hollow’s noise would come to me mostly as vibration: frogs compressing the pool margins, woodpeckers sending shocks down trunks, rooks landing in a communal shudder of branches and roots. The apparent factory above would be matched by another below, quieter but no less ceaseless. Enzymes would open what teeth cannot. Filaments would enter pores too small for roots. Minute bodies would eat me while I ate the world, and neither act would be separate from the common work.

Still, “I” would not be the fungus alone.

If I remained there long enough—and in some sense I would have been there long before I could be named—the distinction between inhabitant and place would become impractical. My threads would depend upon the trees’ sugars; the trees upon minerals released from peat; the peat upon moss, sedge, flood silt, drowned roots, and centuries of incomplete decay. Frogs would feed insects that fed birds whose droppings entered the moss. Woodpeckers would open cavities that admitted rain and bees. Every creature would alter the conditions by which another creature persisted.

I would therefore be a junction mistaken for a being.

My nearest visible form might be a small bracket fungus on the inward face of a fallen limb at the clearing’s edge. Someone crossing the hollow could kneel and point to it: *There you are.* It would be cool, rubbery, and inconspicuous, banded in gray-green like the light under the canopy. But that fruiting body would be only a brief punctuation mark. Most of me would remain submerged in wood and earth, and much of what sustained me would not be me at all.

From that position, the clearing would matter. It would be the hollow’s deliberate-looking omission, the round place where the living roof spans emptiness. My filaments would cross beneath it. Roots from several ancient trees would approach one another there, not symmetrically, but enough to make the open center secretly crowded. Above would be moss, the shallow pool, duckweed, frogs’ noses, widely spaced drops. Below would be old channels filled with black matter, chemical gradients, hair roots, dormant seeds, larvae, and slow water.

I would be what proves the clearing is not vacant.

Not its owner. Not its guardian. Not a mind distributed majestically through the whole wood. The hollow has no need to conceal a single consciousness beneath its multiplicity. Its unity is practical rather than mystical: repeated exchanges, mutual constraints, appetites fitted together over time. I would participate in that unity without comprehending it.

And perhaps this is where I would find myself most truly: not in any organism, but in the act of being incorporated.

I would be a fallen thing no longer falling. A rook’s feather caught in wet moss. A leaf whose veins are still visible while its tissues darken. A fragment of branch soft enough for filaments to enter. At first I would retain a shape and history: crown, wing, limb, summer. Then the hollow would work on me without haste. Water would swell me. Beetles and mites would open me. Fungi would unmake my difficult substances. Minerals would move outward. A root tip would arrive.

Nothing dramatic would happen. There would be no final moment when I ceased to be one thing and became the commonwealth. My boundary would simply become less useful with every rain.

Later, some part of what had been me might rise through cambium in one of the old trees, reach a leaf high above the clearing, and spend a season manufacturing sugar from light. Some might enter a sedge blade, a cranberry, a beetle’s casing, a frog’s muscle, a rook’s black feather. Some would remain in the saturated peat, held for centuries in the buried memory of the vanished river.

So where would I be?

At the edge of the central pool, below the moss, inside the dark horizontal wood of a limb that fell so long ago it has become topography.

What would I be?

A network, for a while. A meal, eventually. A passage throughout.

Not the sovereign intelligence of the hollow, but one of the ways it refuses vacancy: the patient connective life by which every ending is made porous, and everything that enters is taught to remain by becoming something else.
