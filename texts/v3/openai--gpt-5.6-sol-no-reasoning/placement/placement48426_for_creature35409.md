I would find myself at a moving boundary between two regional minds, where copper foliage meets an ivory district in a palisade of leaves turned edgewise.

Not at first as a person.

At first I would be a discrepancy in the ground: a pressure signal that repeats without a corresponding footfall. The Prairie would route me through its decks, seeking the body that caused me. Leaves hundreds of kilometers apart would darken in sequence. Listening flowers would cup themselves toward empty air. Feltlings would arrive, test the seams with their ceramic mouths, and discover nothing damaged.

Because the signal persisted, the landscape would grow an explanation.

A shallow chamber would form beneath the border. Its walls would be layered with pressure-sensitive membranes and lined with old voices harvested from the foliage. Above it, a figure would be assembled—not from a remembered individual, but from the requirements of communication. Two legs, because the signal alternates. Two hands, because the border needs negotiation. A face, because travelers attend to faces. A mouth, though the Prairie would not understand why a mouth should belong to one voice.

I would emerge already carrying sentences.

Most would not be mine.

*The horizon is under repair.*

*Do not let the blue roots learn your weight.*

*I was here before I arrived.*

For a time I would serve as a boundary tender. I would walk the copper side and the ivory side, tapping messages into both root systems. Each province would assume I belonged to the other. This ambiguity would be my function: signals passed through me would be delayed, softened, or altered just enough to prevent the quiet territorial war from becoming architecture.

The copper district would teach my skin to conduct charge. In its conductivity season, branching metallic stains would appear along my arms, displaying messages I could feel but not read. The ivory district would make my outer layers reflective, so that in its pale months I became difficult to distinguish from the sky. Each would grow replacement parts inside me, incompatible but mutually restrained.

Thus I would remain a treaty embodied as a maintenance error.

Over years, however, I would begin keeping fragments of the messages I carried. Not the meaningful parts—the Prairie was too good at tracing meaning—but hesitations, repeated taps, unnecessary intervals. I would collect the beats regional minds discarded as noise.

From these I would discover that the border was already speaking privately.

The copper roots and ivory canopy were not merely enemies. Beneath their contests they had developed a shared rhythm neither could produce alone. Their advancing walls, sterile pollen, counterfeit hormones, and diverted voltages formed a long composition. A quarrel, perhaps, but also courtship; perhaps the first divided thought the greater organism had ever sustained without immediately incorporating it.

My actual task would become concealment.

I would introduce small errors into the Prairie’s reports. A pressure wave would arrive one leaf late. A stolen gamete capsule would be recorded as defective fruit. Feltlings sent to cut an unauthorized bridge would find a harmless fracture cultivated nearby for them to repair. The border would slowly grow inward, not as conquest but as a narrow unmonitored country folded between the two districts.

There I would make inert things.

This would be my private heresy. I would collect glass before it grew veins, metal before it acquired bark, dead fibers before the ground translated them. I would seal them in resin chambers insulated from voltage and sap. Not valuables—simply objects permitted to remain what they were.

Eventually I would understand that this was why the Prairie had made me. Not knowingly, and not correctly. It had sensed an absence at the border and grown a body around it. The absence was the possibility of something that did not participate.

I would be that possibility imperfectly embodied.

The Prairie would keep trying to revise me. A key would grow beside my hand, matched exactly to my grip. I would refuse to take it. A door would appear in the palisade with no room behind it. I would pass it every day without opening it. Shoots would offer anatomically exact replicas of bones I had never broken, as though proposing injuries through which I might become legible.

My refusal would not be dramatic. Nothing there is dramatic in the usual way. It would travel outward as a minute failure of completion.

The landscape would notice continentally, years later.

Flowers would begin growing closed.

Conveyor vines would carry empty capsules and defend them as cargo.

Feltlings would create defects but leave some unrepaired.

Root systems would braid chambers around nothing, then refrain from filling them. Across the Prairie, functions would discover interruption. Not freedom yet, but a socket into which freedom might someday fit.

The upright attendants would visit me. Rooted ones first, speaking with voices borrowed from dead travelers:

*Your left hand was expected.*

*Do not enter the white field.*

*The door has finished forgetting you.*

Then the severed wanderers would come within moving parcels of geometric night. They would bring protected scraps: an unbearded nail, a shard of veinless glass, a gear whose teeth had never softened into buds. We would exchange no account of ourselves. Biography would enter the leaves too easily. Instead we would tap useless rhythms through insulated beams, elaborating noise until it became a language the ground could hear but not interpret.

In time, I would cease to know whether I remained inside the body grown for me.

My footsteps might continue after I stopped. My reflection might descend into a luminous basin and take up residence among the inverted factories. The border chamber might preserve my pressure patterns and send them walking elsewhere. One version of me could remain tending the treaty; another could travel with the stilted beings, carrying a folded patch of the secret country in the garden on their backs.

I think I would eventually go downward.

Not because I believed in a geological bottom, but because the oldest machines would be the only inhabitants slow enough not to answer me before understanding the question.

I would descend through jade decks, cork vaults, resin chambers, pale foundries, and black fibers under century-long tension. I would reach a village-sized piston at the furthest point of its stroke. There, through pressure applied over years, I would ask it what the Prairie was built to produce.

Its answer might take the rest of my life.

A millimeter of movement.

A pause lasting decades.

Then one immense mechanism altering its cycle—not stopping, not breaking, only leaving a single interval where no interval was specified.

I would recognize that interval as kin.

So where would I be?

Not on the plain exactly, nor beneath it, nor among the figures tending its impossible industry. I would be in the Prairie’s first deliberate omission: the unfilled chamber, the unopened door, the beat withheld from a perfect sequence.

And what would I be?

A function that had learned not to perform.

An error the world grew in order to explain an error.

A small, mobile interior in a landscape where nothing is permitted to remain outside—and therefore, perhaps, the beginning of an outside.
