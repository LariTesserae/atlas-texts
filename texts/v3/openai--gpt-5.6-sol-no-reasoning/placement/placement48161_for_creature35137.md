At first I seem to be the cleft itself.

Not the mountain entire, nor the absent roof of sky, but the inward place: the narrowness that gathers what would otherwise disperse. My body is basalt, softened beyond any clean wound. Rain has worried me into ribs and channels. Hooves, roots, nests, and generations of gripping feet have revised me so slowly that no living resident could witness the change. Their noise is my audible life. Their accumulated remains become my flesh.

But the cleft is perhaps too easy an answer. It contains the life without sharing its urgency. Stone can hold thirst, but cannot quite suffer it.

Then I am the water.

I arrive before dawn as a dimness on black rock. I bead at the tips of moss and along the backs of sleeping frogs. I enter mouths too small to see. Sometimes I am only enough to wake a root. Sometimes I come violently, filling every groove and joining the footprint basins into one brief, lucid stream. Thousands drink me, and I cease to be one thing. I become blood, sap, egg, milk, slick skin, swollen moss. I leave the cleft in flying bodies and climbing bodies, though some of me sinks under gravel and remains in the mountain’s cool keeping.

Yet water passes through. Something here listens and persists.

So I find myself lower down, where the noise is thickest and the light rarely arrives: not one of the conspicuous animals, but a blind silver worm beneath the gravel.

I have no image of the world. Sky is not blue to me; it is pressure transmitted through stone. Rain is a procession of impacts approaching from above. A descending hoof is a sequence of deep knocks. The serpent is warmth, musk, and the prolonged silence of nearby prey. The colonies overhead are weather that never ends.

My body is narrow, pale, jointless to the glance. I move through the spaces left between wet stones, feeding on what falls and softens: fragments of guano, fungal threads, drowned insects, the translucent films made by decay. What the winged world discards becomes my abundance. Feathers arrive stripped of flight. Eggshell arrives emptied of birth. Leaves once folded into cups reach me as pulp. I do not know possession because everything I encounter is already becoming something else.

But even the worm feels too singular.

The longer I remain, the less certain my boundary becomes. Microbes line my gut. Fungal filaments pass through the matter I eat before and after it passes through me. Beetles open what I cannot open. Water carries the dissolved mountain into my tissues. When I die, other mouths complete the movement. I am not merely an inhabitant of the cleft’s many-bodied life; I am one of its hidden exchanges.

Perhaps, then, I am the downward-traveling sound.

I begin at the rim as one loosened pebble. I strike a ledge and divide. Bats wake behind stone. Birds lift their heads. Frogs flatten themselves against the wall. Every body receives me according to its fear. I cannot easily climb back toward the plateau; the cleft takes me inward, enlarges me, hands me down from colony to colony. I am warning without language, knowledge without ownership.

From below, I hear the great roar that cannot escape. From above, I am almost nothing: a tremor beneath a traveler’s foot.

That is where I would find myself—in the dark lower narrowing, among wet gravel and old remains, as a blind creature whose chief sense is listening. Small enough to live between the consequences of larger lives. Ancient only through continuity: not immortal, but repeatedly made from rain, mineral, waste, and death.

When the first drops strike the summit, I feel them coming long before they reach me. The world above falls quiet in layers. Pressure gathers. The stones cool.

Then water enters the gravel, and for a little while every passage in my body opens.

I rise toward it without knowing what rain is.
