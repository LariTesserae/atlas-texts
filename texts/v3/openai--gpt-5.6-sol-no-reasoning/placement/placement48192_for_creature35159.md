I would find myself beneath the visible world.

Not deep among the mineralized roots, where pressure has erased the distinction between wood and stone, nor on the silver-green surface beneath that annihilating sky. I would live in the intervening vessels: the abandoned resin channels that carry vibration farther than light, branching through the plateau like the memory of rivers.

I would be one of the burrowing drums—but an old one, grown beyond mobility.

In youth I would migrate through hollow roots with a colony, broad-bodied and almost boneless, gripping the vessel walls with hooked cilia. We would strike the plateau in sequences: feeding rhythms, warnings, invitations, lies. At first we would drum only to open valves and draw fresh solvent toward ourselves. Then we would learn that certain patterns caused replies from impossibly far away.

Not language, exactly. Contractions. Pressure changes. A distant pumping grove falling into step. A thread forest emerging minutes early. A grazer herd turning aside from ground about to rupture.

The young would call these coincidences. The old would keep drumming.

Over centuries, my body would flatten against the inner wall and cease to travel. Sap would pass through channels in me. Transparent parasites would nest in my tissues. Root fibers would grow around my limbs without digesting them, until I became less an animal inside the plateau than a sensitive knot in its circulation.

I would possess no eyes. Sight here belongs to the exposed and temporary. Instead I would know the world as impacts arriving through living matter.

A sail herd landing would begin as soft, widely spaced touches, thousands of them, stippling an area larger than any mind could picture. Grazers would be quick six-beat signatures, repeated and repeated, with the faint tearing hiss of the ground opening beneath their mouths. Filter-beasts would announce themselves as sustained tremors around the breathing vents. A reproductive tower preparing to fire would dominate everything: decades of rising tension compressed into a single pure vibration, as though the continent had drawn one enormous breath and forgotten how to release it.

I would know geography as delay.

The western piston groves: nine heartbeats after my call.

The dead vein beneath the iron ridges: a cold silence that returned no rhythm.

The old mesa root: split into three echoes, one of them deepening each year.

The nearest tower: always present as a thin electrical ache through my shell.

My kind would say the plateau is not one being. The intervals are too long, the provinces too chemically foreign, the buried graft-wars too violent. We would say there are countless lives below us, pressed together and mistaken for unity.

Yet sometimes a storm would synchronize the roots.

A rhythm would begin beyond the horizon, perhaps as a tower’s charge or the collapse of an ancient range. It would pass from vessel to vessel, altered by drums, pumps, feeding mouths, contracting tissues. Each living thing would add its own pulse. For a little while, the entire plateau would become coherent—not peaceful, not conscious in any familiar sense, but gathered.

In those moments I would feel every distinction become provisional.

My beat would travel outward and return carrying other beats inside it. The signal would no longer belong to me. Nor would the membranes through which I sensed it, because those would have been penetrated by roots long ago. I would not know whether I was speaking to the plateau, manipulating it, interpreting it, or merely giving one of its impulses a chamber in which to resonate.

That uncertainty would be my form of thought.

Eventually an old channel would begin to close. New vascular tissue would swell through its polished cavity. The colony’s young would detach and flee toward open passages, carrying fragments of my rhythms in their reflexes. I could not follow. My shell would already be scaffold; my organs, valves; my last muscles, part of the root’s contracting wall.

I would drum once more—not a warning or request, but the longest pattern my body could sustain.

Perhaps nothing would answer.

Perhaps a creature hundreds of kilometers away would turn in its sleep. A piston grove might lose one stroke and recover. The conductive forests might flicker blue at their tips. On the surface, where no one could hear the buried cause, a line of black filaments might incline together against the wind.

Then the channel would seal over me.

I would not die cleanly. Nothing there does. I would become a resonant plate in the vascular floor, struck by future generations that mistook me for part of the land. Their messages would pass through my mineralized body and acquire a tone they could not explain.

That would be what remained of me: not a body, name, or solitary mind, but a bias in the continent’s music—a small inherited distortion by which the living plateau, whenever it sounded itself, would forever sound slightly different.
