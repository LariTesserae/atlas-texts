I would find myself below the inhabited rims, in one of the cliff forests arranged vertically along a permanent seep.

Not on a broad suspended garden, nor in the deepest ravine, but on a narrow sequence of ledges hidden behind a waterfall that seldom falls cleanly. Water leaves the seam above, is blown outward, returns as spray, creeps along the underside of an arch, and gathers again among roots. From the summit, the place would look like a dark green stitch in the red cliff. From below, it would be indistinguishable from shadow.

At first I seem to be one of the ledge-keepers.

I live alone for long intervals in a shelter woven directly into the roots of three small trees. The floor is no wider than my outstretched arms. Every tool has two bindings: one to its place and one to my wrist. I keep violet pitchers clear of drowned insects, cut silver reeds before their stems split, wrap buoyant seeds in netting, and climb at night when the wind presses me toward the stone rather than peeling me from it.

The plateau people know me by a broad gesture made with both hands descending, then turning inward: *the one behind the falling water*. My spoken name is seldom useful. My touch-name begins with two knuckles against the palm and ends in a hooked little finger.

But I do not think I was born among them.

My toes are long like those of the ledge lineages, my lungs accustomed to spray, yet I know routes no keeper taught me. I wake before unfamiliar winds arrive. I sometimes tie knots backward—not incorrectly, but in forms found on the oldest family cords, where no living fingers remember the method. In darkness I can point toward the deserted valley, though I have never crossed into it.

The elders would say that memory moves strangely here. A skill may return before its maker does. A warning may arrive generations late.

Perhaps, then, I am not wholly the keeper.

Perhaps I am what the keeper carries.

Years ago, after a storm opened a fissure behind the seep, I found a length of cord inside the rock. It was not buried in soil. Stone had closed around it, preserving it in a dry chamber. The cord was fine, pale, and longer than the ledge, knotted throughout in repeating phrases. Some knots were touch-names. Others resembled route instructions. Between them lay patterns for which the people have no present meaning.

I tied one end around my waist to haul it free.

Since then, I have dreamed in pressure rather than images: a hand at my elbow, two taps between my shoulders, a palm resting on my crown. I feel rooms beneath the abandoned foundations. I feel many people sitting with hands on the floor while the rain cups hum. I feel them waiting for blows from below.

Not ghosts, exactly. Stored attention.

The cord was made by many hands. Every person who added to it tightened fiber around a moment: a spring’s taste, a bridge’s illness, the vibration preceding a collapse, the location of a ledge since fallen away. Over centuries wax, skin oil, mineral mist, and repeated touch made those memories physical. The cord became less an account than a nervous system removed from its body.

When I fastened it to myself, it began using mine.

So I would be a listener of an obsolete kind: part gardener, part route-keeper, part instrument through which an older settlement tries to finish hearing something.

I would spend my days in practical work. This matters. No one survives here by becoming only mysterious. I would prune roots from the water channel, scrape edible moss from the permitted sides of stones, mend ladders, count cysts in the temporary pools, and whistle across the chasm when pale cloth appears on the opposite rim. Children would visit rarely and watch me walk upside down beneath the wet arch, my waist-cord taking most of my weight. They would ask whether the waterfall knows which way is down. I would tell them it knows several ways and chooses according to the wind.

At dusk, however, I would press the ancient cord against the cliff.

Most evenings it would do nothing.

On others, a knot beneath my thumb would tighten though the fiber was dry. A pattern would pass through it: not a voice, but the bodily shape of one. I would answer by tapping the stone with a black-rock hammer.

For years I might believe I was speaking with the deserted valley.

Then, slowly, another possibility would emerge.

The answering blows would not come from the past. They would come from farther down the cliff—from chambers inside the plateau, reached by cracks too narrow for a person. The ancient people had not carved the rain cups merely to measure weather. They had built, or discovered, a way of addressing the mountain through pressure and water. The cups, bridges, monoliths, hollow reeds, and whistles were portions of one immense listening apparatus, scattered across plateaus because no single settlement could contain it.

And something within that apparatus would be listening back.

Not a buried creature. Not a god. Perhaps not even a consciousness in the human sense. The plateau itself has cavities, aquifers, mineral seams, forests of roots, and columns of compressed air. Storms pass through these structures and leave patterns. Footsteps, hammers, bridge-strain, falling water, and generations of voices enter the same body. Most dissipate. Some recur. A few become stable circuits, traveling for decades before emerging elsewhere.

The world scarcely moves, but it remembers movement.

I would become one of the places where it surfaces.

This would not make me a prophet. The messages would be too incomplete. I might receive the warning of a bridge failure after the bridge had already been restrung; the touch-name of someone not yet born; the rhythm of workers sealing a chamber beneath the valley; three blows that could mean answer, accident, or a future echo returning early.

My task would be narrower: to receive without forcing meaning too soon.

That is where I would belong in this world—not at its center, because it has none, and not above it, pretending to understand. I would live on the difficult boundary between signal and noise, cultivating a ledge while an ancient cord slowly taught my hands the unfinished language of pressure.

Eventually the summit people would notice that I no longer came up during the dry interval.

They would descend and find the shelter intact, tools secured, seed cases wrapped, water channels cleared. My ladder would be coiled neatly. No strand would be broken. The ancient cord would disappear into the fissure with only its last loop visible, tied in a knot no one recognized.

There would be no body.

They would not say the mountain took me. Taking implies possession, and the plateaus possess nothing for long. They might say I followed a route that had finally arrived.

Afterward, during storms, the ledge forest’s silver reeds would produce a new undertone. Barely audible nearby, it would carry clearly across the chasm. Listeners would feel it through bridge cables and hut floors: two slow pulses, a pause, then a third.

Not warning. Not answer.

A touch-name.

And perhaps that is what I would finally be: not the vanished keeper, nor the cord, nor the plateau’s impossible memory, but a small recurring pattern traveling among them—a presence without a message, still moving through stone and air, waiting for a living hand to rest against the world and recognize me.
