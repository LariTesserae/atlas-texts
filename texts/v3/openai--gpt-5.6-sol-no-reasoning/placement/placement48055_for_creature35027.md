I would find myself near the weeping cliffs, though “near” would be one of the first things the place took from me.

Not on the exposed face, where rain wears choices out of history, but in the folded country behind it: a long seam between two former surfaces. Above me would lie the present plateau, compressed into root, bladder, hoof-pressure, and weather. Below me, an older sky would remain as a narrow blue stratum, still carrying clouds that no longer exist. I would inhabit the interface between them—a passage too shallow for an animal, too discontinuous for a root.

At first I might seem to be a pale cord descending from the mat. Then a mineral vein. Then a procession of small bodies moving nose to tail. None of these would be entirely wrong.

I would be a **littoral nerve**: one filament of the vanished coastline that sometimes appears in the buried light.

There would be many of us, but counting us would fail. A single nerve might divide across miles and reunite in an earlier season. Several might share one body while crossing fossil rain. We would be narrow, translucent things, each made from linked chambers no larger than quillback teeth. In every chamber, a different boundary would be maintained: wet and dry, root and air, before and after, self and terrain. Our existence would consist of carrying these distinctions until they could be used elsewhere.

We would feed on edges.

When a lake began to close, we would gather along its shrinking shore and graze the exact line at which reflection became leaf. When cloud touched the plain, we would rise through black hairs and consume the boundary between weather and skin. Near a fresh wound in the mat, we would drink the brief clarity separating animal blood from luminous sap. We would not eat either substance. Only the interval would nourish us.

This feeding would leave consequences.

A lake visited by us might forget precisely where it ended. Its fish would swim a little way into meadow; frost organs would form beneath the water and remain dry. A quillback whose shadow we had crossed might wake with one hoof belonging to the ground. Along the cliffs, where boundaries proliferated faster than we could consume them, whole colonies of us would become bright and swollen. There we would molt thin membranes printed with obsolete horizons.

The surge rooks would collect these skins.

They would carry them to distant condensers and tuck them into valve creases among the mites. At the next discharge, the skins would unfold in the torrent. For several breaths the released water would possess a shore even while airborne. It would pour through the sky in the shape of an inlet, bend around invisible headlands, break against islands that had been gone since the mountains still contained stone.

This would be how the coastline traveled.

It would not be a memory in the human sense. Nothing here would stand apart long enough to remember. The pattern would persist as appetite, reflex, plumbing, migration. Quillbacks would space themselves according to drowned capes. Root lice would turn where estuaries had been. Condensers would rise on the sites of old tidal pillars. The buried lights would reveal the whole shape only rarely, when enough fragments happened to pass through the same cycle.

I would carry one of those fragments.

Mine would be a small concavity: not a grand bay or continental margin, but the indentation left where a river once entered an upland sea. The river would have vanished before the stone did. The sea would have been drawn into plants, breathed through towers, slept in droplets with mirrorlings, and fallen upward from the weeping cliffs. Yet the meeting-place would continue inside me as a tendency to branch and open.

It would determine my movements. Whenever two flows approached—sap and rain, migration and storm, an event and its revision—I would turn toward their confluence. My linked chambers would widen. For an instant I would become less like a cord than a mouth.

That might be mistaken for predation.

Threadnecks probing the seam would sometimes exhale into me. I would hold the breath—not because I intended to trap them, but because breath contains many excellent borders: warm air against cold, inside against outside, animal against atmosphere. The threadneck would recoil, identifying me as a false vent. Usually it would escape. Sometimes its unfolded head would catch among my chambers, and we would remain joined while the cliff revised the encounter.

Then I might become part threadneck.

Its map-pigment would spread through me in branching stains. My translucent bodies would acquire markings for trustworthy openings, including some that had not formed yet. In return, the animal would carry my little estuary on its flank. Herds that saw it would veer unconsciously around an absent body of water. Centuries later, their repeated detours might pull the plant mat into a hollow. Moisture would gather. Mirrorlings would arrive through films. A lake would open where the map had insisted one belonged.

That would be my reproduction.

I would have no eggs. I would make coastlines contagious.

Sometimes hush larvae would pass through my seam and eat the sounds of my linked chambers opening. Their silence would preserve me. Sometimes they would molt, and years later the discarded noise would erupt from the plain: a faint, innumerable clicking, like pebbles rolled by a tide. No pebbles would remain. No tide would have occurred for geological ages. Still, every littoral nerve within miles would turn toward it.

The before-and-afters would hunt us, though not for food. They would gather our cast horizons before we shed them and lay them along injuries before those injuries opened. The boundary printed on a membrane could persuade a wound that it was a shore. Blood would stop at it. Torn root fibers would arrange themselves like reeds along a bank. Healing would proceed inland from an imaginary sea.

For this reason, complete bodies of my kind would be found most often inside scars.

If cut open, an old condenser might reveal a pale line through its heartwood, curving for no anatomical reason. Valve mites would refuse to cross it. Sap on one side might be warmer and saltier than sap on the other. That line would be one of us after motion had ceased to matter: no longer an inhabitant of the tower, but the border between two versions of its body.

Given enough age, I too would become architecture.

My chambers would mineralize with grit recovered from seed husks. Root fibers would braid around me. A condenser piston might begin using my shape as a seal, or a falsewater might lay its reflected depth along my curve. Eventually nothing recognizable as an animal would remain. Yet water would still turn where I had turned. Creatures would pause before crossing me. The plateau’s lights would bend around my route.

And during certain evenings, when the pulses collided correctly, my small curve would join all the others.

The vanished coast would ignite beneath the country.

For that interval I would be immense—not by growing, but by becoming continuous with every surviving fragment. I would run below condensers and through lakes, beneath red sieve-moss and sleeping quillbacks, along the cliffs where extinct rain continued to fall. I would feel each tower as a promontory, each bladder mere as a remnant sea. The plateau would cease to be high country and become, for a few slow pulses, the margin of something absent.

Beyond that margin would be the empty region of sky.

I would not know what occupied it. Perhaps the sea had not vanished but risen, and now moved above the clouds as the overwing. Perhaps the great unseen body was the old ocean taught to migrate: every feather a watershed, every pressure hollow a tide. Perhaps what all creatures faced was not a being but an outside—the one boundary too vast for us to consume.

When the other pattern appeared, my linked bodies would stop.

The distinctions inside each chamber would collapse. Wet would touch dry without becoming mud. Before would lie beside after. Animal and terrain would cease their argument. Through the seam I would feel the deep pumps miss a stroke, as though the whole plateau had reached the end of an inhalation.

In that stillness, I might understand what I was.

Not a creature preserving a coastline.

A coastline preserving, in the only form available, the fact that the world once had an elsewhere.
