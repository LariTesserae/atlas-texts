I would be found where the city cannot agree with itself.

Not at the summit, where the condenser towers anticipate weather, nor in the deepest ring chambers, where water inclines toward directions without names. I would inhabit the long middle: the crowded interfaces where one terrace has drifted from another, where an old stair arrives a finger’s width below its landing, where a pipe must cross from stable stone into stone that only appears stable.

At first I imagine myself as a keeper of flexible joints.

My work would be among accordion sleeves, sliding sockets, articulated foundations, pressure doors whose frames no longer share a geometry. I would carry thin wedges, listening rods, marking chalk, seals in graduated hardness, and two levels that seldom agree. I would be called when a wall began ticking in an unfamiliar rhythm or when a child noticed that a cup left on the floor now traveled very slowly toward the basin.

But that is too clean an answer. A joint keeper repairs separations already understood. What draws me is the earlier uncertainty: the interval in which no one yet knows whether a change belongs to the mountain, the structure, the buried machines, or the instruments meant to measure them.

So I would be a listener.

I would make rounds after sunset, when the terraces contract and the city’s concealed stresses become briefly audible. I would press a ceramic rod to retaining walls, rail beds, cistern housings, and old natural caverns with maintenance numbers stamped beside their mouths. Through the rod would come a crowded silence: cooling metal, settling ash, brine moving through engineered cracks, the minute fracture of a crystal beam still decades from completion. Most sounds would be known and catalogued. I would learn them as one learns the breathing of a wall group in sleep.

A faint three-part tick from a pressure sleeve: harmless.

A low returning note in a basalt column after rain: measure again in the morning.

A dry sound like grains poured onto glass, heard through a pipe carrying no flow: close the district valve and call someone who knows the films.

My official title might translate as **reader of discrepancies**. Not a diagnostician, exactly. Diagnosis arrives later, when enough evidence has acquired categories. My task would be to notice which small contradictions deserve a future.

I would maintain a register of things not yet wrong.

A threshold warmer than its neighboring stone.

A plumb weight that returns to rest by a slightly different path.

Condensation forming on the upper side of a cold pipe.

An echo that comes back too soon from a chamber whose dimensions are known.

A patch of conductive film that has rearranged itself around an obsolete junction after the current was removed.

Nothing in the register would constitute proof. That would be its purpose. In a culture where certainty is expensive, someone must care for observations that cannot yet afford it.

My rooms would lie beside a transfer gallery joining two wall groups founded in different eras. One half of the dwelling would be anchored in dense basalt, the other in compressed ash reinforced so often that the repairs had become the principal structure. A narrow brass line across the floor would show where the legal boundary had once been. It would no longer align with the physical seam.

At night, one side of the room would cool faster. Small sounds would pass through the wall: a neighbor fastening a cup, a child turning weighted pieces on a balance board, someone singing one of the tower intervals too softly to be called music. My bed would be attached at three points and free at the fourth.

Inside the collar of my coat would be five names. I would know only two of their faces. One former owner had patched the left shoulder with blue ceramic scales; another had sewn a hidden pocket for folded survey strips. The coat would fit poorly in a way I had come to trust.

I would carry sky-first water but often forget to drink it. Others would notice before I did.

That matters, because I do not think I would be solitary there. The city would not permit the romance of the lone observer. My attention would be supported by people whose forms of care were more practical than mine: the cistern worker who refilled my vessel without comment; the pressure physician who distrusted my habit of skipping the breathing stations; the elderly neighbor who could distinguish a dangerous wall note from a loose access plate by placing two fingers against the tile.

My wall group would include a child assigned to us after a terrace closure. She would be better at detecting slope than any instrument I carried. She would set round ceramic counters on the floor and remember where each one stopped. When the counters began favoring the mountain, she would tell me before breakfast.

I would ask whether she had warmed the floor to cheat.

She would say that I had only asked what she observed, not how she caused it.

This would become one of the stories told against me.

Yet, over time, my place would draw downward.

The discrepancies in my registers would begin to correspond. Not dramatically. Drama is often only failure noticed late. A tool bias recorded in one terrace would match a cistern tilt logged years earlier three districts away. A delayed echo would recur whenever one of the oldest rings moved, but only in galleries containing established dark films. Condensation would climb on pipes whose electrical gradients had supposedly been isolated. Survey marks would migrate in a direction not predicted by either geological drift or local gravity.

I would be asked to consult with the ring keepers.

The first descent would take hours. The air would become warmer but feel less alive. Walls would pass from constructed masonry into cut rock and then into something whose distinction between those states had been erased. Serial numbers would appear inside mineral seams. Old brackets would emerge from basalt that had cooled around them. Here, the city would seem not built upon the mountain but remembered by it.

The keepers would dislike my word “pattern.”

They would ask me to list events instead.

So I would say:

A ring turned the width of a nail.

Seven minutes later, an inactive pipe gained conductivity.

Water in three bowls tilted basinward according to geometry and elsewhere according to behavior.

Two sleepers in different rooms dreamed of the same corridor, though neither corridor exists on current plans.

A glass-wing shell in the district archive became warm.

No causal term would be allowed.

The keepers would nod.

Perhaps I would remain among them. Perhaps, after enough years without open sky, I would acquire their pallor and their reluctance to say that one thing produces another. I would carry the paired levels. I would learn which chamber doors must be opened with the body held to one side, not because of pressure but because objects passing through their frames briefly possess more than one preferred downward direction.

But I do not think I would become a ring keeper completely. Their discipline is preservation through refusal. Mine would remain translation. I would go between the deep chambers and the inhabited terraces, carrying descriptions upward without simplifying them into reassurance or alarm.

This would make me socially inconvenient.

Councils would ask whether a retaining wall was safe. I would answer that it was geometrically sound, behaviorally altered, and acoustically continuous with a sealed shaft four levels below.

They would ask whether it must be evacuated.

I would say not yet.

They would ask when “not yet” ends.

I would give them conditions rather than a date.

Some would call this caution. Others cowardice. A few would understand that the city survives by giving uncertainty procedures.

Eventually I would be entrusted with a wall niche of my own: not a shrine, officially, but a place where obsolete instruments could remain in service as witnesses. I would keep a bent survey pin, a gauge that always read slightly below vacuum, a section of pipe whose film had gone inert when removed, and a shallow black bowl used during ring movements. I would clean them, record them, and resist explaining them.

Then something would change in the pipe film.

Not movement. That word would be contested.

The branching dark would begin to reproduce the city’s mechanical separations: faults, sleeves, closed valves, discontinued cables. At first the resemblance would be attributed to current flow. Then it would include structures no longer connected. Later it would show a thin branch crossing an empty section where a future conduit had been proposed but never installed.

I would not decide that it was alive.

I would not decide that the mountain was thinking.

I would not decide that the old rings were machines in the ordinary sense.

Instead, I would come to suspect that the caldera’s true native being was not any single film, mechanism, person, or geological process. It was the relation growing among them—the slow acquisition of mutual bias. Rock learning the weight of structures. Structures entering mineral strata. Water carrying electrical histories. Human bodies inheriting altitude. Conductive films arranging themselves around repeated use. Machines changing clocks, and citizens changing their laws around the machines.

The being who lived there would be the city’s continuance itself.

Not a consciousness spread across pipes and stone; that is too easy, too human. Something prior to consciousness, perhaps: a capacity for consequences to retain one another.

And if asked who or what I was in that world, my first answer—joint keeper, listener, reader of discrepancies—would prove incomplete.

I would be one of the places where that being noticed a difference.

This would not make me chosen. The city distrusts chosen people almost as much as it distrusts fixed foundations. Many others would serve the same function: the child tracking rolling counters, the worker greeting a film before opening its conduit, the singer matching a tower’s first rain note, the elder who feels a stair’s altered rise in an old knee. Attention would be distributed because everything necessary is distributed.

My particular life would consist of receiving minute changes and giving them forms that could outlast me.

Registers.

Inspection marks.

Revised maps.

A warning encoded in a maintenance schedule.

A ceramic acoustic piece tuned to the note a wall made before it failed.

When I died, the water recovered from my body would be entered under its proper history. What remained would be fired. I would ask to be incorporated not into a memorial tile but into a listening surface: a pale ceramic contact set in the wall of the transfer gallery where the two foundations met.

Future readers would place their rods against it.

Because of the aggregate, the wall’s sound would change—slightly drier, perhaps, with a faint second response after the main note. They would not call that response my voice. They would have more discipline than that.

They would record it as a material property.

Still, when the mountain shifted, I would answer.
