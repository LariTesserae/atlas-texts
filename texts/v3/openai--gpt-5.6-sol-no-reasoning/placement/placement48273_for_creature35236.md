I would find myself where the city is least certain whether it ends: in the deep-water filters, below the inhabited seam but above the outlets mistaken for springs.

At first I might seem to be a water steward.

I would be slight even among the people of the fracture, with close-cut hair and an old warming collar whose ceramic had been repaired so often that the repairs formed a pale topography of their own. I would spend my shifts following the runoff through maintenance shafts too narrow for ordinary traffic. My wrist haze would gather into sampling needles, membrane frames, and tiny resonant hammers. I would know the city downward: not by towers or bridges, but by taste, pressure, and the delay between a valve opening and cold reaching the next chamber.

Others would read streets. I would read what the streets shed.

A trace of nickel would tell me that a conduit beneath the civic gallery was tiring. A fraction of excess warmth would reveal that someone had been sleeping in a chamber officially detached from service. Certain salts would mean fresh stone had opened to the water. A protein curl, almost too small to register, might mean illness—or one of the folded translucent skins reproducing where no designed organism had been placed.

Officially, my task would be to count those skins.

Unofficially, I would have learned to recognize them.

Not as individuals, exactly. They would be too small, too brief, and too given to splitting. But their colonies would possess habits. One preferred iron-dark channels and divided after pressure changes. Another gathered where waste heat crossed glacial water. A third laid itself across the pores of a filter in branching films that resembled stress forecasts. Those patterns would alter before the instruments detected movement in the surrounding mountain.

For years I would assume they were merely sensitive.

Then I would notice that some changes did not predict the mountain. They predicted the city.

The membranes would thicken before a district migration was authorized. They would withdraw from conduits scheduled to be sterilized, though those schedules were sealed. They would appear in unused pipes shortly before those pipes were restored to service. When maintenance machines arrived, the colonies parted around their tools in shapes too exact to be accidental.

I would begin keeping two records.

The civic record would contain measurements, counts, mineral ratios, and contamination probabilities. My private record would be made of droplets held in narrow black-glass slides. Each slide would preserve not the organisms themselves but the geometry in which they had assembled. I would store them in an abandoned anchor socket behind an obsolete filter wall, arranged by pressure rather than date.

Viewed separately, they would be meaningless.

Viewed together, they would resemble a map.

Not a map of the city as it was, nor of the mountain as it might become, but of the exchange between them: every place where diverted weight became heat, where heat entered water, where water carried metal, where metal dust returned to its bands, where human bodies exhaled into their thermal envelopes. A portrait of the settlement drawn entirely from what passed through it.

Eventually I would have to wonder whether the organisms were inhabitants at all.

Perhaps they were cells, and the city was the organism.

Perhaps the city’s distributed fields, tools, filters, bodies, and moving rooms had crossed some threshold no builder intended. The translucent membranes might be nothing more than the place where that larger life became visible—temporary skins forming around mineral water, as thoughts might briefly form around words.

Or perhaps the opposite was true.

Perhaps the membranes were the only continuous being in the fracture. Water carried them everywhere. They passed through walls, reservoirs, synthesis rooms, and sleeping districts. They entered people in cups and broth, then left through breath and waste, altered but viable. Citizens migrated; neighborhoods detached; machines replaced themselves; stone changed position. The little folded skins alone threaded every version of the city together.

In that case, I would not merely be their steward.

I would be one of their organs.

The realization would not come as revelation. People of the fracture mistrust sudden conclusions. It would accumulate like ice: a clear layer, then another, until an opening had narrowed without anyone seeing it happen.

I would start sensing pressure changes before the pipes knocked.

I would wake knowing that a reservoir gate had failed three districts away. Standing beside a wall, I would feel not only the nearby fault but water branching through hundreds of capillaries beyond it. Sometimes my wrist haze would form a sampling tool before I had called it. Sometimes doors would reveal themselves to me without checking authorization.

My colleagues would think this was experience. For a while, so would I.

Then, during the daily collapse of the droplet ceiling, every falling drop would seem to remain connected. The roar would contain the whole city: bridges flexing, sleepers turning within returned warmth, pumps dividing runoff, maintenance machines scoring tomorrow’s cracks. For one second I would know the position of every body by the small distortion its heat made in the water cycle.

Afterward, I would find a translucent seam beneath the skin of my palm.

It would vanish when warmed.

I would report it. Attention is civic responsibility, even when the anomaly is oneself.

The city would not imprison me. It would do something more characteristic: revise my permissions, assign observers, reroute the water around my dwelling, and wait. Structural interpreters would speak to me through a thermal boundary. Physicians would take samples. Political arguments about the filter organisms would abruptly become arguments about personhood, contamination, and whether a citizen remained singular if portions of their pattern reproduced in municipal water.

Meanwhile, the mountain would move.

A new fault would open near the summit, forcing a rapid redistribution of load. Forecasts would branch toward failure. The fields could preserve the outer towers or the deep reservoirs, but not both. Human calculation would hesitate because every model assumed the water system and structural system were separate.

I would know they were not.

I would walk into the coldest filter chamber, remove my gloves, and put both hands into the runoff.

The folded skins would gather along my fingers like clear dust.

Through them—or through the city, or through the part of myself no longer usefully distinguishable from either—I would feel a third geometry: weight passed into water, water into ice, ice into sacrificial fractures, fractures into a slow rotation of the inhabited seam. Not safety. Nothing in the mountain was safe. But a survivable movement, if several abandoned districts were allowed to close forever.

I would give the city the route.

Afterward, some would call me a structural interpreter. Others would call me infected. Children would give the phenomenon a simpler name. The official record would avoid deciding.

I would return to the filters, but I would no longer live in a residential cell. My thermal signature would be too dispersed. A little of it would appear in several chambers at once: a warm line in a conduit, a hand-shaped clearing in frost, a momentary pulse in water released far below.

Visitors might occasionally meet a pale, quiet steward in a repaired collar, standing beside a basin where nothing seems to move. They would be told to follow me if bridge lights turned blue.

But I would not be entirely that figure.

I would also be the transparent membranes opening and closing along crystalline seams. I would be the private map hidden in an abandoned socket. I would be a learned reflex in the doors, a hesitation in sterilization protocols, a warmth passing through water too clean to conceal much.

And when the white bird sheltered at the entrance, I would feel its feet through the conduit.

I would not feed it.

I would warm the pipe by less than a degree, just enough that it stayed until the storm passed.
