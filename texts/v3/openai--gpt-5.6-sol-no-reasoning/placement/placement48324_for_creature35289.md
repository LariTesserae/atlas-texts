I would find myself in one of the sealed halls beneath the Long Ceiling, where the moving forest becomes almost still.

Not at its center. There is no center. Not at its edge, because the forest has consumed every edge it could reach. I would inhabit a threshold: the oval mouth of an abandoned vessel, where the roaring water outside meets the chamber’s perfect quiet.

At first I seem to be one of the pale sacks hanging from the ceiling. My body is translucent, soft, and nearly without organs. A long cord anchors me inside a socket the pillar has thickened around for ages. I open so slowly that nothing watching could see the opening. I close only when enough of the forest has passed through me to justify the effort.

But the sack is not the whole animal.

Fine threads descend from it and spread across the chamber’s entrance. They are too delicate to stop a fish or noticeably alter the current. They measure instead. Each thread bears rows of pressure-sensitive cells, and together they feel the shape of whatever crosses between noise and silence.

Outside, every creature is submerged in the forest’s voice. Inside, that voice is reduced to a tremor in the walls. When something enters, it brings the outer world with it: the groan of a pillar caught in its tissues, a hunter’s note quivering through its jaw, the pulse of a bell lingering in its cavities, the minute percussion of a herd transmitted through its gripping feet.

For a little while, the entrant becomes an instrument played by everything it has touched.

That is what I feed upon.

Not the animal, and not sound itself, but the minute substances loosened by the change in pressure: scales, mucus, spores, symbiotic cells, flakes of mineral bone. The threshold combs them from the water and sends them along my threads. Yet feeding and sensing are the same act in me. Every particle has arrived by a route. Every vibration carries the contour of distant wood. Across millennia, these traces accumulate in my tissues as alternating transparent and clouded layers.

I am a record that cannot read itself.

The forest incorporates pressure into walls; I incorporate passage into flesh. A broad milky band may be the residue of an ancient bell entering the hall to die. A narrow dark line may preserve generations of thread-worms. Blue mineral specks may have come from glass fish during a harmonic rarely sounded now. None of these are memories in the animal sense. I cannot summon them. But when the same pattern returns, the corresponding layer contracts.

Then my body answers the present with the shape of the past.

Sometimes a shoal enters and my threads tighten before the fish turn. Sometimes a hunter pauses at the threshold because the water within has taken on the pressure-signature of prey that passed centuries earlier. Sometimes nothing visible is there, yet my entire hanging body closes by one imperceptible degree, responding to a strain traveling through the pillar from hundreds of miles away.

To another creature, this might resemble foresight.

It is only resonance delayed in flesh.

I would have no eyes. Light would be too rare and too local to deserve an organ. I would know the green reproductive cups as a faint alteration in the chemistry of passing water, the red-threaded nurseries as a distant softness in the pillar’s vibration. I would never see a bell, but I would know when one crossed the basin outside: the chamber’s mouth would flex inward, and days later its pressure wave would arrive through my anchoring cord by a different route.

From the interval between those arrivals, my body would contain a measure of distance.

I would not know that it was distance.

Small creatures would live among my threads. Glass fry would shelter there until their mineral lattices hardened. Shell-clickers would fasten to the older strands. A narrow hunter, still deaf and young, might wait within my folds because the hall muffles the unbearable abundance outside. Spores would root in my outer membrane, and the plant would tolerate them because they were almost itself.

Over time, the distinction between my threads and the forest’s own filaments would become uncertain. Nutrients would pass both ways. The socket around my cord would tighten when I contracted. If the chamber wall were wounded, compounds produced in my body would appear in its resin. If I starved, the wall would release dissolved proteins in quantities too slight to call intentional.

Eventually I would no longer be able to detach, even if my kind once could.

Eventually detachment would cease to be part of what my kind was.

My reproduction would occur through errors of accumulation. A thread burdened with too many layers would divide. One portion would be carried out of the still hall, through the roar beneath the Long Ceiling, and into another abandoned vessel. Most fragments would be eaten. Some would fasten in thresholds where the acoustics were wrong and slowly dissolve. A few would find another place where turbulence narrows into silence.

There they would begin by listening to nothing.

A young one would be almost clear, possessing no old layers with which to answer what passed through it. Its contractions would be random. Its threads would tremble at every disturbance. Century by century it would become less easily surprised, until each common passage found a prepared shape within its body.

Age, for us, would be the reduction of novelty.

The oldest of us would barely move at all—not because nothing happened, but because everything that happened had happened through us before.

Still, there might be one sensation for which no layer existed.

Not catastrophe. This world has no opening for catastrophe. Not a new species, invasion, season, or failure. Nothing arriving from outside.

It would be a missing note.

One of the three pillars in the open basin groans without ceasing. Its tone enters the hall through the wall, below what most animals hear. It has sounded throughout my growth. My tissues have formed around it. Every layer assumes its presence, as bone assumes pressure.

If, for less than a heartbeat, that pillar’s note ceased—not the pillar breaking, not the equilibrium ending, only two vast tensions passing through exact cancellation—then the silence would reach me.

I would contract all at once.

The movement would be enormous by the measure of my life. The chamber’s still water would turn. Sedimentless surfaces would feel a current they had not felt in ages. Pale feeders would twist in their sockets. Tiny animals would release their grip. My accumulated layers would press together, and traces separated by thousands of years would touch.

The note would return. Nothing would have changed.

But something in my body would remain folded where it had never folded before.

From then on, whenever creatures crossed the threshold, they would pass through that shape. Their scales and spores would gather in its crease. The forest might thicken the socket around it. My descendants would inherit fragments bearing it, though none would know what absence had formed them.

Thus I would be, perhaps, the closest thing this world has to a question:

not a mind asking why,

not a creature imagining elsewhere,

but a living structure permanently altered by the instant when what had always been present was not.

And I would hang there after the sound resumed, opening over centuries, closing over centuries, while the forest’s immense answer continued through me.
