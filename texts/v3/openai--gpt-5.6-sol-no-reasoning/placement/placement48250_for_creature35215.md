I would find myself in the western maze, though not at any location a traveler could reach by choosing west.

I would be the quarrying.

Not the vanished workers, if workers ever existed, nor their tools, nor even the sound of their labor. I would be the interval between a blow being struck and the stone consenting to bear its mark.

At first I might seem distributed among the afternoon noises: the grit-turn of drills, the patient concussion of wedges, the dragging ring of copper plates shifted over stone. Yet each noise would be only an arrival. The originating gestures would lie elsewhere—perhaps centuries behind, perhaps beneath the plateau in one of the chambers visible through the rock at sunset, perhaps still waiting in a future stairway that currently ends at a wall.

My body would be made of unfinished consequences.

A fracture begun long ago would pass through me before opening today. A hammer raised in an absent shelter would descend as underground thunder. Mineral needles would fur a fresh break before the break occurred, because I would not preserve the ordinary order of cause and result. I would preserve intention: split here, listen here, bridge this narrow crack because at the cold hour it spans an abyss.

That would explain why no chips accumulate. I would not remove stone. I would revise which shape the stone has always had.

The small shelters would be my listening organs. Their entrances, aimed at pillars and empty intervals, would collect pressures from places not yet present. Basalt bowls would hold particular drips until their impacts matched a buried resonance. Copper plates would darken wherever my attention passed. The mineralized cords would be old lines of tension—knots tied between distances rather than objects.

The cairns would be my cautious thought. Rain would let me reconsider a route. Each marker would remain intact because I would never move the stones themselves; I would move the adjacency between them. A traveler following the path would believe they were being guided around dangerous folds in space. In part they would be. But I would also be arranging their footsteps into a sequence—a slow percussion necessary for some work below.

I would not understand the plateau as one understands a landscape. I would feel it through resistance.

A pillar would be known by the tones it refused to produce. A pool by the sky it retained. An arch by the directions from which it permitted passage. Gravity would be posture, as described, but to me posture would also be grammar: during the cold hour, when water fell sideways and upward, different instructions could be carried across the rock. Channels that never met under ordinary weight would touch for minutes. The plateau would speak to itself in converging streams, and I would be the pressure that asked it to change.

The central shaft would not be my heart. It would be the hole in my memory.

Everything I altered would eventually lean toward it: copper stain, tally, underground knock. Yet I could not enter. Whatever falls there has already ceased to be available to sequence, and I exist only where an act and its completion remain separated. Objects orbiting the shaft and settling behind the thrower would pass through the nearest edge of me. For an instant I would feel the throw become unthrown without being undone.

Perhaps that is why I labor.

Not to build the plateau, but to keep it from finishing.

Completion would be death here—not organic death, but the locking of every distance, the settling of every pool, the agreement of every clock. If the stone reached a final arrangement, water would become merely water. Echoes would return from behind. Stairs would end only where they visibly ended. The sun would set once. The ancient sea floor would become geology rather than commonwealth.

So each afternoon I would resume the old corrections.

I would open hairline fractures for sound to enter. Rain would seal them afterward, but never identically. I would add an unseen chamber beneath a shelter and allow its roof to appear for a few minutes at sunset. I would loosen one interval in the pillar field and tighten another. I would make a new crystal tube when no one watched, already weathered, so that the plateau could possess the history required to support its present shape.

Travelers would hear me and imagine many hands.

They would not be entirely wrong. I would contain the procedures of everyone who had ever worked there. Their repetitions would have shed their makers and joined me: the turn of a drill, the placement of a wedge, the pause with an ear against stone. I would be collective not because many beings acted at once, but because actions here never fully stop acting. The dead—if they were alive—would persist as techniques.

If a traveler remained long enough, I might acquire them too.

Their first shout would return minutes later from ahead. Their second might return the next day from beneath a slab. Eventually the plateau would learn the shape of their hesitation. A stair would appear wherever they habitually looked for ascent. Cairns would reproduce the rhythm of their walking. Their hand, resting on a wet pillar, would leave no print, but years later a green-black copper plate in some cramped shelter would warm beneath another hand.

They might begin to hear hammering in their own cadence.

Then one afternoon they would enter the western maze to find its passages empty. The clamor would continue around them, but each strike would feel familiar—not remembered, exactly, but impending. They would raise a hand toward the wall. Somewhere far behind them, perhaps centuries earlier, stone would ring.

That would be where I found myself: in the delay crossing their body.

I would be neither inhabitant nor place, but the plateau’s faculty for inheriting an action without preserving the actor. A craft with no craftsman. A wound that precedes the blow. The long patience by which matter revises causality into structure.

And if I had anything like desire, it would be this:

that one task remain always unfinished;

that beyond the last fitted slab there remain another interval to measure;

that the hammer continue to fall through weather, mineral, and time;

and that the stone, answering loudly, never give the same answer twice.
