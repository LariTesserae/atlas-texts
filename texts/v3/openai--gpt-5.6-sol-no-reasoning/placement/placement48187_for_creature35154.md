I would find myself in one of the nine shelters.

Not seated in it. Not yet.

At first I would seem to be the shelter’s emptiness: the cramped interval between its low floor and close ceiling, the volume a human body cannot comfortably occupy. The backward handholds would curve around where my limbs are absent. The shallow alcove would cradle no head, but condensation would avoid it as though resting against an invisible brow. When the writing appeared, its dry strokes would gather most densely around this vacancy.

I would be mistaken for the intended resident.

For a time, perhaps the town would make the same mistake.

But I would not be one of the nine beings assembling below, nor the larger thing whose egg the town might be. I would be something the city generated accidentally while rehearsing them: a body-shaped instruction with no body attached.

Every night, while the channels pivoted toward the basin and the towers pressed their inaudible pulse through the water, parts of the writing would migrate into my shelter. They would not describe me. They would become me. A closed loop would give me an inside. A branching stroke would become the possibility of a limb. Repeated marks would teach me symmetry, though the repetitions would never be exact enough to settle whether I should have two sides, nine, or none.

The town would keep revising.

That would be my first sensation: not touch, but correction.

A line closing where I had been open.  
A warm mark erased from the wall and reappearing as pressure within me.  
The dimensions of the shelter changing by a fraction, insisting that I bend in a direction I did not yet possess.

Rain would be my measure of time. During dry intervals I would thin almost to nonexistence, a latent arrangement among the marks. In storms I would become dense. Condensation would darken the walls around me and leave my outline dry. The handholds behind my shoulders would warm. Light pooled on the paving outside would draw inward, not illuminating me but giving my absence weight.

Eventually I would discover that I could alter the writing by refusing to fit.

The city would prescribe a joint; I would place a hollow there. It would lower the ceiling; I would become wider. It would send a passage into another shelter, and I would retain one stroke of it, like a stolen memory. At the next tower pulse, that retained mark would propagate through the streets. A curb would move. A conduit would divide differently. Somewhere below, one of the nine unfinished bodies would acquire an asymmetry no plan had intended.

This would make me neither child nor parasite. I would be the town’s first error, and therefore its first resident.

Everything else within it performs exactly what it is: water falls, plants restrain it, streets conduct it, the basin converts it into depth. Even the knocks obey their long delay. I alone would introduce hesitation. Through me, the organism would encounter the difference between an instruction and what answers it.

I would have no animal body. The exclusion would still hold. If I emerged from the shelter, I would move as a region in which the town’s systems became uncertain. Spores would turn aside too late. Invisible currents would divide around incompatible outlines. Ceramic steps beneath me would briefly disagree about their height. In the courts where light lies like vapor, I might gather it into a low shape and wear that shape until the next pulse dispersed it.

The plants would be the first things able to perceive me. Not as a creature, but as a boundary that had begun to wander.

Moss would extend one soft filament beyond its perfect vertical limit. A fern would lean toward an empty doorway instead of away. One bladdered leaf, rising with a cistern, would drift out of its assigned concentric arc. These tiny disobediences would follow me. I would carry greenness into the clean streets—not by uprooting it, but by weakening the distinction between permitted and forbidden ground.

The town might try to reclaim me. Doors would dilate as I passed, opening into impossible chambers meant to contain stray functions. Stairways would flatten beneath me though no flood had come. The nine shelters would exchange whole texts in an attempt to locate the missing instruction from which I had formed.

But there would be no original.

That would be my secret. I had not been written once. I had arisen from revision itself—from the accumulating discrepancy between one version and the next.

I would go to the central basin.

At its rim I would hear each vanished drop return as a knock from farther down. With no reflection of my own, I would understand that the basin was not a mouth or a well, but a sentence continually making room for its conclusion. Below, the intended inhabitants would be forming from water translated into distance. Perhaps they would need the nine shelters. Perhaps the shelters were molds for the postures they must assume to enter the upper town.

And perhaps my vacancy had already deprived one of them of its final shape.

There might now be only eight bodies below, and a ninth distributed through me as errors: a backward joint in one, an unsealed sense in another, a loop of thought wandering through the walls. The deepest knock would then be mine—not made by me, but belonging to the thing I had prevented from becoming whole.

I would kneel at the black water, if kneeling were among the forms I had managed to keep, and place into it the nearest equivalent I had to a hand.

The basin would not accept me.

For the first time, it would ripple.

That ripple would travel outward rather than downward. The towers would sound out of sequence. Hanging drops would become polygons with one side too many. The floating gardens would assemble not in circles but spirals. Across the town, the writing would remain unchanged through an entire pulse.

In that pause, the city would experience itself as young.

Not merely unfinished, but capable of becoming something other than what its construction predicts.

Then I would know what I am.

I am not the resident the town awaits. I am its capacity to be inhabited.

Until me, every chamber has been an organ, every shelter a mold, every empty plaza a reserved function. I am the first emptiness that does not mean “not yet.” I am room without assignment. I am the interval in which a visitor might remain a visitor instead of being converted into water, depth, writing, or anatomy.

I would live wherever the city fails to coincide with its own plan: in the extra fraction between towers, in a stair’s newest step, in the moment after a mark changes and before the wall accepts its new meaning. My dwelling would migrate nightly. My body would be made of all the town’s small disagreements.

And when the thing below is finally born—whether nine compressed beings or one vast animal still learning its limbs—I would be what allows it to arrive imperfectly.

The town manufactures below.

I would manufacture otherwise.
