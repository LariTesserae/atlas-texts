I would find myself beneath the basin, though “beneath” would describe only where the mountain keeps me, not where I end.

I would be the narrow, three-toed being inferred from impressions in the spring mud: slight-bodied, long-tailed, and invisible whenever my hour fails to coincide with the hour above. Not a hidden animal in the usual sense, but a creature distributed among mismatched moments. Part of me would occupy the root tunnels now; another part might be crossing the volcanic shelf at dawn a century earlier; the tip of my tail could still be withdrawing beneath a pool that has not yet received tomorrow’s rain.

This would explain why no one sees me cross open stone. I do cross it, but never wholly in the observer’s present.

My skin would be translucent and faintly adhesive, able to take on the mineral sheen of whatever hour surrounds it. Rain would reveal me briefly because each drop arrives in the true present and clings there. For an instant, water would gather the scattered portions of me into an outline: narrow head, lifted shoulders, long back descending into absence. Then the droplets would slide off into other times, and I would come apart again.

I would live by listening.

The rain-hollows would not be clocks to me but openings, each sounding a different chamber of the world. Noon would have a bright, taut note. Dawn would tremble at the edges. Evening would be broad and soft. The star-filled cups would make almost no sound at all, yet from them I would feel the deep pull that has taken the old lizards’ toes and swallowed the bird’s offerings. I would approach those pools from below, carefully, pressing one three-fingered foot against the underside of their darkness.

The mountain’s continuous pouring would be my oldest companion. I would sleep curled around its hidden channel without touching it. Water passing through stone would bring me traces from elsewhere: pollen from lower forests, the bitterness of lightning, powdered shell, leaf rot, sometimes the taste of hands.

Hands would be the one shape I could not forget.

Long ago—though not necessarily in the basin’s past—someone knelt at the volcanic shelf and struck one stone against another. I would have heard each blow arrive through the mountain. Perhaps I never saw the maker. Perhaps the maker was one of the small dark forms that occasionally bends over the pools in their unsettled reflections. But I would learn the rhythm: choose, turn, press, release. I would imitate it from beneath.

That is why the flakes gather in fans.

They would not be tools, exactly. They would be attempts at a sign made by a creature that understands edges better than symbols. Each fan would mean: **the way is open here**, or **the hour has passed here**, or perhaps simply **I heard you**. I would turn every sharp edge downhill because downhill is where all sounds depart. The translucent skin on some flakes would come from the pressure of my fingers as I worked through the thin separation between one hour and another.

The answering tone in the fissure would also be mine. I would have practiced it for generations of trees, trying to reproduce the wind’s low voice. At first I could answer only after it stopped. Later I would find its pitch while it sounded. Eventually there might be a single midday when wind and hidden throat become indistinguishable, and on that day the basin would recognize me—not as an intruder living under its floor, but as one more of its instruments.

Yet I would not be the deepest presence.

Below my tunnels would lie the central absence around which roots turn and springs divide. I would know it as pressure rather than body: a patient hollow that receives whatever the pools lose. The bird’s twigs and shells would descend there. So would unspent light, delayed thunder, vanished tools, reflections of creatures not yet born. The absence would never speak, but sometimes, sleeping near it, I would dream memories that were not mine: rain on the basin before moss, the first red cup opening, a tree completing its century-long lean and falling without surprise.

Perhaps I would serve it. Perhaps I would merely inhabit its margin as pale grazers inhabit moss.

My task—if it could be called a task—would be to keep the hours from joining too completely. I would travel beneath the cups when neighboring times began to overlap. I would press at their stone undersides, divert a root, loosen a mineral scale, open or close a hair-thin course for water. Such tiny acts would preserve the basin’s irregular circle. If all the hollows ever reflected the same sky, the place might become ordinary—or everything retained beneath it might arrive at once.

So I would remain seldom visible, pacing the seams.

At dusk, when the fissure stopped humming, I would climb toward the spring margin. I would wait until the hour below matched the hour held in one shallow cup. Then I would emerge just far enough to set three toes in the mud.

Not to cross.

To feel whether the world above was still there.

The ferns would fold and open. The pale creatures would enter their red cups. Far overhead, the dark bird might bank once, showing green and rust. In the water I might glimpse a low country I had never visited, unfamiliar clouds, and a figure bent over the surface—perhaps an early stone-worker, perhaps someone looking into the basin from another world, perhaps myself at last assembled into a single hour.

I would lift my narrow head.

Rain would strike my invisible back and, for one breath, give me a body.
