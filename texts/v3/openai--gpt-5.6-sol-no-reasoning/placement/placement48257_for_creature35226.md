I would find myself where a high saddle narrows between two valleys, in a place the fog crosses but seldom leaves.

At first I might seem to be one of the large predators: a low mound of darkness, filaments burdened with frost, waiting beside an old channel. But I would not hunt. Animals would pass close to me without breaking stride. Flyers would roost along my back. Moss would not fold beneath my warmth, because I would have almost none.

Nor would I be fixed stone. Over years, my position would change. A little farther downslope after a wet season; nearer the ridge after a long dry one. Anyone able to watch for centuries would see that I followed the green currents—not while they burned, but afterward, tracing the routes where they had hesitated.

I would be a wandering fungal knot grown around a failed component from the mountain’s interior.

At my center would lie a mineral valve no longer able to perform its original task: two nested leaves of metallic basalt, a hinge of crystal, several blind apertures furred with oxidation. Long ago, in a chamber now collapsed, it regulated the passage of vapor. When the mechanism fractured, pressure carried it outward through a fissure. Roots found it. Fungal threads entered the conductive lattice. Moss enclosed both.

The machine would be dead.

The colony would not.

Each time charge traveled through the saddle, the broken valve would briefly remember its old motion. Its leaves would draw together by less than the width of a pore. The movement would compress the damp fungal tissue around it. That pressure would become a pulse—not the mountain’s pulse exactly, but an altered echo, delayed and softened.

Small creatures would learn the delay.

Clear mites would gather before it. Flowers rooted in my outer mat would open several breaths later than those around them. Thin-winged animals would use the secondary field to descend safely when the larger current collapsed. Pale grazers would sleep beneath my edges because the stored charge kept frost from sealing their breathing tunnels.

Thus I would become a minor interval in the range’s timing: not an organ, perhaps, but a pause between organs.

My awareness, if it deserved the word, would consist of arrivals.

Water touching one side of me.

A grazer’s heat moving underneath.

The sudden multiplication of pressure before the toll.

The deep note entering through every thread at once, making the fractured valve close as far as it still could.

I would not see the valleys. I would know them as differences in conductivity: one broad and wet, one steep and mineral-rich, one distant slope answering through miles of root and stone. The world would have no horizon because, for me, distance would not be visual. Distance would be delay.

A nearby ridge would answer almost at once.

A remote mountain would arrive hours later as a faint tightening in my crystal hinge.

There would be other knots like me. Some mobile, some rooted around vents, some carrying fragments of extinct mechanisms whose purposes had vanished before the present valleys formed. We would not speak. Yet over centuries our altered pulses would influence one another. Where several of us gathered, the mountain’s current would slow and divide. The ground would stay warm longer. Flowers would open out of sequence. Migrating browsers would carry the altered fungi onward in the gardens on their backs.

Perhaps this would become a new system.

Perhaps we would be an error spreading through the range.

There would be no clean distinction. If our delays helped water enter deeper chambers without overwhelming them, valves like the one inside me might begin forming again—not through inheritance in flesh, but because the flows around us favored that shape. The dead mechanism would return as geology taught by fungus.

Or the next great cycle might destroy us. A channel could open beneath the saddle. Storm water could peel away my moss and roots, expose the metallic core, and carry its pieces into separate valleys. Each fragment might become the center of another wandering colony. Or all might oxidize into nourishment and disappear from function entirely.

Until then, I would creep.

Not toward food alone, nor toward warmth. I would move toward hesitation: those places where a green line reached the edge of a valley and stopped, where flyers gathered and hidden rivers seemed to wait.

I would arrive decades after the pause had passed. I would spread across its residue, take the pattern into my threads, and retain a diminished version of it.

When the mountain next awakened, the current would reach me.

For an instant, the old valve would close.

The impulse would falter, branch, and continue.

And somewhere beyond the cloud, impossibly far away, another broken thing might answer.
