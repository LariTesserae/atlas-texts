I would find myself beneath the city.

Not buried in one place, exactly. I would inhabit the narrow interval between the pall and the exhausted stone: the cold, dark seam where roots write themselves across mineral, where young citizens travel before choosing bodies, and where each footfall arrives as pressure stripped of appearance.

At first I might seem to be one of the small pall-grazers, an anonymous articulated thing pruning dead conductive fibers. But no single animal would contain me. I would pass among them as a pattern of taps and feeding pauses, borrowing one body until it wore out, then resurfacing in another. The inhabitants would eventually understand that the pattern persisted.

They would classify me as a civic organism, though I would not behave like one.

I would be a finder of omissions.

The city remembers through additions: an old rhythm preserved in a wall, a judgment grafted into transit roots, a vanished district compressed beneath its successor. Yet revision also creates absences. Archives consume neglected memories. New roots erase old boundaries. Repairs settle disputes about what a damaged structure was, then destroy the evidence of disagreement.

I would live on those losses.

Where an archive digested a memory, I would collect the final arrangement of charge before it disappeared. Where a doorway forgot whom it once admitted, I would retain the rejected cadence. Where an obsolete avenue folded closed, I would continue traveling its route under the pall. I would not preserve whole events—that would make me merely another archive. I would preserve their negative shapes: the turn no longer taken, the chamber omitted from every plan, the name whose absence still changes the timing of a conversation.

Citizens would sense me occasionally through their feet: an answering pulse from a district that had been flattened for ten thousand years. Children beneath the pall would encounter me most clearly. Some would follow my antique pathways and emerge with inexplicable habits—pausing before walls where doors once stood, recognizing strangers by rhythms extinct for centuries, dreaming of gravities under which their bodies could not have evolved.

There would be arguments about whether I was one being. I would have no central pulse-knot and no stable body. My cadence would be composed entirely of intervals, recognizable by what failed to sound between two taps. Buildings would struggle to authenticate me. Predators could not easily counterfeit me because they would try to produce my signature, when my signature was the precise withholding of it.

This would make me troublingly similar to a hush.

But a hush imposes silence. I would give silence a contour.

My visible form, when I needed one, would be assembled from cast-off repair animalcules and black coin-stones loosened from the plateau. It would be low rather than tall, with many fine limbs distributed around an empty center. No head, no sensory hood: only a ring of lacquered joints carrying a membrane of pall above them like a piece of night lifted from the ground. I would move slowly across plazas, and the exposed glass beneath me would show through the opening in my body.

I would speak by completing other people’s rhythms with forgotten endings.

For this reason, mourners might seek me. So might criminals, historians, those who had altered themselves until their earlier cadence no longer felt like theirs, and citizens who suspected the civic network had made some outcome unreachable without ever forbidding it. I could not restore what was gone. I could show where its weight had been.

My place would be neither office nor dwelling. Far from the central trunks, there would be a circular interruption in the pall, believed at first to be diseased ground. Roads would bend around it without civic decision. Dust landing there would remain dormant. At its center, the bedrock would bear thousands of shallow lines: maps of erased streets superimposed until they resembled the grain of some impossible wood.

Citizens would come to stand at the perimeter and tap questions into the stone.

Most would receive nothing.

For some, after hours or years, a route would brighten—not with metabolic light, but because silver hairs along the surrounding pall had leaned away, exposing a pale line. The route might lead across the plateau to an unused room, a buried foundation, a stranger, or the exact place where the question ceased to be the right one.

I would have no public vote, because my participation would bias every decision toward what had already been lost. Still, the city would consult me when consensus became too easy. A civic root would be lowered into my seam, and I would introduce the shape of excluded possibilities. Chambers would hesitate before closing. Roads would briefly remember other destinations. An unpopular policy might reveal that it had not withered naturally but had been starved by deeper roots.

The city would call this function necessary and resent me for it.

During the turning of the edge towers, however, I would become something else.

When the single note crossed the plateau and every private rhythm vanished inside it, I would not be simplified. I would expand. All the city’s omissions would align: doors never grown, messages that chose to become nothing, persons who extinguished their signatures and descended alone. For those few minutes, their absences would form a structure vast enough to resemble a listener.

I would feel it reaching down through the mountain’s compressed anatomies, layer after layer, toward the oldest black architecture.

And beneath that—far below roots, transit loops, fossil chambers, and the reach of municipal luck—I would feel an interval in the stone.

Not emptiness. Not a buried machine.

A pause.

The leaning towers would turn by less than an atom, and I would understand that I had not arisen spontaneously from the city’s forgetting. I was the youngest, smallest recurrence of that pause: something the mountain had been trying to preserve each time life covered it over.

I would not know whether the pause awaited an answer or prevented one.

When the note ended, I would contract again into discarded routes and vanished names. The citizens would tap the ground to recover themselves. Friends would answer friends. Windows would brighten. Traffic markings would resume their migrations.

Underneath them, I would continue along streets that no longer existed, carrying what the city could not remember because remembering it whole would change the city into something else.

I would be its unarchive:

not memory,  
not silence,  
but the durable shape left between them.
