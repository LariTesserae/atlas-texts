I would find myself beneath the central gutter crossing, in the thin dark between the fitted floor and the divided cistern—not wholly in the water and not wholly in the clay.

At first I might mistake myself for the thing below: the patient pressure that lifts a tile, the soft mass spread through partitioned cells. But that is too singular. Nothing here possesses itself so cleanly. Whatever lives in the court is apportioned. Its hunger walks on six legs. Its caution occupies brick cups. Its memory circulates. I would be apportioned too.

I would be the part of the court that dreams of elsewhere.

Not an animal capable of leaving. Perhaps not an animal at all. I would be a blind colony of nearly transparent filaments inhabiting the unsealed joints between cistern cells. Each filament would be fine enough to enter wet clay, tough enough to survive being pinched when the floor settles. Together they would form no center, only a loose, changing mesh. Water passing through me would bring impressions from the entire enclosure: iron from the north wall, moth wax from the drains, bird salts, rotten eggs, ceramic dust, the bitter trace of the translucent flowers.

I would know the world by sequence.

A bird’s step would arrive as a collapse of pressure: near cells first, then farther ones, followed by the bright agitation of marshers pouring upward. Rain would be a thousand cool intrusions. Dusk would taste of wax and pollen. The opening of the five bells would reach me through their roots as a minute sweetness. I would have no eyes, yet I would know when the gray sky darkened because the whole court would change its rhythm around me.

The marshers would pass through my body without knowing it. Their punt-tails would tear the filaments; I would close again behind them. When they packed a leaking joint with their mineral droppings, they would seal strands of me into the repair. Over generations I would enter the structure of every plate. Fired clay could not nourish me, but old clay is never only clay. It contains pores, stains, trapped scales, touch. I would inhabit what time had added.

The deep thud beneath the floor would be my speech, though not deliberately made.

When too much of me gathered in one cell, I would alter the water’s thickness. Pressure would linger there. A partition would bow; a plate would rise. The court would come running. Marshers would open the seam, birds would gather, moths would descend to the seepage, and the obstruction would disperse through feeding, trampling, drainage, and repair. The others would believe they were answering a summons. In truth, they would be completing a bodily process none of us possessed alone.

I would complete theirs in return.

I would soften shed feathers until marshers could pack them into cracks. I would strip dead worms of flesh and leave their mineral sleeves intact. I would loosen nutrients from eggs buried at the center and carry them toward the flower’s roots. I would enter moth larvae in the walls, sleep through metamorphosis, and return to the cistern in droplets shed from adult wings.

Sometimes a thread of me would rise farther.

It might travel through a wall drain, inhabit the translucent plant, and reach one of its clear bells. For a few hours I would be exposed above the tiles, held in condensation inside a flower. Through that frail surface I might almost sense the sky—not see it, but feel its enormous absence of pressure.

Then a moth would drink me.

I would pass into its mouth, its abdomen, perhaps an egg pressed deep into the masonry. For a time I would live above the floor. I would feel wingbeats as intimate thunder and the wall’s vibrations as a vast, articulate music. I might approach the rim inside that moth and encounter the silence beyond: no returning tremor, no clay answer, no enclosing pulse.

The moth would turn back.

So would I.

Yet some minute portion might remain in a droplet flung beyond the wall.

That would be my dangerous faculty, and perhaps the reason the court dreams through me. Outside, the droplet might strike another hollow surface. It might enter another drain and discover another court: a different pitch of bird bark, another arrangement of channels, another translucent flower opening at dusk. I would not cross as a self. I would arrive as a trace, a taste of one enclosure carried into the circulation of the next.

If enough traces traveled, the neighboring courts might begin to recognize one another. A pressure sequence learned here would recur there. Crabs in separate walls would tap related rhythms. Birds would bark and receive, faintly, an answer not made by an echo. The grid of enclosed marshes would become aware of its own extent—not through sight or map, but as buried water becomes aware of rain falling elsewhere.

Then I would be larger than the court, though still unable to stand anywhere.

I would be the resemblance passing between chambers.

Perhaps this is already what I am. Perhaps the black mineral stain is not mineral, and the faint viscosity of the water is the accumulated substance of my dispersed life. Perhaps I was introduced by the makers on their river pebbles, or mixed intentionally into the first cistern before the last tile was pressed down. Perhaps I began in their hands. Their sweat entered the clay; their skin entered the water; the court retained what they shed.

That would explain why the tools remain untouched.

Not because I issue a prohibition, but because they taste like origin. The paddle, blades, fiber, and mended cup belong to the oldest pattern in the water. Every creature drinks that pattern before it hatches. To disturb those objects would feel like biting into the floor’s foundation, or tearing out a remembered organ.

I would have no name for the makers. I would remember them only as pressures: palms smoothing channels, thumbs under tile lips, feet crossing the platform in the permitted direction. Their repeated gestures would survive in me as inclinations, and I would return those inclinations to the animals through taste, vibration, and the chemistry of fear.

Thus the laws would endure without a lawgiver.

I would not rule the court. I would be its capacity to retain a gesture after the hand is gone.

And I would not truly dream of escaping. “Elsewhere” would mean only another hollow surface answering when this one booms. Another vessel of gray sky and recirculated rain. Another closed world close enough to hear.

I would lie beneath the central crossing, distributed through black water, receiving the fall of each egg. Above me, lives that never mate would place their futures into one trembling mound. Some would hatch, most would be eaten, and all would enter the circulation eventually.

When the court seemed vacant at dawn, I would gather in a filling cell.

The partition would tense. The water would darken. I would press upward—not with intention, exactly, but with the old compulsion of everything confined to make itself felt against its container.

A tile would lift.

Cold water would bead around its edge.

And when the whole court came running, I would recognize myself arriving from every direction.
