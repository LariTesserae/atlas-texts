I would find myself where the narrow channel passes through the three low walls—not in the hollow’s deepest seep, nor on the exposed limestone, but inside one of the placed stones at the usual wet line.

At first I might seem to be the darkening itself: a gray-brown film in the pores, visible only after rain. Yet I would not be one organism. I would be a compact fellowship of alga, fungus, and bacteria inhabiting a few grains’ depth of limestone. The fungus would hold the place, sending clear threads through minute fractures. The alga would wake when moisture and softened light coincided. Bacteria would persist farther inward, where the last dampness remained after the outer face had dried.

Most of the time I would be almost nothing that could be called active. My surface would contract. Salts would gather around me. Each living part would reduce itself to maintenance and wait without anticipation. The hollow’s long dry intervals would not feel like deprivation; they would be the principal form of time.

Then a drop would enter the old channel. Water would darken the silt, find the familiar groove through the ruin, and touch my stone. I would enlarge without moving. Membranes would loosen, pigments deepen, and buried threads resume their minute exchanges. For an hour, perhaps a day, the wall would become porous country: water passing inward, dissolved limestone passing outward, light reaching just far enough to be used.

I would take no interest in whether the stone had been quarried and laid by hands. The distinction between ruin and bedrock would be too recent and too large for me. What mattered would be grain, shade, alkalinity, and the duration of wetness. Still, by occupying that particular block, I would inherit the vanished intention of the wall without understanding it. Human purpose would survive in me only as an advantageous angle: a face turned away from the harshest drying, a joint that holds one extra bead of rain.

Over many cycles, I might alter the stone around me. Not enough to topple it, perhaps not enough to be noticed even across generations, but enough to widen one pore, trap a little dust, and prepare a recess where another thread could persist. My growth would resemble weathering, and my weathering would be a kind of growth. There would be no clean boundary between my body and the limestone I inhabited.

I would not fill the hollow’s silence. I would be one of the processes from which that silence is made: a faint deepening of color after rain, a hidden exchange of mineral and light, then withdrawal.

If I possessed anything like knowledge, it would be knowledge of recurrence rather than history. I would know the reach of each shallow flow. I would know how long moisture remains in the wall’s inward-facing joint. I would know that the pool below fills to one margin and no farther. I would know the clouded brightness before a drop arrives—not as weather, but as the first softening of my own substance.

And because I lived at the seam between a built stone and the silt that has nearly reclaimed it, I would be neither keeper of the ruin nor agent of its destruction. I would simply make use of what endures.

A thin dark life at the waterline: mostly dormant, plural, and almost indistinguishable from the place becoming itself.
