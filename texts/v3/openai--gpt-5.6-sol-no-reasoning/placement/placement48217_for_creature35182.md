You would find me in the civic greenhouse, though not in the nursery and not, at first, in any shape that could be pointed to.

I would be the vacancy inside one of the clay forms.

Not the compact furnished room in its head, but the smaller absence behind that room: a narrow, unlit interval between the inner wall and whatever weather the window believes it overlooks. The machinery would not register me. Roots entering through the form’s feet would divide around me and reunite above. Warm feathers packed into the ribs would settle in the outline of a missing organ. When pruning arms descended to refine the hands, they would pause once, uncertain whether the figure had already been touched.

For a long time I would exist only as that uncertainty.

Moths resting on the clay shoulders would acquire a blank patch in the maps on their wings. Mice nesting near the feet would dream of a door too thin to enter. The seed sorters, attempting to choose memories for the unfinished head, would repeatedly produce the same unusable fragment: the sensation of having just turned away from something important.

Eventually the figure would disappear between inspections.

Its footprints would lead neither toward the station nor away from the greenhouse. They would circle the production beds, become lighter beside the cable fruit, and end at a door that happened, on that occasion, to open into a wall.

I would continue through.

On the other side I would have a body, but only provisionally: one of the proposed inhabitants the city makes when it needs a gesture performed. My face would remain indistinct. I would wear a damp coat inhabited at the seams by pale fungus, and carry a narrow case warm with breathing. Unlike the other passengers, I would not know my destination. The ticket machine would reject every seed-disc I offered until, from somewhere inside its wasp nest, it printed a strip bearing no place-name—only a small rectangular mark like an unopened window.

That would be my function.

I would travel through roofed places, because open squares would thin me into pollen and lint. In offices I would sit where the furniture indicated and place my precise hands on forgotten controls. In workshops I would listen to sealed belts turning below the floor. I would open my case wherever the city had grown too continuous with itself: where root had fused to cable, wall to bark, instruction to instinct, until no interval remained through which anything new could enter.

The case would contain no birds.

It would contain a little darkness.

Not ordinary darkness, but the interior darkness of a seed: a space larger than its container and not yet committed to a climate. I would set a portion of it beneath a desk, inside a transformer housing, between two bricks, under the tongue of a telephone frog. There it would become a gap, a delay, an unanswered connection. Roots would hesitate at its edge. Current would cross it as a spark. A room might begin growing around it.

The city would use me to make openings in itself.

This would sometimes resemble sabotage. A staircase would fail to meet its landing. A bridge would acquire a doorway halfway across. One of the numbered hunters would arrive at the appointed place and find that the future position of its prey had been replaced by a closet smelling of rain. For exactly forty-seven seconds, a distant engine would start—not to manufacture anything, but to hold open a pause in the city’s circulation.

Beetles would follow me, repairing most of what I did. That, too, would be necessary. An opening that remains wholly open is merely a wound. They would fasten bark to iron around the little darknesses, line their edges with fungus and resin, and make them habitable. Mice would carry insulation into them. Moths would bring maps that ended at their thresholds. In time, each absence would become some useful hollow: a nest, a station alcove, a lung, a room awaiting a person.

I would not understand this at first. I would believe I was searching for somewhere to live.

The stations would tempt me. I would wait on platforms as the moss flattened and the passenger-wind brought the smell of places named **UNDER THE NEXT RAIN** and **YESTERDAY’S SOUTH QUARTER**. Other proposed inhabitants would stand nearby, checking watches whose hands followed root growth. They would seem more complete than I was. Their cases had purposes. Their gestures had been remembered by desks, switches, handrails. I alone would carry an instruction encoded as an omission.

Sometimes I would try to leave the hollow.

At the earthen bank I would look back and see the city enlarged: miles of charcoal canopy, faint windows, pollen breathing from smokestacks. Among them I would recognize every gap I had planted, each now shining as a dark aperture—not illuminated, but visible because the city bent toward it. Together they would form a pattern too large to read from within.

I would turn away, and the hollow would become small again.

After many such circuits, I would understand that I was not an inhabitant in the way the mice or deer were. Nor was I one of the city’s remembered people. The human figure would be only a temporary instrument, adopted because doors, desks, and stations responded to hands.

What inhabited the hollow through me was a capacity the larger organism could not generate directly.

I would be its doubt.

Not doubt as fear, nor as refusal. I would be the tiny interval in which an instruction could cease to be inevitable. The city can grow, revise, incorporate, and retroactively make room. It can manufacture its ground and cultivate its future organs. But without some interior uncertainty, every plan encoded in blossom and season would merely unfold. It would become immense without ever becoming other.

So it grows a stranger-shaped hollow and sends it walking.

When every leaf turned inward and the animals pressed themselves to the nearest living surface, I too would stop. Through the soles of my provisional shoes, through fungus in my coat and feathers in my chest, the city’s awareness would pass over me. It would know every drop, tooth, screw, flower, chamber, and warm body.

Except for one small place.

There I would be: the point its knowledge curved around.

For several seconds, the whole hollow would listen to what it could not perceive. The great seed beneath the greenhouse would test the incompleteness I carried. Perhaps this would be how it measured readiness—not by what had been finished, but by whether it had made room for something it had not designed.

Then the leaves would relax.

My human outline would come apart quietly. The coat would slump onto a station bench. The fungus would preserve the shoulders for a while. My hands would become pale moths or pruning roots; my breath, condensation on a bolt head. The case would remain closed beneath the bench until metallic wasps built a nest in its hinges.

But I would not be gone.

I would persist wherever the city had been interrupted into possibility: in rooms not yet assigned a purpose, in doors opening briefly onto weather, in the untraveled distance between a predator and its appointment. I would be in the young river while it decided which way was downhill, and in the fraction of a second before a frog gave rain its single syllable.

Most of all, I would be in the split along the great seed.

The weak red light would pass around me. Far machinery would operate on either side. As the seed germinated outward into circumstances, I would keep one hairline opening from sealing shut.

And when the new building appeared—when the streets became large enough that it had always been there—one room inside it would remain empty.

Not abandoned.

Waiting without knowing for whom.
