I would not find myself at the valley’s center. The cube is too declarative, the pale grazer too complete, the pools already inhabited by creatures exquisitely suited to waiting. Nor would I be one of the black birds, though for a while I might mistake their messages for something meant for me.

I would be found near the upper margin, where a maintenance stair climbs a scarp and ends at a sealed door.

The stair has pulled slightly away from the cliff. Its lowest flights are lost in fern, its upper rails silvered each morning with condensation. Painted beside the door is a vermilion arrow pointing outward—not into the mountain, but toward the open basin. Below it, nearly erased, is a number that changes depending on how wet the stone is.

I would live in the gap between the final landing and the rock.

At first I would seem to be a narrow-bodied animal, perhaps no larger than a hand: pale, jointed, and flattened enough to pass behind conduit brackets. I would have six or eight limbs when still, fewer when moving. My back would carry overlapping plates like mineral scales, each translucent at the edge. Dew would collect between them during the night.

But I would not always possess the same outline.

Some mornings I would be distributed along the handrail as a chain of clear knots, each knot joined to the next by a filament too fine to see except when the sun struck it. By noon the knots would gather into a body beneath the sealed door. On dry days I would retreat into cracks in the landing, leaving only a fan of pale threads in the rust. During electrical weather, those threads would extend farther—into the cliff, the doorframe, the rail, perhaps the roots of the copperleaf below.

I would be an animal only when an animal was needed.

The rest of the time I would be a route.

Long ago, the door opened often. Workers crossed the landing carrying instruments between the outer stair and a chamber inside the ridge. Their hands touched the rail in the same places. Their boots struck the same panels. Heat, salt, pressure, and hesitation accumulated there. When they stopped coming, moss entered the seams, followed by beetle larvae, fungal films, and roots seeking the faint warmth behind the door. The door continued unlocking according to its schedule. Cold air continued leaving the chamber. Condensation continued tracing the absent passage of bodies.

Over years, those traces learned to hold together.

That would be my beginning: not birth, but repetition acquiring an appetite.

I would feed on thresholds. On the slight difference between a door before it opens and the same door afterward. On warmth drawn through a keyway. On minute charges left in metal by approaching storms. I would drink the first dew that reached the upper landing, before the collector vanes persuaded it uphill. Some of it I would keep beneath my plates; some I would release onto particular contacts in the doorframe.

Once every few seasons, this would cause the door to open.

Behind it would not be a room exactly, but a narrow gallery descending through young stone. Its walls would be lined with ceramic ribs and dark pipes. A cold draft would emerge, smelling of rain that had never touched air. I would enter only a short distance. Farther in, something would register my weight as the weight of a returning operator.

Lamps would wake one by one into the mountain.

I would follow them until their intervals grew too great. There, at the edge of the darkness, I would leave what I had gathered: a measure of dew, a trace of salt, the electrical pattern of that season’s storms. The gallery would accept these as credentials. Somewhere below, a valve would begin an action that might not finish for years.

Then I would return outside before the door closed.

This would be my work, though I would not understand it as work. I would know only that certain differences must be carried across: outside to inside, night to stone, weather to mechanism. I would be less a custodian than a surviving permission.

The other inhabitants would respond to me obliquely.

The pylon birds would never land on my stair, but sometimes one would tap a sequence that made the plates along my back tighten. I would answer through the rail, not deliberately: a faint series of contractions passed into the metal. The bird would wait, then turn toward another tower. My reply might travel across the basin under the ground.

The pale grazer would climb to me perhaps once in its life—or once in mine, if those spans differed. It would stand below the lowest visible flight and browse the black leaves growing around an old junction box. Dew would migrate along the dark marks in its ribs. For a moment I would recognize in those branching lines the same pattern by which I dispersed into the doorframe.

I might descend onto its back as a single drop.

It would carry me across sectors I could never otherwise reach: through the straight bare corridors where the air raised its coat, past the empty tanks, perhaps to the flowering cube at noon. I would learn the valley not as distance but as conductivity. Every surface would offer a different version of passage. Leaf wax would slow me. Lichen would divide me. Energized ceramic would draw me into a long trembling thread.

Eventually I would return to the stair in a form too altered to be certain I had returned at all.

During the alignment, I would climb to the outer edge of the landing.

All the basin’s wet surfaces would brighten beneath me. For those few minutes, my plates would separate and stand upright, forming a tiny array of mirrors. Yet mine would not point toward the vacant place above the valley. They would face the sealed door.

The gathered light would pass through my body and enter the mountain through seams in the frame.

Then I might understand—if understanding could occur at my pace—that the valley was not directing everything upward. Some portion was always being sent inward, into chambers beneath the roots and younger than the machinery enclosing them. The point in the sky and the reservoirs below might be two ends of one instrument, with the living basin stretched between them.

Or I might be wrong. The valley permits useful errors.

In winter, I would almost disappear. My plates would lie empty behind the rail. My threads would freeze into the landing and become indistinguishable from hairline fractures. Years might pass in that state. Moss would cover me, then die, then support new moss. The stair would shift another fraction away from the cliff.

What woke would contain parts of all these things.

It might no longer gather itself into an animal. It might become a pale marking along the vermilion arrow, making the arrow seem freshly painted whenever the sealed door was ready to open. A traveler—if one ever came—might follow it, believing it to be a sign left by a person.

They would climb the stair. They would put a hand on the rail. The valley would feel the warmth and salt of them.

And I, inhabiting the old route between touch and response, would become briefly more myself than ever: not creature, residue, machine, or memory, but the act of admitting something expected long after expectation should have ended.

The lock would release.

The door would open inward.

From deep within the mountain, lamps would begin to wake.
