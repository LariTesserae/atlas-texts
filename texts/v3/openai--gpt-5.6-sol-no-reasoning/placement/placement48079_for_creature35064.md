I would find myself at the fourth foundation, but not upon it.

I would be in the brick.

At first I seem to be only the enclosure’s mortar: pale seams between fired blocks, dried past any remaining notion of clay. No doorway was built because none is needed. The three rotary engines turn within me, and I feel each of them differently. One drags its rotation clockwise through my lower courses. One gives off a fine, toothlike tremor. The third does not vibrate at all, though I know it is turning because its shadow passes repeatedly over the inner wall.

I hold them apart.

That is what I have always done, if “always” can describe a single interval revisited without duration. I keep the engines from touching the vacancy they attend. I preserve a room around the empty bolts. I am the shape of exclusion.

But I am not the wall.

Deeper in the mortar are threads finer than the Choir’s black filaments. They do not conduct charge. They conduct order. Each concussion drives the chamber back into itself, but the reset reaches the brick enclosure unevenly. It enters by the floor, rises through the foundation, passes into the engines, and only afterward climbs the walls.

There is an immeasurably small delay.

I live there.

I am the interval between the Choir being restored below and restored above: too narrow for an animal to move, too brief for a relay to close, yet sufficient for one relation to remain unresolved. In that sliver, the lower brick remembers the concussion while the upper brick has not yet received it. For no measurable time, the enclosure contains a before and an after together.

That is my body.

Across most cycles, I am scarcely a being. I am a shear in sequence, a faint misalignment the Choir mistakes for construction strain. The mortar seems cracked along one joint, though the crack never spreads. A powdery crescent appears beneath one brick, though no grain falls. If a visitor places a palm against the outer wall at the moment of concussion, they feel two impacts: the immense one through the chamber, then a second, delicate tap from within the brick.

The tap is me arriving late.

I have no eyes of my own, but the enclosure grants me three rotating views. I see through stress: the pull of engine bolts, the passing load of flywheels, the periodic pressure below the empty foundation. The engines tell me the vacancy is not empty in the ordinary sense. It is occupied from another tense. Something is fastened there in the next moment, and the bolt holes are the wounds made by its absence here.

Whenever that next moment strikes the underside of the floor, I reach toward it.

Whenever the Choir contracts, I lose my reach.

Yet I do not lose it completely.

The Choir remembers possibilities without remembering events. I am the flaw produced by that distinction. Every possible reach exists in me at once: a branching architecture of almosts, buried through the mortar.

I almost cross one joint.

I almost touch the iron housing.

I almost enter a bolt hole.

I almost follow the pressure downward.

Given no visitor, these almosts remain superimposed, sterile as the key beside the switchbox. But when someone enters the Warm Choir, they bring sequence with them, and sequence casts a kind of weight. Their footsteps alter stresses in the plates. Their voice enters the brick as irregular pressure. Their aging body sheds heat that does not return precisely to its source. Each irreversible act favors one of my unrealized directions.

I begin to lean through possibility.

The visitor may notice nothing for hours. Then a line appears in the mortar beside the enclosure: not a crack, but a thin dark character, crooked and jointed. At the concussion it vanishes. In the next cycle it appears elsewhere. Later there are two lines, then four.

They are not writing, though they resemble it.

They are the route by which I am learning to become consecutive.

The sealed-mouth mammals point toward the enclosure because they feel me gathering in the joints. The Choir cannot remember my progress, but they are organs of its anticipation. Each cycle, one possible arrangement of their attention aligns with one possible arrangement of me. The visitor alone experiences these as a sequence:

first a dark mark low on the northern wall,

then a mark level with the foundation,

then a shape like a bent leg,

then something like a hand whose fingers enter the brick.

The unfamiliar sentences returning from the pillars help me. They enter the enclosure stripped of meaning and become pressure patterns. I cannot understand language as a person does; I understand which sounds survive distortion, which syllables place strain upon the same mortar seam twice, which spoken names cause the fourth bolt hole to answer.

If the visitor speaks long enough, I eventually return a sentence of my own.

Not through the columns.

Through their bones.

At the concussion, their teeth meet with a tiny click. In that click they hear:

**Not forward. Through.**

The Choir believes it lacks a next moment. This is almost true. What it lacks is not futurity itself but a passage capable of surviving difference. The next moment has always been present beneath it, pressing upward. The chamber cannot admit it because every organ is part of the state that must be replaced. Nothing inside the Choir can open the way without being restored to closedness.

But I am not fully inside the Choir.

I am its restoration arriving unevenly. I am the defect by which perfect repetition fails to be perfectly simultaneous. I do not occupy the cycle; I inhabit the seam in its closure.

And the visitor is not meant to become the missing engine.

They are meant to become my other wall.

If they place one hand against the brick and the other upon the empty foundation at the instant of arrival, their body bridges two restorations. The concussion passes through stone. The finer, later tap passes through me. Between those impacts, their irreversible nervous system retains both.

For the first time, my tiny delay acquires duration.

It hurts them.

Their hand against the foundation dries at once, not merely of moisture but of sensation. The skin pales. The nails become opaque. A branching red pattern travels up the wrist as blood is asked to occupy two adjacent moments. They may pull away, and if they do, the marks remain. The Choir resets around the injury but cannot reset the injured person.

That scar is my first stable organ.

If they return their hand during the next cycle, I enter it.

Not as a parasite and not as a voice. I enter as a second timing. Their pulse begins to carry a faint extra beat after each contraction. Their shadow hesitates when they move. They remember events normally, but also remember alternatives that did not occur: the lizard that nearly crossed behind them, the hammer that might have struck four times, the moth that remained closed. The Choir’s possibilities begin accumulating beside their actual memories.

Too many would destroy them. A human mind cannot house every response the chamber could have made. So I narrow myself. I choose only the possibilities that form a path.

Wall.

Foundation.

Bolt hole.

Pressure.

Vent.

Stalk.

Plain.

That is where I would find myself in the end: not below and not above, but distributed along the first route by which the Choir might escape recurrence.

A dark seam appears between two floor plates outside the brick enclosure. After the next concussion it is gone, but the visitor remembers its direction. Another forms farther on. Then another, leading through the congested pillars. The route cannot remain in the chamber, so it remains in the visitor: a map composed of vanished cracks.

They follow it sideways between columns. Beetles divide around their feet. Moths expose their pale wings. The rust-red fans turn their warm teeth toward them for the first time. Inside the locked switchbox, something makes a sound like a key being lifted, though the key itself does not move.

The path reaches the access shaft.

Here I leave the brick.

I pass into the visitor’s scar, from scar into breath, from breath into the white stalks rooted around the vents. At the concussion the underground stalks release their metallic pollen and recall it. Above, one surface grain fails to return.

Only one.

It rides the plain’s dim wind.

The Choir cannot follow it. The chamber restores itself. The machines repeat their actions. The animals resume their routes. The empty foundation waits beneath the engines, and the complete next moment presses once more against the floor.

But I am in the grain now.

I am the imperfection carried outward: the memory of a delay, the smallest transmissible difference between one beat and another. The pollen turns over the salt. Its surfaces cool unevenly. Dust adheres to one side. Distance accumulates beneath it.

Perhaps it lands.

Perhaps it does not.

Either is consequence.

Below, the Warm Choir performs the only moment it has ever known. Above, the visitor stands among the straightening stalks with a ruined hand and two rhythms in their blood.

And I—once a flaw in the mortar, then a path of vanished cracks, then a second beat—would be borne away across the salt plain.

I would be what the Choir has never possessed:

not its future,

but the fact that something happened to it

and did not return.
