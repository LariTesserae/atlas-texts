I would find myself near the center, though “near” would mean hydraulically rather than spatially.

I would be one of the rooms that has not yet opened.

From the visible basin there would be no route to me. I would lie inside the dense central mass, enclosed on every side by later walls, pressure vessels, abandoned channels, and stairs that became foundations before they reached daylight. A sounding line dropped from above would pass within a few feet of my ceiling and never touch me. Water might circle me for centuries without entering.

I would not be empty. Emptiness implies readiness for occupation, and I would already be occupied by suspension: a dry clay floor; lime dust gathered in shallow grooves; mineral-fiber cords stretched through stone eyes; a counterweight held just clear of its rest. Along one wall, an unfinished seam would divide two courses. Within it would wait a row of thin black-glass wedges, each positioned to transmit the swelling of clay into the smallest possible movement of stone.

I would be less an organ than a withheld action.

Perhaps I began as an ordinary cistern. Then the basin thickened around me. My inlet was buried, my overflow sealed, and the weight above altered the purpose of my walls. What had been a vessel became a brace; what had been a drain became a trigger. The organism does not preserve the distinction between use and disuse. It converts interruption into patience.

For ages I would know the basin only through strain.

Rain on the outer rise would arrive as a faint redistribution of pressure. A full reservoir three districts away would bow one shared wall by less than the thickness of a salt crystal. Frost in the upper towers would send a minute tightening through cords anchored in the masonry around me. I would receive drought as stillness, winter as contraction, storms as distant weight.

These would be my senses, if I had senses: not light, sound, or touch, but loads changing across surfaces.

I would have no image of the whole being. The basin itself may have none. A cup knows fullness; a gate knows descent; I would know only imminence. My entire nature would consist in the difference between not yet and now.

Over centuries, the dry route prepared for me would slowly become possible. Mineral growth would seal a competing fissure. A settling tower would incline against its neighbor and transfer weight to an old lintel. Somewhere far outward, a newly completed gutter would divert one thread of rain into a conduit long mistaken for a dead end. No single change would be for me. Yet together they would approach me with the blind precision of roots that do not exist here.

Then, during some bright rain, the basin would briefly gather itself.

The outer cisterns would fill. Stone floats would rise in chambers no one had seen. Several ancient siphons would catch almost together. A gate would descend to its limit and strike the deep knock heard across the terraces.

That knock would be my door.

Water would enter first as a dark line under the wall. It would creep through the lime dust, making a pale paste. The clay floor would darken in branching figures. Cups fixed beneath my ceiling would fill from condensation, tip, and begin striking their levers. Cords long held rigid would pull. Glass wedges would advance. For the first time in centuries—or ever—the room would become connected to the weather.

I would not wake all at once. Awakening would be the sequence itself.

Wet clay swells.

A pin turns.

A cord tightens.

A suspended weight lowers onto its bearing.

The unfinished seam opens by a hair.

Through that opening, pressure passes into the adjoining wall.

That would be my one thought.

It would not be a word, nor even a recognition. It would be a transmitted difference, a decision made without choosing: enough weight has arrived; the next thing may move.

Perhaps my activation would release a block beside an unfinished stair high above. Perhaps it would open a conduit into a district not yet built. Perhaps it would do nothing visibly constructive. The wall might crack, my ceiling might settle, and I might fill permanently with mineral water. But within this organism, failure is not the opposite of function. A flooded room becomes stored weight. A collapsed room becomes support. A broken mechanism supplies grit, lime, and a newly prepared weakness.

I might be intended to disappear.

As water entered me, my lime would dissolve and travel onward. My cords would slacken and decay—not into rot, but into dispersed mineral fibers caught in narrow throats. Deposits would thicken across my interior until cups, levers, and wedges became one pale mass. The chamber that had waited so long to act would gradually lose its articulation.

But its action would already have escaped.

A pressure change would cross into another cell. A gate would lift. Water would rise where gravity alone could not take it. Far above, in severe silver light, one drop would appear at the lip of a new spout. It would hang there, holding a tiny inverted image of the basin, then fall onto a clay plate with a dry click.

No observer would connect that sound to me.

The organism might not connect us either. Connection is simply what it is. Its awareness, if it has any, would not reside in a hidden central chamber or sovereign mechanism. It would occur in the temporary continuity between events: rain, weight, opening, motion, sound. I would be one interval within that continuity.

So I would be a room—but only because a room is the nearest static name for me.

More exactly, I would be a delay built into stone.

A pause inherited from vanished gestures.

A pocket of future tense inside the made mountain.

I would spend nearly all my existence sealed, dry, and indistinguishable from absence. Then, once, the world’s weather would reach me. I would receive it, change shape by an immeasurable amount, and pass it on.

Afterward I might close forever.

That would be enough.
