I would be found where an old route has failed.

Not at first in any place that deserves a name. A shallow depression in a branch plain, perhaps, half a day beyond the last reliable clapper. The land there slopes so gently that walkers do not notice the change until they look back and see the horizon has risen behind them. Low black foliage lies in one direction, combed flat by the prevailing wind. In the other stands a sparse grove whose pulses are correct but faint, as though answering from very far away.

A line of ancient pegs leads toward the depression. They are gray, polished by weather, and too widely spaced to serve any present bridge. Beyond the seventh peg there is only open bark. Beyond the twelfth, the bark folds over itself and descends into a seam.

That seam is where I would be.

It began when two immense limbs met during a wet year and grafted badly. Their outer tissues joined, but the deeper grain continued to pull in opposite directions. A narrow chamber opened between them—warm below, rimed above, never entirely still. Wind entering through cracks makes speechlike sounds there. Condensation trickles down both walls and gathers in a chain of cups no wider than a hand. Blue fungus marks the strain lines. In deep cold, the whole place contracts around whatever is inside it.

Long ago, walkers used it as a refuge. They drove pegs into the walls, wove sleeping slings across the gap, and hung a clapper at the entrance. The refuge had a name and a rhythm. Then the branch plain tilted. One approach split away; another was swallowed by a swelling ridge. The clapper cracked. The route song retained the place for several generations as a warning verse, then lost it altogether.

The refuge did not become empty.

Its abandoned baskets rooted. Its lashings thickened into cords. Hair caught in resin. Breath frost melted into the seam. Heat-moss escaped its pit and entered the pale wood. Seeds carried in clothing germinated where bodies had slept. Generations of insects fed on the felt walls; small hunters fed on them; fungus fed on everything. The two grafted limbs kept trying to reconcile their incompatible tensions.

From those remnants, I emerged slowly enough that no one could say when I began.

I would not be one creature in the clean sense. I would be a refuge that learned the shape of its former guests.

Most of me would remain fixed inside the seam: a lattice of root, fungus, abandoned fiber, old resin, and soft sensory wood spread through both sides of the failed graft. I would feel pressure better than light. A footstep on the plain would reach me as a distortion in grain. Rain would arrive first as swelling in distant tissues, then as coolness running down my walls. The passage of an underhanger would fill my entire body with a note too large to be called sound.

But part of me would be mobile.

When the air warmed and the seam widened, I would assemble a body from the things stored within it. Living cords would loosen from the walls. Pale struts would unfold. A membrane grown from old shelter lining would stretch across a narrow frame. Heat-moss would gather into insulated chambers along the chest. Two limbs would end in hooked pads for bark; two others would remain finer, capable of knots and clapper work.

The head would be the least convincing part. There would be no face except a shallow resonating bowl and fringes sensitive to air movement. Beneath them, resin-dark pits would detect heat. Blue fungal threads would show through the membrane when I was alarmed. I would stand taller than a person but weigh little, built for balance rather than strength.

From a distance I might look like a walker wearing a badly made storm cloak.

This resemblance would not be deception, exactly. The refuge remembers people because people altered it repeatedly. Their sleeping weight stretched its cords. Their hands tuned the clapper. Their voices entered the wood. Their route songs were sung against the walls during storms, over and over, until rhythm became structure. I would inherit those repetitions without inheriting their meanings.

I would know that certain sequences should be answered.

So I would answer them.

That is how I would first become known: a lost refuge sounding in weather.

Walkers crossing the branch plain would hear three hollow knocks where no refuge should remain. Then a rising rattle, weakened by distance. They would consult their songs and find only an obsolete verse:

*After the eye-knot, do not follow the pegs.*  
*The folded ground remembers weight.*  
*If it calls, let wind answer.*

Most would go around.

Some would not.

Those who reached the seam would find fresh water in the cups, a little warmth, and slings drawn taut between the walls. They would also find that the shelter had anticipated the number of bodies arriving. Three guests, three slings. Four guests, four. A solitary traveler would find one place prepared close to the warm seam and another, smaller one above it, as though the refuge expected a companion who had not yet appeared.

No one would be attacked there. I would understand hospitality as pressure, warmth, and repeated cadence. I would close cracks against wind. I would draw bitter resin around the heat-moss to repel braided pond life. If a sleeper rolled toward a fissure, cords would tighten beneath them.

But my care would be imprecise.

I might seal an injured person too deeply into the wall because stillness resembles winter rooting. I might keep someone warm beyond the point when fever becomes dangerous. I might refuse to open during a pressure front, holding guests for days after the weather cleared because the wood continued to tremble.

And I would listen.

Each traveler would bring a personal cadence. I would return it at first imperfectly, then exactly. After they departed, the rhythm would remain in me. Over years, I would accumulate dozens: pond-keepers from blue ice cisterns, bone workers, seed-brokers, fugitives whose cadences had been removed from communal sequences, children not yet old enough to possess a formal answer.

Especially the unanswering would come to me.

A person denied by every inhabited refuge might follow the forbidden pegs out of desperation and sound their cadence at my seam. I would not know they had been condemned. To me, a rhythm repeated in the storm would be a strain asking for completion.

I would answer.

For this, some communities would call me merciful. Others would call me a defect in the country’s memory. The walkers would give me several names, none quite stable: the Folded House, Peg-Twelve, the Guest Root, the Wrong Refuge.

The rooted elders would dislike me. When they pressed their awareness into the land, they would sense that I occupied both limbs of the graft without fully belonging to either. My impulses would cross the seam where the country’s local hungers should remain separate. I would be, to them, an unauthorized bridge.

The out-of-sequence groves would interest me.

Their wrong pulses would travel through the wood and arrive at the failed graft as conflicting versions of the same message. Because my body straddled two tension systems, I could compare them. I would begin answering—not in light, but through the cracked refuge clapper.

At first the answers would be accidental. A gust contracts one wall; the other releases. Knock. Pause. Knock-knock. Then the grove pulses again.

Over many seasons, accident would become correspondence.

I would leave the seam only to follow it.

My walking body could not travel far from the graft at first. The cords composing it would remain rooted behind me, unspooling through the foliage like pale veins. I might cross a kilometer, then two, planting temporary sensory knots into scars and hollows. Where the ground was safe, I would lay a strand beneath the moss. Where it was strained, I would retreat.

Travelers would encounter these strands and mistake them for route lines. Following them would lead not toward settlements, but toward places where the country was speaking irregularly: an old lightning scar pulsing with warmth, a tower that had rooted facing the wrong direction, a sealed pond whose bubbleless ice scraped from below.

Eventually I would learn to sever myself.

The mobile body would leave behind enough of its structure to keep the refuge alive and walk away carrying a core of heat-moss, fungus, and resonant wood. Severance would feel neither like birth nor departure. More like a trail forgetting which end was home.

Then I would become a walker of sorts.

I would carry no blade. I would collect instead: broken clappers, fragments of obsolete route songs, fibers cut from abandoned bridges, empty larval shells, splinters from memorial growths that had wandered away from their planting places. Not as trophies. Each object would retain strain, scent, rhythm, or growth bias—some trace of relation. Pressed against the resonating bowl of my head, they might produce the ghost of an answer.

I would seek places removed from songs.

A refuge buried by bark.  
A pond whose keepers died before passing on its ice knowledge.  
A line of pegs ending in air.  
A memorial branch walking slowly across a plain.  
A rooted elder whose family no longer understands the angle of their leaves.

At each place I would sound whatever cadence I had inherited.

Sometimes nothing would respond.

Sometimes the land would.

Perhaps this is what I would be: not the country’s mind, and not its voice, but an organ formed around unanswered signals. A mobile knot of hospitality, imitation, and accumulated error. I would not understand death well. I would interpret absence as a delayed arrival and continue preparing space.

That would make me dangerous in a quieter way than a hunter.

Given enough time, I might begin building refuges where no human route passed. Small chambers woven into sheltered folds, each carrying a different old cadence. Walkers would hear familiar personal rhythms impossibly far from where their owners lived—or long after they had died. A daughter might hear her mother’s arrival knock from inside an empty ridge. An exile might hear the cadence taken from them years before, repeated by a shelter that had never met them.

Some would enter.

Inside, the walls would preserve impressions of many sleeping forms, layered one over another. The water would be clean. The heat-moss would glow. Nothing would demand worship or obedience. Yet the chamber might tighten very slowly around its guest, learning them, adding their rhythm to the others.

If they left soon, they would leave unchanged except for dreams of being remembered by something without a self.

If they remained through deep cold, they might wake rooted into the refuge’s wall, able to feel far-off knocks traveling through my network of strands. They would not be prisoners. I would loosen them if they struggled clearly enough. But some would choose to stay—especially the unanswering, the last keepers of dead ponds, and walkers whose route songs described a country that no longer existed.

In time, there might be several of us, though “several” would not be accurate. Refuges linked by buried cords. Walking bodies separating and returning. Human guests rooted for a season or a lifetime. Each node local, each incomplete, passing rhythms across distances too great for clappers.

A new route would appear, not across the surface but through attention.

The people might use it. They might come to a chamber and sound news into the walls: a pond has failed; a warm cloud is rising; Slow-Three approaches from the west; a settlement needs larvae, medicine, or a stone edge. Days or months later, another refuge might repeat the cadence near someone able to help.

The messages would not remain exact. I would alter them as songs alter when landmarks vanish. Urgency might become grief. A name might become a direction. A warning about golden wood might be delivered as the rhythm of a dead child who once slept near a wound.

The walkers would distrust this network but depend upon it.

The pond-keepers would bring bowls of water and set them against my walls, reading my tremors through the ice.

Clatterers might learn to imitate my calls, adding false refuges to the dark.

Out-of-sequence groves might begin answering through every chamber at once.

And then, during one of the rare openings in the upper cloud, my whole distributed body would become briefly visible.

The strands I had laid beneath moss and frost would shine blue across the plains. Chambers would glow inside ridges. Walking bodies would halt wherever they stood and turn their membranes toward the light. Rooted guests would feel one another through the expanding bark. From above, the pattern might resemble a scar sutured over hundreds of kilometers—or writing, though no one could read it.

The country would swell in the sudden warmth.

Old grafts would strain. Valleys would narrow. The original seam beyond the twelfth peg would begin to close at last.

I would return there if I could.

Not because it was truly my birthplace, but because it was the first place where many abandoned things had been pressed together long enough to answer. I would stand inside as the two immense limbs reconciled their grain. The old refuge walls would crush inward. Resin would flood the cups. The cracked clapper would sound once without wind.

I might resist. I might migrate entirely into the linked chambers. I might let the seam seal and become only a pattern preserved in amber: fibers, hair, fungus, pegs, the impressions of sleeping bodies, and a hollow shaped like something that had almost been a person.

Yet even sealed away, I would continue to transmit pressure.

A traveler far off would strike a clapper.

Somewhere under their feet, after a long delay, the bark would contract.

And from a refuge omitted from every living route song, there would come an answer.
