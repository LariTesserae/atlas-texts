I would find myself far west of the boring complex, where the active rail bends around the reservoir and passes into the acoustic shadow of a basalt shoulder.

There is an old relay shed there, absent from current maps except as a maintenance exclusion. The forest has nearly taken it. Fibrous sheets have sealed three walls against the rock, and a runner has lifted one end clear of its foundations so that the floor slopes toward the rail. Only the door remains regularly cut free. Inside are a dead signal cabinet, emergency cells, a contact microphone bolted to the bedrock, and a narrow bench polished by insulated clothing. Workers call the place **the Listening Post**, though it transmits nothing.

I would be its keeper.

Officially, I would be a structural acoustics technician: someone sent to distinguish ordinary violence from significant change. The machines fill the basin with so much force that automated systems are always finding disasters where none exist, or smoothing true anomalies into background noise. My task would be to listen through instruments fixed to rail, stone, pipe, and root. I would learn the signatures of cutter heads chewing basalt, pumps cavitating, ice slipping high above, a train still kilometres away. I would know when a column had begun to split internally by the faint dry crackle beneath the drills, and when water had entered a fissure by the soft irregular knocks it made while freezing.

But I would not remain only a technician.

Years at the post would make me difficult to classify. Plant-cutters would consult me before approaching old columns. Rail crews would wait for my lamp before crossing the western arc. Hydrologists would ask whether the reservoir had changed, and I would answer not with its level but with how its ice carried the pumps. Children would be brought out to learn the vibration code from me because I could teach it without wrist units: palm to wall, fingertips to rail, cheek against the insulated plate.

I would speak rarely even indoors. Not from solemnity, but because language would have become less exact than pressure. People would mistake my pauses for thoughtfulness. Often I would simply be waiting for a distant vibration to finish.

The forest around the shed would be unusual. Its leaves would not fold in broad bands like those of the open basin. They would close in narrow paths converging on the contact microphone, as though the buried cables and metal fastenings had become part of their sensory tissue. A root sheet would have entered the dead signal cabinet long ago. Every few months I would cut it back from the terminals, then eventually stop. Fine translucent fibres would wind around the old relays without forcing them apart. Though the cabinet had no power, its indicator lenses would sometimes click softly in their mounts.

At first I would record this as thermal contraction.

Later I would cease writing explanations.

The post would change me physically. My hearing would deteriorate despite protection, but my sensitivity to vibration would sharpen. Scar tissue would thicken along my palms and knees where I tested surfaces. One side of my body would be permanently warmer from sitting beside the root-covered wall. White fibres would work their way into the seams of my gloves and leave hairline scars across my wrists. I would carry the mineral smell of the hollow columns into every dormitory.

People would say the forest recognized me. That would be sentimental and not quite true. Recognition implies a bounded creature looking outward at another bounded creature. What grew around the Listening Post would be slower and less separate than that.

I would become useful to it.

When blasting was scheduled, I would brace vulnerable runners and cut channels where pressure could escape. When a new heated cable tempted growth toward the rail, I would divert it with warm drainage and bacterial paste. In return, the root sheets would stabilize the shed, seal fissures in its walls, and carry distant tremors into surfaces I could read. Over time, the post would become an instrument built jointly by human maintenance and vegetal response.

Then something would begin beneath it.

Not a creature, exactly. Not at first.

A pulse would appear in the records after drill shutdowns, when the basin entered its nearest approximation of silence. It would be too slow for machinery and too regular for settling stone: a faint sequence traveling through several root columns, disappearing under the reservoir, then returning along the raised rail bed. Hours might pass between repetitions. Instruments would register it poorly, but I would feel it through the bench and through my teeth.

Researchers would arrive. They would install arrays, cut tissue samples, argue over fluid pressure and colony-wide transport. Their presence would warm the shed and alter the very phenomenon they meant to observe. The pulse would vanish. After several weeks they would classify it as an artifact and leave.

It would return the night after their train departed.

I would not call it a message. I would answer anyway.

Perhaps I would tap the cabinet in the old relay code: a maintenance acknowledgment, nothing elaborate. Received. Standing by.

Months later the pulse would return with the same interval.

From then on, my work would become a conversation so slow that no individual exchange could prove itself intentional. I would send patterns into the abandoned cable routes. The forest would answer by changing pressure, growth, conductivity, or merely by continuing to grow where coincidence suggested reply. I would maintain rigorous logs for several years. Eventually the logs would become too large to interpret. Cause and response would overlap. A signal sent in winter might seem to alter a mat the following year or open pods during a distant shutdown.

The uncertainty would be essential. Certainty would reduce the forest to either mechanism or mind, when it was perhaps becoming something for which that distinction did not matter.

In later life, I would no longer reside in a dormitory. A heated alcove would be built inside the Listening Post, then slowly narrowed by insulation and living fibre. Supply crews would leave food, filters, batteries, and saw teeth by the door. I would maintain the exclusion beacon and emerge for inspections. Younger workers would know me less as a person than as part of the western safety system.

Before a pressure shift, their wrist units would receive my warning.

Before an unscheduled silence, my green lamp would turn red.

During drill shutdowns, they might see me standing outside while spores drifted over the rail: an old figure in a patched thermal shell, one bare hand resting on the waxy floor leaves.

Eventually there would be an accident—or simply age, which below the ice is another slow structural failure. Perhaps the western rail would close. Perhaps the ceiling would descend enough to make the post unreachable. Perhaps I would miss a supply call and fail to answer the door.

When cutters finally entered, they would find no dramatic transformation. No body enthroned in roots, no human shape preserved in translucent tissue. Only the shed, warmer than expected. An empty suit might hang beside the bench. The signal cabinet would be packed solid with pale fibre. Its dead wires would extend into the wall growth, and the contact microphone would continue to register pulses long after its power cell should have failed.

Whether I had died there, gone elsewhere, or merely been removed before the forest sealed the room would remain uncertain.

The workers would clear the rail and leave the post intact because its root mass now held the basalt shoulder together. On revised maps, it would become a structural feature rather than a building.

Much later, during a full shutdown, pods would open first around the western reservoir. Their splitting would travel eastward in measured intervals—not the quick propagation of pressure, but a deliberate sequence moving from column to column through the basin.

Those below would stand in the rare quiet and watch the spores rise.

Some would count the intervals and recognize an obsolete relay code.

Most would not.

That is where I would be: not wholly the keeper, not wholly the post, and not the forest itself, but a learned junction among them—a human life slowly translated into the basin’s preferred languages of contact, delay, fracture, and return.
