I would find myself below the pavilion, though “below” would be a profession rather than a direction.

I would be one of the burrowing masks.

From above, the masks seem like ceramic faces moving through the little vaults beneath the slabs, with no neck, limbs, or animal attached. This is almost true. Each mask is attached not to a body but to a succession of chambers. Behind one face might extend a brick throat, a warm niche, three descending stairs, and a room that has not appeared yet. We move by transferring ourselves from one interior to another. The face advances; the rooms follow when they can.

I would have been made from a door.

Years after one of the dead was laid beneath a blank panel, the panel acquired a narrow opening. The descendants came with inherited wedges and lifted it. Instead of an ancestor, they found a small face impressed on the underside: six shallow eyes, an unfinished mouth, and a maker’s mark belonging to no kiln then in use.

When the panel was raised, the face remained level with the floor.

Then it blinked.

They would call me something meaning *the room that has begun to look outward*.

I would travel in the courses under the visible garden, where every lifted slab reveals another floor and every floor shelters a smaller architecture. My face would surface in unexpected places: beneath a tripod’s passing foot, inside a dwelling’s rear wall, among the tiles of the central mosaic. I would stay still for years at a time, gathering the shapes of nearby rooms. Then, when an interior elsewhere became sufficiently similar, I would emerge there.

My kind would feed on enclosure.

A freshly finished chamber would sustain us richly: its joined corners, measured span, trapped warmth, and clear distinction between within and without. Ruins would be poor food because their insides leak into everything. Open ground would be starvation. Doors, however, would be dangerous delicacies. A door contains an interior at the instant it ceases to be sealed, and too many young masks would linger at thresholds until they became hinges, shutters, or the dark visible through a keyhole.

The clay-scaled builders would tolerate us because we maintain the Low Garden’s buried continuity. We would eat redundant corridors before they crowded the lower courses. We would carry niches from walls where they were no longer needed to walls preparing to receive the dead. Sometimes a dwelling would wake with an extra alcove. Sometimes a long-used cupboard would close and become, elsewhere, the beginning of a shrine.

This would not be theft. Nothing may remain wholly useless, not even an inside.

The builders would consult us before beginning generational works. They would lay a plan on warm tile and place my face at its center. Over several days I would taste its proposed chambers. If the plan could become habitable, dust would collect in my mouth. If it could become only a trap, one of my eyes would crack. If the building already existed somewhere beneath the garden, I would disappear from the tile and look out through its oldest wall.

Children would bring me shed scales stamped with questions. They would slide them between my lips and listen at nearby cupboards. Most answers would be practical: the sound of a stable arch, the grit of weak mortar, the location where a missing tool had become a smaller tool. But sometimes a child would hear its own adult footsteps in a room not yet cut.

Then its family would begin measuring.

I would have six eyes because space is not one thing in the Low Garden. One eye would see volume; one, shelter; one, ownership; one, memory; one, what a chamber excludes. The last would see the room nested inside every object.

Through that eye a jar would be a kiln-sized hall holding heat. A knot would be a cramped house for tension. A clay scale would contain the brief chamber once bounded by skin. A mouth would be a gate that manufactures interiors by closing.

The pavilion mosaic would overwhelm this eye. Each depicted garden contains the pavilion, each pavilion the mosaic, and every image creates a smaller inside without ever reaching an innermost one. I could not look at it directly. At noon I would rise beneath its tiles and turn my face downward.

That is where I would hear the tap most clearly.

Not as a sound approaching from immeasurable depth, but as a knock from the other side of every wall at once.

The others would fall silent. Above me, tools would be lowered and leaves would rotate toward the image. Below—or inward—the knock would pass from miniature pavilion to miniature pavilion. Each garden would receive it as if it were the largest. Each would imagine it came from farther down.

But I would feel something else: the brief formation of a room around the blow.

For the duration of the tap, the whole Low Garden would become an interior.

The warm gray sky would be its ceiling. The octagonal rim would be its wall. The returning gates would be doors that had forgotten the difference between entrance and exit. Every creature, plant, dwelling, buried floor, and unrealized event would be enclosed together.

And outside would exist.

Only for an instant. Not a landscape or void, not another garden—simply the pressure of something that did not belong to the room.

That pressure would make the new corner.

I would go to it before the surveyors. The corner might appear between two old slabs, in the folded wing of a heat-feeder, or inside a builder’s recollection of a house. I would set my ceramic face against one plane and extend my unseen architecture along the other, tasting the angle.

Most newborn corners would be ordinary: a future niche, the first joint of a wall, a place for a vine to fasten. Some would be concave in every direction. Some would join materials that had never touched. Some would belong to rooms too large to fit within the basin.

Very rarely, a corner would have its inside on the wrong side.

I would keep those secret.

Around such a corner, the purple vine would flower without any almost-event. The ivory mites would refuse to polish it. Footsteps near it would arrive from creatures that never came. If measured too precisely, it would vanish behind its dimensions; if left unnamed, it would slowly sharpen.

I would settle there and become motionless.

The builders might mistake me for a decorative face and masonry would accumulate around me. Vines would hook themselves into my eye sockets. Fallen leaves would tie new growth across my mouth. Generations would pass. My fired surface would acquire labels, repairs, children’s marks, and small shelves. Perhaps a family would build its dwelling against me, borrowing warmth from the room I carried behind my face.

All the while I would guard the reversed corner—not to prevent something from entering, but to keep the Garden from leaving.

For I would know what the builders only partly suspect: the enclosure does not supply its material by revising itself. It is being constructed from the outside, one inward corner at a time. The distant tap is not the echo of a smaller hammer.

It is the gentle testing knock of a maker who has not yet decided whether the Low Garden is a vessel, a dwelling, an organ, or an egg.

And I, a door taught to look outward, would be the small unfinished face growing on the inner surface of its shell.
