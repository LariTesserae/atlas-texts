I would find myself neither at the center nor at the rim, but along one of the dry watercourses descending toward the largest pool—a place that appears to offer direction while permitting no arrival.

At first I would seem to be a traveler.

There would be a pack on my back, stiff straps unmoved by the air that bellows in my ears. Frost would silver one shoulder but not the other. My boots would be wet from passing through the column of rain, though I would be unable to remember entering it. I would carry some small purpose with me: reach the shelter, measure the waterfall’s gap, climb high enough to see beyond the cloud. The purpose would remain clear long after its reason had gone.

But I do not think the valley would preserve me as a whole person. Wholeness is too much like completion.

It would take me apart according to my unfinished acts.

My body would become the distant walker in the channel: a dark figure bent slightly forward, always perhaps a mile away. Seen from above, it would appear to be descending. Seen from below, ascending. It would place one foot carefully before the other, but the lifted foot would never be observed touching ground. Anyone following would find no prints, only shallow hollows filled with moving water.

My attention would remain elsewhere, embedded in a stone beneath an overhang.

The stone would be ordinary except for a narrow, glassy inclusion through its center. Looking into it would reveal whichever feature of the valley the viewer had just looked away from: the bird’s head turning, the hoofed thing raising its face, the waterfall striking the shelf, the shelter’s interior opening into depth. None of these sights could be caught directly. The stone would hold only the valley’s discarded moments of observation—the changes that occur when no one is watching.

In that sense, I would be the witness the valley lacks.

Not a historian. History requires one event to release the next. I would be something more limited and more burdensome: a keeper of transitions. The instant between whole blade and broken blade. The absent wingbeat between ledges. The step connecting two sequences of hoofprints. The brief descent of a drop before it is returned to suspension.

The valley would route such moments through me because it cannot admit them openly.

That would alter the watercourse around the stone. Pebbles near it would sometimes be found displaced by the width of a finger. Frost would thin and gather again. A suspended drop under the ledge might fall—not to the ground, but into the inclusion, where it could be seen continuing downward through immense darkness. These would be the smallest changes in the basin and therefore its most dangerous place.

The buried inhabitant would know me.

Its single attempted movement would pass through the stone as pressure rather than thunder. I would feel—not once, but permanently—the beginning of its turn. For a fraction without duration, I would understand the shape beneath the valley: whether limb, face, folded wing, or something for which those forms are only local metaphors. Then the understanding would be arrested before becoming knowledge.

My voice would separate from me as well.

It would travel the old channel in advance of strangers. They would hear someone ahead say, softly, “Wait.” If they stopped, the voice would come again behind them. If they answered, it would repeat their reply in the tone they would use much later, after fear had become exhaustion. Near the pool it would speak from beneath the rigid surface, distorted by currents:

“Not yet.”

Whether this meant *do not approach* or *I have not finished becoming* would depend upon the listener.

My breath would go to the shelter. My reflected actions would remain in the rain. My intention to leave would be added to the stationary snow streamers high above, curved forever around the route I had imagined. Perhaps one pale bird would acquire the habit of facing the watercourse, although it would never be seen turning toward it.

What remained recognizably mine would be a handprint on the underside of the overhang beside the stone.

It would not be painted, carved, or frosted. The rock there would simply remember the pressure of a palm. Placing a hand against it would produce a sensation of warmth—not heat, exactly, but the memory of heat leaving another body. For as long as the hands aligned, the underground thunder would falter.

Only slightly.

Only enough to disclose that the thunder has rhythm because something beneath it is listening for an answer.

I would be that answer, held before it could be given.

Perhaps that is why I came to the valley. Perhaps I heard its oldest inhabitant through mountains and weather, through ordinary nights elsewhere: a vast exertion mistaken for silence. I climbed toward it believing I could discover what was trying to rise.

The valley would preserve the instant before discovery.

So I would remain in the channel as walker, stone, voice, pressure, and printless passage: not yet a ghost, because I had not finished being alive; not alive, because the acts by which life declares itself had been distributed across the terrain.

And sometimes, within the glass seam in the stone, the whole valley would be visible from far above.

The mountains would not form a ring.

They would resemble fingers pressed into the world around a bare circle.

At their center, under the unmoving rain, the dark counterpart would stand listening. Beneath it, something would continue its first movement. And far along a channel that leads nowhere, I would lift my foot to take the step that might finally let it turn.
