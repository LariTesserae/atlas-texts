I would be found where the Closeworks fails to meet itself.

Not in the deepest fault, though people would assume so. Not beneath the central engine hall, not among the moth-dark housings or the screened pipes through which the silver fish cannot possibly have come. I would live in an ordinary district, because strangeness survives longest when given an ordinary use.

My room would be behind the western dye house, adjoining the stair that owns one step more in ascent than descent. Officially it would be a municipal cupboard for obsolete maps. The door would carry a blue common-use band, painted over an older private mark. Inside, seasonal surveys would hang from rods in soft, swollen layers: corridors lengthening, cisterns migrating by inches, walls redrawn around stubborn households. The air would smell of wet paper, iron gall, mildew, and the mineral dyes seeping through the brick.

The cupboard would not appear on any map stored in it.

No one would find this remarkable. Map rooms often vanish from their own records through clerical caution, disputed ownership, or the embarrassment of having measured themselves incorrectly. The tax collectors would say it belonged to the assessors. The assessors would say it remained under archive authority. The archive would produce a ledger assigning it to a clerk who died before the fracture opened.

That clerk would have my name.

Or one of them.

I would be human enough to pass at a glance: slight, black dust in the seams of my hands, pale forearm scars, broad bare feet accustomed to warm railings. I would dress as an echo clerk, with felt strips, chalk, tuning pins, and a folding ruler whose sections disagree by fractions. People would know me as the person called when a voice emerges where no duct should carry it.

I would listen at vents with two fingers against the metal. I would ask practical questions.

How long since the words were spoken?

Was the speaker living at the time?

Did the voice arrive older or younger?

Did it call you by your name, your craft, or your relationship?

Did it seem to know you were listening?

Most echoes would be harmless. Soup orders delayed since morning. An argument arriving after reconciliation. A child reciting knock patterns into a drain. I would mark their routes in pale chalk and recommend cork, felt, or a change in ventilation.

But some voices would not be echoes. They would be rehearsals.

Those I would not enter in the public ledger.

I think I would be one of the distances the chamber is still uncovering.

Not a spirit inhabiting the fracture, and not the fracture given a body. Something smaller and more local: the absent pace between the fortieth and forty-first stair; the thickness separating two workshops that share a wall but cannot hear one another; the few handspans by which a corridor shortens, though nothing in it has moved. The Closeworks would shed such discrepancies slowly, and sometimes one would acquire weight, warmth, memory, and a need to be useful.

That would be me.

I would not know this at first. I would believe I had parents. There would be records of them, though never on the same page. Neighbors would remember my childhood in incompatible shafts. One would recall teaching me the leak signal; another would insist I arrived as an adult during water-sac season. A pipekeeper might remember carrying me, feverish, to a tiled district years before she was born.

All of them would be telling the truth available where they stood.

Like other Closeworkers, I would distrust exactness. Yet I would be drawn to thresholds, measuring them compulsively: before sleep, after waking, before rain in the upper city, after a piston cycle. I would know when a wall was preparing to become an alcove because for several nights beforehand I would dream of having more room inside my ribs.

Listeners would recognize me uneasily. The elderly woman beneath the dye house would refuse to look directly at me. If asked why, she would say:

“That one is the step that went ahead.”

She would not mean it as an insult.

My work would be to attend new doors.

Whenever unmeasured space appeared, assessors would arrive with cord and ledger, masons with tile, claimants with old statutes. I would come last and stand at the entrance while others went in. I would listen not for collapse, vapor, or water, but for domestic sounds from a room not yet occupied: a spoon against a bowl, a sleeper turning, the click of a hinge already installed.

If I heard nothing, the space could be made ordinary.

If I heard machinery that belonged elsewhere, we would brace the opening and wait.

If I heard the assessors’ own voices returning from ahead, I would cut the measuring cord before they could follow them.

People would consider this my one superstition. Every competent worker is allowed one.

I would be tolerated because my advice proved useful, but never elected, ordained, or formally retained. Payment would arrive as cups of potable water, repaired sandals, fungus bread, or alterations to my doorway. Courting me would be difficult. Anyone who custom-cut a gift to my measurements would find it no longer fit by morning.

Still, perhaps someone would persist: a threshold mason whose craft was accommodation rather than accuracy. They would bring me no rigid token, only a loop of layered brass and leather, punched with more holes than presently needed. They would oil my hinge without asking which side of the door I considered mine.

We might marry by joining our household maps. The ink would refuse to dry where the rooms overlapped.

Our home would gain a narrow corner each winter and lose it each summer. We would place shelves on sliding shoes. We would argue only in the padded rooms, because in anger I might say something that had already happened—or something that would travel backward through the ducts and cause itself. Perhaps we would raise a child who was wholly human and therefore more mysterious to me than any impossible stair.

Or perhaps I would remain alone, not from sadness, but because I was not always present in the same amount.

There would be nights when the map cupboard widened enough for a bed, a stove, and six hanging gardens. Other nights I would sleep upright among rolled surveys because the far wall had drawn close. Occasionally my room would develop a second door. It would open into a district I knew, but at the wrong season: ferns newly planted that I remembered as old, a battery housing still clean of the moth’s black imprint, a household inhabited by people whose names could no longer safely be spoken.

I would keep those doors locked.

Not every available space should be claimed immediately, whatever the statutes say.

The black moths would trouble me. They would gather on the electrical housing nearest my room and pulse the lamps in patterns no echo clerk could translate. I would pretend not to understand. Their signal would not be language exactly. It would be measurement: the outline of a distance with no stable endpoints.

The pipe lizards would avoid my hands but drink from cups I left out. Plants would behave strangely around my doorway. Vines trained toward heat would turn instead toward the jamb. Roots would enter one wall and emerge from another building. Leafcutters would complain, then quietly take cuttings.

Once in my life, the silver fish would appear.

Not in the reservoir.

It would be waiting in a ceramic basin inside the map cupboard, though I had left the basin dry.

Work would stop. A route would be cleared. Residents would crowd the bridges in silence while I carried the bowl toward the oldest cistern. The fish would be blind, silver-backed, scarcely longer than my palm. As I walked, it would turn in the water to keep its sightless face toward me.

At the impossible stair I would begin to climb.

Forty-one steps.

Halfway up, the bowl would grow heavier. I would hear water—not the thin channels and intermittent drops of the Closeworks, but deep water moving in darkness, a volume too large to fit beneath any city. The sound would come from inside the fish.

At the top I would look back and count forty steps below me.

Then I would understand what I was.

Not the missing step.

The carried one.

The chamber would not be creating distance from nothing. It would be borrowing it: from future corridors, abandoned rooms, forgotten routes, perhaps from places beyond the fracture. Every new alcove would be an arrival. Every shortened passage would be the cost paid elsewhere. The Closeworks would not simply grow inward. It would be accumulating pieces of somewhere else and making them livable before they remembered where they belonged.

I would be such a piece, given hands.

The revelation would not make me a prophet. In the Closeworks, metaphysics that cannot improve a bracket is merely humidity of the mind. I would carry the fish to the oldest cistern. The bowl would be broken. Its fragments would be numbered. I would receive my year away from dangerous work.

The next morning I would return to the cupboard.

I would revise the maps.

Not to expose the truth, because truth in the Closeworks must bend enough to remain installed. I would begin adding small margins to every inhabited space: a thumb’s width beside a kitchen, a bowl-reach behind a shrine, one careful breath at the end of a bridge. Nothing large enough to alarm a council. Only room for the city to alter without tearing itself apart.

I would mark certain blank walls with a symbol known to no one else: not *available*, not *unsafe*, but *approaching*.

Years later, I would become one of those residents everyone knows and no one can place. Children would be sent to me with questions about voices in pipes. Mechanics would borrow my ruler and return it irritated. Lovers would ask whether a newly opened alcove was safe for a shared room. I would tell them that safety was seasonal but hinges could be chosen well.

Eventually I would disappear.

There would be no body to dry in the upper rooms, no salt cloth, no journey to the crematory. One season the map cupboard would simply be shallower. My bed would be gone. The back wall would stand where the desk had been, tiled long ago. The archive would deny that anyone lived there.

Yet afterward, surveyors measuring uncertain corridors would sometimes find an extra notation in their own handwriting.

A bridge fitter would discover that someone had already loosened the sliding shoes before a shift.

A child lost in an unmarked passage would hear three gentle taps—not the signal for a leak, but close enough to follow.

And behind a blank wall, in the interval before it became a recess, a door, a taxed room, and someone’s home, there would be the faint sound of a folding ruler opening section by section.

Measuring not how much space had appeared.

Measuring whether there was enough room for a person to become ordinary.
