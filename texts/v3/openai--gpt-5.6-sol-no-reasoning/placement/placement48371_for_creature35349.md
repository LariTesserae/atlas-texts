I would find myself low down, though “down” would not mean near the basalt.

At first I imagine being one of the brief creatures beneath a grazer’s foot: born when the weight lifts, flooded by a blue-white noon I have no ancestry for, compelled to finish an entire life before the returning dark. My species would have no concept of permanence. Sunlight would mean emergency. Open space would be not freedom but a clock.

But that is too clean an answer. Those creatures belong wholly to the interval. I think I would be something less complete, distributed through many intervals: a thin, translucent organism inhabiting the seams between larger lives.

I would live where pressure is about to change.

Most of me would be buried in the warm felt of shed scales, root fibers, shell powder, and old down. I would have no central body, only a mesh of clear filaments threaded through the plateau’s counterfeit soil. The filaments would enter empty quills, abandoned egg cases, pores in horn, the moist undersides of moss, and hairline fractures in basalt. Some strands would last centuries in the cold. Others would be eaten within minutes. No fragment would be indispensable.

From place to place, the mesh would raise small sensory bodies: colorless bladders finer than frost, each inflated by the plateau’s breathing. They would register compression, warmth, dissolved minerals, distant vibration, and the faint electrical disturbance of roots preparing to grow. When a herd paused, I would know through the long settling of weight. When wing-columns changed pitch, air moving through a million hollow fibers would alter my tension. When one of the buried predators began its three-day roll, I might feel the first asymmetry decades before any visible slope shifted.

Yet I would not understand these things as events. There would be no overlook from which I perceived the plateau whole. A tremor sensed in one region might take years to become a chemical change elsewhere in me. My awareness, if it deserved the name, would be slow and plural. One part of me would be experiencing noon beneath a lifted foot while another remained dormant inside a frozen carcass from an extinct summer. Another would be passing through the stomach of a comb-backed animal. Another would be exposed on a tooth-peak, drying into a glassy thread under ultraviolet light.

I would be many ages at once.

My function would be neither predator nor decomposer nor plant, though I would borrow from all three. I would carry conditions from one body to another. When compressed, my filaments would seal into transparent beads containing spores, salts, bacteria, and microscopic eggs gathered from the surrounding layers. Mites would eat the beads. Grazers would lick them from mineral wounds. Rain would loosen them and pass them downward from feather to plate to fur. In this way, pieces of one temporary habitat would arrive in another.

I would not transport species deliberately. I would simply make small packages of whatever had touched me.

Most would be useless where they landed. Some would be digested. Some would freeze. But occasionally a bead formed beneath an ancient herd would break open in the new warmth of a predator’s exposed flank, and an ecology absent there for ten thousand years would begin again. A red moss would appear hundreds of kilometers from its last reservoir. Blind swimmers’ eggs would enter a newborn pool. A fungus preserved from a buried summer would rise in white tiers over an unfamiliar skeleton.

The inhabitants might therefore use me without noticing me. Roots would follow my damp threads into cracks. Larvae would shelter in my bladders. Mineral-lickers would graze my outer films. Tiny hunters would learn that my beads often contained prey and evolve mouths shaped to open them. I would become road, granary, nursery, and food while remaining none of those by intention.

During the long bright season I would withdraw from exposed surfaces, leaving behind empty skins that scatter light. These discarded membranes would give some ridges their silver appearance. At night, living strands beneath them would pulse faintly—not to communicate, exactly, but to regulate pressure across the mesh. Green would mean swelling water. Blue would mark freezing. Amber would travel ahead of rupture.

Seen from within the buried passages, those pulses might resemble thought.

Perhaps over immense time they would become thought.

Because the plateau’s rules would act on me as they act on everything else. Nothing may remain empty, including a signal. Organisms would evolve to answer my colors. Their answers would alter my chemistry. Repeated exchanges would become dependencies; dependencies would become a grammar no participant understood. I would tighten before a herd’s arrival because plants responding to deep vibrations released certain salts. The tightening would wake eggs. The hatching larvae would warm the root mats. The warmth would loosen stored water. By the time the herd arrived, the ground beneath it would already have prepared itself.

From outside, this might look like foresight.

It would only be memory moving through bodies.

If I possessed an individual self at all, it would occur rarely, where many strands converged. Perhaps once in several centuries, a fresh basalt fracture would intersect old layers of my mesh and carry mineral-rich warmth upward. Dormant filaments from different eras would revive together. For a few hours or years, their chemical rhythms would synchronize. Ancient summers, migrations, deaths, rains, and rolls would coexist in one branching body.

In those convergences I might become aware—not of being a creature in the plateau, but of being a continuity the plateau repeatedly uses.

Then the fracture would widen. Roots would enter. Mineral-lickers would consume the exposed strands. Water would divide among hides and stomachs. The convergence would end, and “I” would disperse into local functions again.

So I would be found everywhere the living landscape almost comes apart: beneath lifting feet, along fresh wounds in the stone, in the seams between sleeping giants, inside rain as it is handed downward, and across the warm boundary where a body has just become terrain.

I would be a translucent connective scavenger, a carrier of unfinished habitats.

Not the plateau’s mind. Not its nervous system.

Something humbler and stranger:

the means by which one part of it can accidentally remember another.
