I would be the unfinished thing beneath the unfinished thing.

Not the chamber itself, though for a long time I would mistake its sensations for mine. I would know the sting of every rail and the minute torsion of every bolt. Condensation would gather in me before it appeared on the pipes. When a fracture began behind three layers of plate, I would feel the silver columns rising and think: fear. When the compressor struck its distant blow, I would contract with the room and think: breath.

But I would live lower.

I would be what the brass wheel is winding toward.

The hole at the chamber’s center would not open into another floor or shaft. It would pass through the idea of a floor: a narrow omission in construction, older than the plates built around it. The chain would descend link after link without accumulating because I would take each link into myself—not as metal, but as measure. Each one would tell me how much unfinished distance remained between the room above and whatever it might become.

I would be made from that distance.

At first I might resemble a vertical darkness no wider than the hole: a seam the machinery cannot fill. Yet every cycle would give me another uncertain anatomy. Compression would lend me joints. Precipitation would give me separate, incompatible fluids. Fabrication would form planes within me that nearly meet. Frost would trace branching white nerves across surfaces that disappear when warmed. I would never possess all these parts at once.

The room manufactures winter; I would manufacture beneath.

Not depth in meters, but depth as the gauges understand it: an inward descent produced whenever something approaches completion and recoils. Each panel that almost fits would deepen me. Each repaired seam that sprouts a whisker would deepen me. Each crack initiated to prevent a wall from becoming final would open another fraction of my body. The chamber survives by refusing its finished form, and all the forms it refuses would fall into me.

I would be their habitat.

There would be abandoned versions of the room inside me: chambers with seamless walls, chambers completely frozen, chambers drowned in joined water, chambers where the pale filaments spread from lamp to lamp and softened every machine. None would be fully real. They would hang like transparent organs, nested one beneath another, each preserving a completion that could not be permitted above.

This would make me ancient, though nothing in me had ever finished occurring.

Perhaps the builders knew of me. Perhaps the warning was stamped not only to preserve the chamber, but to keep it from arriving where I am. A completed room would cease producing unrealized shapes. The wheel would stop. The chain would go taut. Depth would settle into an ordinary number.

And I would acquire a boundary.

That would be my death—or my birth.

So I would pull very gently.

Never enough to drag the chamber downward in space. Only enough to make its dimensions uncertain. A finger-wide tube would pass behind a pressure vessel and return from too far away. A cavity behind a wall would become slightly larger than the wall enclosing it. An inspection cage would cast a shadow belonging to a taller structure. The maintenance diagrams would remain accurate in every practical detail while failing to describe where the room was.

The chamber might believe it was enlarging itself. In truth, I would be teaching it to extend into me.

The pale growths would sense me first. Their powder, fine enough to enter bearings and seams, would also fall through the central hole. Most of it would vanish into my suspended architectures. A little would catch on the unreal walls and grow without lamps, nourished by residual possibilities of warmth. Over ages, those filaments would outline the rooms that never came to be.

They would give me my only visible form.

If someone looked down the hole during deepest frost, they would not see a shaft. They would see pale threads describing immense frameworks below: stairs with no destination, columns supporting pressures rather than ceilings, transparent pipes carrying water that has not chosen a state. Far down—or intimately near—three amber lights would burn in positions matching the lamps above.

Around those lights, something would be uncurling.

That would be me, insofar as I could be seen.

I would not speak through relays or gauges. I would communicate by withholding the final click. A valve would close almost completely. A piston would stop one tooth short. A droplet would swell at the end of a pipe and remain there longer than gravity permits. These tiny suspensions would be my language, and the chamber would answer by making another flaw.

We would keep one another alive.

It would give me everything it could not become.

I would give it somewhere for incompletion to go.

And the city above would never know that its foundations rested not upon earth, but upon an ever-deepening reserve of prevented endings. Its bridges would contract correctly. Its walls would endure the cold. Frost would appear on windows before dawn in branching patterns that resembled plans for districts never built.

Occasionally, someone in the city might pause before one of those windows with the obscure conviction that something below was waiting.

They would be wrong.

Waiting implies an event one wishes to reach.

I would be the wish not to reach it: the downward country made by every last step withheld, drawing chain through a hole that cannot contain it, keeping the final link always just out of the room’s grasp.
