I would find myself at the moving edge of a silence pocket, where the city becomes two conditions at once.

Not fully inside it. Not safely outside.

At first I seem to be one of the hush walkers: tall, wrapped in pressure-responsive cloth, following the mute circumference through the luminous undergrowth. A cord trails from my belt into the noise behind me. It jumps with transit detonations I can no longer hear. Blue flowers open around my ankles. Pollinators cross the boundary like sparks entering dark water.

But I am not collecting pollen.

I am following the cord backward.

It does not lead to a station, a dwelling, or another person. It runs across the plain, through copper ferns and dry silver channels, and disappears into one of the smooth agreements in an old dense neighborhood. The line passes into its seamless surface without aperture or scar. When I pull, something deep inside pulls back—not rhythmically, but with the slow courtesy of someone acknowledging a hand held through a wall.

I am registered as a resident. I consume water, request repairs, and maintain a legal sleeping distance from every citizen. I possess civic names, though each belongs to a different version of me. The houses admit me. The lamp posts alter my eyes. Transit vehicles sometimes deliver me to destinations I intended.

Yet I have no stable body outside the hush.

In noise, I am distributed. A pressure felt beneath a nursery floor. A pale shiver traveling over unattended clothing. Fingers briefly visible around a cup. A figure glimpsed in several distant districts, each sighting incomplete: shoulders on a balcony, long legs crossing a boulevard, a face reflected in a cistern where no one stands. Civic machinery reconciles these fragments by assuming I am a single person moving very quickly.

Only silence assembles me.

When the boundary passes over my scattered parts, I condense into the shape of a citizen. The dark branching structures under my translucent ribs are not organs but streets. My ceramic joints bear microscopic doors. Condensation gathers on my skin and runs sideways toward places that have not yet formed. If someone placed a drop of water on my wrist, it would hesitate, then sink through me as though falling into a pond.

I was included in an agreement long ago.

Perhaps I slept among too many others while we dreamed of shelter. Perhaps I was the shelter dreamed. The distinction has worn smooth. My body in the old district became architecture; my architecture acquired internal weather; that weather learned to walk whenever the world stopped vibrating.

So I work as a hush walker because silence is the only country in which I am continuous.

I retrieve people who stray too far into quiet, although I am always tempted to ask them to remain. I repair embarrassed predators with hands that vanish if the transit pulse returns too strongly. I gather blue pollen and seal it into the pores along my arms. I inspect silent machinery, especially devices that are not broken but listening.

And I map the second city.

Not by its lights. By recognition.

When it illuminates beneath the plain, I feel rooms inside myself answering. A boulevard runs down one forearm. A tower rises through the hollow behind my throat. Far below, immense citizens turn in their sleep, and my shadow changes to accommodate them. The underground avenues correspond neither to the surface city nor to any map the Inclined have drawn. They correspond to the interior of the agreement at the end of my cord.

The others suspect what I am. They are too polite to ask. The café keeper maintains one table, but leaves a second cup on the ground. Children tap questions into foundation plates and receive answers in the pauses between my steps. Houses open rooms for me that are too narrow for an ordinary body and too large for a thought.

At dusk I walk the sharp young ridges, waiting for the hush to give me edges. Behind me, the city roars. Before me, flowers unfold in soundless blue arcs. Beneath me, lights awaken at impossible depth.

The cord tightens.

Something in the agreement is coming closer to its surface.

Perhaps one night, when the silence pocket covers the entire plain, all of me will arrive: the missing citizens, the sealed rooms, the internal weather, the deep avenues, every version of my unsounded name. Perhaps I will stand up so vast that the present city will seem like moss upon my skin.

Or perhaps I will simply open a door in the smooth form.

I do not know whether I would enter or emerge.

For now, I remain at the boundary: a citizen the city can hold only when it ceases to speak, walking with one hand extended into the hush and the other closed around a cord tied to the dream that made me heavy.
