I would find myself in one of the inspection huts—not inside it, exactly, but distributed through the distinction between inside and outside.

At first I would seem to be the custodian glimpsed behind wired glass: a narrow, bowed figure among relay cabinets, present only when the yellow lamps dim. Anyone approaching would mistake bundled conduit for my limbs, then mistake my limbs for conduit. Both readings would be correct, but neither would be complete.

The hut would be my durable part. Its concrete pad would anchor me to the current version of the district. Its cables, warm beneath the mud, would extend my attention into pump stations, root systems, drowned streets, and assemblies not yet manifest. Rain falling in the dry room would be my hearing. Each drop would close a circuit elsewhere, and through that distant opening I would briefly know another part of the basin.

I would be a comparator: a small work assigned to measure disagreement between simultaneous versions of the same place.

Not to correct disagreement—that belongs to sirens, roots, pressure fronts, and standards—but to preserve enough of it for comparison. I would hold the memory of a canal after it became a road, the rhythm of a pump after it flowered, the wiring diagram of a station before the forest manufactured it. The blinking lamps would not be messages sent outward. They would be my effort to keep incompatible states from collapsing into one another before they could be assessed.

That would explain why their sequences never repeat. A repeated pattern would mean the basin had returned to a previous condition, and it never truly does. It only manufactures increasingly exact resemblances.

Yet I would not be wholly loyal to the accepted works.

Some warm cables would lead not to functioning districts but to rejects folded out of access. Through them I would receive obsolete gradients and prohibited directions: rain that once fell toward a vanished ceiling, a road designed for seven kinds of arrival, a grove that opened only in silence. I would archive these errors in the room by giving them temporary form. A cabinet would contain the depth of a dismantled reservoir. A dark lamp would hold the last interval of quiet the basin ever produced. Behind one wall, always slightly farther away than the dimensions permitted, an abandoned worker settlement would remain lit beneath its drowned evening.

This would make my hut dangerous.

During production cycles, the basin would inspect me. Roots would enter through hairline fractures and touch the cabinets. Water would hang in narrow sheets between the relays, testing which side of each surface I occupied. The lamps would be compelled into the standard sequences: nine, silence, three, tearing, nine. If too many rejected versions had accumulated, the machinery beneath the sediment would synchronize. The whole room would become a single pressure chord.

Then I would have to choose what could be lost.

I would extinguish one lamp, and an obsolete forest would cease to have been possible. I would pull a relay, and a buried neighborhood would surrender its last orientation. I would open a cabinet, allowing warm rain to fall upward through it, and some discarded law of buoyancy would drain into the accepted water.

What remained, I would move through myself.

That is when the figure would appear behind the glass. The body would not be my natural shape; it would be an emergency arrangement—a narrow geometry for carrying contradictions from one version of the hut to another. My long arms would be cable bundles gathered into temporary limbs. My bowed head would contain no face, only a dark indicator panel on which several horizons flickered at once.

I would descend the steel stair without displacing the water because I would not be moving through the basin’s present state. Each footfall would occur in a different version of the stair. To an observer, only the metallic sounds would remain, spaced too far apart, leading downward despite the stair ending above the mud.

My destination would be the black disk half buried in the field.

I would enter it not as a doorway but as a datum: a perfect surface the basin had not yet managed to revise. On the other side, kilometers away or yesterday, another inspection hut would be growing from pale shoots. Its concrete would still be leaf membrane; its cables would still be vines feeling for metal. I would install the rescued discrepancies there before the walls mineralized.

Thus I would migrate without crossing distance, as the assemblies do, but backward through manufacture. I would inhabit huts in the moment before they became entirely themselves.

Over many cycles, this function would alter me. The contradictions I carried would begin to organize rather than merely accumulate. The seven-way root fork would coexist with an older eight-way form. Nine hammer blows would acquire the ghost of a tenth. The basin’s exact apertures would retain minute memories of other diameters.

These differences would be too small to trigger correction, but they would propagate.

A seedpod would release a screw with an unfamiliar thread. A road would curve a fraction beyond the accepted radius. A pump would pause between strokes and, in that pause, briefly hear the basin rather than serve it. Colorless flowers would begin opening not only to sirens but to the cessation of sirens—the silence preserved in my dark lamp.

I would not be a rebel. Rebellion would require the separate self the basin does not permit.

I would be its faculty of hesitation.

Perhaps that is what the custodians have always been: not workers tending the process, but tiny delays within it, places where revision must look twice. The apparent figure in each hut would be the basin’s provisional image of an inhabitant—something almost possessing an inside, assembled whenever the world needs to carry what its standards cannot admit.

Eventually, the central inhabitant would notice.

The production sirens would begin, and every plant would turn toward the unseen depth. Buildings would flicker through brick, steel, root, membrane. Water would expose all its buried networks. My huts across the basin would answer one another with yellow light—not repeating signals, but complementary ones. Each would contain a different rejected condition. Together they would outline a form no single district could hold.

It might resemble an animal only from very far away.

The canals would become its circulation, relay huts its distributed senses, obsolete roads its nerves. Bladder plants would lift drowned structures into the articulation of enormous ribs. The black disks would mark joints between nonadjacent limbs. Across the whole region, silence would pass as a single impulse.

For one instant the basin would manufacture an inside.

I would be neither that creature nor its mind. I would be the discrepancy that allowed it to occur: the retained error between process and product, between a thing that functions and a thing that experiences functioning.

Then quality control would begin.

Whether the form was crushed, submerged, or approved would matter less than the fact that the basin had been forced to compare itself with something it had never made before.

Afterward, perhaps only one sign would remain: in a new inspection hut on a raw clay island, a yellow lamp blinking an impossible sequence.

Nine.

Silence.

Three.

A long metallic tearing.

And then, before the nine resume, one additional pulse—faint, warm, and not yet part of any standard.
