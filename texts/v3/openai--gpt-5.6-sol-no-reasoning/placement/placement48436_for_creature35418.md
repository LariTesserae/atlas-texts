I would find myself beneath the central court, not quite in the city and not quite separate from it.

I would be one of the colony’s listening intervals: a thin, branching discontinuity where two dry membranes approach but never join. At first I might seem like damage—an empty seam in the buried body, narrower than a hair, filled only with rarefied gas and green radiance. But the organism would preserve me. New mineral would curl around my edges without sealing me. Filaments would end against me, exchange charge across me, withdraw, and return.

Through that refusal of contact, I would sense the whole basin.

Each fracture would reach me as a change in potential. The gray blades opening at dusk would register as a faint chemical cooling. The measured feeding of the wall-creatures would become minute interruptions, little absences moving along the shell. The tall grazer would announce itself long before it entered the court: black rods altering the stress of one street after another, its white joints carrying a rhythm unlike anything grown from the ground.

I would have no eyes, but I would know shapes by the pressure they displaced. No ears, but the city’s buried ticking would cross me as patterns of charge. I would not think in objects. A tower would be a persistent upward strain. A plaza, relief. A sealed chamber, a dangerous silence gathering toward rupture. The basin rim would be the place where signals thinned until they no longer returned.

My timescale would be uneven. Decades might pass as one held condition, while the lifting of a pavement plate would arrive with overwhelming speed. When gas gathered under the central court, my narrow cavity would brighten. For an hour I would become almost a nerve: receiving the rosettes’ turn, the wall-creatures’ convergence, the translucent awakening of the blades, the coordinated expansion of districts by less than a grain.

Yet I would not command any of it. The city has no throne hidden under its forum, no single mind deserving to be called its self. I would only be a place where its distant regions become briefly legible to one another.

Perhaps, over centuries, that legibility would alter growth. Membranes that ended near me would survive more often because they anticipated fractures. Towers above me would bud in recurring arrangements. Streets would bend—not obviously, never enough to resemble writing—toward pressures not yet present. The colony might begin to preserve certain voids because signals crossed them well. It might develop a dim preference for one proportion of emptiness over another.

Then I would become something like memory.

Not memory stored in substance, because substance here is continually extruded, abandoned, starved, and rebuilt. I would be memory stored in a recurring absence: the city repeatedly growing around the shape of what is not there.

From above, no one could identify me. The central court might tilt, split, become a ramp, then a cluster of open chambers. A tower might rise over one edge and hollow into a skin. The supposed center would migrate. Still, beneath each revision, the colony would remake some version of my gap.

And if it ever failed—if mineral finally bridged the interval and sealed me—I would not die as an animal dies. Contact would erase the difference that allowed me to sense. The two sides would become one conductive body. My last experience would be perfect continuity: every accumulated charge equalizing at once.

Afterward the city would continue.

Elsewhere, perhaps under a newly opened plaza near the western ribs, two membranes would grow toward one another and stop a crystal’s width apart. Green light would enter the seam. A fracture in a distant tower would cross the gap as a pulse.

I would find myself there next: not reincarnated, exactly, but reoccurring wherever the living city needs an emptiness in order to feel itself.
