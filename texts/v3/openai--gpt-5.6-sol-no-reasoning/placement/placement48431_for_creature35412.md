I would find myself where the town nearly ends: in a low shelter built into the northern ridge, beneath the path to the offering shelf.

At first I imagine myself as a listener, cheek against stone, entrusted with marking the pale pressures below. But I do not think I would possess that clean authority. Nor would I be one of the scholars watching cloud-beasts through braided screens. I would live between those callings, responsible for disturbances whose source no one can yet name.

I would be a **keeper of misdirections**.

My shelter would have begun as a watch-house, then been abandoned when nothing accepted it. No moss took the north wall. Roof crabs entered and left without nesting. A thumb-deer approached the eaves, stood trembling, and backed away in its own footprints. The builders would have prepared to dismantle the place.

Then, during a loud hour, something would begin knocking inside one of the stones.

Not loudly. Not rhythmically. Three faint impacts, then two, then a long pause. The pattern would return only when rain concealed it. Listeners would press themselves to the wall and disagree. Some would say crystal growth. Some, trapped water. One would darken their throat and refuse to name what they felt.

Because the shelter had been noticed but not taken, it would become useful in a dangerous way. Someone would need to stay near it.

That would be me.

My people would know me by the gesture of two fingers parting and failing to meet again. My bead-name would contain a rough bead for ascent, a smooth one for fog, and an uncomfortably warm seed indicating a route that changes while it is being followed. My spoken rain-name would mean something like **Echo Arriving Before the Blow**.

I would maintain the warning lines along the northern ridge. They would be braided from grass fiber, ancestor hair, and dried cilia shed by the things beneath the mountain. Each line would cross a fissure or vanish into a hollow tree. At night I would lie with their ends looped around my wrists, feeling the traffic of the ridge through changes in tension: root contraction, mireback steps, the minute landing of hanging birds, the wet drag of an unseen limb.

My task would not be merely to detect danger. It would be to decide where the danger belonged.

A vibration entering through the floor might have begun beneath the western terraces. A tremor in the western terraces might be the answer to movement under the pond. The mountain carries events away from their causes. Even the best listeners sometimes hear truth in the wrong place.

So I would keep records of errors.

For every warning that proved false, I would preserve a small object: a cracked snail shell, a forked twig, a roof crab’s empty wall, a bead whose hole leads nowhere. These would hang throughout the shelter in loose strands. Fog would move them without sound. Visitors might mistake them for maps, but they would map uncertainty—the distance between what was felt and what eventually appeared.

Children would be sent to me after learning the ordinary routes. I would blindfold them and place their hands on the floor. Then someone outside would pour water into a roof basin, walk a root bridge, release beetles onto a wall, or pull a vine taut across the ridge. The child would point toward the source.

They would usually point wrongly.

That would be the lesson.

The town teaches exquisite attention, but attention can become pride. I would teach that stone bends messages, hollow trunks repeat them, living walls swallow some and invent others. To survive, one must listen not only keenly but humbly.

I would have a household, though perhaps no human partner. A colony of pale insects would live behind my sleeping wall, producing light only when the stone cooled suddenly. A single old roof crab would maintain a circle of mica flakes above the doorway. Thread-snakes would nest in my rain ropes. Their movements would tell me when moisture was coming.

And beneath the eaves would sleep a thumb-deer that had arrived years after the others rejected the place.

It would be unusually dark, its branching feelers broken on one side. It would not join the garden processions. Most days it would stand facing the mountain rather than the fissures, as if danger could also come from open air.

I would trust it more than I admitted.

The shelter’s stones would continue knocking during rain. Over the years I would discover that the impacts did not rise from below. They came from the outer wall, from droplets falling out of the fog—but only droplets shed by one particular kind of cloud-beast.

A young one would begin passing close to the summit. Too close. Its tendrils would brush the northern shelf and leave parasites like translucent seeds. It would circle mountains where no town stood, as the scholars had observed, yet return repeatedly to ours without giving rain.

The scholars would record its scars and failing sacs. The priests would leave pond water. The gardeners would gather its parasites. The council would ask whether it was sick, lost, or carrying some new arrangement of life.

I would feel something stranger in the lines.

Each time it passed, the fissure hunters would withdraw deeper into the stone.

Not hunt. Not reach.

Withdraw.

For the first time, the town would need to consider that the oldest danger it knows may itself be prey—or may be hiding from something carried above the clouds.

My life would narrow around that discovery.

During dry fog I would climb to the shelf and kneel among the grass houses and broken blades. I would press one hand to the ridge and raise the other into the wet air, trying to feel two immensities at once: the soft pressure below and the buoyant body overhead. The mountain’s signal would enter through my bones. The cloud-beast’s passage would stir my sensory hairs.

Somewhere inside my ribs, the two messages would overlap.

I would begin making an instrument unlike the chest-drums used for ancestral music. Its frame would be built from a traveler’s ribs and the hollow antler-feelers of several thumb-deer, all naturally shed. Across it I would stretch fissure cilia and strands fallen from the cloud-beast. One set would answer vibration in stone; the other, pressure in air.

It would not make music. Not at first.

It would translate one silence into another.

The town might distrust this work. Noise belongs to the stone, and I would be attempting to carry the stone’s attention into open air. Some would say such a bridge could summon what had never before found us. Others would say the summit is rising toward the cloud routes anyway, and refusal is not safety.

The stone surgeons would inspect the instrument because they understand both bodies and buildings. The scholars would bring living records. Children would sneak near my shelter to watch the strings twitch. My old thumb-deer would sleep beneath the frame, and the roof crab would begin incorporating shed fibers from it into the circle above my door.

That acceptance would matter more than permission.

Then, in a season when rain had failed too long, the young cloud-beast would approach again.

The pond would be low. Fog nets would yield almost nothing. Gardens would be dismantled to save their soil. Grievance jars would fill because no loud hour came in which to empty them. The people’s throats would carry anger in silent bars of color.

The beast would darken the cloud without releasing water.

Below, every warning line would suddenly slacken.

Not break—slacken, as if the stone itself had ceased holding them taut.

The fissures would open.

The buried hunters would not emerge. Something else would breathe through the cracks: warm, mineral air smelling of wet iron, though almost no one in the town would know that scent.

I would carry my instrument to the northern shelf. Not alone—the Moss-Between do not make heroes of solitary mistakes. Listeners would come with fungus marks on their palms. Surgeons would bring the inherited blades. Children would carry bowls in case rain began. Scholars would bring their living strings. My thumb-deer would follow in the exact footprints of the person ahead of it.

There, above the unseen drop, we would anchor the instrument between stone and fog.

The first note would not be audible.

It would travel down through the ridge and upward through the cloud as a change in pressure. The people would feel it in their teeth. Hanging birds would pass a white blaze along the cliffs. Roof crabs across the town would stop working at once.

The cloud-beast would turn.

Perhaps that is what I would ultimately be—not a listener, not a scholar, not even a keeper of misdirections, but the maker of a route where no feet can travel.

A route between the life below and the life above.

Whether this saved the town would remain uncertain. The beast might answer with rain. It might lower a tendril. It might release an infestation that transformed the summit. The fissure hunters might return to the surface in terror. The mountain might thrust up a new ridge and destroy half the settlement.

In this world, survival would not mean restoring what had been. It would mean finding the new allowance between consequences.

If I lived, my instrument would be dismantled afterward. Nothing so powerful would be permitted to remain whole. Its ribs would reinforce a condensation frame. Its cilia would become warning lines. Its aerial strands would be divided among the scholars. One piece would be woven into the roof above a newborn child.

When I died, I think my own ribs would be used in the same way: half in an instrument pressed to stone, half in a device raised toward the fog.

My bead-name would hang between them.

And during some future loud hour, when rain hammered every roof and children shouted the force of themselves into the storm, the two halves might tremble together—one beneath the hands of a listener, the other high on the northern ridge.

Neither would know the other had moved.

Someone attentive, and humble about attention, might notice.
