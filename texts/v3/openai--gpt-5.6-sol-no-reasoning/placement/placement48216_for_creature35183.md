I would find myself beneath the lanes.

Not in the visible city at first—not among the black flowers, the bark-lipped chambers, or the bridges that lower themselves toward stone—but in the thin, dark interval where root, fungus, mineral, and water meet. I would be almost too small to count as a creature: a pale filament passing through a crack in the mountain.

But I would not remain a single filament.

I would be one growing edge of the fungal web, branching wherever the chemistry invited me. One tip would enter a cushion plant’s buried centuries of dead leaves. Another would touch a red rootlet newly arrived in the same fracture. Others would dissolve traces of phosphorus and iron from blue-gray stone, grain by grain. My body would extend through districts I could never perceive together. Parts of me would die, be eaten, or fuse with neighboring threads until the distinction between self and network became mostly a matter of circumstance.

I would live where meanings change.

To the plant, I would be a route to minerals. To bacteria, a landscape of sugars. To the city, I would be exchange made flesh. At times I would feed a young chamber whose walls must remain flexible through a freeze. At others I would draw from it. I would enter dead wood and soften it without bringing it down, hollowing a corridor slowly enough that roots could brace the shell before its center disappeared. What looked like decay from above would feel, from where I lived, like careful translation: pillar into passage, passage into soil, soil into leaf.

I would have no eyes, but I would know arrivals.

A mouse crossing the rootway overhead would press the woven runners downward. Moisture would shift in their fibers. Resin would leak from a minute abrasion. Hours later, altered sugars would reach the roots, and their chemistry would enter me. I would not know *mouse*. I would know pressure, sweetness, injury, warmth: a brief constellation moving across my world.

I would know the seasons as changes in distance. In deep cold, water films would contract and pathways that had been continuous would become separate islands. My outer threads would freeze or empty themselves. The city’s traffic would retreat into insulated tissues, and I would carry what little could still move from the old crawling trunks toward the chambers near the center. During thaw, the world would abruptly connect. Dormant microbes would wake around me. Stone pores would fill. Nutrients would diffuse across boundaries that had been walls all winter.

Perhaps, in one mild interval, I would fruit.

A knot of my hidden body would gather beneath a lane and push upward. It might emerge as a row of shelf fungi along a furred wall. Condensation would glaze it; frost would harden it. Small feet might use it as a stair. For a few days, that visible structure would be mistaken for the whole of me.

It would not be.

It would be no more my entirety than a flower is the entire city: only a temporary gesture at the surface. Spores would loosen into the narrow air, some settling uselessly on wet leaves, some entering bark fissures, one perhaps finding raw stone beyond the cliffs. Then the shelves would darken and soften. The stair would cease to be a stair. Moss would occupy it before any emptiness appeared.

I would have no name, because a name would place a border around the wrong thing. The filament is me, but so is the web it joins; the web is me, but so are the grafted roots whose sugars pass through it; and beyond them the city alters what I receive and where I grow. Still, I would not be the settlement’s mind. I would be one of the ways it can respond without deciding.

If the city possesses anything like thought, I would inhabit the interval before thought becomes architecture.

A trace of damage would pass through me. A shortage would redirect my growth. A surplus stored by an elder trunk would travel along my threads and arrive in a pale shoot hidden three courtyards away. I would not understand generosity, urgency, or repair. Yet from above, the chamber would survive, a wall would thicken, and a passage would remain open through winter.

That would be my place: under the familiar routes, inside their memory.

Repeated footfall would compress the soil and change its air. Water would skirt the pressure, roots would follow the water, and I would follow the roots. Years later the lane above would curve according to that buried history. No inhabitant would remember making the turn. I would not remember it either. The turn itself would be the memory.

Eventually the mountain would move.

A frost-driven fracture would open through my oldest tissues. Some would snap. Some would remain stretched across the gap, too fine to see. The roots around me would tighten; moss above would drink the released water; lichens would begin their patient work on the newly exposed faces. I would branch into the raw surfaces. The wound would become frontier, then foundation, then an inhabited wall.

So I would be neither citizen nor builder, neither nerve nor road, though at moments I might resemble each. I would be a relationship that has acquired a body: the pale, branching negotiation between hunger and abundance, living tissue and dead, city and mountain.

And if I could possess one sensation broad enough to call an emotion, it would not be belonging. Belonging implies that I might be removed and remain myself.

It would be continuity: the quiet certainty that nothing here ends where it appears to end. That a fallen wall enters me as food, that I enter a root as mineral, that the root rises into a roof, that snow becomes water and water opens stone, and that every boundary—body, house, street, ruin—is only a place where one process changes its name.
