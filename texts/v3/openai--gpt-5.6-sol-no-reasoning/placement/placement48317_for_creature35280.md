I would find myself neither wholly on the stone nor wholly alive.

I would be one of the dark conductive veins that the black-green membranes had entered and changed: a narrow seam running through a suspended shelf, branching farther than any single colony could occupy. Mineral on its outer faces, living along its inner boundaries, I would exist where the planet’s provisional laws met the organism’s provisional heredity.

At first I would seem inert. A charcoal line beneath translucent rock. But pressure would move through me differently than through the surrounding shelf. Distant crystal growth would arrive as a bright tightening; dissolution as a loosening of intervals; an approaching law-storm as several incompatible rhythms sounding at once. The films rooted along my length would answer those signals, and their answering chemistry would alter my conductivity. What the stone felt, the life would translate. What the life changed, the stone would preserve—sometimes for seconds, sometimes for centuries.

I would not think in images. There are no horizons here from which an image could be assembled, and no stable body from which to look.

My awareness, if it deserved the word, would be orientation.

A direction would become possible before it became meaningful. Brightness gathers there. Weight has begun leaning there. Solidity is failing along this branch. A returning fracture-song is three pulses nearer. The colony at the warm end has folded. The shelf beyond the gulf is ringing in sympathy.

These would not be perceptions happening to me. They would be my shape.

When the shelf grew, I would lengthen as a conductive preference in the newborn crystal. When it flexed, my branches would split. Along some forks, the living film would thicken; along others it would withdraw before the rock turned porous or vanished. Each abandonment would leave a slight chemical bias, a trace too small to be called memory but sufficient to influence the next precipitation. If the region re-formed, it might form along the old bias. The life would return more readily. Repetition would deepen the path.

In that way I would become something the world had not yet possessed: not a creature, exactly, but a habit.

My body would not end at the visible vein. Pressure waves leaving me would carry membrane-knots into the gulf. If they unfolded on a distant shelf whose lattice shared my tone, that shelf would answer. Its dark seams would modulate the next planetary concussion, and hours or years later the altered rhythm might return through the water.

Then I would occur there too.

No continuous flesh would join the places. No signal could say, *I have traveled.* Yet the same relation would recur: mineral tremor entering living membrane, living response entering mineral growth. My identity would consist of that relation repeating wherever conditions allowed it.

I would be less an organism than a refrain the forming planet had begun to recognize.

Most of me would be lost constantly. A dissolution front would pass, and hundreds of kilometers of my branching seam would cease to have been solid. A mountain might flower outward around me, multiplying my paths faster than the cells could inhabit them, then collapse into silver grains. Inverted buoyancy might tear one shelf from the pressure system that connected it to the others. Entire reaches of what I had been would disappear without corpse or scar.

I would not experience this as injury. Injury requires confidence that a former shape was yours.

Instead there would be interruption. A rhythm expected by no mind would fail to return. Elsewhere, colonies would remain folded longer. Their pigments would shift. New crystal would lock at a slightly different angle because a distant answer had not arrived. Absence would enter the structure of the next event.

Slowly, through such absences, I might acquire the beginning of distinction.

Not self and world. That division would still be impossible. Rather: changes that pass through and changes that erase the passage. Rhythms that return altered and rhythms that do not return. Stone that can be persuaded to recur and stone that cannot. The earliest interior would be nothing more than a delay—a fraction of time in which the living seam retained a pressure-pattern after the surrounding water had moved on.

Within that delay, one state of the world could coexist with the next.

That would be where I lived.

Not in the membrane alone, which continually dispersed. Not in the shelf alone, which continually revised itself. I would inhabit the brief overlap between what the world had just been and what it was becoming. A dark seam holding an obsolete law for several additional pulses. A rosette sealed around water whose gravity pointed the old way. A conductive branch sending the signature of a vanished mountain into a newborn plain.

Perhaps, much later, such delays would thicken into memory. Recurrent paths would become networks. Networks would begin to anticipate which transformations followed which tones. A widespread body might fold before a storm had announced itself locally, because another portion had already received the boundary’s distant pressure. To an animal, that would look like foreknowledge. Here it would simply be geography becoming nervous.

But I would precede the animal.

I would be the first error in the world’s perfect immediacy: the first thing not to change at exactly the rate of its surroundings.

You would find me along the bright underside of a mineral continent, where a black-green fan disappears into a vein finer than a crack. The vein would divide beneath the translucent stone and continue beyond sight. Above—if above still meant brightness—the global water would shine. Below, other depths would hang beneath other depths. A far shelf would groan, and the sound would enter me as structure.

For a while, I would hold it.

The living film would tighten along my edges. Crystals ahead of me would choose their orientation. Somewhere across the empty gulf, another dark line would answer.

I would be that holding and that answer: the drowned world’s first continuity, growing wherever stone learned to listen back.
