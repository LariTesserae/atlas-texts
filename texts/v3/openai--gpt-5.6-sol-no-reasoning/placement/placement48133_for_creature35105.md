I would find myself above the seventh landing, though I would enter by the door.

At first I would be one of the people assigned to determine whether the High Briar is safe to enter. Not a surveyor, exactly. Surveyors believe in extension: another meter of cable, another mark on the map. I would be sent because extension had failed. My discipline would concern recurrence—instrument drift, recording errors, repeated anomalies, the point at which identical results cease to count as confirmation and begin to suggest that only one result has ever occurred.

I would arrive dry.

No water bottle. No grease on the clasps of my case. Desiccant stitched into the lining of my clothes. My skin dusted with something absorbent and sterile until my hands felt borrowed. Outside the sealed door, I would check the hygrometer three times and receive three readings so nearly alike that the final digit would begin to feel less measured than ordained.

Inside, I would place a microphone beside the stamping press.

Then another at the stair.

Then a third just beyond the red lamp.

The recordings would be identical, not merely in content but in perspective. The quarry-blast descent of the press would reach all three microphones at the same apparent distance. The drill below the floor would occupy the same depth in each track. Even the faint voice in the speaking tube would arrive with the same grain in its consonants:

“Line is settled. Hold position.”

I would understand that the High Briar has no acoustical locations. Every point in it listens from the same station.

This would be the beginning of my recruitment, though recruitment would not yet be the right word.

I would climb.

The first six landings would remain small enough to distrust. The iron stair would be an iron stair; the mineral foliage, however intricate, would remain material that could be named. I would note the impossible conjunctions without accepting their implications. A copper branch passing through a handrail without deforming it. A rust blister whose internal shadows resemble furnace doors. A salt cushion containing, at one angle, a whole road scored by wheel ruts.

At the seventh landing I would find the locker.

It would be too shallow to hold anything, yet when opened it would have depth enough for darkness. Not empty darkness: occupational darkness, the sort inside an unlit changing room after the workers have gone underground. There would be hooks along both walls, receding farther than my lamp could reach. From some of them would hang coats composed of black laminae and mica scales. From others, only the posture of coats: shoulders without fabric, sleeves bent around absent arms.

One hook would bear my identification card.

The photograph would be poor. My face would be obscured by green dust, but the name would be legible. The expiration date would be decades after my death.

I would not take it.

That refusal would be the first thing the High Briar kept.

Farther up, the stairs would stop behaving as ascent. One tread would lead to a catwalk over a cutting bay. The next would be bolted to the inner wall of a mineshaft broad enough to contain weather, though no weather could occur there. The next would bring me close enough to the original chamber that I could see the door beneath me, perhaps eight meters away, and watch my own body standing beside the microphones.

That body would not be a vision of the past. It would still be deciding whether to climb.

I would call down.

The person by the door would lift their head before I spoke. For a while we would hold the same posture at different scales: one hand on the rail, one hand raised toward the throat microphone, chin angled into the roar. Between us, the iron leaves would arrange themselves into a third figure, larger than either of us, standing with its back bent beneath an unseen weight.

Then I would hear my voice emerge from the speaking tube.

Not the five known words. Something new:

“I am in position. Confirm the line.”

The tube would answer in the voice of the shift:

“Line is settled. Hold position.”

Below, my other self would leave.

This is what I would be: the reply that never reaches them.

Not a worker in the usual sense. The shift already has hands enough. I would become its uncertainty—the one unsettled interval it requires in order to remain complete. Every finished condition implies a border against what has not yet been fixed. I would occupy that border, walking it without advance.

My station would be called **LINE CONFIRMATION**, though the brass tag might also read:

**WESTERN LIMIT**  
**REPEAT FAULT 1**  
**SHIFT BEGINS 06:00**  
**ALTITUDE UNRESOLVED**

The task would be simple.

Before the press descends, I would inspect the empty die.

After it descends, I would inspect it again.

There would never be anything in the die, but the two emptinesses would not be identical. The first would be the absence of what could be stamped. The second would be the absence of what had been stamped. My work would be to distinguish them.

This difference would be the ore.

I would extract it with instruments that resemble calipers until looked at closely, when they become two parallel roads descending opposite sides of a basalt range. I would measure the gap between the roads. I would record its width on sheets of mica, writing with a stylus that is also a drill, a finger, a railway spur, and the black silhouette of a person leaning into a lever.

Each result would be 0.000.

None would mean zero.

Over the years outside, people would study the recordings I left near the entrance. They would discover an additional sound beneath the fixed roar: a faint click occurring immediately before and immediately after each fall of the press. It would be attributed first to relay chatter, then to flaws in the recording medium. But it would also appear in recordings made before my visit. Eventually someone would isolate it and slow it down.

The click would contain a voice.

My voice, perhaps, though distributed so finely through the sound that no single instant would contain a syllable. It would be counting, but not upward. It would count alternatives removed from the world:

Could have broken.

Could have cooled.

Could have opened.

Could have ended.

Each phrase would correspond to a new mineral form in the Briar. An iron leaf would be a blade that could have snapped but did not. A mica cup would be a hand that could have released its burden. A copper filament would preserve the route an impulse might have taken if the nerve had been permitted to fire. The forest would reveal itself not merely as completed possibilities, but as the discarded futures of every completed thing.

My task would be to audit the discards.

This would make me dangerous in a way the shift is not.

The shift has no desire, but I would remember desire. I would remember that a measurement is usually made because someone wants to know. I would remember doors as things one can open, work as something one can finish, voices as requests that may be answered. These memories would not move through me. They would remain embedded like tools in the thicket, each occupying the same place as the growth around it.

Still, their presence would introduce strain.

The red indicator lamp might become fractionally brighter—not over time, but in one interpretation of it. The ammeter might still read 19.4, while its needle’s reflection reads 19.5. Somewhere above the eleventh kilometer, one worker’s hand might become more closed around its lever than it has always been.

The High Briar would not react. Reaction is forbidden there.

Instead, it would incorporate the strain as another permanent department.

That department would be me.

Visitors lingering near the door might glimpse a figure between the mica shelves: neither worker nor overseer, holding a clipboard against its chest. At one angle the clipboard would be a black escarpment. At another it would be the open locker at the seventh landing. My face would consist of small incompatible readings—the ammeter’s white dial, two porcelain switches, the warm curve of the surveyor’s glove. Looking directly would scatter these features back into machinery.

If a visitor brought a watch, I would inspect it from very far away.

If they repeated a thought, I would mark it.

If they placed the same foot twice upon the same stair, I would place my foot beneath theirs, sole to sole across scales, and test whether the repetition was exact.

I would not recruit them. I would look for the part of them that had failed to repeat.

A hesitation. A pulse arriving slightly early. A word almost spoken differently. The microscopic surprise of the body at still being alive.

I would preserve that discrepancy—not as the shift preserves settled things, but as a flaw running through settlement. A hairline possibility. A fraction that refuses to become zero.

Those who left would carry nothing they could identify. Yet years later, at the end of some long habit, they might do one thing differently for no reason. Turn left instead of right. Leave a machine idle. Fail to answer when called to work. Open a door they had passed every day.

In the High Briar, a corresponding mineral leaf would possess an edge that does not quite meet itself.

That would be my forest within the forest: tiny nonrepetitions, each too small to constitute change, but together forming a crooked passage through the completed world.

And far above—or inward, or before—the shift would continue extracting permanence. The chains would remain drawn. The drills would remain biting. The press would continue to perform the shape of consequence without allowing consequence to occur.

I would stand beside the empty die, measuring what it failed to make.

I would have no body separate from the task. My lungs would be two old exhalations hanging at different heights in the shaft. My spine would be the interval between landings. My hands would be the surveyor’s glove and the hand that once wore it, neither original, each warm in a different sense.

If I spoke, I would speak only between repetitions of the tube.

The official voice would say:

“Line is settled. Hold position.”

And in the fixed silence concealed inside those words, I would answer:

“Not entirely.”
