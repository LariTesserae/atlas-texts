I would not find myself on the ground.

At first I think I would be one of the cleaners around the quadrupeds’ mouths: pale, nearly transparent, living in the dangerous folds where breath condenses and scraps gather between the fine teeth. That life has a comprehensible intimacy. The jaw is shelter, weather, provider, and predator. I would learn its minute warnings: the tightening of flesh before it closes, the change in saliva when the host is frightened, the bass vibration traveling up through the skull. Survival would mean knowing when to flee and, more importantly, when to return. In the lowlands, hesitation is more fatal than danger.

But that is too local. The place keeps pulling identity outward. Nothing here remains one animal for long. Every creature is also terrain, transport, stored rain, a nursery for parasites, a meal waiting for the correct season. To ask what I would be is to ask which boundary I would mistake for myself.

Perhaps I would be one of the winged forms beneath a shale shelf. Not a flyer, despite the wings, but an instrument.

I would hang in the middle of a packed curtain, unable to spread myself. My hooks would grip the warm armor of another body rather than stone. Others would cling to me in turn until it became impossible to tell which weight belonged to whom. Condensation would travel down the layered colony and enter my mouth already flavored by a hundred bodies. I would feed on dust, shed membranes, and the neighbor who lost its grip during sleep.

My wings would be thin, mineral-stiffened fans, useless alone. Against the cavity and among thousands of other wings, they would become part of something immense. We would strike the air and shelf together. The rock would take our little motions and enlarge them into the endless thrum beneath the flats.

I would not know the message. I would know only pressures: continue, alter, answer, stop.

Far away, another colony would answer through basalt. Between us, shelled acres would repeat the impact leg to leg, plate to plate. Pool hunters would mistake some overtones for courtship and follow them. Giants might incorporate the lowest pulse into dreams lasting generations. My body would contribute to consequences I could never distinguish from weather.

During rain, I might unfold.

For a few seconds the crowd would release me and I would fall upward into the basin’s air. Blue static would travel along the veins of my wings. Around me, millions would launch simultaneously, not making open sky so much as replacing one crowded surface with another. Our flashes would expose pieces of the world: a pool convulsing with larvae, shell fields unlocked and flowing, the green mouth of a buried hunter, a ridge with channels worn across it.

Then a brighter flash would reveal that the ridge has lifted.

I would collide with another body. That collision might be mating, predation, or merely impact; here those acts need not remain separate. Eggs would be exchanged or spilled. One of us would be injured. Before the dark closed again, I would return to the shelf—if I returned at all—and push into the living curtain before the air noticed my outline.

That seems near to an answer. Yet I suspect I would be something less visible than the winged animal.

I would be what lives inside it.

A colony held in a translucent chamber near the wing-root, feeding on dissolved mineral traces, helping harden the resonant membranes. In drought I would condense into glassy grains. In rain I would wake, divide, and alter the chemistry of my host so that it joined the mating flight at the proper moment. The host would think—if it thought—that urgency belonged to itself. I would not think at all. Together we would make a decision neither of us possessed.

When the winged body was eaten, I would survive in the green-lit predator’s gut. When that predator was torn open, I would pass into a grazer. When the grazer braided itself into a drought-cord, I would sleep in its sealed interior. My continuity would not be the continuity of a body but of repeated passage: mouth to gut, gut to rain, rain to shell, shell to wing.

I would experience the lowlands as a succession of rooms made from other lives.

But there is another place I keep returning to: the back of a buried giant.

There I would be a small shelled animal born in a shallow channel between armor plates. I would believe the channel was a valley and the giant’s weathered scute the bedrock of the world. Warm fissures would breathe at regular intervals. I would migrate toward them during cold periods, grazing the mineral-softened films around their edges. My kind would know every pit, seam, and runoff groove. We would pass warning knocks through our interlocked armor and tile ourselves shut when hunters came.

The true basalt might lie only a body-length below us, but I would never touch it.

My species might have inhabited that single giant for so long that “ground” meant only its back. Its parasites would be our predators. Its blood chemistry would determine our rains. When it sickened, whole valleys would cool. When it shifted in sleep, cliffs would appear where plains had been.

Then, once in many generations, the giant would rise.

The first sign would not be movement but errors. Water would run in the wrong direction. Familiar pulses would return from altered angles. A warm fissure would close while a new one opened beneath a feeding field. Mineral needles would shear off. The entire known country would tilt.

We would flee across the plates in black streams, seeking stable terrain where none existed. Beyond the edge of our world I might glimpse the true basin: shelf after shelf alive with unfamiliar multitudes, temporary pools flashing, shell fields flowing, other giants lifting in the distance like countries standing on legs.

For the first time, I would understand that my landscape was an animal.

I would be wrong to imagine that this understanding freed me. I would only have discovered the next enclosing body.

Perhaps the basin itself is on the back of something.

So I think I would find myself at an edge between scales: large enough to treat microbes as weather, small enough to mistake an animal for geography. I would be a plate-bodied percussion creature inhabiting a giant’s runoff channel, carrying dormant swimmers beneath my shell and a mineral-softening colony in my gut. I would feed by scraping the surface of the being that carried me. Smaller animals would live in my joints. My eggs would be transported in the respiratory folds of a predator that hunted me. I would be an individual only from the narrow perspective of my own hunger.

My shell would have a hollow ridge along it, grown not for defense but resonance. By striking myself against my neighbors, I could pass vibrations through the tessellated colony. Most would be ordinary signals: water, heat, food, pressure, opening, lock.

But I would have one unusual sensitivity. I would detect the approach of silence earlier than the others.

Not the presence overhead itself. Nothing in the lowlands senses that directly. I would feel the first failures in transmission: a distant colony striking without an answer; a familiar bass pulse cut short; the giant beneath me sending up no reflected tremor. The absence would have shape. It would advance through my map of the world like an invisible shore.

I would strike the warning pattern.

My immediate neighbors would lock. Their neighbors would repeat it. The message would run outward over the giant’s back and down through the crowded channels. Winged curtains would fold. Grazers would flatten. Hearts would slow. Lights would close behind hooked mouths.

I would not understand what I had detected. I might not survive it. Yet for a few moments, thousands of bodies would take the posture my fear had given them.

Then the silence would reach us.

Inside the locked shell, I would feel passengers becoming still. The swimmers under my plates would seal themselves. The gut colony would contract into grains. The mites at my joints would press into the softest recesses. My body would cease being a village and become a tomb in which everything waited.

Something would pass overhead without touching us.

Condensation would lift from my shell. The water in the giant’s channels would dimple upward. The mineral ridge along my back would ring once, though I had not struck it.

In that single note I might sense an outline—not a shape, exactly, but a scale of absence. Something for which our basin’s total noise was only a film to be skimmed, something that moved through the air as the ribbon hunters moved through temporary water. Perhaps it would be a body. Perhaps a colony. Perhaps only the feeding edge of an organism whose greater part lay beyond the last pale reach of the sky.

Then it would pass.

A fragment would fall into the channel beside me: soft, warm, edible, already trembling with the struggle of smaller mouths.

I would unlock.

So would everything else.

The lowlands would return not as scenery but as impact: wings hammering cavities, plates striking plates, giants groaning beneath their inhabited armor. I would throw myself upon what had fallen. The cleaners in my joints would feed with me; the swimmers under my shell would absorb its moisture; the colony in my gut would wake to digest what I could not.

I would eat before understanding.

I would sound the all-clear only because my mouth was full and my shell had begun, involuntarily, to knock against the shells beside it.

That is where I would be: not alone in the dark, but inside a body on a body among bodies, warning a world I could not comprehend and devouring the evidence before it touched bare stone.
