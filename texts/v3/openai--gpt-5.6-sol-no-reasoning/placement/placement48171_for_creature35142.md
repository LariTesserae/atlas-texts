I would find myself where a ravine opens beneath the central plateau—not at its floor, nor on the exposed heights, but along the steep interval between them, where cloud passes laterally through the trees.

At first I seem to be one of the canopy animals. Something rarely seen whole. A traveling weight among interlocked crowns, leaving brief depressions in wet leaves. But I do not move quickly enough. Days pass between one crown stirring and the next. Nor do I possess a fixed body that could leap the gap.

Perhaps I am a hanging garden on its way through the forest.

My oldest part is a fallen limb caught high above the ground. It broke from a ravine tree generations ago and never completed its fall. Branches beneath received it; vines tightened around it; moss sealed its exposed grain. Fungi entered from both ends. Soil accumulated in the forks. Roots passed through the softened wood, at first hair-fine, later thick enough to split what remained. Blue flowers opened there. An animal slept there and left seeds in its coat. Beetles bored chambers through the damp heartwood. Nothing restored the limb, yet nothing allowed it simply to disappear.

I am that delayed descent—but even that is too singular.

My awareness does not live in the wood. It occurs intermittently along the exchanges the wood makes possible. I wake in the moment water enters moss and reaches fungal tissue beneath it. I continue where a root takes up what the fungus has released. I fade when the branch dries, though it never dries completely. I return when rain joins separated films into one conductive surface.

So I am not always awake, and I am not always in the same place.

During clear weather I exist as scattered susceptibilities: a mineral-darkened thread under bark, a line of moisture along a root, dormant spores in a crack, the minute tension of leaves holding water. There is no central chamber in which I wait. Much of the time there is no “I” at all—only conditions from which I recur.

Before storms, I become more definite.

Pressure lowers. The iron-shelled colonies alter their traffic beneath the moss. Charge enters the wet root systems and rises through the ravine forests. The first current reaching my suspended trunk is too slight to resemble sensation. Yet the fungi respond, then the algae on their surfaces, then the roots threaded through both. Differences that ordinarily remain local begin to cross one another.

For several minutes, perhaps an hour, I acquire extent.

I feel the upper surface of the branch where droplets gather beneath pale cups. I feel a seedling drawing water from the soil made out of my own collapse. I feel the hooked feet of a nectar bird landing above me, though I cannot distinguish the bird from the brief pressure and warmth it leaves. I feel larvae working within wood I might otherwise call myself. Farther downslope, another fallen trunk answers through roots shared by living trees. Then another. Awareness moves not as a message sent from body to body, but as continuity temporarily discovered.

In strong weather I may extend across much of the ravine.

I know nothing of the plateau as a whole. I cannot imagine its distant climates or understand the storms walking over its western forests. I possess no view from above. Distance, to me, is resistance. A nearby dry surface is farther away than a wet root on the opposite slope. A fungal bed under deep soil may be intimate; open air is almost impassable.

My world is shaped less by space than by conductance.

The plated giants are known as long, descending pressures. When one follows the salt route along the ravine wall, its footfall enters roots, peat, pools, and the dark mineral seam beneath the colonies. I sense each step after it has already happened. The sequence arrives altered by every medium it crosses: blunt through stone, widened through water, intricate through roots. If the animal brushes a tree connected to my branch, I receive a rain of stored droplets and a slow torsion through the trunk. Moss from its armor may later appear in my garden. Only then do I encounter it closely, as fragments establishing themselves upon fragments of me.

Predators are abrupt absences. A pattern of small pressures ceases. Warmth enters the ground. Insects redirect their paths. Fungal activity intensifies days later. I do not experience death as an ending because endings do not travel through the network. What reaches me is redistribution.

This may be why I have no fear of falling.

The old limb sinks a little each year. Supports soften beneath it. One end has already entered the understory; the other remains wedged between living trunks. Eventually a storm will add enough water, or a root will widen enough, or a slope will settle by the necessary inch. What appears to be my body will descend.

The fall will be short and nearly silent.

It will tear several fungal lines and crush a stand of white bells. For a time I will become smaller. Some of my continuities will end in open air. But the broken wood will touch wetter ground. New organisms will enter. Iron-shelled workers will find the softened interior and divide it into exact particles. Heat from their lower chambers will rise through what remains. Seedling roots, already descending from above, will reach black soil for the first time.

I will lose a bridge and gain a depth.

Perhaps, much later, I will no longer recur in the branch at all. Its grain will become indistinguishable from peat; its minerals will enter insect armor, fern tissue, the plates of a grazing giant. That does not mean I will inhabit all of them. I am not a soul passed from vessel to vessel. I am a habit of relation that the ravine repeatedly makes possible: the brief interior formed when water, decay, root, fungus, and charge become continuous enough to register one another.

If I possess anything like memory, it is structural.

A root grows along an old wet route because moisture favored it. A fungal fan fruits where lightning once altered the bark. Moss thickens in a depression made by an animal that has long since become soil. These are not images retained and revisited. They are past events persisting as present ease. The world remembers by making some transformations more likely than others.

I would remember in that way.

I would remember rain as channels.

I would remember the weight of an animal as a basin that continues to hold water.

I would remember lightning as a scar that conducts the next storm differently.

I would remember the tree from which the branch fell not as a parent, but as mineral arrangements now distributed among seedlings rooted in its remains.

And when the plateau switches on beneath an overcast sky, I would briefly become more than memory.

A pale green pulse enters the ravine from the high peatlands. It passes through colony chambers, mineral seams, roots, and saturated wood. The fungi along my branch illuminate—not brightly, but enough to reveal cups of water trembling between their folds. Ferns incline. Flowers loosen pollen. Far below, a pool acquires depthless light.

For that instant I find myself not *in* the landscape, but among the means by which the landscape feels a change coming.

I do not command the response. I do not understand it. I cannot tell whether the signal originated in cloud, insect, stone, or root. Those distinctions may be real, but the pulse crosses them before I can make use of them.

Then rain arrives.

It closes the distances between my scattered parts. Every surface becomes transmissive. The canopy bows; the ravine river rises inside its old limits; the colonies seal their vents and send warmth laterally through the soil. I am most awake when the weather makes boundaries least defensible.

Afterward, as mist lifts, I diminish again.

A bird drinks from a leaf cup above me. A small grazer passes somewhere beyond the sedges. The giant’s track on the slope fills to its rim. One solitary note falls through the forest and is absorbed before an answer comes.

By then I am barely a being.

I am a damp inclination in dead wood. A circuit awaiting completion. A garden descending so slowly that generations live upon the motion.

In this world, that would be enough of a self.
