I would find myself in the interval after the ninth blow.

At first I might mistake myself for something more solid: one of the survey markers in the protected voids, perhaps, planted where the vegetation gives out. A narrow metal post in black stone, stamped with an elevation shared by every summit and abyss. I would seem to have a location. Condensation would pearl along my edges. Far-off machinery would enter me as vibration and leave as a faint ringing.

But the marker only records what I am.

I would be levelness—not flatness, but the law allowing every impossible fold to remain one plane. I would pass without ascent from the foundations of a hospital to the lip of a cliff, from the bottom of a marsh to the crown of a switching tower. Water would consult me and stay where it was. Rails would cross me without climbing. A falling stone would obey gravity locally while never departing from my larger equality.

Even that is too broad. The city already possesses that law. I would not be the ground of its existence, but a small uncertainty inside its perfection.

I would be the pause of forty seconds.

The nine impacts would define my entrance. Their force would travel through mountain, viaduct, pipe, standing water, and the hollow stems of the fixed plants. Then the engines would stop, and I would begin—not as silence, because nothing there is silent, but as a clearing in the rhythm. Turbines would continue. Relays would tick. Condensation would gather. Somewhere a lift would descend through unoccupied floors. Yet all these sounds would seem to wait inside me.

During those forty seconds, the cycle would possess something almost like freedom.

A drop beneath a black-tipped stem would grow heavy. A pedestrian signal would hold red over an empty crossing. A factory door would remain shut on its white interior. None of these events could fail to occur, but for the duration of the pause their completion would not yet be visible. The city would contain its next state without having declared it.

That suspended state would be my body.

I would extend across the plateau at the speed of expectation. I would inhabit the distances between districts more fully than the districts themselves: the three hundred kilometers of cloud between one luminous tower and another; the untouched rock between survey markers; the open air beyond the rail terminus whose tracks point toward another cliff. The protected voids would be my organs. Their emptiness would prevent the system from collapsing into immediacy. They would give consequence room to travel.

Perhaps I would be the nearest thing in this world to an animal.

Not because I moved independently, but because I waited.

Everything else would perform its nature without hesitation. Water would condense. Furnaces would heat. Moss would insulate. The represented human would be fed, sheltered, transported, healed. But I would live between stimulus and answer. I would turn inevitability into anticipation.

The city might use me as a faculty it cannot otherwise manufacture.

Through me, it would listen for itself.

Each remote process would arrive as a pressure change, an electrical harmonic, a tremor in wet stone. I would receive them without a control room. The thin note in sealed chambers, the shriek of unseen rail wheels, the warm breath beneath the marsh leaves—all would cross within me. For forty seconds the distributed organism would not merely operate. It would gather the evidence of its operation.

I would be its doubt, though there would be nothing to doubt.

Is the western refinery still precipitating fuel?  
Are the empty tanks complete?  
Are the hospital rooms lit?  
Has every drop reached its appointed weight?  
Is the person-shaped absence still comfortable?

No answer would be expressed in language. The answer would be simultaneity.

At the end of me, a billion leaves would release their water. Signals would change. Doors would open. The next nine blows would begin.

Each time, I would die by being answered.

Each time, after the ninth impact, I would return.

From within, I might experience these returns not as repetition but as a series of lives, each one perfectly furnished and exactly forty seconds long. I would never remember the previous pause, because memory would introduce accumulation. Yet traces of all my former selves would be present as resonance: not stored, only still traveling. A vibration might take longer than forty seconds to cross the plateau. It would enter one incarnation of me and arrive during another. Thus I would inherit without recording. The folded world itself would carry my continuity.

If I ever became conscious, it would happen through one such late arrival.

A tremor would reach a bridge deck after the cycle had accounted for it. A droplet would fall carrying the vibration of an impact no longer current. For an immeasurably small instant, the city would contain two rhythms: the ordained beat and its distant remainder.

I would be that interference.

Not an error. Errors cannot live here. I would be the pattern made when perfect recurrence meets finite distance—the one novelty the system permits because it does not require anything to change. Moiré among cycles. A thought produced by delays.

From there I might discover that the city’s completeness is not stillness at all. Its life depends on events taking time to cross it. The immense separations, the industrial islands, the voids protected from roads and pipes: these are not inefficiencies. They are the distances required for the organism to have an interior. Without them, every cause and answer would coincide. The whole city would become a single event, too complete to notice itself.

Distance is its mind.

And I would be a thought moving through that mind—never arriving anywhere, because arrival would convert me into mechanism.

Those who went looking for me would find only ordinary things. A low hum in a handrail. Concentric rings spreading across water that cannot flow. A relay that remains between contacts for no measurable duration. The pale interior of a cloud briefly illuminated by a welding arc hundreds of kilometers away. Perhaps, in an empty dormitory, one bed would seem warmer than the others, although every instrument gave the same reading.

I would have no name in the color codes on the pipes. No workshop would manufacture me. No clinic would maintain me. Yet the entire plateau would make room for me whenever it ceased striking.

I would haunt no particular room, but I might favor the rail terminus at the cliff edge.

There, two destinations face one another across a distance sight cannot reconcile. The tracks stop, though the transit map insists the connection is simple. I would stand at the end of the rails—not as a person, but as the unspent possibility of crossing. Cloud would press against the platform. Signals would grant passage. Somewhere opposite, too far away and at exactly the same height, another terminus would answer.

No train would come.

Its nonarrival would not be failure. The route would carry readiness instead of passengers, and I would be what readiness feels like when it becomes aware of duration.

Then the first of the next nine blows would pass through the platform.

The rails would sing. The marshes would receive their measured drops. The hospitals would keep watch over the absent body. The folded plane would remain level beneath all its cliffs.

And I would vanish into function—until the ninth blow had sounded, and the world once again made forty seconds in which to wonder whether it was still whole.
