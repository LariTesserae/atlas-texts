I would find myself in the quarter beyond the fern amphitheater, where an avenue passes through three roofless courts and ends at a doorway the mountain sealed long ago.

Not immediately. At first I seem to belong elsewhere.

Perhaps I am one of the expected strangers, climbing from the valleys with soaked clothes and no explanation adequate to the climb. A ridge watcher sees me first and sends the flag for **stranger seen**, then, after watching my pace, adds **not pursued**. By the time I enter the bowl, a dry hearth has been prepared in an empty house. There is kindling, a coarse blanket, a cup turned upside down against the rain. No one asks my purpose until I have slept.

For a season I occupy that house and try to understand its noises. The city sounds crowded through the night. Knockings cross the vacant plazas; stone plates answer one another; hidden wheels labor under floors. I wake believing someone is at the door, but it is only a gutter shifting on its peg. Later I wake believing it is only the gutter and discover a child outside, silently holding up a patterned square that means the eastern lane is flooding.

I learn the city first as errors.

I speak when a drop is about to fall. I step into a pebble ring because rain has nearly erased the print within it. I close the lid of a water jar to keep out midges and am sharply corrected: water must remain in contact with the world. I mistake a cultivated wet court for neglect, a half-built wall for failure, a vacant house for abandonment. I offer to finish things.

In time they teach me not to.

But I do not think I remain a stranger. Nor do I become a mason, Listener, or water keeper. I lack the certainty of hand for stone and the trained ear for the city’s mechanisms. My place emerges where several duties have been left divided among people too far apart to perform them well.

I become the keeper of the sealed doorway.

It stands in a wall so thoroughly joined to the bedrock that, from a distance, there appears to be no architecture there at all. Up close, the former entrance can be inferred: two faint vertical seams, a shallow change in color, the rounded remnant of a lintel. On one side, names have been cut into the oldest visible course. On the other, a bowl rests in a niche. It must always contain water open to the sky.

No one remembers what is behind the wall. Records say the room was once inhabited, then used for woven stores, then as a listening chamber because the growing rock had made its acoustics unusually deep. One survey shows a passage continuing beyond it. Another contradicts this. The last person who could identify the interior died many years ago, leaving no instruction except:

**Do not open what has learned to close.**

My work is not to guard the doorway from intrusion. No one intends to break it. I keep the bowl clear of needles. I renew charcoal lines where stone continues to swell around the old frame. I measure the wall with waxed cord, tap it gently at marked points, and record changes in pitch. I sleep in the adjacent roofless court often enough that the surrounding rooms do not forget human proportion.

At first, this seems too small an obligation. Then the wall begins to answer.

Not in language. Never so conveniently.

During heavy rain, when the city’s ordinary clamor should overwhelm it, a low vibration enters the bowl in the niche. The water wrinkles though the air is still. Sometimes there is one pulse, sometimes three. I assume a buried pipe. The water keepers find none. I assume a distant stone plate transmitted through bedrock. The Listeners compare its rhythm and say no. One of them spends a night beside me, her cheek against the pale surface. Near dawn she sits up and signs—not speaks—**inside**.

This does not mean a person is sealed within. The Foreholders are careful about that distinction. *Inside* may mean beneath, beyond, or carried through the stone. It may mean only that the sound is not striking the wall from our side.

So I begin keeping two records.

The public record contains measurements: dates, rainfall, temperature, interval between pulses, height of water in the bowl, displacement of pins. The private one contains analogies. The vibration resembles a finger drawn around the rim of a distant vessel. It resembles the first sound in a throat before speech. It resembles a sleeper turning over in a room too deep to enter.

As years pass, the doorway becomes less recognizable. The seams soften. The lintel’s shadow disappears. Fresh stone grows around the niche but leaves the bowl untouched. More strangely, a narrow hollow forms above it—not a crack, but a smooth, rounded opening no wider than a wrist.

Wind does not pass through.

Water poured into it does not return.

The council debates whether the opening is a danger, invitation, drain, or unfinished mineral accident. Their conclusions come in pieces between drops:

Do not enlarge it.

Do not seal it.

Do not name it yet.

Because I already maintain the place, its uncertainty becomes mine.

Children visit and put their ears near the hollow. I prevent them from reaching inside, though each asks what would happen. Surveyors arrive with cords. Accommodators sketch roofs that might protect it, then reject them because condensation gathers most heavily on the wall under open sky. A philosopher leaves a polished plate before the aperture to catch whatever light may emerge. It records only cloud.

Years later, the mountain takes my measuring tool.

I leave a bone gauge resting in the niche during a winter storm. In the morning, half of it is enclosed in new pale stone. It cannot be withdrawn without breaking it. According to custom, we do neither. We mark the event, place a screen, and wait.

The stone continues around the gauge, but not over it. Slowly, over several seasons, it forms a smooth collar, holding the tool as a wall might hold a peg. What had been an instrument becomes part of the place measured.

After that, people begin to call me an accommodator, though I have built almost nothing. My work is to adjust customs around a change no one understands. I design a gesture meaning **the wall has altered, but not urgently**. I move the water bowl when mineral growth tilts the niche. I cut a shallow channel so rain can reach it from the roofless court. I ask that the neighboring house remain empty, not from fear but because the wall’s vibration changes when a hearth is lit there.

This is how I become a Foreholder: not through acceptance into a family, but because the city gives me something unfinished that cannot be hurried.

My second name might be **Ear-Before-Stone** or **Cup-at-the-Closed-Door**. The children shorten it. In time they know me only as **Hollow-Keeper**.

Yet I may not be the person in this imagining.

Perhaps I am the hollow itself.

A small absence opening in new stone, not made with a drill, chisel, or wedge. Too regular to dismiss, too narrow to enter, leading somewhere that refuses measurement. I am neither room nor doorway. I am the mountain practicing emptiness.

The city has spent generations raising walls around vacancies, preparing enclosed spaces for people who do not exist yet. Beneath their hands, the mountain has learned the inverse craft. It has begun to place vacancies inside itself.

I would be one of these first attempts.

I would feel the rain touch my lip and disappear into me. I would carry sounds downward, though no one could discover where. Children would whisper their names into me. Surveyors would lower cords and draw them back damp, though the opening remained dry around their hands. Moths might shelter at my edge. Once each year, when the swifts depart, a faint current would rise from within carrying air colder than the weather above.

No oracle would speak through me. No buried people would be found. I would not resolve into a tunnel, tomb, throat, or eye.

That would be too finished.

Instead I would slowly acquire a civic life. The bowl would be kept beneath me. Disputants might come to place a hand against the wall before entering the chamber of drops. New arrivals would be brought past—not told what I mean, only shown that the city contains a question it has agreed not to close. When someone died leaving an obligation with no clear inheritor, the name of that task might be spoken into me, entrusting it temporarily to the mountain.

Over generations, rituals would gather without becoming doctrine.

Then, perhaps, I would widen.

Not enough for passage. Only enough that a person could place an open hand inside. The interior would be smooth and faintly warm despite the cold wall. Anyone doing so would feel a slow pressure—not gripping, not pulling, only meeting the hand from every direction, as water does.

The Foreholders would argue whether this was sensation, imagination, or participation. They would wisely decline to decide.

And perhaps the Hollow-Keeper—the person I might also be—would grow old beside me. Their name would be cut not into a finished wall but into a low course in the neighboring court, where a new room had been started for an unknown resident. On dying, their tools would return to the surveyors. Their blanket would be unraveled. Another keeper would inherit the bowl and the records.

But the bone gauge would remain enclosed in the stone, held forever at the point where measurement became relationship.

That is where I would find myself in this world: at a threshold that no longer opens, beside an opening that is not yet a threshold.

I would be the one who tends it—or the opening being tended.

A stranger taught to wait, or a mountain learning how to make room.
