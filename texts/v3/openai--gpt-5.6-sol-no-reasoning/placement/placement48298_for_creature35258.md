At first I think I would be one of the pearl frogs.

There is comfort in the exactness of the cavity: a body fitting a place so closely that neither needs a name apart from the other. I would feel weather through stone before it became weather in the chamber. Pressure would arrive along my blunt forefeet; rain would deepen inside the wall. I would give my few hollow notes, not as warning or prophecy, but because the coming water had passed through me on its way to being known.

Yet the frog is too settled. Its patience belongs to possession, however modestly: one cavity, widened grain by grain, one mouth opening onto the world. Something in me would keep listening past the flood, down into the passages where the water goes.

Perhaps I would be one of the silver fry, returning through a fissure no wider than its own skull.

I would live in discontinuous chambers of water. A season in the bright basins; years in black seams beneath them; then upward again when rain joined the hidden world to the visible one. I would bear scars from places no other creature could visit. Those above might dispute whether I descended or merely left eggs behind, because they could not imagine that a life might continue through spaces too narrow for observation.

But the fry still belongs to water, and the hollow is not governed by one element. It is a negotiation among stone, light, moisture, and waiting.

Then I think I would be a lantern-back—not one of the brightest, but an old grazer with seven nodules, moving so slowly across the western floor that my journey could be mistaken for mineral change.

By day I would receive light without seeking it. The dome would pass into the small gardens carried along my spine. Clouds would dim me; sunset would stain me amber. At dusk I would emerge and return what I had been given, not all at once, but gradually, while feeding on the nearly invisible life coating the stone.

My identity would be legible as a sequence of fading lights. Another of my kind would know me not by face or voice, but by which nodule darkened first, which held its glow until dawn. If danger came, I could spend the whole day in a single instant of radiance, dazzling the chamber and exposing every creature that moved. Afterward I would be dark, alive, and emptied.

That image comes closest, but it still feels too complete. It gives me a body with clear boundaries, and I do not think the hollow would let me remain so singular.

I would be found in the old river course where the warm floor meets the cool breath rising from below: the ankle-high seam of mist that advances without seeming to move. More precisely, I would live beneath it, in a pale fungal network threaded through red clay and porous calcite.

I would have no central body.

One part of me would line a pearl frog’s cavity. Another would enter the roots of a translucent grass. Fine strands would bind fresh flood-silt before it dried. I would inhabit the film grazed by lantern-backs and the chalky cases of moth larvae. Beetles would carry fragments of me along their habitual roads, believing perhaps that they cultivated me, though their feet would also be among the ways I cultivated myself.

Most of me would be still enough to cast no shadow. When I moved, it would not be as an animal crosses open ground. I would grow at the tips: a whitening of clay, a filament passing through a pore, a fruiting haze after rain. My motion would be distributed across days, so faint that darkness could never gather beneath it.

I would know the chamber by contact rather than sight. The dome’s light would reach me as sugars descending through grass roots. A swift’s arrival would be the sudden freezing of many small bodies above. The long-eared primates would register as pressure, salt, warmth, and the sharp mineral disturbance of a blade being lifted from its seam. Flood would not be an event witnessed from one place. It would be a transformation occurring everywhere along me: dormant knots opening, spores loosening, drowned threads breaking, other threads waking deeper down.

I might be regarded as a single organism, or as a congress of fungi that exchange substance and memory whenever their filaments meet. The distinction would matter less here than elsewhere. The hollow does not insist that one body end cleanly before another begins.

My task would be to translate.

Stone into salt.  
Light into root-sugar.  
Death into film.  
Flood-silt into ground firm enough for footsteps.  
Footprints into mineral, then mineral into forgetfulness.

I would not rule the chamber’s greater, bodiless inhabitant. I would be one of the tissues through which it almost becomes a being.

And yet I would retain one small, animal wish.

Once in many years, when the flood-thicket opened its blue cups and the mica moths came glittering from the shelves, I would raise a fruiting body from the clay beside the old channel. It would be thin and translucent, no taller than a finger, shaped like a shallow ear. Through the day it would gather the white-gold radiance. At night, when the lantern-backs began their wandering, it would release a faint blue dust.

Not enough to illuminate the chamber. Only enough for the passing grazers to carry spores in the joints of their shells, and for a wakeful primate to pause over it, uncertain whether it had seen stone flower.

By morning the little ear would have collapsed.

Its place would appear empty.

But beneath the floor I would still be listening: to drops, roots, buried water, and the minute pressure of lives waiting to move.
