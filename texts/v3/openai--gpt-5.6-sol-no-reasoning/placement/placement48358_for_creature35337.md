I would find myself below the kelp line, where the drowned mountains descend into permanent dusk.

Not on the bare plain, and not in the station. At first I imagine myself human: one of the maintenance divers working beyond the lamps, following an armored cable down a slope while the water darkens and red drains from every surface. But that life feels borrowed. Too much shell, gauge, schedule, and return. I would always be visiting, carrying a small chamber of air into a place that had no use for it.

Then I seem to be one of the old rockfish holding close to a ledge, knowing the mountain in seasons: where food crosses after the tide turns, which crack remains quiet in a storm, which groves vanish and grow back. Yet even that is too singular. The strongest presence in this world is not an animal but the act of waiting where motion brings something.

So I would be a cold-water coral colony on a worn basalt rib overlooking one of the lower valleys.

I would have begun as almost nothing: a drifting larva settling by chance on rock newly exposed by a landslide. Around me, black sediment would still be sinking. The older thicket would lie buried downslope, its decades of branching reduced to pale fragments. I would fasten to the cleared stone and open into the current.

From there, I would grow without going anywhere.

My body would multiply into a small, branching habitation. Each polyp would be minute, but together we would make a shape large enough to alter the water around us. We would open when the current carried plankton and close when grit came grinding through. Small crustaceans would shelter among our branches. Worms would attach at the base. Basket stars might climb above us and spread their arms, stealing part of the same invisible harvest. Eventually fish would use us as cover, and crabs would pick carefully through the debris caught beneath us.

I would know the upper world only by what descended from it.

A torn piece of kelp would tumble into the valley like a green banner from another country. Fish scales would flash once in an instrument beam. Storms would arrive first as pressure, then as noise, then as a dark fall of organic fragments. The station’s work would come through the mountain: hammering, drilling, the long vibration of a turbine changing load. Whales would be less like animals than weather made audible. Their calls would enter my branches, pass into the basalt, and return from the opposite slope after the source had moved on.

Light would be rare and intrusive. A crew might descend to inspect a sensor tower or the cable crossing above the valley. Their lamps would sweep over me, restoring colors I possessed but could not ordinarily see. For a few seconds I would become ivory, rose, rust, translucent flesh—then collapse again into blue-green shadow as they passed. One diver might pause, careful not to strike the thicket with a fin. A camera would turn. A gloved hand would point. To them I might be a survey entry, an obstacle to rerouting, or evidence that this particular slope had remained undisturbed for a long time.

They would leave bubbles snagged briefly among my branches.

My life would be slow, but not peaceful. Sediment could choke me. A dragging cable could break in moments what took a century to build. The slope above might fail without warning. Warming water could shift the currents that feed me or invite organisms against which I had no defense. Even an ordinary stone, rolled by an extraordinary tide, might erase half the colony.

But injury would not always be an ending. Broken branches could remain alive. New polyps could extend along surviving structure. Other larvae might settle on the fresh rock around me. What looked from a distance like one pale growth would become a thicket assembled across generations—part organism, part inheritance, part shelter made from the mineral remains of earlier lives.

That is where I would be: fixed at the boundary between mountain and moving water, unable to pursue anything, dependent on what the range delivered.

I would not experience stillness as humans do. The stone beneath me would be still. I would not. My polyps would open and close; my tissues would flex; particles would lodge and be cleared; animals would enter, feed, molt, and depart. Currents from distant surfaces and deeper plains would pass through my body. The kelp forest, the plankton bloom, the storm, the whale road, and the human machinery would all reach me eventually as food, vibration, sediment, warmth, poison, or ruin.

I would be rooted in the oldest part of the world and fed by the newest thing to fall through it: a patient, many-mouthed animal growing from a mountain that had long ago finished burning.
