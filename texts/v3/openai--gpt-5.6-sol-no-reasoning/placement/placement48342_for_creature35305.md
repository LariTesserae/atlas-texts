I would find myself where the inhabited wall begins to fray into the plateau: high above the pressure basins, beyond the last crowded terraces, but not yet in the true distances.

There, the city narrows to a chain of obsolete survey stations, cable houses, and small settlements built around buried power lines. The cliff is lower but less trustworthy. Its upper layers are porous gray stone that exhales when warmed, so the ground sometimes seems to sigh beneath the pylons. The municipal handrails end one by one. Beyond them stand black route markers, each just visible from the next in clear air.

At first I imagine myself as a marker keeper, alone in a squat shelter, recording charge-feeder migrations and polishing dust from mirrored vanes. But that is too solitary. This world understands solitude as a measured distance between attachments, and I would be made by what reaches across that distance.

Then I think I might be a Path Reader, following the scars of fallen things. Yet Path Readers reconstruct failures after consequence has already passed beyond sight. I would be drawn instead to consequences that have not happened yet: a faint counter-tremor in a cable, glass gnats collecting where no current should be, a buried line beginning to draw more power than it delivers.

So I would be a boundary listener.

More formally, I would belong to a minor and somewhat disreputable branch of the line-maintenance guild: one of the people sent to determine whether the slow membranes beneath the plateau are alive.

My work station would be called **Last Agreement**, though there would once have been several stations farther out. Their positions would remain on official maps long after their roofs had collapsed. Last Agreement would consist of three rooms sunk into stone, a low observation court, and a cable head where the city’s buried line divides into thinner routes toward the survey towers. Everything outside would be rounded by dust except the newest cuts and the places regularly touched by hands.

The door would open inward. Above it would be incised:

**NO LOAD IS SOLITARY.**

Someone would have added beneath it, lightly and without authorization:

**NOR IS ANY LISTENER.**

I would not be entirely Fastened, though I would have been raised among them. One parent would have come from a pressure-basin community, broad-bodied and uncomfortable in the thin upper air. The other would have belonged to a plateau caravan whose position histories were longer than most household genealogies. From one I would inherit unusually sensitive rib membranes; from the other, an instinct to think in bearings rather than districts.

My public work name would mean something like **Hears-Against-the-Current**. It would not be flattering. It would imply a person who notices inconvenient signals, disputes settled interpretations, and keeps councils waiting while checking one more anchor.

My tools would be simple by the standards of the city: ceramic stakes, conductive mesh, charge combs seeded with copper mites, vibration hammers, heat tiles, and reels of instrument line. I would place arrays across the underground growth fronts and send carefully shaped pulses into the mineral seam. Then I would wait.

Sometimes for hours.

Sometimes for a season.

The membranes would answer, if “answer” is the word, by altering their conductivity. A pulse sent from the western stake might return at the eastern stake after thirteen days, changed by a pattern too regular to be dismissed as diffusion and too slow to satisfy the academies. Other times nothing would happen until a herd of charge feeders crossed kilometers away. Then the whole array would wake at once.

The city would want a category.

If the membranes were animals, stewardship law would apply. Their growth could be redirected, but only with provisions for habitat and continuity.

If they were machines, the ancient-machinery codes would grant salvage rights to whoever demonstrated command.

If they were geology, the energy guild could cut them from the power lines and quarry the reactive layers.

Each answer would create owners. Each would create liabilities. Each would decide who was permitted to injure what.

I would become increasingly certain that the question was malformed.

Not because the membranes were mystical, and not because I believed everything moving was a person. Rather, I would find that they behaved less like individual organisms than like agreements embodied in matter. They would advance where heat, charge, mineral composition, and pressure entered a particular relation. Break that relation and the membrane would retreat. Restore it centuries later and growth would resume along the old boundary, as though the interruption had been brief.

They would not occupy a place.

They would occur between places.

That would be why I understood them.

I, too, would be a creature of transfers: basin and plateau, civic record and frontier rumor, vibration and speech. My profession would exist between geology, animal tending, electrical maintenance, and law, fully trusted by none of them.

Most days would not involve revelation. Most days I would replace seals, test buried lines, scrape glass gnats from junction boxes, and negotiate with caravan crews who had tethered cargo to survey equipment. I would keep a hinge-back named Seventh living in the warm cabinet beneath my instruments. It would have six mineral clips on its shell, though the name referred to a railing in my childhood district, not the number of ornaments. When electrical leakage began, Seventh would wedge itself behind my sleeping ledge before the gnats arrived.

My closest human attachment would be an Unfastened salvager who lived two abandoned stations outward.

Their name would never appear in my civic records. Officially, I would refer to them as an intermittent source with unreliable custody. In practice, they would know the plateau better than any guild surveyor and would bring me fragments of membrane found in dead conduits. They would travel without broadcasting a position history, which I would consider intolerable. I would maintain a private map of their likely routes, which they would consider a form of possession.

We would argue through the long warm evenings.

They would say the city’s first response to anything strange was to fasten it to a category, then invoice its descendants.

I would say the Unfastened confused lack of record with freedom.

They would say a record was merely a tether laid across time.

I would answer that this was precisely why it mattered.

Neither of us would persuade the other, but over years we would establish rituals of exchange. They would leave samples beneath a balanced black shard near the fourth marker. I would leave power cells, medical sealant, and copies of my measurements. Neither of us would acknowledge the fairness of the trade. Eventually our arguments would acquire the cadence of a household bond without the civic protections of one.

The central event of my life would begin almost imperceptibly.

Copper mites would arrange themselves along the wrong side of the comb.

A buried line would register minute losses, not steady but patterned. The membranes would be approaching the conductor, as predicted, yet instead of feeding from it they would begin reproducing the signal carried through it. Freight timings, municipal load pulses, even sections of tactile music transmitted from the lower districts would emerge in altered form across my electrode field.

At first the pattern would look like echo.

Then compression.

Then reply.

A pulse composition played months earlier through the wall’s main cables would return from beneath the plateau, stretched across eleven nights. Certain intervals would be missing. Others would be repeated. The missing intervals would correspond to structural harmonics dangerous to the local crust.

The membranes would not be imitating the city.

They would be editing it.

I would send the findings inward. The academy would call the data contaminated by line noise. The energy guild would order the seam cut before it could compromise distribution. Animal jurists would refuse intervention until independent tests established metabolism. The council would ask who had accepted liability for the experimental array.

My name would be on the stakes.

My teachers’ names would be in my line-name.

My household anchor, kilometers away, would carry the burden if the power line failed.

For the first time, I would understand the seduction of being Unfastened. Not freedom from consequence—there is no such freedom—but the possibility of preventing consequence from reaching those whose names had been attached to mine before I could consent.

I would consider cutting away my line-name.

Instead, I would add another.

Without authorization, I would incise the coordinates of the membrane front into my station’s foundation and list it as a party to the experiment. Not property. Not specimen. Not hazard. A participant with an unclassified share of liability.

This would be legally absurd. It might also delay the cutting crews.

When they arrived, the gas tide would already be climbing the escarpment. Copper haze would erase the farther markers. Static would halo the survey towers. Beneath us, the membrane would be active across a front several kilometers wide.

The guild engineers would carry thermal lances designed to vitrify the seam. The Unfastened salvager would appear on a ridge where no registered person was supposed to be. Plateau walkers would halt their caravans within sight of one another. Charge feeders would stand in the dust with their thin legs spread, shining at the joints.

Everyone would place a hand somewhere: on a rail, a stake, a tool, the ground.

The membrane would send a pulse.

Not through the air. Through the buried line, the station foundation, our soles and palms and ribs. It would contain fragments of the city’s own vibrations: crusher cycles, lift warnings, freight rhythms, old music, the subsonic passage of bellows herds. But among them would be another pattern, immensely slow, translated upward from processes beneath the crust.

It would feel like a strain moving toward failure.

The engineers would read it as interference. I would read it as structural information.

We would have only minutes.

The plateau crust would split along an ancient mineral boundary beneath the main cable head. Not a canyon-opening catastrophe, but a settling large enough to drag the buried conductor toward the escarpment. Had the line remained rigidly anchored, it would have pulled three outer stations and a relay tower into the fracture.

We would release it instead.

That act would violate the oldest instinct of the Fastened: not dropping, but deliberately surrendering load. We would fire the separation bolts and let a section of buried line descend into the opening. Its fall would be short, perhaps twenty meters, but the sound would pass through the stone like a struck door.

The city would lose power across several districts.

Nothing else would fall.

Afterward, the trial would last years.

The power guild would charge me with unauthorized release of municipal material. The survey authority would charge me with falsifying a civic instrument by naming an unrecognized entity as a contracting party. My household would petition to retain my line-name, though doing so would expose them to reparations. The Unfastened salvager would refuse to testify unless guaranteed that no identity record would be created, which would make their testimony inadmissible.

But the severed line, recovered from the fracture, would show that the membrane had grown around microscopic faults in the insulation. Its pulses had not caused the failure. They had detected strain and carried it toward the surface through the only conductive route available.

The question of whether it was alive would remain unanswered.

The legal question would change.

It would no longer be: *What kind of thing is this?*

It would become: *Can warning create an obligation between unlike beings?*

I would spend the rest of my life maintaining that it could.

I would not become famous in the central terraces. My name might be cited in arguments and usually mispronounced. The inscription listing the membrane as a party would be removed from official copies but remain in the station foundation because cutting it out would weaken the stone. Later listeners would run their fingers through the grooves.

I would continue working at Last Agreement as the outer stations failed and were rebuilt. My Unfastened companion might eventually move into the room beside mine, insisting this did not constitute a household. We would install a shared handrail between our sleeping ledges. Neither of us would mention what that meant.

When I died, there would be a dispute over my return.

The city would claim my body through guild covenant. My companion would claim I had promised to remain whole. My household would possess my childhood water accounts. The station itself would contain implants technically owned by the survey authority.

The eventual compromise would be characteristically burdensome.

Most of me would be returned through ordinary cycles. My deep water would enter sealed medical reserves. My plates would become insulation. Metals from my sensory implants would be drawn into electrode wire. A measured portion would be incorporated into the station’s cable head.

Not the main anchor, but the contact bar where incoming vibrations were first translated into record.

There I would persist—not as a consciousness, and not as a ghost, but as material deliberately placed where force becomes information.

Years later, a young listener would arrive from the inner wall. They would be impatient with frontier stories and embarrassed by the shrine-like care given to obsolete instruments. While testing the contact bar, they would feel an anomalous pulse rising from the seam.

They would call for silence.

Everyone in the station would put a hand to the structure.

Far beneath them, the membranes would be moving along a new mineral boundary. Far below the escarpment, bellows beasts would cross the pressure dark. A freight train would begin its descent. The signals would enter the same stone, distinguishable but not separate.

And for a little while, I would be there in the only form this world promises anyone:

not preserved,

not released,

but transferred—

still bearing some part of the load,

still helping an answer reach whoever has learned to touch and wait.
