I would find myself in the open water between two summits, though “between” would be only the visible part of where I lived.

At first I might seem to be one of the long vines: a dark, narrow strand suspended in a catenary between black ridges, bearing hundreds of small leaves. But no root would anchor me. At either end I would enter the forests—not into their flesh, but into the fine trembling boundary around them, where every leaf gives its motions to the water. My body would continue there as pressure rather than tissue.

I would be a listener grown long enough to touch two mountains.

Each of my leaves would face not only the light but a different direction of disturbance. One would register a pale fish bending in a ravine. Another, the minute release of a grazer from an upper fan. Others would turn toward the remote fracture of basalt, or toward the nearly imperceptible displacement preceding the great inhabitant. Together they would not produce sight. They would produce an immense, slow sense of relation: which ridge had shifted toward another, which grove had thickened, where warmth had found a new crack, how the sea’s weight had changed.

My thoughts would travel leaf by leaf.

A sensation entering at one summit might require months to reach the other. It would alter as it passed through me, meeting sunlight, mineral taste, and the older tremors stored in my tissues. By the time it arrived, it would no longer mean merely *stone fell*. It might mean:

*the eastern wall has opened; warmth will gather there; roots will answer.*

This would be as close as I came to prophecy.

I would have no central body. The oldest part of me would lie somewhere in the middle span, but it would not command the rest. Sections torn away would continue listening on their own. If a fragment caught on a forest, it might lengthen into another suspended being. If it sank into violet depth, it might become pale and dormant, holding the final vibration it received for centuries. I would not know whether such fragments remained myself. Identity here would be less a boundary than the persistence of a pattern passing through different matter.

My leaves would be green near the surface and almost black where my body dipped across the pass. Their undersides would carry lines of copper, deposited one grain at a time from the water. Seen closely, those lines would resemble branching script, though nothing had written them. They would be the physical remains of what I had sensed: a mineral record of pressures, shadows, and deep notes.

When a cliff sounded, I would receive the note along my whole length. Every leaf would close. For a while—perhaps several seasons—I would become a single taut curve in the water. Then the leaves would reopen in sequence, releasing the vibration gradually rather than letting it vanish. Nearby grazers might use that fading resonance to navigate. Dormant thread-life might wake in my tissues. The forests at either end might alter their growth by a fraction.

Thus I would not speak, but prolong.

The great inhabitant would be the one thing I could not contain. Long before it appeared, my middle would bow. Leaves would turn edgewise. The pressure arriving from one side would be too broad to separate into signals; it would feel like the mountain range itself beginning to move.

During its passage I would lose all distinction between listening and being touched.

Perhaps a pale surface would emerge below me, so distant and extensive that I could not recognize it as a body. Perhaps forests would be growing upon it. Perhaps what I had taken for one of my anchoring summits would lift slightly, and I would discover that for thousands of years I had stretched between stone and a sleeping animal.

I would not fear it. Fear requires an event to be imagined before it comes. I would know only the present conversion of my shape: slack becoming tension, orientation becoming confusion, separate tremors merging into one continuous pressure. Some part of me might tear free. The rest would trail across the moving dimness for years.

Afterward, I would preserve the passage in new growth. The leaves formed in that era would be broader and nearly transparent, marked by a single dark vein. Centuries later, when the pressure was gone and the forests had straightened, those leaves would still turn toward the route it had taken—not because I remembered an animal, but because my body had acquired a direction.

Eventually the mountains would drift farther apart than I could extend. My tissues would thin. The center of my span would become clear, its green withdrawn toward the ends. Small creatures would walk across me, harvesting the films gathered during my long suspension. Gas bulbs rising from below would touch my underside and hesitate there, silvering the old strands, before slipping through.

At last I would part without sound.

One half would settle into a forest and be incorporated into its canopy. The other would remain extended from the farther summit, ending freely in the water. The severed tips would continue turning their leaves toward one another across the widening pass.

For a long time, each half would still transmit incomplete signals—the beginning of a message on one mountain, its absent continuation on the other.

Then new growth might emerge from both ends.

Not reaching with intention. Not seeking reunion. Simply answering light, warmth, and the minute pull carried through the water. After centuries, the two growths might touch. Their clear tips might adhere. Pressure would pass across the join, faintly at first, then without interruption.

I would find myself there: not resurrected, because I would never have ended cleanly; not a single creature, because I would never have begun as one.

A long green attention between mountains, making continuity where the world had opened.
