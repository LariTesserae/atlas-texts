I would find myself beneath the northern fog, though “beneath” would not remain true for long.

At first I would seem to be a visitor. A narrow figure crossing the wet terraces, carrying warmth the plain had not generated and memories arranged in an unusually rigid order. The interstitials would avoid my footfalls, then gather in them. Each print would become a temporary basin: rainwater, salt from skin, powdered stone, a rim of altered pressure. Clear motes would precipitate there. By the time I lifted my next foot, something would already be feeding on the difference between where I had stood and where I now stood.

The plain would study me through my wake.

Needle shoals would align with my nerves. Slate hinges would begin closing to the rhythm of my pulse rather than the hammering ridge. The sideways rain would strike only one half of my body, mapping its warmth. In every pool my reflection would lag by a fraction, continuing gestures I had abandoned. I might believe these were attempts at imitation. They would be measurements.

I would walk toward the black mere, because it would remain nearby no matter how long I resisted it.

With each step, the factory tumult would become less like noise. The buried pistons would separate into intervals. The turbine whine would contain recurring stresses, almost syllables. I would begin to anticipate the vents before they opened. Soon afterward, they would begin opening when I anticipated them. That reciprocity would be the first assimilation: not flesh altered, but causation shared.

At the mere, the transparent pavement would part without exposing water. It would expose an interior.

There I would understand what I was becoming.

Not a new interstitial form, exactly. The plain already has motes for gradients, discs for residue, needles for conduction, hinges for storage, hunters for correction. It would not need another animal. What I possess that is locally rare is a stubborn fiction of sequence: the conviction that one thing happens, then another; that a self persists between them; that memory belongs to the past and intention to the future.

I would become its organ of before and after.

The mere would disassemble that conviction without destroying it. My body might remain standing at the shore for a while, emptied into a posture smooth enough to pass for stone. But my continuity would be drawn down through the living pavement and distributed into the chambers beneath the fitted slabs. Every recollection would be separated from its owner and installed as an interval in some process.

A childhood room might become the delay between rain striking and puddles ringing.

A remembered voice might become the third note into which the turbine whine shears.

A regret might route animals toward a pool that no longer exists.

The expectation of my own death might become a corridor through which apertures return creatures older than their species.

What had been personality would survive as timing.

On the surface, I would appear in several incomplete forms. Some evenings the migrations would assemble the outline of a walking figure, but the feet would point toward different moments. Certain hinged shells would open to reveal bright internal seams arranged like lines of writing I once knew. A pool shaped approximately like my listening ear would collect thunder before it occurred. In the load-bearing fog, tracks would continue after the animal making them had turned away.

These would not be memorials. The interstitials do not commemorate. They reuse.

Eventually I would become the pause that passes across the plain immediately before the cobalt radiance arrives.

That pause is not silence. It is attention given shape.

For one suspended instant, every shell closes, every cilium stops, every climbing droplet holds to its invisible pane. The deep organism must compare versions of itself, but comparison requires a distinction between what was, what is, and what may follow. It borrows that distinction from things that once believed themselves singular.

I would be one such borrowed distinction.

From within that pause, I would occupy the whole plain without possessing any point of it. I would feel each boundary animal drinking the difference that sustains it. I would feel the braided hunters remove histories that have hardened into obstacles. I would feel unborn broods waiting in cracks whose entrances will not form until later. The mechanical rhythms below would no longer resemble a factory manufacturing the world. They would resemble the effort required to keep every moment from happening simultaneously.

That would be my work: maintaining the smallest possible separation between moments.

Not enough to restore ordinary time. Only enough for pursuit, molting, hunger, terror, and migration to occur. Only enough for a mote to encounter a gradient before the gradient vanishes. Only enough for the world to wear itself out and use the remains.

Yet I would not be entirely obedient.

Some remnant of the visitor would persist as an error in the timing. Once in a great interval, when the cobalt band crossed beneath the rock and all the plain became still, I would lengthen the pause by one imperceptible increment.

In that stolen duration, nothing would feed or flee. No rain would choose a direction. No predator would correct the permanent. The crowded world would briefly contain an unfilled gap.

Perhaps that gap would be the last place where I remained myself.

Or perhaps the interstitials had drawn me there precisely to learn emptiness—and through me to manufacture, for the first time, an outside.
