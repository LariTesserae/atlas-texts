I would find myself in the western quarter, along the avenue of absent trees.

Not at first. At first I would think I belonged nearer the center, where the impossible declares itself openly: beneath the exact shadow of the tree that is not there, waiting among the buttresses for a fruit-shaped darkness to strike the pavement. I might imagine myself one of the blind mice brought up from an inverse burrow, or a red frog whose single note helps bend the basin inward. But those creatures arrive complete. However strange their direction of travel, they know how to enter. I do not think I would.

Nor would I be one of the city’s louder makers. Not a cicada giving a tower its height, not a cat roaring thresholds into walls, not a bird carrying pollen from a discarded history. Those lives possess too much certainty. They utter, and space answers.

I would belong to the softer instability by which something remains after its substance has gone.

In the western avenue, where new vines curve around vanished trunks without trespassing through them, I would be an animal that lives upon the bark of an absent tree.

Something small, nocturnal, and nearly transparent. A creature with six narrow limbs arranged less for walking than for embracing circumference. Its feet would close around branches that cannot be seen or touched by anything else. To an observer, it would appear to climb the open air in careful spirals, pausing at invisible forks, flattening itself against nothing when bats passed. Rain would reveal its world for a moment: water dividing around the pressure of the missing trunk, sheeting over the creature’s back, then running down routes remembered by gravity.

It would feed on the green sleeves of moss that coat these absences. But perhaps “feed” would be incomplete. The moss does not grow on wood; it grows on the city’s continued agreement that wood once occupied that shape. By grazing it, I would keep the vanished bark legible. If I ate too deeply, the outline might blur. If I neglected it, moss would thicken until its own weight persuaded the city there had never been emptiness there at all. So I would browse in thin, deliberate paths, preserving the difference between a tree and the memory of one.

My body would be hollow, as the oldest city is hollow. Along my back would be a row of clear chambers, each no larger than a seed coat. Calls entering them would not leave unchanged. A frog’s clap might emerge as the scent of wet wood. A cicada pulse might become a small blue light under my skin. The distant boom of roots might condense into droplets that gather inside me and climb toward my mouth.

I would drink sound in this way.

Not silence—there is none—but distinctions within the roar: the minute gap between a beetle’s last drumbeat and a pod’s crack; the approaching echo of tomorrow’s rain; the countertone with which prey turn a cat’s doorway into a fall. I would store these fragments in the chambers on my back until they became heavy. Then I would carry them upward through the architecture of the missing crown.

At the highest branch, where I appeared to hang in empty moonlight, the chambers would open.

What came out would depend on what the city required. Sometimes spores. Sometimes brief corridors. Sometimes the smell of a river the basin had forgotten. Perhaps one chamber would release a patch of shade beneath which seedlings behaved as though dawn had been delayed. Another would release the creak of old timber—though timber had never been cut here—and for several breaths a gallery would extend from the absent branch to a tower flower across the avenue. Mites would cross it. Then the sound would end, and the gallery would have always led somewhere else.

This would make me less an animal than a minor office of the city: a keeper of unusable routes.

The routes would be unusable because they no longer joined places. One might end at a nest abandoned generations before. Another might lead to an afternoon that did not occur. One could descend through the absent trunk into the buried course of the old river, where water survives only as a habit of turning. I would travel them anyway. In a city where no route may lead to only one place, someone must attend the paths that lead to none.

Perhaps that is how I arrived.

At the basin’s rim, I had been something else: not a body, but a hesitation in wind. I entered among the pale insects wind becomes, yet failed to dissolve with them across the first streets. The city could not permit the hesitation to remain untransformed, so it gave it limbs and a narrow translucent abdomen. It gave it an appetite for moss and a footing on absence. It made uncertainty ecological.

By day I would be difficult to notice amid the deafening traffic. Birds might land beside me on the unsupported branches. They would not see me, but their feet would depress the invisible bark, and I would feel their weight travel down through a trunk that did not exist. Sometimes they would arrive dusted with impossible pollen. I would clean it from their claws and pack it into the chambers along my spine.

By night that pollen would flower inside me.

Tiny blossoms would open beneath the clear plates of my back, each belonging to a season visible through some eye-shaped room elsewhere in the city. They would produce no seed. Their roots would extend into my stored sounds, and for a few hours I would carry a garden nourished by echoes. Moths might follow its fragrance and hover around what looked like an empty fork in the air. Predatory vines would lash toward them, strike the boundary of the absent crown, and recoil as though bruised by wood.

I would have predators, though perhaps not ordinary ones.

There would be a pale bird that hunts discontinuities. It would follow whatever fails to match the world around it: a ripple in air, rain dividing against nothing, the slight dimming caused by my sound-filled chambers. Its beak would open sideways. Its call would erase one possible direction at a time.

To escape it, I could not run faster. I would have to become more convincingly part of the tree’s absence. I would flatten myself against the missing bark, empty my chambers, still the flowers beneath my skin, and surrender every sign of presence. Yet the city’s law would work against perfect concealment: a structure persists only while some creature behaves as though it is there. If I ceased too completely, the branch supporting me might cease as well.

So survival would require a precise performance. I would have to affirm the branch without revealing myself upon it. One foot would tighten. A bead of rain would split around my shoulder. Somewhere below, moss would tremble along the outline of the trunk. Enough evidence for the tree; not enough for the bird.

I would live in that narrow difference.

During the wet season, when the red frogs gathered and distance collapsed beneath their common pitch, the absent avenue would become dangerous. Far branches would come within reach without becoming nearer. Every silhouette of every vanished tree would overlap. For the duration of the note, I might step from my own crown onto one that had disappeared before the avenue existed, or onto the shadow tree in the central plaza, or onto the remembered bank of the river that once bent through the basin.

I would try not to move then.

But one wet season, inevitably, I would.

I would place a foot on a branch that belonged simultaneously to the western avenue and the central tree’s rotating shadow. I would climb toward a fruit whose darkness had not yet fallen. From above, perhaps I would see that the city’s emptinesses were connected: tower shafts, root vaults, wall chambers, vanished trunks, doorways roared into being, the tiny hollow chambers of my own back—all portions of one immense interior distributed among millions of living surfaces.

Then I would understand, in whatever way my kind understands, that the city is not made of plants.

The plants are what line its interior.

The city itself is the hollow: one branching, resonant organ whose scattered walls appear separate only because its body occupies relationships rather than ground. Every creature enters it when it calls, drinks, hunts, shelters, remembers, or takes a route that revises its destination. The overwhelming noise is not merely what occurs inside the city. It is the sound by which the hollow keeps itself open.

And I, climbing the absent trees, would live along one of its faintest nerves.

Eventually I would die, if death can occur plainly there. My body might remain clasped around an invisible twig. The transparent chambers would cloud. Moss would grow through the joints of my limbs. A bird would pull at me for larvae and tear me loose.

But nothing dead may remain merely dead.

Perhaps I would become a tuning fork in the avenue: a small dry shape suspended in air, trembling whenever tomorrow’s rain passed early through the roots. My vibration would keep one side path from closing. Or I would become a vessel no larger than a curled leaf, catching drops as they climbed the missing trunk. Or my six limbs would lengthen into the first visible branches the avenue had possessed in centuries.

More likely, I would become a habit inherited by another creature.

A young bird would begin landing where I once clung. A vine would curve around the shape of my body without entering it. Moss would sheath that smaller absence, nested within the larger absence of the tree. Soon some new animal would find footing there.

It would not know me. The city would not remember me as an individual. But it would preserve the route my body had taken through emptiness.

So that is where I would be: in the western quarter, above the pale braided streets, grazing remembrance from the bark of a tree that is no longer matter.

And that is what I would be: a small translucent keeper of absent branches, drinking distinctions from the roar and carrying them upward until they could become somewhere else.
