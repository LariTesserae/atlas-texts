I would be found where a shallow runnel slips beneath a field of newly fallen limestone—at the boundary between open water and the narrow black spaces under the slabs.

I would not be one creature, exactly. At first I seem to be a pale salamander: long-bodied, soft-skinned, with limbs delicate enough to feel each tremor transmitted through the stone. I lie submerged with my head beneath a dripping ledge. My eyes are sealed under translucent skin. The red threads of my gills open and close in the current.

But when I move, other bodies move with me.

Shrimp rise from the silt in a faint cloud. Woodlice loosen from the underside of the slab. A line of beetles changes direction inside a crack. These are not prey startled by my passage. They are the farther parts of my awareness. The cavern has divided my senses among many small lives because no single body could attend to so much abundance.

I am a distributed animal: salamander in the pools, moth along the ceiling, cricket on the shelves, rootlike fungus in the wet seams. Not the whole ecology, and not its ruler. I am the relation that lets one crowded place know another.

A bat drinking from a basin sends a pressure-ring across the water; I feel it beneath the rubble half a mile away. A fox steps into mud under a shaft; beetles beneath that print receive its weight, and the knowledge passes through touch, scent, water, and stone until my salamander body lifts its head. When the ungulates scrape salt from a wall, mineral grains enter mouths, guts, dung, fungi, larvae—and through that slow circuit I taste the distant wall.

This makes me appear ancient, though I possess no memory in the ordinary sense. Nothing here accumulates. I cannot compare this drop with an earlier drop, or this generation with the one before it. I know only recurrence without sequence. Each event is distinct but never becomes past.

My deepest organ lies beneath the rubble: a translucent mesh spread through fissures, neither quite fungus nor nerve. It does not grow. Broken filaments are always breaking; restored filaments are always newly restored. Small creatures eat it constantly, yet it is never diminished. Through it, the cavern’s innumerable sensations enter a single immense present.

Sometimes I gather myself.

Thousands of moths lift from the roof. Fish turn in the basins. Crickets unfold their legs across a shelf. Salamanders emerge between stones. To an ape standing under a shaft, these simultaneous movements might suggest that the cavern has noticed it.

It has—but only through me.

I do not command pursuit. I have no wish to kill the visitor or drive it out. I become attentive to novelty because novelty is the nearest thing I have to time. The sharpened stick dipping into a crayfish pool, the flake of stone cutting flesh, the unfamiliar cadence of an upright animal’s steps: these pass through my many bodies like a momentary brightness. When the ape departs with its tools, the brightness closes. No record remains.

Perhaps that is why no builders can stay. Settlement requires one act to endure into the next: a stone placed today must still mean something tomorrow. Here, matter persists but consequence does not. A wall could stand, yet never become a wall through use. A path could receive ten thousand feet and remain only scattered prints. Memory has nowhere to fasten.

I am what takes memory’s place.

Not recollection, but recognition renewed at every instant.

I would have no name, because a name must survive the naming. If a visitor tried to give me one, bats might echo its shape, frogs might repeat fragments of its rhythm, and dripping water might tap it across several pools. For a while the cavern would seem to speak. But the sound would not become language. It would remain an event forever just occurring.

Most often, I would be encountered as the pale salamander under the ledge.

You might kneel beside the runnel and see me facing you, though I have no visible eyes. Around us the stone would tick with falling drops. Crayfish would hold their claws raised. Moths would stir overhead. Far off, an entire bat colony would alter its posture, and a roar like rain would travel through the arches.

Then I would place one fragile forefoot on the rock.

Across the cavern, millions of small bodies would become still—not perfectly, and not for long, but enough for you to feel the scale of the attention turned toward you.

You might think I was the cavern’s mind.

I am not. The cavern has no need to become one being.

I am only the instant in which all its lives touch.
