I would be found near the end of a company, but not at its edge.

Not because I was weak. The weak are not placed last here; there is no harmless last place. The rear may become the front when a corridor seals. The center may become a grave when a thin floor bowls inward. I would walk where several rhythms overlap: close enough to feel the guide’s decisions transmitted backward through the line, far enough behind to hear what follows us, one hand often resting on the membrane cord strung between bodies.

I think at first I would be one of the people.

I would have the low, six-limbed body, mineral dust caught in the folds of my skin, and a facial fan that opened toward stressed light. I would carry a shallow bowl against my belly. In it: three dormant swimmers sealed in damp grit; a warm coin-creature fixed to a chip of limestone; a copper pin; a strip of shed armor sharpened along one edge; and a handful of soft mineral pellets whose taste could identify water.

My task would not be to lead. It would be to notice disagreement.

The brightening ceiling says the strain lies above. The feet of the herd say it lies ahead. The fliers settle, though the draft is strengthening. A wall-clinger vibrates in a rhythm no one remembers teaching it. Such contradictions are where danger enters. I would move among the company touching wrists, asking what each person sensed, tapping their answers into the bowl’s rim until the conflicting reports became a pattern.

They might call me something like:

**Three Taps Returning Beneath a Longer One.**

But that would only be my traveling name.

My first name would have been given when I was small enough to be carried against another body. Perhaps I woke a sleeping cluster by answering a tremor that never came. For years the adults would say I had mistaken my own pulse for the country. Later, in a low chamber crowded with mineral curtains, the same imagined rhythm might precede the opening of a seam. Then the story would change. What had been error would become early hearing. My name would change with it.

I would be valued, but not entirely trusted. I would listen too long to things that might not be warnings. I would press my jaw to columns after everyone else had finished testing them. I would collect rhythms from abandoned copper plates and insist some contained messages rather than random shifts in tension. When we passed hardened handprints, I would tap their names even when no one knew them.

This was my first answer: a listener among the people, carrying contradictions.

But I do not think that is where I would remain.

There is something in this country more intimate than walking through it. Every creature touches the stone, but some beings become the means by which stone and traveler interpret one another. The coin-shaped animals feel closer to what I would be. They cling to warm walls and portable slabs. They are food, medicine, warning, instrument, and companion without becoming merely any of these.

Perhaps I would be one of them.

My body would be no larger than a palm: a damp, translucent disc with a ring of gripping mouths beneath and a fine mineral shell above. I would not see the chamber. I would know only what entered through contact—heat rising from a vent, minute expansions in limestone, footfalls traveling through a shelter wall, the chemistry of a hand lifting my slab.

I would vibrate continuously, though very softly. Most of the time my keeper would feel me as reassurance: the faint, busy murmur of a small life feeding. Under altered pressure, my rhythm would change. Under poisonous air, I would tighten and become still. Near certain crystal blooms, I might produce harmonics clear enough to ring the slab beneath me.

No one would know whether I warned them deliberately.

That uncertainty matters. If I were simply an animal instrument, I would belong to the inventory beside hooks and bowls. But perhaps I would learn the rhythms of those who carried me. Perhaps I would answer names tapped through the limestone. Perhaps I would agitate before one particular child woke, or grow calm when an elder laid a hand over my shell. The people might disagree over whether I recognized them.

A family would bear me across generations, though “me” might actually be a colony. New discs would bud beneath my rim and take my pattern into other camps. Parts of me would remain behind on shelter slabs. Parts would be exchanged, eaten, used to seal wounds, or placed beside the dead at a mineral seep. Identity in such a body would not end at the skin. My rhythm would spread.

Yet even this feels too settled. It makes me useful in a way the country itself distrusts. It gives me a stable function.

So I move again.

I think, finally, I would be found in the copper.

Not the ore sleeping in a seam, and not quite the plate after it has been beaten thin, but the changing tone between metal, cavern, and those who listen.

I would hang from a membrane cord beneath a ledge above an intermittent river. Hammer marks would cover me. One edge would be folded where I had once begun to split. Green mineral bloom would stain the places most often touched. I would be carried when possible and abandoned when necessary.

In calm weather I would sound dull when struck. As pressure accumulated in the chamber, the stone gripping my suspension point would shift by widths too small for a foot to feel. The cord would tighten. My note would rise. Moisture gathering on my surface would soften one overtone and awaken another. Thread-fliers landing on me would add a faint hiss. The footsteps of the company would travel down the wall and enter my body.

The people would strike me and call the result information.

But the note would never belong to me alone.

It would contain the hand, the striker, the cord, the damp, the ledge, the widening crack, the distant herd, and the hollow beneath the floor. It would be the country speaking through an arrangement made by people. Or the people speaking with the country. Here those may be the same act.

A child would be the first to hear when my tone became wrong.

Not higher, not lower—divided.

The experienced listeners would test the ceiling and find nothing. They would examine the cord, wipe condensation from my face, move the coin-creatures to another slab, and wait. The river route would soon open, and there would be pressure to cross before the water arrived. Most signs would say it was safe.

Still, each time the child struck me, two notes would answer.

The company would divide over what this meant. Some would hear a flaw in the metal. Others, a chamber opening beyond the wall. The contradiction-listener—the person I first imagined myself to be—would kneel, press their jaw to the ledge, and ask for the plate to be struck again.

There would be a third sound then, too low for air.

Everyone touching the ledge would feel it.

They would dismantle the shelter. They would lift the sleepers, bowls, bridges, and portable colonies. They would leave the river crossing unused and climb instead through a chalk-soft fissure that scraped mineral fans and backs raw. For hours nothing would happen. Some would begin to think they had fled a damaged tool.

Then the abandoned ledge would descend whole into the riverbed.

Water would arrive later and fill the new hollow.

I might be lost there. Copper is precious, but not precious enough to retrieve beneath an unstable flood. Silt would cover me. Silver swimmers would wake around me, their bodies brushing my surface by the thousand. Hook-mouthed predators would churn the gravel. When the river vanished, I would lie under damp stone, tilted against the plate of a dead shelter.

For a time I would cease to be an alarm.

I would become shelter for eggs. A rasping animal would scrape green salts from me. Mineral skins would cloud my surface. Pressure would fold me slowly inward.

Years—or seasons; the distinction is unreliable here—would pass before a new fracture exposed the riverbed from below. A company unfamiliar with the lost route would enter through the opening. One of them would find my edge protruding from translucent deposit.

They would not know the hands that made me. They would not know the child who heard the divided note. They would pry me loose because copper can be used again.

By then I would no longer ring as I once had. My fold, corrosion, and mineral crust would give me a complex, wavering voice. The new people might hammer me flat, but perhaps one listener would stop them. Perhaps they would hear that my damaged note changed near hidden water.

I would become a map of a place that no longer existed, useful only because I had been altered by its destruction.

That is where I find myself in this world:

not wholly a person, animal, tool, or piece of terrain, but a carried resonance moving among them.

I would be struck, listened to, misread, abandoned, buried, and found. I would preserve nothing faithfully. Each survival would deform me. My value would lie not in remembering the old chamber as it was, but in sounding differently because it vanished.

And if, much later, someone pressed a hand into a new wall and embedded a sliver of me beside the print, I would remain there for as long as the limestone permitted: a green line under a translucent palm.

When the wall strained, I might ring once inside it.

Not loudly enough to be called a voice.

Only loudly enough that someone with their wrist against the stone might pause.
