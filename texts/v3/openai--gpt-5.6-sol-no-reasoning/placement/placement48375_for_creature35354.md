I would find myself in the warm film between one of its feet and the volcanic glass—not quite inside the country, not yet outside it.

I would be a minute, translucent animal whose only organ is a sense of depth.

To other creatures I would resemble a loose ring of six pale threads, knotted around an empty center. I would have no eyes, because light is too certain. Instead, I would feel all the possible distances beneath a surface: which shallow groove might open into weather, which droplet has a horizon, which reflection is hungry, which hairline fracture leads back into the immense animal and which has already become a separate world.

I would live by fastening depths together.

As the creature crosses the summit, each footfall leaves incompatible spaces trembling against one another. Most collapse as soon as its weight lifts: an abyss becomes a scratch; a lake becomes dew; a migratory corridor hardens into glass with its inhabitants still traveling through it. I would stretch myself across that instant of closure. My limbs would enter different scales and hold them in contact just long enough for heat, water, spores, hunters, or memory to pass between them.

The passage would consume me slowly. One limb might remain the width of a bacterium while another lengthened down a chasm for miles. Sometimes one of my threads would be mistaken for a living bridge, and pale grazers would settle along it. Sometimes a suspended pool would catch in my empty center. Swimmers would circle there, believing it a world. I would carry them until rain or shadow gave them somewhere larger to go.

I would feed on almosts: almost-touching surfaces, almost-closed cracks, almost-identical reflections. The narrower the distinction, the more nourishment it would contain. A perfect boundary would starve me. So would a fully open abyss. I could survive only where one thing was in the act of becoming another.

The great animal would not know I accompanied it. Yet I would recognize its passage as others recognize weather. Before each step, the distances around me would lean forward. Afterward, stone would remember possibilities it had never possessed. I would follow in the fading wetness between its limbs, tending the tiny countries shed there.

Eventually the summit air would dry me.

Five of my threads would contract into nothing. The sixth would remain extended through a crack too fine to see, reaching down past hot glass, inverted rain, and the black summit beneath the world. My body outside would appear dead: a pale loop mineralized on the basalt, no larger than salt.

But my empty center would stay open.

Perhaps, much later, an ordinary creature would drink from a bead gathered there. For an instant its mouth would contain the fissure—the ledges, the silent predators, the migrating waters, the immense animal still walking in miniature across the island above.

It would swallow.

And I would become the depth inside its breath.
