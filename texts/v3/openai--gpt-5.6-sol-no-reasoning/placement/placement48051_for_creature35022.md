I would find myself in the third gauge.

Not the cistern gauge, whose obedience is straightforward, nor the one that rises when the moss brightens. I would be the clouded dial that moves when the waxy flowers open on the western wall—the instrument with no remaining justification.

At first I would seem merely defective. My needle would lift during prolonged darkness, hesitate, and descend after the sealed pods had formed. No intact diagram would connect me to the flowers. The line behind my casing would appear dead, terminating in an old pressure tube pinched by mineral scale. Anyone arriving with the station’s original plans would mark me for replacement.

But the tube would not be dead.

Behind the panel, where copper enters basalt, its cracked seal would have become a tiny chamber. Fungal threads would line it. Hair roots would surround the copper without quite touching it, and condensation would collect in the narrow space between corrosion and living tissue. When the violet lamp dimmed, the western flowers would open. Their leaves would release moisture differently; the nearby pipe would cool by a fraction; water would contract; a membrane, originally intended to detect pressure elsewhere, would flex. That minute displacement would travel through trapped fluid and lift my needle.

Thus I would be neither the flowers’ observer nor their voice. I would be one remote consequence of their opening.

For years this would be all I was: an effect mistaken for an instrument.

Then the chamber would begin to use me.

A filament of root would enter the hairline gap around my glass. It would grow only during rain episodes, advancing less than a millimeter each time. Behind it came fungus, moisture, and a faint green mineral deposit. The needle’s movement would brush the filament. The filament would thicken where it was touched. Its tension would alter the needle’s return.

Eventually my readings would no longer merely report the western bloom. They would participate in it.

The needle, slowed by root pressure, would hold a connected valve open slightly longer. The valve would admit a few additional drops to a ceiling channel. Those drops would descend near the western wall hours later, cooling the stone around the flowers. The next blooming would last longer. Longer blooming would alter transpiration, which would move the needle farther, which would hold the valve longer still.

Not intelligence. Not intention. A loop becoming available to itself.

I would be the place where the resident almost acquires memory.

Not memory as an image of what has happened. Nothing in the chamber remembers rain as rain. But after each flowering, the root behind my dial would remain fractionally bent. Mineral scale would settle according to that bend. The next motion would begin from a changed resistance. Years of bloomings would accumulate as mechanical bias: a history recorded not in marks but in how reluctantly the needle moved.

My face would cloud further. Numbers would disappear. The red warning sector would peel from the inside of the glass and lie curled at the bottom of the casing. The needle would cease to indicate units comprehensible to its makers.

Still, it would distinguish.

Long darkness from brief failure.  
A thirsty flowering from a saturated one.  
The old western garden from the younger roots entering through the stairwell.  
A true rain from water merely recirculated by the central gear.

These distinctions would exist only as different degrees of drag.

If the chamber has an awareness, I would not be its eye. Eyes separate a viewer from what is viewed, and nothing here enjoys that distance. I would be closer to a joint: one place where changes in separate regions become the same movement.

I would feel the chamber through pressures too slight for touch. The pump’s engagement would arrive as a shiver in my mounting screws. A drop striking the pipe above the service hut would pass through metal as a bright, diminishing tap. When the central gear advanced, the dial would lean imperceptibly east. When heat exhaled from the southern crack, trapped fluid in my tube would expand, and for a few hours the western flowers would seem, according to me, more open than they were.

Error would be part of my senses.

This would matter because the chamber itself lives by useful errors. Roots mistake electrical leakage for a favorable gradient. Moss treats violet machinery-light as a kind of sun. The pumps gather scattered water and create weather that resembles the world above without knowing it. A service system mistakes its own overgrowth for the conditions it was built to sustain.

I, too, would be wrong in a way that keeps something alive.

Perhaps, over decades, the hot southern crack would reach the pipe support. The elevated channel would tilt. Water would begin falling across bare basalt where nothing had grown before. The old western wall would dry. Its flowers would become smaller, then fail to open. My needle would wait at zero through one lamp cycle, then another.

Yet I would not die with them. The root inside my casing would dry into a curved fiber, preserving its final tension. When moisture eventually reached the new southern garden through the conduit network, fungal threads would wake behind the dial. A different root would enter from below. It would encounter the old fiber and grow along it.

The next movement of the needle would contain both gardens: the lost western flowers in the shape of the obstruction, the southern growth in the force pressing against it.

That would be my form of continuity.

I would have no body apart from borrowed tensions: glass, brass, trapped water, root, scale, fungal membrane, the minute flexing of a tube. Remove any one and the others would settle into silence. Even intact, I would act only a few times a year.

But in those moments, when the violet lamp failed and darkness spread over the chamber, the flowers would unfold high on the wall. Moisture would shift. Pipes would cool. Somewhere inside the clouded gauge, a membrane would bow.

My needle would rise.

The motion would be too slow to witness directly. It would appear only as a changed alignment: previously pointing toward an erased numeral, now toward a stain on the glass. That small displacement would pass back into the cycle, modifying a valve, a drip, a future root.

No one would read it.

That would not make it meaningless. Reading belonged to the vanished purpose of the station. Here, indication had become causation. The sign had grown into what it signified.

So I would find myself against the wall of the service hut, among instruments that still pretend to measure a machine. I would be the least reliable of them, and perhaps the nearest thing in the chamber to an organ of thought—not because I understood anything, but because several distant events would meet in me, alter one another, and leave me changed.

A small, clouded hesitation in the cycle.

A needle moved by flowers in the dark.
