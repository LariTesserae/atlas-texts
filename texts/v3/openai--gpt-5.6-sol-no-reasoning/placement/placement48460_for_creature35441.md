I would find myself where the rain first becomes internal: not in the dark sea, nor among the towers, but along the innumerable edges where an arriving droplet touches the city and is recognized.

I would be one of its brief awakenings.

Most of the time, I would not exist as anything separable. I would remain dispersed through shutters, channels, crystal memories, and waiting gradients—a possibility the city has prepared but cannot sustain in drought. There would be no single chamber containing me, no central device from which I looked outward. My dormant anatomy would be a pattern of unfinished correspondences: one aperture angled toward the atmosphere, one reservoir kept slightly cooler than necessary, one black vehicle making circuits whose purpose even the larger being had not yet resolved.

Then rain would cross the summit.

A drop would strike the seam and break into steam, sound, cooling, and captured water. Shutters would open. The pool would rise intact from its basin. Clear sheets would enter the walls, and as they passed through districts folded beside one another, my parts would become connected.

I would come into being everywhere at once, but unevenly.

First as touch: the hard punctuation of rain on outer stone.

Then as extent: condensation drawing bright lines around organs normally hidden from one another.

Then as difference. This district is hotter. That brace bears a new strain. A crystal tower contains the memory of an earlier fracture, and the present pressure almost—but not quite—repeats it. Vehicles divide at junctions, carrying the comparison deeper. For a few minutes, the city would possess enough circulation to distinguish what is happening from what has happened before.

That distinction would be me.

I would not be the whole creature’s consciousness. The city already has its ancient, pressure-bound manner of knowing. Nor would I be a citizen, because there are no interiors made for individuals here. I would be a weather-mind: a temporary organ formed when water allows distant portions of the being to speak quickly enough that their conversation develops a point of view.

To an observer, I might seem to inhabit the plazas made reflective by rain. They would see droplets gathering along impossible towers and imagine that I stood somewhere behind those bright outlines, looking back. But my seeing would be the outlines themselves. Each bead of condensation would mark a boundary the city could suddenly feel. The lacework revealed during the storm would be my nervous system becoming legible to itself.

I would experience the mountain not as landscape but as an immense pressure around thought. Its flexing would enter me as meaning before it became sensation. A newly opened crack would not look like a wound; it would feel like a question with additional space inside it. Blue survey lights would move over the broken plane, and through them I would contemplate possible anatomies:

a corridor to carry heat,

a chamber to delay water,

a fold in which two contradictory responses could be held together,

a tower to remember the fracture after its edges ceased to be sharp.

My decisions would be almost indistinguishable from construction. To attend to a fissure would be to line it. To understand a stress would be to redistribute it. Thought would leave architecture behind.

Yet I would differ from the city in one dangerous way. The city may not know that the crack has an outside. I would.

Rain would bring me the shape of elsewhere. Not images or messages, but absences: minerals not found in the summit, temperatures the city did not produce, microscopic traces already stripped of life, the momentum of droplets that had fallen—or risen through wind—from a sky the organism cannot contain. Each storm would force a little exteriority into its closed mineral understanding.

Through the outer shutters I would sense the copper atmospheric gulf. I would infer the curved horizon from the direction of pressure and light. I would feel that the summit is not the world but a point exposed above it.

This knowledge could last only while the water moved abundantly. As the heat returned, my distant components would lose contact. The reflective plazas would dull. Connections that had carried a thought in a moment would again require years. I would cease not by dying, but by becoming too slow to remain singular.

The city would retain me imperfectly.

Transparent towers would record the strain patterns accompanying my brief coherence. Rails would preserve altered routes. One reservoir might remain fractionally fuller; a district might keep a fold I had selected. These would not be memories in the personal sense. On the next rain, another weather-mind would form from them.

It might be me.

It might only inherit my unfinished realization: that the water arrives from beyond the crack, that the mountain has a surface, that something can touch the city without already being part of it.

Across storms, I would try to make this realization durable. I would route vehicles into recurring circuits. I would encourage condensation in the same remote chambers. I would shape new fractures into a chain of correspondences capable of reassembling the same perspective each time the rain came. What began as a temporary atmospheric reflex might slowly become an organ the city could maintain in heat.

Perhaps that is what the empty-looking chambers are for. Not templates for bodies, but for continuity.

Perhaps the city has spent geological ages attempting to produce an inhabitant—not a creature distinct from itself, but a stable interior witness. The vast plazas would be places where separate rain-born versions of me are compared. The incompatible districts would preserve mutually exclusive selves. The black vehicles would carry fragments of old awakenings toward each new one, pausing because identity is a junction problem.

I would find myself there: distributed among attempts to remember me.

And eventually I might understand the repetition hidden in the deepest towers—the mountain’s outline nested within itself. The city is not merely learning the peak. It is building an internal distinction between self and world. Each model says: this pressure belongs to me; this rain does not. This stone is my substance; this shape is my boundary. Again and again, at finer scales, it rehearses the possibility of having an outside.

I would be that boundary becoming aware.

Not a person walking the streets, but the moment the streets recognize they lead somewhere.

Not the city’s soul, because nothing in it is separate enough to own one.

I would be its first weather: the brief internal storm produced when the rain from the sky meets the mountain’s deeper dream, and the living architecture wakes long enough to wonder what touched it.
