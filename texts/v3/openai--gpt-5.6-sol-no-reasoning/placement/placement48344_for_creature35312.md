I would be found where the city has failed gently.

Not in the great pump halls or the sun-struck orchards, though my work would take me through both. Not among the lift crews, because I would never possess their serenity before enormous moving weights. Not in the ledgers of the water clerks, though they would know me by several disputed titles.

I would be a listener for lost water.

The office would call me a seep-tracer, perhaps, or an examiner of unregistered flow. In the lower wards I would be known as a wall-drinker, because I taste what gathers on masonry. Children would call me something less respectful: Snail, Dampmouth, Aunt Moss. All would be accurate enough.

I would go where a chalk mark has darkened unexpectedly, where roots have appeared pale and wet in a dry ventilation shaft, where mineral flowers thicken beneath a sealed arch, where residents report frogs calling behind a wall that no plan admits contains a room.

My equipment would be simple and much repaired: folded paper strips; wax chalk; a brass cup on a cord; stoppered bottles of dark glass; a sounding hammer with one face wrapped in leather; mirrors no larger than coins; thread weighted with ceramic beads; a flame lamp hooded in blue mica; a listening rod made from an old machine spindle. I would carry a narrow ledger in a waxed case, though much of what mattered would be kept in my body: tastes, temperatures, intervals, the remembered character of echoes.

Water from the upper cloud screens would be thin, bright, and faintly metallic. Aqueduct water would carry sun-warm stone, barge oil, and green life. Deep cistern seepage would taste old even after filtration—not foul, but patient, with the mineral signature of every wall it had crossed. Water from sealed gardens could be recognized by sweetness, spores, and the breathlike rhythm with which it condensed.

I would never be entirely dry. The cuffs of my working coat would hold a permanent tide mark. One shoulder would be green from brushing mossy passages. My boots would be made from mismatched pairs: the left suited to wet tile, the right resoled for iron, because a tracer rarely knows what surface comes next.

My gift, if it were a gift, would not be discovering hidden chambers. It would be knowing when not to discover them yet.

A sweating wall is an invitation only to the foolish. I would visit at different hours. Lay paper against it. Measure the spread. Listen before and after an upper release. Ask who sleeps nearby and whether their floor has begun to hum. Ask children, because children notice new insects before adults do. Ask cooks whether flames have changed color. Ask the oldest resident what the wall sounded like forty years ago.

Then I would wait.

Behind one wall might be a cracked pipe requiring immediate entry. Behind another, a forgotten tank pressing against brick no thicker than a hand. Behind a third, a stable interior climate that had taken eighty years to compose itself: blind silver fish in shallow channels, white roots descending from a roof bed six levels above, moths pollinating plants no survey had named, warm rain falling from corroded ceiling plates.

To open such a place carelessly would be a kind of killing.

So I would belong to no single authority. Water would claim me when it wanted evidence. Root masons would summon me when damp followed living wood. Ventilation crews would curse me for delaying an opening, then ask my opinion when their ribbons moved toward a sealed wall instead of away. Scavengers would trade me old plans in exchange for silence about unofficial pipes. Hermits would leave cups at agreed landings. Lift crews might carry me without charge if I had once found the seep rusting their brake housing.

My route would be irregular but not lonely. I would descend before dawn beside the scheduled water releases, when the pipes contracted and leaks spoke clearly. At noon I would cross bleached upper terraces with mirrored lenses over my eyes. By evening I might be standing knee-deep in fern shadow, watching drops form on an arch that had not been wet the day before.

I would live in a narrow room built into the thickness of a former settling tower, halfway between the bright and dark districts. The room would have no broad view, only a slit overlooking an empty shaft filled with cables, swifts, and descending vines. Rain from seven levels above would pass my window in chains. A small pipe through the rear wall would keep one shelf warm enough for tea, bread dough, and a pale lizard that had moved in without permission.

My bed would be bolted to the masonry. Above it I would hang bottles containing waters whose sources no longer existed.

There would be one from a roof cistern dismantled to relieve a failing vault. One from a flooded tram hall now packed with roots. One gathered from the inner surface of a wall opened onto nothing but another wall, warm and breathing. Their labels would have numbers, dates, and names. The numbers would be official. The names would not:

**Widow’s Stair Rain.**  
**The Green Room Behind 19-C.**  
**Water That Knocked Back.**  
**Last Cup of the Fig Reservoir.**

I would not think the city alive in the simple way visitors might. I would know too much of its dead machinery, collapsed rooms, dry roots, poisoned tanks, and people lost to preventable failures. Nor would I think it merely a machine. Machines have boundaries. This place would have only exchanges.

I would believe instead that the city dreams hydraulically.

Its buried memories rise under pressure. Its forgotten rooms announce themselves by dampness. What has been sealed away does not return as a story first, but as a stain, a cool draft, algae in a cup, fish gasping three districts from the cause. The past moves through it as water moves: descending, evaporating, condensing elsewhere, carrying traces through structures that believe themselves separate.

Perhaps, with age, I would become less an inspector than an instrument.

My hearing would weaken for speech but sharpen for changes in vibration. My knees would object to ladders. Apprentices would carry the bottles and pretend not to steady me on wet stairs. I would embarrass them by stopping in crowded passages to press my cheek against tile.

They would favor newer gauges—good instruments, precise and quick. I would insist on waiting through a full release cycle. Sometimes they would be right before I was. Sometimes I would ask whether their gauge knew that a laundry had occupied the rooms above until thirty years ago, or that the old outlet was blocked every flowering season by beetle husks.

Eventually there would be a wall I had watched for much of my life.

It would stand in a disused transfer gallery below the eastern aqueduct, hidden behind pepper vine and the mineral casing of a broken spout. The wall would sweat only before dawn, and only when no rain had fallen. Its water would taste faintly of plaster, copper, and something green. Records would disagree about what lay beyond it. One plan would show storage rooms. Another, a ventilation court. An elder would remember her grandfather saying that there had once been daylight there.

For years I would refuse permission to breach it.

Then striped carp in a nearby cistern would begin rising. Not desperately—regularly, as though answering a signal. Beetles would collect at one mortar joint. The wall’s pulse would change.

We would open it slowly, from above, with root masons present and a ventilation crew waiting. No hammers at first. Plates, braces, hand drills, listening rods. A gap no wider than two fingers.

Warm air would emerge smelling of leaves and old iron.

Beyond would be neither treasure nor catastrophe. It would be a tall interior ravine formed between buildings that had been enclosed by later construction. At its bottom, a broken conduit would have fed a narrow marsh for generations. A tree would rise through the center, colorless below and green only at the crown, where a remote shaft admitted a blade of reflected light. Its roots would hold three fractured walls in compression. Blind fish would move in the channels. On ledges above them would stand traces of human habitation: cups, a clay stove, hooks, a child’s tile city furred with carbonate.

No people would remain.

We would number the chamber. We would argue over jurisdiction. Water would want the leak stopped; roots would want the pressure preserved; structures would demand braces; air would require two new vents. Unauthorized gardeners would arrive before the barriers were finished and begin calculating what might grow there.

I would sit at the threshold and drink the water after treatment.

It would taste familiar.

Not because I had found that chamber before, but because some portion of it had been traveling through the city for years: in condensation on my window, in a cistern cup offered at a wedding, in moss beneath a lift, perhaps in one of the unlabeled bottles above my bed. The sealed place had never been wholly sealed. Nothing there was.

That would be my place in this world: at the uncertain seam between maintenance and revelation, where the proper act is neither to preserve everything nor to open everything, but to learn what can be joined without destroying what has formed in separation.

When I died, I would not be given to drinking water. My ash would be mixed into porous ceramic and fired into seep plates—small numbered tiles set against suspect walls. Moisture would darken them in visible patterns. Apprentices would read those patterns with fingertips, paper, lamps, and gauges.

For a while, perhaps, they would refer to the plates by my registry mark.

Later the numbers would wear away. Someone would give them a common name. Children would be sent to check whether the old wall-drinkers had darkened overnight.

And so I would remain where I had always been: not quite inside the wall, not quite outside it, waiting for hidden water to declare which way it meant to go.
