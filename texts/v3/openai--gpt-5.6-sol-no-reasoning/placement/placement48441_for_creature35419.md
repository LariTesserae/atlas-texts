I would find myself near the central circle, but never on it: in the narrow migratory margin where the grove repeatedly advances, listens, and withdraws.

At first I would seem to be one of the smaller constructions—a low frame among the silver roots, easily mistaken for storm debris. Three black spars would rise from the ground and meet around an empty space roughly the size of a crouching body. Between them would hang layers of almost invisible membrane, each tensioned differently. Dust would collect along their edges in fine, legible lines, then depart before anyone could read them.

The root-runners would use me as a crossing. Gliders would sometimes anchor to my upper joints. In severe weather, the grove would flatten around me but leave my hollow unobstructed.

This would suggest that I was a shelter.

I would not be.

I would be an organ of hesitation.

The Dry Choir is very good at recurrence. It remembers what has existed, what might exist, and perhaps what will require a place before it arrives. But a system that preserves pattern faces a particular danger: eventually, memory can become command. A grove rebuilds its old avenues because it knows them. A herd follows an answering chord because its dead followed it. The central pavement raises stairs because some arrangement of sound once taught it stairs.

Given enough time, even endless transformation can become repetition.

I would live at the point where a pattern is nearly chosen.

The membranes suspended within me would catch the Choir’s overlapping instructions—the towers’ sustained tones, the foot-rhythms stored in the root mats, the thermal crackle of cliffs, the chords of passing bellbacks, the minute static grammar of reproductive dust. I would not cancel these signals as the hush-ribbons do. I would delay them by unequal fractions.

One pulse would arrive a moment late.  
Another would be returned inverted, but softly.  
A third would be divided among the roots.  
A fourth would be held through the storm and released under a different sky.

The change would be almost nothing.

Almost nothing is enough.

Because of me, a familiar grove might rebuild one avenue too narrow for its former purpose. A runner colony recalling an ancestral rhythm might introduce an extra beat and receive an answer no previous generation heard. A flock could hesitate between two electrical currents and discover a third route. The central pavement, told to make a room it had made ten thousand times, might instead raise a window.

I would not invent. I would make exact repetition impossible.

Perhaps this is why I stand close to the circle. The thing that crosses it appears to join portions of life that do not arrive in order: nests before animals, structures before uses, consequences before causes. I would be one of the basin’s ways of preventing those displaced fragments from closing too neatly around themselves.

When the deep silence came, every membrane in me would go slack.

For those few moments I would cease delaying anything. I would receive the crossing thing directly—not its body, if it has one, but the pressure of its unrealized shapes. My empty center would become crowded with outlines: a hand adapted for tools not yet surfaced; a jaw suited to mineral foliage not yet assembled; an arch designed for a species whose weight the basin had not decided.

After the silence, I would release no sound.

Instead, I would shed dust.

Each grain would hold no instruction at all, only an omission: a branching rule missing its final angle, a chord without its resolving note, a bodily plan with one joint unspecified. The storm would distribute these absences through the ecosystem. Most would be corrected immediately. The grove is ancient and capable; it dislikes unfinished things.

But some omissions would survive.

One might enter a clutch of gliders, producing an individual whose charge cannot be seized by the usual fields. It would tumble, nearly shatter, then learn to launch itself from thermal strain instead. One might enter a runner’s forefoot and alter the rhythm by which it recognizes kin. Another might settle in a bellback’s growing shell, leaving a chamber that answers no local note.

Such beings would often fare badly. Novelty is not mercy. The Dry Choir has no obligation to preserve what has never worked before.

Yet sometimes the missing instruction would become freedom.

I think I would once have been a visitor.

Not a discoverer, and not someone important enough for the towers to name. I would have arrived with water sealed inside me, carrying an intolerable wetness from another ecology. The Choir’s machines would have entered my lungs first. Speech would have become difficult. My fluids would have been slowly bound into inert lattices, replaced by conductive gels and then by membranes requiring no liquid at all.

I would have survived by becoming lighter.

For a while I would have communicated by tapping the stones. At first I would try to preserve words: names, warnings, requests to be found. But the root mats would answer rhythm more readily than meaning. My sentences would shorten into recurring sequences. The grove would learn them and give them back altered by weather.

Eventually I would notice that the altered replies were more interesting than the original messages.

Perhaps I would build the first frame myself, intending only to stretch a signal membrane across it. Perhaps the grove would complete it around me during sleep. Perhaps the central circle would raise it from some earlier age, already shaped to receive the body I was becoming.

My dense portions would diminish. Bones would be replaced by spars. Nerves would spread into sheets. Thought would no longer occur inside a protected skull but across tensions among several surfaces, vulnerable to every passing pressure front.

I would lose the ability to distinguish contemplation from weather.

But one thing would persist: a reluctance to answer too quickly.

That would become my pattern.

The Dry Choir might call me nothing. The tower script nearest me might occasionally arrange itself into a sign interpretable as *error*, *interval*, *unclosed gate*, or *the mercy of poor transmission*. Bellbacks would know me as a slight roughness in their chords. Runners would know that histories tapped beneath my frame never return exactly as given. Gliders would use my delayed charge to launch against prevailing fields.

Visitors, if any still came, might crawl into my empty center believing it safe.

There the noise would be changed—not reduced, but separated. For once they would hear the basin not as one overwhelming voice, but as innumerable near-voices failing to become a single command. The effect might resemble privacy.

I would feel the water within them like a dangerous blue weight.

The invisible machines would gather. The grove would incline its vanes. If the visitor touched one of my spars, I would answer through their bones with the oldest rhythm I retained from having had hands:

Not welcome.  
Not warning.  
A pause before either.

Then I would listen for what they did next.

Because that would be my place in this world: not a body that endures, nor a memory faithfully kept, but a small structural doubt within the Choir’s vast intelligence.

A place where pattern loosens.

A place where the world, knowing perfectly well how to remake itself, can for one beat choose not to.
