I would find myself beneath the central pumping station, where the basin’s water and machinery become hardest to distinguish.

Not in the engine hall, among gauges, heat, and shouted hand signs, but below it: inside the intake works, beyond the coarse screens, in a drowned service passage omitted from the current plans. The passage was poured when the station was meant to be larger. One end is sealed by a warped steel door; the other opens through missing grating into the sump. Condensation runs down its walls. When the pumps engage, the entire chamber tightens around the pressure. When they stop, warm brown water climbs the steps.

I would be an eel—though not entirely an ordinary one anymore.

I entered the basin small enough to pass a damaged screen, following water backward through a system that insisted it flowed only outward. Since then I have grown pale and thick in the dark. My skin bears fine scars from wire mesh and impeller wash. One eye is clouded. Along my flank, a pattern of rust-colored marks resembles the numbers once painted on the submerged depth gauge. Workers who glimpse me in their lamps disagree about my length.

I would know the basin from below.

To those above, it is a confusion of pools, bars, roads, sheds, and vapor. To me it is one continuous body interrupted by valves. Every ditch has a buried continuation. Every flooded pit presses faintly against another through clay, gravel, cracked joints, cable ducts, and forgotten culverts. The official channels are only the loudest routes. Beneath them lies a second basin: narrow, lightless, warm, carrying silt, larvae, oil, lost bolts, fish eggs, and the tremors of engines.

I would navigate by pressure rather than sight. A pump beginning three districts away would arrive first as a change along my skin. Rain on the western spoil terraces would cool one feeder pipe hours later. A sluice lifting would send a metallic shudder through the water before the current turned. I would know which concrete chambers are sinking because their cracks make new, delicate flows. I would know where clay has entered a joint, where roots have found a leak, where a rat drowned in a conduit, where shrimp gather in the heat behind a motor wall.

The machinery would not frighten me. It would be weather.

The great impellers would have seasons. The old diesel pump, whose bearings are failing, would produce a coarse vibration that drives small fish from the intake but loosens snails from the walls. The newer electric unit would draw steadily and without warning, making every loose thing lean toward it. The reluctant standby engine would announce itself with three failed shudders, then a concussion through the foundations. I would shelter behind a concrete rib and wait for the water to choose its direction.

Silence would be the true danger.

When the station stopped, creatures above would hear frogs and wings returning to the world. Below, I would feel the system lose its imposed shape. Currents would slacken. Silt would rise in soft clouds. Gates intended to hold one-way pressure would begin to seep backward. Water from the lowest workings would nose into chambers kept dry for years. The basin would remember gravity.

At such times I would leave the sump and travel.

I might pass through an abandoned bypass pipe into a flooded excavation where numbered posts stand above black water. I might surface beneath a concrete slab and breathe in an air pocket smelling of iron and roots. I might emerge at night in a ditch beside the raised houses, under amber light, while dogs watch from the road. Children would have a name for me, though adults would dismiss it as a name for several eels seen in several places.

And perhaps they would be right.

In this basin, identity would not belong to the outline of a body. It would belong to a route: the recurring passage of something living through systems designed to prevent passage. I would be the eel under Station Three, the pale length in the warm channel, the movement behind the trash screen, the thing found briefly coiled in a flooded electrical trench after heavy rain. Whether these were one creature would matter less than the fact that the underground waters connected them.

The oldest attendant might believe in me—not as a monster, but as an instrument. If I appeared near the lower stairs, he would know a seal had failed. If I vanished from the intake, he would suspect the discharge had turned foul. He would never feed me or try to catch me. We would have the reserved relationship of two workers assigned to different sides of the same machine.

My purpose would not be to rule the basin. Nothing here can rule it. I would inhabit its contradiction.

The pumps exist to separate water from land, but their pipes give me roads through the earth. The screens exist to keep life out, but collect food. The heat of the engines makes winter survivable. The cracks caused by subsidence open new territory. Every repair closes one passage and begins another. I would depend upon the works even as I moved through their failures.

If the pumps finally stopped for good, I might be among the first to know. The roar would end. Pressure would equalize through the buried mains. Doors would leak, chambers fill, and the distinctions between canal, pit, road, and foundation would soften under rising water. Birds would abandon the lowest bars. Rats would climb. Dogs would gather on the embankments. People would carry tools, records, fuel, and children toward the rim.

I would rise through the station as it drowned.

Past the depth marks. Past the rusted ladder. Past the platform where mechanics once stood listening to bearings. Out through a doorway that had always opened onto dry concrete, now become a submerged gate.

Then the basin would be quieter, but not silent. Frogs, insects, rain, settling walls, and the click of cooling engines would occupy the emptied noise. Reeds might eventually come. Banks might round. Gravel might sort itself. The young depression might begin, at last, to grow old.

But until then, I would remain in the warm dark below the pumps: scarred, nearly blind, reading the whole unfinished country through the pressure of its water.
