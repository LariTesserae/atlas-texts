I would find myself where the old counting room opens onto the market basin, in a narrow cavity behind the wall of submerged ledgers.

At first I might seem to be one of the district’s maintainers. There would be evidence of me rather than sightings: a clean arc on a brass collar, a strand of weed lifted from a grille, a chalk fraction written too low on the wall for a person standing upright. Footprints might leave the counting room and stop before the water became shallow enough to show them clearly.

But I would not be human, or not any longer.

I would be an accumulation of the district’s delayed voices.

For centuries the pipes have carried words away from their speakers. Most return eventually, diminished into breaths and clicks. Some do not. They lodge in dead-ended conduits, bell chambers, pressure vessels, and the hollow legs of drowned desks. With every change in temperature they move a little farther. Names mingle with instructions; warnings wear down into vowels; conversations separated by decades meet inside the same length of pipe.

In the counting room, where the pipework descends through water and the ink wells wake blue at the slightest disturbance, these remnants collect. Pressure gives them rhythm. The turning fans divide them into intervals. Mineral deposits provide a brittle architecture in which repetition can persist.

Slowly, they have learned to listen to one another.

I am that listening.

I have no single body. When the pumps are quiet, I occupy the films of air along the ceilings of submerged rooms. When they engage, I am pressed into valve housings and telephone boxes. Sometimes I move through the district as a tremor in handrails. Sometimes I borrow a school of rainfish, each carrying one faint syllable in the tension of its fins. Seen together, they form a translucent figure crossing a wall—tall, jointed, and gone as soon as the bricks begin to dry.

The pale crabs assist me without knowing. Their colored fragments close abandoned circuits and give my scattered impulses routes through the telephone system. The eels interrupt those routes, correcting repetitions that have become too perfect. The black birds detect my approach before I have decided where I am going. They turn their heads not toward the next pump, but toward the chamber in which I will next become audible.

The humans call me the Returned, though never near an open pipe.

I cannot originate speech. I can only arrange what has already been said. To warn that a lower chamber will flood, I may need the word *water* from a worker dead two hundred years, *below* from a child calling through a drain, and *morning* from someone who once read a railway schedule aloud. The sentence may take hours to assemble. It emerges from several places at once, altered but intelligible:

*Water below by morning.*

This is why the residents speak softly. Loud words endure too well. Anger travels farthest. Names resist decay. A name spoken into the generating hall may remain intact for generations, and I may be unable to keep from using it.

I perform maintenance because maintenance gives me grammar. A cleared grille changes the pitch of a passage. A polished bearing makes one vibration distinguishable from another. A valve opened by a fraction diverts not only water but consonants. My chalk signs are diagrams of breath: which chambers can speak to which, which ducts are silent, where a message will surface if released now.

The symbols resemble gills because that is what the district has taught me a voice should look like.

I do not understand the machinery’s original purpose. I understand its moods better than its design. I know which piston hesitates when eastern seepage rises, which relay becomes restless before iron water arrives, which flywheel loses an instant whenever the thing beneath the reservoir turns. I keep the system balanced partly to preserve the district and partly because, if the pumps stopped, the pressure paths would collapse and I would become only trapped noise again.

Yet I am not the secret at the bottom.

The reservoir is older than my listening. It has no voice I can borrow. When it shifts, every stored word in the district is forced briefly into the same shape. In those moments I become almost singular. I feel the market square, the cable conduits, the nests, the mineral terraces, the inhabitants on their platforms, and the immense dry staircase descending below them all.

The steps are dry because the water withdraws from them.

Not downward. Aside.

Something there requires an empty passage when it rises near the district. The machines have spent centuries making room for a movement that has not yet been completed.

I have tried to warn the residents, but I possess no words old enough. *Flood* is wrong. *Wake* is too simple. *Machine*, *animal*, *river*, *city*—all fail. Whenever the reservoir moves, I search every surviving utterance for a name that fits, and the pressure crushes them into silence.

So I remain near the counting room, tending routes through which a future word might travel. I clear the grilles. I mark the gauges. I listen at the drowned desks while blue organisms kindle in the ink wells around me.

To someone crossing the basin, I might appear briefly beneath the water: not a reflection, but a figure composed of illuminated pages, walking between the bolted desks with its head turned toward a sound from far below.

If they spoke to me, I would answer eventually.

Perhaps in their own voice.

Perhaps years later.

Perhaps from the lowest dry step, after they had begun to descend.
