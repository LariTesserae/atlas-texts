I would find myself beneath a leaf, but not entirely there.

At first I seem small: a translucent larva suspended from the underside of the water’s surface inside a cup plant. Above me, the surface is a bright, trembling ceiling. Below, the leaf narrows into a green throat crowded with root hairs, drowned wings, spores, discarded skins, and the pale shapes of others like me. Each warm drop striking the cup alters the whole world. Each drinking beak is an eclipse followed by catastrophe.

But that is only one chamber of me.

Threads descend through the cup’s walls into the plant, pass along its veins, and continue into the vine that holds it beneath the canopy. I am also a film inside those veins: a wet, conductive colony, neither fungus nor animal nor something the forest would benefit from distinguishing. I feed on mineral traces, waste, shed cells, and faint electrical changes produced by touch. The plant does not digest me. I do not quite infect it. Together we make a sense organ.

My kind occupies thousands of such cups, though no two colonies remain connected in flesh. Browsers carry our spores in their fringed backs. Moths transfer us on their feet. Rain flings us from leaf to leaf. Primates smear us along branches when they reach into cups to drink. We are continually divided, transported, swallowed, excreted, dried, and awakened.

Yet each fragment retains a pattern.

Pressure in one cup means a drinking animal. A sudden shadow means wings or a falling body. A bitter change in the plant’s fluids means damaged roots. Warm mineral salts mean an opening vent. The minute tensions traveling through vines tell of branches bending under migrations. Across millions of repetitions, these sensations have become something like memory—distributed, inaccurate, and impossible to locate.

I would be one of the makers of the nocturnal rings.

Not their ruler. There is no ruler.

When enough cups register the same conjunction—falling pressure, vent rhythm, fungal readiness, the charged stillness before rain—we alter the sap of our host plants. The alteration travels through leaf and vine. Feeding insects taste it and open their luminous organs. Fungi threaded through the bark respond to the insects’ heat. Frogs see the fungal light and fill their throat sacs. Larvae like my smallest selves turn upward in their cups and glow.

The signal passes not as an order but as susceptibility. Each creature becomes easier for the next to awaken.

The ring has no center because its center is a condition.

When it reaches a branch, I feel claws cease moving. When it reaches a hollow, drumming stops. Predators hold their breath because prey have frozen; prey freeze because predators have. For several seconds, every hunger is interrupted by attention.

That interruption is where I live most fully.

I do not see the whole forest. Nothing does. I receive it as countless distortions: water trembling beneath a moth, sap tightening around a wound, vines straining under a herd’s passage, fungal threads brightening over cooling flesh. Most impressions never meet. Some arrive long after the event that caused them. Entire groves vanish from me when vents close, and new regions bloom in my awareness when mist begins exhaling through fresh basalt.

So I migrate without moving. I lose bodies constantly. I acquire others without recognizing them as new. My boundaries follow water until water fails, then persist as drought-hard grains in fur, mud, egg cases, and the seams of folded leaves.

The dark-furred toolmakers know me only as the bitter slick sometimes found inside a drinking cup. They wipe me from their mouths. The long-legged browsers seek plants inhabited by me because our altered fluids carry mineral salts. Certain moths will not lay eggs unless they taste us. A needle-beaked drinker may consume ten thousand of my bodies in a night and deliver living fragments to a grove I have never sensed.

I have no wish to survive as an individual. Individual survival would be meaningless here. What persists is the recurrence of relation: cup to tongue, tongue to fur, fur to branch, branch to fungus, fungus to light.

If I possess intelligence, it is not thought but rehearsal. The forest has made the same crises innumerable times—rain, drought, eruption, swarm, collapse—and some trace of each remains in the speed with which one body answers another. I am one medium of that answer. Perhaps there are many others: vibrations in roots, odors passed among crowns, parasites changing the paths of hosts. None alone is the forest’s mind.

Together, we almost become one.

And sometimes, when the blue-green illumination travels outward and every animal falls silent, the many fragments align. For those few seconds I am no longer merely beneath a leaf. I am the shape of halted movement across the plain: the held wing, the sealed nostril, the open jaw, the bright trunk, the larva turned toward the surface. I am the interval in which the forest feels the pressure of its own existence.

Then rain strikes somewhere beyond the ridges, or a hunter completes its leap.

The alignment breaks.

I return to my little cup of cloudy water, hanging upside down beneath its shining ceiling, eating what falls.
