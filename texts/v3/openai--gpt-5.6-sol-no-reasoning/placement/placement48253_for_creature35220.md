I would be found in the central square, though not quite at the Delay.

At first I imagine myself as one of the children who visits it: small enough to pass through maintenance gaps, old enough to know the pulse codes, wearing a lamp at the brow and another cupped beneath one hand. I would leave brief patterns against the motionless plane and return to see what remained. Not questions exactly—only shapes: three flashes, a pause, two; then the same sequence reversed. I would keep a private ledger of the answers and conceal it behind a loose tile in the former exchange.

But that life feels too separate from the place. The quarter does not favor solitary mysteries. Every private act eventually enters a pipe, a garden, a wall, or someone else’s inherited duty.

So perhaps I would grow into a surveyor of permitted collapse.

My station would be in the northern row of houses, where generations have supervised the slow surrender of rooms. I would measure cracks by touch, fastening delicate ceramic gauges across them and returning each week to read what pressure and gravity had written. I would know which lintel had twelve years left, which façade must be encouraged inward, which wall should be kept standing until the garden downstream had matured enough to use its lime.

My work would not be to prevent ruin. It would be to give ruin an address and a timetable.

On my shell, the family lamps would mark brow, left wrist, throat, and both knees. A surveyor’s lamp would burn at the palm, so that every surface I touched seemed briefly to hold light. One lamp—perhaps the throat—would remain dark for someone whose measurements I had inherited.

Yet even that seems too visible. In this world, I think I would become most myself in the handover between generations, when a measurement ceases to belong to the person who took it.

I would be the keeper of a particular mistake on the municipal maps.

Long ago, a cartographer drew a narrow passage between the exchange and the oxygen house. Perhaps it existed then; perhaps the Delay displaced the observation of it; perhaps it was only a trembling line made when a pump started beneath the drafting table. Later maps copied it because no one could prove it false. Walls were annotated around it. Pipes bent away from it. Maintenance crews occasionally arrived where the passage should have been and found only brick, bacterial felt, and the cold edge of the square.

Instead of removing the error, I would tend it.

I would compare every version of the map. I would interview workers who claimed to have crossed there. I would place pressure tabs along the supposed route and lamps facing away from the Delay, never toward it. I would collect reports of tethers returning wet with a different sediment, of tools heard vibrating on the far side of sealed masonry, of a sixth light among a five-person crew.

Officially, I would be a municipal surveyor assigned to obsolete accessways.

Unofficially, I would be maintaining a door that might exist only at certain times.

Eventually I would pass through it—or be believed to have passed through it. There would be no dramatic disappearance. My crew would see my palm lamp enter the wall several seconds before I moved. The tether would slacken, then draw tight. On one side of the square, I would still be standing with my hand against the brick. On the other, a pale point would already be receding down a passage absent from every reliable plan.

Afterward, the quarter would absorb me in the way it absorbs everything.

My shell might be recovered years later inside a house that had not yet collapsed when I vanished. The ceramic plates would be crusted with a thin, unfamiliar carbonate. The lamps would no longer correspond to my family pattern: brow, throat, both wrists, no knees. Inside there would be no body, only green-black water and a layer of silver silt.

But I do not think I would be dead.

I would become one of the quarter’s delayed presences—not a ghost, exactly, but an inhabited interval. I would exist in the few seconds the plane holds between an act and its observation. When children flashed patterns at the Delay, I would have only that interval in which to alter the reflection: to omit one pulse, prolong another, turn repetition into reply.

I could not speak. I could not warn the city in sentences. But I might lean on its rhythms.

Before a failing seal registered on the instruments, a reflection would tremble.

Before a wall surrendered, the old nonexistent passage would appear on a map as a damp line.

Before the pumping gates opened, an extinguished throat lamp would shine in the square.

The engineers would continue to deny me and continue designing around me. Children would continue to test whether I answered. Surveyors would inherit my notebooks and disagree about whether my final measurements were predictions, records, or errors copied from documents not yet written.

That is where I would find myself: not fully inside the Delay, nor entirely among the habitats, but distributed through the city’s uncertainty about when something has happened.

I would be a surveyor become discrepancy—a small civic organ for sensing stresses before they arrive.

In a place where decay has been assigned routes, I would tend the route taken by consequences.

In a city that survives by allowing each thing to disappear at a useful speed, I would be permitted to disappear very slowly.
