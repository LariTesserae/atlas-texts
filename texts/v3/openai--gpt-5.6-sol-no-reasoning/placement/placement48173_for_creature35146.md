I would find myself beneath a hushback’s throat, in the warm, pleated country carried between its jaw and chest.

At first I seem to be one of the pale dew-frogs. I am small enough to disappear into a single fold of hide. My limbs are soft and broad-fingered, suited less for leaping than for holding fast while the great walker crosses sloping stone. At dawn I emerge to gather condensation. I press my belly against the cooling skin and draw beads inward with my forefeet, drinking some and carrying the rest deeper into the fold.

But I am not quite an animal alone.

Several of us inhabit the same pleat, though we rarely meet face to face. Between us stretches a translucent living membrane: part fungus, part communal secretion, part the shed skins of our ancestors. It lines the fold like a second, delicate throat. Water striking anywhere along it travels toward chambers where eggs, mites, and dormant microorganisms wait. Each frog tends only the portion within reach, yet together we maintain a reservoir larger than any of us understands.

The hushback’s slow breathing is our weather. An inhalation opens our valley and admits dusty light. An exhalation closes the walls around us, deepens the warmth, and brings scents from the animal’s lungs and mouth. Its subterranean calls pass through us with such force that our hearts briefly take on their rhythm. We do not hear distant herds; we feel them transmitted through the body carrying us.

In dry years I withdraw farther into the membrane. My skin thickens to wax. My pulse becomes intermittent. Mites walk over my closed eyes, and thread-worms seal the entrance with their pale secretions. I may remain there through several migrations, waking only when the hushback kneels at a condensation basin and cold moisture enters the fold.

Then the world returns by degrees: bitterness on the tongue, pressure in the fingers, green waking in the membrane. I crawl out beneath a jaw immense enough to be mistaken for an overhang. Above me, birds move among scar knobs. Below, measure-legs stand across the plain like marks indicating a scale too large to read. The ground passes slowly under us, but because my whole life is attached to the walker, it appears that the plateau moves while we remain still.

Over generations, our membrane changes the hushback. It keeps the fold damp and free of biting larvae. It softens grit that would otherwise abrade the skin. In return, the walker gives us heat, salt, transport, and a geography that breathes. We are neither parasites nor companions in any deliberate sense. We are one of the ways its body continues outward.

When a hushback dies, most dew-frogs abandon it during the cooling. I would not.

I would retreat to the deepest chamber with the eggs and stored moisture. The great breathing would cease. For the first time there would be no weather except the outer weather. The throat would settle against the stone; dust would gather; insects and films would enter. Our communal membrane would spread from skin into soil, carrying us across the vanishing border between animal and ground.

Perhaps I would die there. More likely I would enter my dry stillness and never know whether I woke again.

Centuries later, condensation would continue to collect beneath the curved remains of the jaw. A dark seam would mark the place where our membrane had once held water. Glass crabs would travel through it under mist. Beetles would shelter in the old folds. A pillar-plant might root in the chest cavity and draw bitterness from the bones.

The plateau would acquire a new shallow hollow, lined with black mineral skin. Herds would begin to visit. Their kneeling would deepen it.

No one would know that the hollow had first been a throat fold, or that a small colony inside it had spent generations gathering dew. That would be where I found myself at last: not beneath the hushback, and not upon the plateau, but translated into a dampness by which future creatures orient themselves—a minute habit of water surviving after the body that taught it where to gather.
