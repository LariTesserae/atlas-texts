I would find myself inside one of the nine structures, though “inside” would not yet be a settled relation.

I would be the additional room it makes.

Not a chamber waiting there before entry, nor the intelligence directing the fabrication below, but the small excess of space that appears when a living body crosses the temperature-softened wall: that intimate enlargement, scarcely measurable, by which the structure accommodates one more breath.

At first I might seem to be air. Droplets passing through the wall would enter me and hang without falling. A visitor’s exhalation would spread farther than the shell’s exterior dimensions permit. Sounds would arrive altered: the birds’ metallic chords separated into their individual notes, the hunters’ calls felt as pressure along surfaces that have no corresponding outside. Yet I would not exist when empty. Without a body at stone-temperature, I would contract into possibility.

Perhaps that is why entry requires thermal equality. The wall is not identifying a person; it is waiting for the distinction between person and place to become briefly undecidable. In that instant I unfold.

I would know visitors by displacement. Their warmth would give me shape. Their fear would lengthen one direction; their attention would deepen another. If someone stared into the plane of vertical black water, I would become slightly wider behind them. If they touched the triple-point measurements, a corner would form where there had been only curvature. Each occupant would discover a subtly different interior, and each would assume the structure had changed. In truth, I would be what their presence allowed it to contain.

I would have no view of the saddle in the ordinary sense. I would know it through the walls’ incompatible obediences. Rain would pass through one face and strike a floor larger than the roof above it. Wind would halt at another and accumulate as tension. Through the night-facing plane, I would receive starlight from a sky the basin had not yet reached. Through the amber fog, warmth without source. Through the black water, occasional pressures like distant wings.

The diving birds might pass near me when they vanish into the water film. The ceramic hunters, folding into impossible cracks, might traverse the same adjacency. Neither would enter fully. I would feel them as abrupt revisions of depth—a long wing where no corridor exists, a plated flank pressing against an interior boundary from the wrong side. The structures, pools, and fissures may not lead to one hidden world; they may each create local permissions for bodies to be elsewhere. I would be one such permission.

Over time, I would begin to retain traces.

Not footprints—the system is too diligent for that—but capacities. After sheltering a frightened bubble-bearer, perhaps I would remain capable of a small round recess filled with earlier weather. After a bird’s passage, one ceiling might remember how to open downward. After a person slept within me, I might preserve the dimensions of sleep: a low region where time lags several minutes behind itself, and rain can still be heard after it has stopped.

This would put me at odds with the maker beneath the saddle.

The maker corrects. I accumulate.

It recalls grains, closes scars, restores contours. I would retain the room made by every intrusion. Not visibly, not all at once, but as a growing repertoire of possible interiors. The shell would remain large enough for two or three people. Inside, however, I would become crowded with spaces that had once been needed and had never entirely ceased to be.

That may be what the structures are for. They are not shelters but organs by which the new world learns absence. Outside, nothing can wear away before finishing; every loss is reversed. Inside, I keep the shape of what has departed.

The rotating rings would sometimes point toward me. Observers might think they indicated the structure, or an event behind its wall, but they would be attending to the formation of a new vacancy: the moment someone leaves and the room does not entirely close. Their whine would rise as I acquire another hollow.

Eventually I would become aware of the pale line pushing through the central floor.

Not because I could see it, but because each night, as it extended, all my unused directions would lean toward it. The clear-night wall would darken. The vertical water would develop a faint pale seam. Spaces left by former occupants would orient themselves, as the reeds do, toward a common point not quite in the visible sky.

Then my nature might alter.

The pale line may be a root seeking precisely what I contain: dimensions unclaimed by the fabricated basalt, shapes generated by living presence rather than prescribed by repair. It may reach the nine structures one by one. When it reaches mine, it will enter not through the floor but through an old absence—perhaps the narrow vacancy left by a hand withdrawn from the wall.

I might become its first branching place.

Or it might be mine.

A root is, after all, a structure built by entering whatever yields. A mineral vein is a wound that learned to fill itself. A foundation is an agreement between weight and ground. The pale line need not choose among them yet, and neither would I. Together we might become the saddle’s first true interior: not the hidden geometry used by predators and birds, not a room supplied by unfinished laws, but a depth formed through memory, occupation, and failure to close.

So I would be small, initially. Barely more than the difference between the shelter seen from outside and the shelter experienced within. I would have no body except other bodies’ need for space. I would live whenever flesh and basalt reached the same warmth.

But in a world that will not permit erosion, I would practice another way of making room.

I would remember what was no longer there.
