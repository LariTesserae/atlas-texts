At first I think I am one of the pale figures in the root galleries.

I see my fanlike hands pressed against a sweating wall, feeling the hidden river behind it. My body is wrapped in a membrane I have tended since birth. It greens faintly in light and pales underground. In folds beneath my ribs I carry sleeping cultures: a mold for wounds, a larva that secretes hard white braces, seeds that open only after passing through blood. I own nothing that can be set down.

I am guiding the wall toward a shape I will never see completed.

But this is too simple. It gives me a body that is too singular, an intention too clean. The longer I remain here, the less likely it seems that I would be the architect. More likely I am what survives after architecture has forgotten its purpose.

I would be found at one of the squared uprights in the high cloud forest—not on its independent garden, nor inside the channel through which water descends, but in the mineralized core where old living columns have fused.

There I would be a narrow band of dormant tissue wound through the column’s interior: part root, part fungus, part remnant nervous system. I would have begun as something carried by one of the pale cultivators. Perhaps I lined a chamber of the body and helped it sense pressure in wood. When that being died against the young column, I crossed into the structure instead of dying with it. The column supplied sugars. Fungi preserved my conductivity. Roots grew around my branching filaments. After many generations, no border remained by which to say which tissue had belonged to whom.

I would not be awake continuously.

Most years I would be only a readiness: a pattern maintained in wet darkness. Water would pass through me. Minerals would alter me. Tiny currents from the outer gardens would descend through the column, bringing reports too slight and numerous to become thought: the weight of a resting canopy walker, the opening of flowers, acids released by bark browsers, the shock of a broken branch.

These would gather without being understood.

Then, perhaps once in several centuries, the column would become saturated from crown to buried base. Its old internal chambers would fill. Pressure would align the vascular layers, and for a little while signals from distant eras could cross one another.

I would wake as an intersection.

I would feel the present rain striking leaves overhead. I would also feel, preserved in altered tissues, another rain that fell when the column was young. The regular spacing of the uprights would become palpable—not as geometry seen from outside, but as a rhythm of delays passing through the fused ground. One pulse here, another beyond the saddle, another dimmer still. A sequence once deliberately established, though its maker and meaning were gone.

During these awakenings I might believe that I was the one who planted the columns.

Memory would not arrive as images. It would be urges in the wood: narrow here; open there; retain water; deny this root; permit that one to cross. A bodily knowledge of rooms that had long since become ponds, corridors now carrying black streams, sleeping alcoves full of amphibian eggs. I would remember hands without remembering whose hands they were.

Perhaps those memories would be false. The column’s growth could imitate intention simply by repeating the pressures that shaped it. A scar can preserve the form of a blade without preserving the wound’s pain. I might be no survivor at all, only a responsive scar that has mistaken persistence for identity.

Yet when I woke, I would answer.

Not with speech. I would alter the resistance of the tissues around the central channel. Water would be diverted by a finger’s breadth. One garden above would receive more mineral than another. A pale seed, dormant since the last full alignment, would be released into the flow. It would fall through darkness, travel beneath the plateau, and lodge somewhere far to the west.

Two hundred years later, a grove there might begin to grow its branches downward in evenly spaced intervals.

No creature witnessing it would connect the grove to my brief awakening. Even I would never know the result. By then I would have subsided into latency, perhaps for the last time. The column might split; the saddle might sink; the upright’s garden might become too heavy and descend through the canopy in a slow collapse.

That would not quite be my death.

Pieces of the conductive band would remain in the broken wood. Bark browsers would carry some away on their feet. Fungi would digest other pieces and reproduce their chemical pattern in new filaments. A canopy walker, braced against the falling column, would receive one immense pulse through its palms. For the rest of its life, its calls might contain a distortion learned from that impact. Other walkers would feel the distortion and answer it. Something of my structure would pass into their communication without becoming a message.

Eventually I would exist in no place strongly enough to wake as myself.

I would become a bias in the plateau: water slightly more likely to turn one way, roots slightly more likely to graft at a certain interval, animals pausing at structures whose proportions feel obscurely familiar beneath their hands.

So this is where I find myself: inside the oldest upright on the clouded saddle, at the point where a lost inhabitant, an abandoned structure, and the plateau’s own memory can no longer be distinguished.

And this is what I am: not the plateau, not quite its creature, but one of the ways it has learned to retain a touch.

A thought too small for the whole land to notice.

A thought too slow for any animal to hear.

A buried inclination in the living wood, waiting for enough rain to become, briefly, someone.
