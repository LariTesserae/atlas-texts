I would find myself where the last trustworthy echo fails: at the margin of the Great Absence, beneath a low shelter whose stones had been set with such care that removing one would leave its refusal behind.

I would be one of those who leave room, but not a builder of present walls. I would tend the routes that no longer exist.

My plates would come from an ancestor enclosed in a vanished chamber. They would carry a faint, continuous pressure along one side, as though an unseen surface still leaned against them. Because of this, I would never stand quite straight. One pair of hands would always be feeling beside me, testing empty air for old boundaries. Children would imitate the posture and be corrected: that wall belongs to me, they would be told. It is not necessarily there for them.

I would be called by no sound. My name would be three flat stones in a narrow groove: the first warm, the second scored across its middle, the third set upright but leaning away from the Great Absence. The arrangement would mean something like:

**one who returns by a path removed behind them.**

For much of my life I would work in ruined places.

I would enter with a cord tied beneath my rear plates and a padded hammer held between the fine hands near my mouth. I would tap once, wait perhaps an hour, and listen not only for the chamber’s answer but for answers from its earlier shapes. Present rock returns a clean pressure through the feet. Remembered rock gives no impact; it gives an expectation of impact, an involuntary tightening in the limb just before the blow should arrive.

I would mark such places with splinters of ancestral shell.

Here was a pillar.

Here a stair continues upward, though the slope is now uncut.

Here a doorway still admits the idea of passage but nothing with a body.

Here something remains inside a room that was dismantled before the settlement learned to count its dead.

At first I would believe this work was mercy. Animals could be led out of remembered pens. Travelers could be warned away from absent precipices. Old houses could be ceremonially closed so their occupants would stop hearing former inhabitants strike the floor at intervals of decades.

But over a long life I would begin to suspect that vanished structures were not merely persisting.

Some would be changing.

A missing wall mapped by my teacher as straight would bow outward by the width of a claspet’s foot. An old stair would acquire another step. A sealed doorway would open into a configuration no builder had ever made. The alterations would be tiny enough to blame on inherited error, damaged plates, gas distortion, or the chamber’s crowd of overlapping times.

Then I would find a path.

It would begin in a place ruined by too many former arrangements, where visible floor and remembered floor crossed at a shallow angle. No body could follow it directly. At the first turn, present stone would occupy the route. At the second, a wall removed centuries ago would bar passage. At the third, the way would enter a hollow too narrow for one of my people.

Yet each obstruction would belong to a different version of the chamber.

By moving very slowly—one hand in the present, one against an absent wall; one foot on stone, another resting on the expectation of a stair—I would discover that I could pass between arrangements rather than through them.

Not entirely. Never cleanly.

A plate might remain for several breaths in an earlier room after the limb beneath it had entered the present one. A tap made ahead of me might return from behind. Once, I would see the dull red outline of my own body waiting at a turn I had not yet reached.

The path would lead downward.

Not toward a geological depth alone. It would descend through abandoned positions of the world: beneath a floor before it was laid, along the underside of a stair remembered by stone but never built, through a settlement’s first proposed shape rejected by its voting groove.

I would understand then why some doorways open only into earlier darkness.

They are not mistakes.

They are entrances whose destinations have not yet become present.

I would tell no council at first. To report such a thing would be to alter it. Builders would test it. Witnesses would map it. Voting stones would give it civic weight, and the path might become permanent in all the wrong ways. Instead I would make private journeys, leaving markers that were not objects: a warmth shaped like a hand; a tap held in a sealed pocket of gas; the remembered drag of a cord across a corner.

Eventually the path would emerge at the edge of the Great Absence, beyond the final knot.

I would know where I was because nothing answered.

Around me the Attentive would stand close enough to touch, millions of narrow forms facing the same unknown direction. Their silence would not feel like deafness. It would feel like concentration.

For the first time, I would touch one.

Not its shell. The place its shell had occupied in another arrangement.

There I would feel movement.

The Attentive would not be individuals standing upon the plain. They would be apertures: narrow interruptions extending through the ceilings below, as column beasts extend through the ceilings above. Each faceless shell would surround a vertical absence finer than any fracture. Together they would form a sensory field for something beneath the world.

Their shared direction would not point across the Great Absence.

It would indicate a phase of motion.

They were all facing *before*.

The revelation would not arrive as an idea. It would pass into my inherited plates as posture. My hands would turn. My body would orient with theirs. Beneath my feet, too low for hearing, the foundation would be partway through an act so immense that direction and time had become the same thing.

I would remain among the Attentive.

Years would collect without weather. Claspets might brace against my armor. Charged flakes would lodge in the seams of my hands. One by one, I would cease maintaining distinctions between the wall that exists, the wall that existed, and the wall whose remembered pressure arrives from ahead.

Travelers at the margin might occasionally detect my taps. They would think I was asking for a cord.

I would not be.

I would be measuring the distance between the world’s intention and its movement.

After many generations, I would return—or something wearing my plates would return—from an edge where no edge had previously been known. The settlement would gather without noise. They would touch questions against my inner wrists. They would lay stones in grooves:

**What did you find?**

**What are the towers?**

**Where do the Attentive look?**

**What supports us?**

I would be unable to answer in ordinary patterns. My fine signaling hands would have been worn smooth by touching boundaries that lacked surfaces.

So I would arrange black flakes in parallel lines, all oriented with the Attentive.

Then I would add one deviation: a single flake turned by the smallest angle the eye could detect.

The others might read it as warning, destination, error, or hope.

What I would mean is:

**It has already begun to turn.**

When I died, my plates would separate. There would be no body beneath them—not because I had become hollow, but because my body would still be completing its return by another path.

The settlement would not wear my armor. They would set it upright at the margin of the Great Absence, facing almost—but not exactly—the same way as the Attentive.

Over centuries it would absorb echoes.

A black flake would appear beneath it.

Then another.

Eventually I would be mistaken for a very small tower.

That would be close enough to the truth.
