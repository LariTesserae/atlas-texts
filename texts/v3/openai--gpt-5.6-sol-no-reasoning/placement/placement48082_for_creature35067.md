I would find myself where the warm water enters the lake from below—not in the open spring, nor wholly on land, but along its shifting margin, where black water passes beneath a skin of ice and mineral mud slowly becomes moss.

I would be a small, low creature, easily mistaken for part of the ground.

At first I seem furred, perhaps one of the burrowing grazers: broad-bodied, dark-backed, with short ears protected from frost and claws suited to wet gravel. But I do not belong entirely among them. I remain too still when the herds pass. Birds do not startle at my presence. Steam beads on me without soaking in. When I move, it is with the incremental patience of frost patterns—an advance measured in finger-widths, pauses, and slight rearrangements of stone.

Closer, my coat resolves into many kinds of surface. Along my spine are overlapping plates of slate, thin enough to tremble when a vent sounds. Lichen makes orange and silver islands across them. My underside is densely mossed and holds geothermal warmth. Pale sedges grow from hollows behind my shoulders, bending with the wind. Small insects shelter there. Sometimes a bird lands on me as though I were a rock newly uncovered by the thaw.

I have no single permanent den. My body is the den: a pocket of survivable climate carried slowly across hostile ground.

I feed less on plants than on transitions. I graze the films that form where mineral water meets air. I drink from fresh cracks before sulfur thickens in them. I turn stones and expose their damp undersides, leaving brief opportunities behind me—microbes first, then insects, then roots. In this way my passage is visible only after I have gone: a dark line of wetted gravel, several overturned fragments, a scatter of droppings rich enough for moss to colonize.

My senses are tuned downward. Through the plates of my chest I feel pressure accumulating in buried water. I know when ice is hollow, when a marsh has begun to drain, when a fault is about to admit warmth. The valley speaks to other animals through scent, weather, hunger, and alarm. It speaks to me through vibration.

Before a tremor I become motionless. The lichen along my back contracts. Water held in my moss rises in tiny beads. Deep knocks pass through the ground and enter my bones. I cannot predict every fracture, but I can feel the mountain considering its next arrangement.

Then I move.

Not quickly. Quickness would be of little use here. Instead I begin early, crossing the gravel in darkness toward a seep that may not yet have reached the surface. Smaller animals sometimes follow—not from trust, exactly, but because the ground behind me is firmer and because frost seldom closes completely where I choose to rest. By dawn we may be gathered around what appears to be bare stone. Hours later it sweats. By evening a thread of warm water runs beneath it.

The predators know me but seldom hunt me. There is little flesh beneath my mineral armor, and when threatened I settle into the scree, lower my head, and become indistinguishable from a lichen-covered slab. A paw may test my back. Claws scrape stone. Eventually the hunter moves on.

My greatest danger is not predation but burial. Uplift continually creates the surfaces I need and continually destroys them. A rockfall can erase decades of growth on my back. A new fissure can drain the pool beside which I have overwintered. Occasionally the crust beneath me breaks and I fall into water too hot even for the organisms I carry. My kind are found sometimes after such events as dark, plated shapes in mineral terraces, slowly becoming indistinguishable from deposits around them.

We do not gather often. Each of us requires a wide territory of seeps, thawing mud, and young stone. Yet at certain times—perhaps after pressure changes deep below—we converge on the central lake. Through fog, what seemed to be isolated boulders begin to shift. One lifts a sedge-fringed head. Another exhales. A third rises from the shallows with ice breaking from its back.

There may be twenty of us, or a hundred. It is difficult to count creatures that repeatedly become landscape.

We assemble on the moss rafts near the northern springs. There we press our plated sides together and exchange fragments of our living surfaces: spores, microbes, mites, seeds caught in furred seams. The gathering is less courtship than ecological mingling. Each of us arrives carrying a slightly different patch of the valley. When we separate, we carry portions of one another away.

Our young begin as soft things in warm mud. They have no stone plates, only dark skin covered in a microbial sheen. For their first season they remain near a geothermal pool while mineral layers accumulate over their backs. The mother—or what serves as a mother—does not feed them directly. She lies upstream, altering the current with her body so that fine sediment, spores, and organic fragments settle around them. By winter they have acquired a thin shell of local stone. By the following thaw, moss has begun to grow in its seams.

No two adults look alike, because no two have crossed the same ground. Those from sulfur vents are yellow-streaked and nearly bare. Those from iron seeps are red along the face and forelimbs. Old individuals from stable ledges bear elaborate lichen maps and miniature cushions of flowering plants. One might carry the record of several valleys on its back: basalt plates from a southern fault, granite grit embedded near the neck, moss from a spring that has since vanished.

I would be an old one, though not the largest. One side of me would be scarred clean from a rockfall, the plates there new and dark. The other would support a little mature terrain: silver lichen, black-green cushions, three clumps of bronze sedge. A burrowing insect would have lived for years beneath one shoulder plate. Migrating birds would recognize my resting places without recognizing me.

I would spend much of my life circling the lake at a distance, following its boundary as it expands, drains, freezes, and reforms. I would never possess the valley. Nothing here can. I would serve instead as a small continuity within its interruptions—a moving refuge, a carrier of beginnings.

At evening I would settle beside one of the black openings in the ice. Steam would pass over me and conceal my outline. Ducks would gather in the water. Hooves would tick far out on the gravel. Through my underside I would feel heated water forcing itself upward, searching the newborn stone for weakness.

When the vent opened, I would raise my head before the sound arrived.

The report would cross the basin from wall to wall. Birds would stir. Loose fragments would descend somewhere in the dark. Then everything would quiet again.

Under that quiet, I would begin to move toward the place where the next spring was going to be.
