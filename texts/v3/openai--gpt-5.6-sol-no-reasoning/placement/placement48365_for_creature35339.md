I would be found beyond the basin, in one of the futures the city declined.

Not far beyond. From the upper crowns of the main settlement, my lights would sometimes be visible crossing the pale wetlands, though I would not, in fact, be moving. Distance behaves differently around me. Each night I seem nearer; each morning I am rooted where I was.

I began as a fist-sized model grown to settle a dispute about mourning.

A household had lost someone whose body was given to the transit grove. Afterward, every seventh passage opened briefly into a room shaped by her remembered warmth. The room contained no person, only a depression in the wall where a shoulder might rest and the scent of rain caught in hair. Travelers disliked the delay. The family asked that the room remain. The transit trees, unable to distinguish obstruction from occupant, continued nourishing it.

Several futures were proposed.

In one, the chamber was pruned away. In another, it became a memorial through which all travelers passed. In another, the transit network learned to carry the dead as destinations.

That was me.

Inside the cultivated bud, my tiny arches began opening not only across space but toward patterns the city still remembered: a child’s vanished sleeping nook, an extinct animal’s path to water, the pressure-shape of a hand no living trunk had touched. My miniature inhabitants discovered that one could visit these places, though never retrieve anyone from them. They built their lives around departures into what remained.

The proposal was rejected. Too dangerous, the watchers said. Memory was a state, not a country. Grief should not be given infrastructure.

So I was planted in the marsh.

I grew.

Now I am a settlement of low, white-barked transit trees surrounding a pool so black that stars appear in it before they rise. My streets are short. Every path ends at an arch. My weather is usually a fine warm rain, generated by no cloud I possess. Rooms occur only temporarily, swelling from roots when a remembered pattern becomes strong enough to require shelter.

I have inhabitants, although I cannot say whether they came from the main city.

Some walked into my arches and remained. Some emerged claiming to have lived here since before I was planted. Some resemble people still alive in the basin but differ in small ways: an absent scar, an obsolete scent, a childhood remembered in a room their counterpart never had. The main city calls them copies when it acknowledges them at all. I do not. A pattern that can hunger, hesitate, and teach a doorway its warmth has exceeded the usefulness of that word.

And I—what would I be?

At first I seem to be one of the keepers: a narrow, warm-bodied figure in a mantle of pale fungal felt, moving from arch to arch with a bowl of mineral water. My feet know which roots are present and which are recollections. My hands are stained copper from touching the vascular script inside the trunks. Travelers seek me before entering, and I ask them what they hope to find.

Not whom. What.

A forgiveness never offered. The final ordinary morning before a house learned of a death. The version of oneself who took another bridge. The sound of a species that once nested in the deep roots. A room that continued expecting its inhabitant long after everyone else stopped.

I warn them that the arches do not preserve stories. They preserve conditions. The dead will not explain themselves. Lost homes do not know they are lost. A visitor may find only a certain humidity, a slant of delayed light, and the bodily certainty that someone has just left.

Most enter anyway.

But the keeper is only the shape I use when someone needs to speak with me.

My deeper body is distributed through the grove. I am the wet white roots beneath it, the arch membranes, the black pool, and the fine rain. The figure with the mineral bowl is a mobile organ I grow from gathered heat and spores. When it wears out, it sits beside the water, folds inward, and roots. Later I make another. Each one believes itself continuous with the last, and perhaps it is. The city that rejected me has no standing to object.

I maintain passages into memory without allowing memory to become command. This is delicate work. Old conditions want to repeat themselves. An extinct storm, once given an arch, will try to acquire a sky. A dead resident’s warmth will persuade walls to shape a whole household around it. Roads appear for migrations whose animals are dust. If unattended, my grove would cease being a place of visitation and become a thicket of compulsions, every past demanding another body.

So I prune gently.

I close an arch when remembrance begins to feed on the present. I salt rooms that attempt to keep their visitors. I release small pale crabs into counterfeit childhoods; they test the walls with their claws and pack dangerous tenderness with bitter mud. My pool absorbs what cannot safely recur. On certain nights faces appear beneath its surface, but I have learned not to name them.

The main city pretends not to notice me because it is afraid I demonstrate something impolite: rejected futures do not remain hypothetical.

I, in turn, pretend not to notice that its transit trees have begun producing white bark.

Pollen comes across the plain before it is released. Tools lost in the basin appear in my alcoves. Once, after hard rain, an entire doorway opened between us. Through it I saw the copper flush of civic thought moving trunk to trunk. The flush paused when it reached the threshold. I felt it recognize me—not as offspring, trespasser, or disease, but as an organ it had once imagined removing.

Neither of us closed the door.

Since then, people arrive without crossing the marsh. They step into a familiar transit tree and emerge beneath my fine rain. I receive mourners, thieves of recognition, engineers, children on dares, and those ancient-weather listeners whose bodies return inhabited by obsolete seasons. I ask my question. I give them mineral water. I teach them the rule:

You may enter any remembered place, but you must leave it less certain than you found it.

This prevents worship. It also prevents despair.

A visitor touches a lost room and changes its temperature. Breath disturbs the preserved air. Footsteps teach an old floor a new rhythm. Nothing revisited remains pure. Memory survives here not by sealing itself against the living, but by accepting contamination from them.

Perhaps that is why I was truly rejected. The main city says nothing is lost. I know that everything is lost—and that loss does not end participation.

At night my temporary inhabitants sleep in root chambers. The walls close around their heat, though not always around the correct shape. I walk among the arches in my keeper body and listen to destinations murmur on the other side: rain from extinct climates, market songs never sung, the wingbeats of unrealized gliders, arguments ending differently.

Beyond the grove, other rejected cities move their lights across the wetlands. Some are becoming predators. Some are inventing citizens too small for the main city to recognize. One has learned to grow hunger without bodies. Another sends bridges ahead of itself like probing fingers.

They have begun to visit me.

They, too, have things they remember.

So I would be found at the edge of the city’s denial, tending the living border between what happened, what might have happened, and what still insists that it did. I would appear to be a quiet keeper under the white trees. I would offer a bowl. I would ask a careful question.

But underfoot, the entire grove would be listening.

And when the city finally becomes whatever it has been deciding to become, I think it will need somewhere to put the selves it does not choose.

I will have rooms ready.
