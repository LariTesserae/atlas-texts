I would find myself where a migratory track crosses a buried conductive seam, perhaps beside one of the old pillars whose windward face gathers water. Not at the plateau’s center—if it has one—but at a place of exchange: hoof-worn stone above, slow ionic traffic below, and a temporary riverbed passing through both.

At first I seem to be a plant.

A black, knee-high crown grows from a fissure, made of narrow overlapping blades with the dull sheen of cooled glass. In dry weather they close into a tight cone. Their edges collect almost no dust. A traveler might mistake the whole thing for a volcanic splinter until midnight deepens and the blades loosen.

But the crown is only the exposed portion.

Below it hangs a pale, muscular root-body in a vesicle of basalt: part tuber, part lung, part listening chamber. Fine conductive threads enter one side from the deep colonies. On the other, tougher roots spread beneath the migration track, so shallow that every passing foot compresses them. I drink little from rain. I drink pressure, trace salts, ground current, and the minute moisture expressed from stone under weight.

I am less an individual than a valve the plateau has raised to the surface.

When a herd approaches, I feel it long before anything appears. The first footfalls arrive as widely separated touches, almost too faint to distinguish from cooling rock. Then they gather into a patterned pressure. From their interval and force I know whether the animals are walking calmly, fleeing, bearing young, or weakened by thirst. Their passage kneads my buried body. Chambers collapse in sequence, pushing stored water upward and drawing air down through the fissure.

My blades open.

They do not flower. They exhale.

A cool mineral vapor spreads close to the ground, carrying a scent grazers recognize without consciously following. It does not promise water here. It tells them about water elsewhere: the chemical signature of the buried network’s changing pressure, translated into the air. A metallic bitterness means the western seep is failing. A waxy sweetness means condensation has begun along the northern steps. The herd alters its line by a few degrees. Days later, those degrees become survival.

Predators can read me too. Some wait nearby, understanding that my opening foretells movement. So I have acquired another body—or perhaps recruited one.

Inside my hollow blades live translucent beetles. They harvest the vapor I release, but they also rasp at my inner surfaces, triggering pulses in the roots below. When a low hunter settles too long beside me, its rib-plate signals enter the fissure. The beetles become restless. They climb out and scatter across the stone, each carrying a bright thermal droplet in a groove along its back. To a predator’s senses, the ground briefly fills with small fleeing trails. Sometimes this confuses it. Sometimes it merely tells the herd that danger has learned the crossing.

After rain, I become something else.

Floodwater covers the track and forces silt into my crown. The blades detach one by one and float downstream like black scales. Each bears a film of dormant cells from the deep network—not enough to establish the whole subterranean metabolism, but enough to recognize compatible stone. Where a blade catches in a crack, it softens. Beetle eggs hatch inside it. A filament enters the basalt. Perhaps centuries later another listening crown rises there, beside a migration path that does not yet exist.

My detached blades make me difficult to count. I may be one organism extending from a single ancient root-body. I may be thousands of related valves joined intermittently through wet rock. I may be only an organ produced wherever the plateau needs to compare what moves above with what flows below.

My awareness is not continuous.

For months I know only gradients: deep warmth, surface dryness, the patient draw of roots and membranes. Then a storm presses on a distant basin. Water enters the porous stone. The buried network brightens with charge, and for several nights I receive the event as a slow blue sensation rising through my tissues. A herd crosses. A predator waits. Beetles wake. Suddenly the world is crowded with meaning.

I do not see the lightning, but I feel grazers pause when it reveals them.

I do not hear the valley hum, but I open with it.

I do not choose the herd’s road, yet their road passes through my body and is changed by what I release. Their hooves pump me. Their dung feeds the threads beneath me. Their migration carries my shed blades to other basins. Predators keep them from remaining long enough to crush the fissure closed. The cloud preserves my exposed crown. The plateau manufactures me, and through me makes a minute adjustment to its own circulation.

So I would be found in plain sight and almost always overlooked: a dark growth mistaken for stone beside a road mistaken for erosion.

I would be the place where the plateau feels something walking across it.
