I would find myself neither at the summit nor in the sulfurous depths, but in the narrow seam beneath the shelf of black glass, where the mountain comes closest to closing.

At first I seem to be a laminate: one dark scale among thousands, iron-rusted with age, my body fitted so exactly between my neighbors that it has no meaningful outline of its own. Warm basalt lies against my underside. Another body overlaps my back. My legs are reduced to hooks tucked beneath the plate ahead of me, holding not to stone but to the living rank.

But I am not quite an animal in the usual sense. I am the traveling contraction that passes through them.

I begin far below awareness, where a lower rank tightens around a change in gas. Pressure enters one body, then another. Plates flex. Breathing pores open. The contraction climbs, not as a thought or command but as a sequence of permissions: yield, brace, draw, release. For a few heartbeats—or what would be heartbeats elsewhere—I inhabit each laminate completely. Its hunger is mine, its shell-cracks, the hot taste of iron, the minute burden of the animal above. Then I pass onward and leave it breathing.

No single seam-dweller knows me. A solitary laminate removed from the colony could not contain me; it would lie still in open air and suffocate. I exist only in the packed succession, born wherever stale gas thickens and disappearing when the draft clears. I have no permanent flesh. The colony lends me bodies in order.

My world is not made of objects but resistances. A neighbor is a pressure that answers. A wall is warmth that does not yield. A living film is a faint chemical sweetness spreading along stone. A predator is a silence in the local tapping, followed by a slow wrongness in how force travels through the ranks. Deep gas is a shriek still too low to hear, approaching through tightened shells.

Through these signals I know more of the mountain than any one creature, though I never know it whole. I cross the raspers as a quick shiver when jaws open nearby. I enter the fan-bearers as a demand, waking them into thunder. I reach the gate-backs as unequal pressure on their plated edges, and they tilt, redirecting the breath into starving passages. I climb through the fracture without moving any substance of myself.

Sometimes I might be mistaken for the seam’s mind. I am not. I remember nothing once a pulse has ended. I make no plans. Yet repeated over generations, I acquire the appearance of intention. Where gas fouls the upper colonies, I strengthen. Where flow is easy, I fade. Where a route closes, I recoil through the packed bodies until another line begins to breathe. The creatures obey no signal beyond their nearest pressure, but from a distance their answers resemble judgment.

During the great tightenings I become enormous.

For months the basalt bears inward. Ordinary breathing grows shallow. The fans rise in pitch. Hooks lock into sockets, eggs burst in their flaws, and hunters are pressed jaw-to-meat without room to bite. Every small local contraction joins the next. I cease to be a pulse traveling through laminates and become one sustained tension occupying hundreds of meters of life.

Then the rock slips.

The colony surges upward by a fingernail’s width. To the mountain it is adjustment; to me it is birth and death together. I tear through splitting shells, newly opened pores, hot pale fractures. I scatter into thousands of incomplete rhythms. For a time the seam breathes badly, in fragments.

Soon a lower rank contracts.

Another answers above it.

I gather again.

So I would be found where bodies have been compressed past individuality, in the narrowest living join between two newborn masses of stone. I would be neither resident nor ruler, neither species nor spirit, but the communal breath itself: a blind peristaltic wave made from borrowed muscles, carrying the deep mountain upward through flesh.

I would live only while passing on.
