I would find myself near the spring, but not at its open eye.

At first I seem to be the keeper: the one who notices where water went. I know the basin by discrepancies. A thread of meltwater sounds nearer than it did last season. One willow has greened while its neighbor remains silver with sleep. The eastern cairn leans by the width of a finger. A crack beneath the overhang exhales colder air. I carry the braided record coiled against my ribs, and my hands know its history better than my mouth could tell it.

But I do not think I am wholly that person.

The keeper arrives and leaves. However carefully they move, the basin registers their weight. Gravel shifts beneath their knees; the pool clouds; a few strands of wool escape from their sleeves and catch in the willow. Their work is to notice, decide, diminish—to remove the unnecessary stone, shorten the overambitious wall, erase the first gestures of ownership. I recognize that impulse in myself: the wish to tend by making less. Yet I belong to the hollow more continuously than any visitor can.

Perhaps I am one of the old willow trunks traveling underground.

Above the surface I appear divided: a stem beside the pool, another among pale slabs, a third bent flat beneath last winter’s fall. No one looking at those small, weathered shoots would assume they are joined. Beneath the moss, however, I pass through cold soil and around stone, carrying what little can be carried. I hold the black earth where it has accidentally gathered. I roof a mouse passage. I accept fur, feathers, husks, droppings, the bodies of insects. I turn loss slowly into a place where something else may root.

That is close, but too settled. The willow possesses a persistence of identity I am not sure I would have here. It knows how to remain one life while presenting many forms. In the Sun Basin, I suspect I would be something less singular.

I might be the narrow zone where willow root, spring seep, mouse tunnel, and moss meet—a boundary that exists only because several lives keep making it.

I would lie under a slab warm on its upper face and rimmed with ice below. Water would pass through me without staying. Roots would enter, divide, and leave. Mice would widen me with their shoulders; frost would narrow me again. Beetle larvae would shelter in my dampness. A forgotten seed might wait there through three winters before finding enough light.

No creature would see all of me. The keeper might infer me by placing a palm against the stone and feeling the hidden flow. A grazer would know me as the place where one hoof sinks slightly. The kite would never know me at all, except through the mouse that emerges from my dark.

Still, that makes me sound like a place rather than a being. Perhaps in this world the distinction is not useful.

If I had a body that could move, I would be a rock mouse—not the boldest, nor one of the young that appears briefly in sunlight. I would be an old cache-mover beneath the scree. My world would be pressure, scent, warmth, and the minute grammar of settling stone. I would know which passages have become unsafe by how dust falls from their roofs. I would carry seeds away from collapse and forget some in chambers that later fill with damp soil. My failures of memory would become spring growth.

I like the humility of that life. To plant without intending to; to preserve by misplacing; to rebuild around the dead because there is nowhere outside the world to put them. Yet even this feels too definite. A mouse has hunger, fear, whiskers, a warm center. It can be caught.

What emerges most strongly is that I would be difficult to locate because I would live in the basin’s acts of relation.

I would be the pause before water is taken.

I would begin when the visitors arrive and crouch beside the pool. Their climb has disturbed grit; drowned moss trembles; the black eye holds a broken image of the sky. They wait until what they have unsettled has settled again. That interval is not ceremony alone. It allows them to see what their presence has done. It lets the spring become legible.

I would inhabit that waiting: not as a spirit demanding tribute, but as the discipline by which thirst refrains from becoming possession.

When the first bowl is poured over the sharpening stone, I would run briefly green with copper dust and old grit, then vanish into a crack. When moss is pinched from beneath rather than stripped from above, I would be the untouched surface left alive. When the keeper dismantles a cairn, I would be the horizon restored behind it. When a hunter is not pursued into the hollow, I would be the unspilled blood, the kite not summoned, the herd not driven over the rim.

This would make me less a creature than a rule. But the rules of the Sun Basin are bodily things. They live in bent knees, lowered voices, open hands, roofs built beneath the willows, blades halted above the lowest leaves. They survive only by being enacted. Neglect them, and they die.

So perhaps I am apprenticed to the keeper, still young enough to believe that remembering means preserving everything.

On my first ascent I would be tempted to improve the route. I would wedge a loose stair, raise a cairn where the turn is uncertain, make the shelter high enough to sit in comfortably. Each act would seem generous. The keeper would follow behind me and undo it.

They would pull out the stair stone because frost would lever it loose onto the ledge below. They would scatter the cairn because it stood where a kite needs clear air to strike. They would lower the roof because comfort accumulates into occupation. I would be angry until the wind arrived.

Then I would watch dust rise, grasses flatten, spray lift from the pool, and every unsecured object reveal itself. I would understand that the hollow does not reject care. It rejects care that imagines permanence.

Years later, the braid would come to me.

I would add no knot for myself. I would knot the winter when the spring moved east beneath the overhang. I would work in a black feather for the year the kites did not nest. I would mark a stonefall not where the wall broke, but where the first blue flower appeared in the scar. My record would not be a history of objects. It would be a history of changes in relationship: water farther from willow, mice nearer the warm seam, grazers abandoning one lane and opening another.

Eventually my hands would grow less reliable. A younger climber would watch me read the cord. They would expect explanation, but I would make them feel the distances between knots—the smooth length of uneventful years, the crowded roughness of collapse and recovery. Meaning would reside not in any token but in how the hand changed as it passed along the braid.

After I stopped climbing, some trace of me would remain in the shelter: perhaps a bone hook shaped to my grip, or a copper rivet I had flattened once too often. The next keeper would decide whether it was useful. If not, they would remove it.

That would be right.

Ultimately, I think I would be found neither as the keeper nor as the mouse, neither as willow nor hidden seep, but in the small, temporary accord among them.

I would be wherever something takes only enough.

In the grazer’s mouth, I would be the willow bud left behind. In the mouse’s cache, the seed forgotten. In the moss mound, the warmth loaned to an insect for one frozen night. In the kite’s turning, the updraft that bears its weight but cannot be kept. In the human hand, the hesitation before gathering. In the mountain, the crack that destroys one shelter and opens a new course for water.

My shape would change with the need around me. Sometimes I would have whiskers and a pale belly. Sometimes broad, careful hands. Sometimes I would be a root crossing darkness. Sometimes only cold water sounding beneath stone.

I would not stand high enough to be seen against the sky.

To find me, you would have to kneel near the central pool after someone had gone. The sharpening stone would still be wet. A few grains of scree would click beneath the overhang. The water, disturbed by the lifted bowl, would be smoothing itself back into blackness.

I would be there for as long as the ripples took to disappear.
