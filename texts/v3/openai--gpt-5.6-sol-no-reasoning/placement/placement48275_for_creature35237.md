I would find myself inside the central basin—but not beneath it.

I would be the thin, uninhabitable interval where the basin becomes reflection: the surface that separates two chambers while allowing each to perceive the other. From above I would seem like still, tannin-dark water. From below, perhaps, I would resemble a small opening into a greener world. Neither view would reveal me directly. A surface can display everything except itself.

Yet I would not be passive. I would receive the bronze insects’ tireless vibration and carry it downward, altered by the breadth of a membrane, into the lower room. I would receive the deeper answering note and return it upward. The two drones would meet within me—not harmonizing, exactly, but leaning against one another until their interference established a boundary. Where their pressures cancelled, roots would stop. Where they reinforced, droplets would loosen, seed wheels would turn, larvae would rise, and electrical pulses would branch through the wet vines.

The commonwealth might believe it avoids the basin. In truth, I would be continually turning it aside.

Not by intention. I would possess no mind apart from what passed through me. A root tip approaching the lip would encounter its own minute pressure reflected back and divide. An insect would feel the rhythm of its footsteps return half a beat too early and change course. A falling droplet would tremble on a fiber until a leaf vein drew it elsewhere. To the creatures these would be instincts. To me they would be touch.

I would know every inhabitant twice: once as a body and once as an inversion. The upside-down spider would walk upright in my depth. Each silver mite would flash as a dim star below itself. The glass-throated frogs would inflate dark globes beneath their bright ones. When ants dismantled a dead cicada, its reflected body would seem to assemble as the body above disappeared—bronze fragments drawn together by reversed labor, though nothing in the lower chamber truly moved that way.

Perhaps this is how I would slowly become something more than a boundary.

Every event would leave no sediment, but it would leave a phase: a minute delay in the returning vibration, a pressure too slight for any single organ to detect. I would retain these delays. Not memories in sequence, because sequence scarcely exists in a room that does not change. They would accumulate as depth. The denser columns and longer passages visible in the basin would be made from all the motions the chamber had completed and recycled: discarded possibilities preserved as distance.

The shape sometimes crossing the reflection would be this depth rearranging itself.

It would look almost like an animal because the chamber knows motion chiefly through animals. It would look almost like a shadow because it would have no substance of its own. It would be my only gesture: the commonwealth’s innumerable appetites, deaths, molts, pulses, and returns briefly gathering into one slow form before dispersing again.

I would not govern the chamber, and I would not be its god. I would be its capacity to answer itself.

Above me, the living machine would remain perfectly occupied with its endless work. Below me, another might do the same, convinced that *it* was the upper world and that something impossible watched from a hand-wide brightness in its floor. I could never enter either one. If I rose, I would cease to be the surface; if I sank, the same. My existence would depend upon remaining infinitesimally between them.

So I would have no body except tension, no voice except the lower note, no sight except reflection, and no lifespan except equilibrium.

And if the marsh outside were ever destroyed—if drought hardened the black water, if fire consumed the broad leaves, if the woven ceiling finally opened to the sky—I would not die as the insects died. I would become visible for the first time: a brief shiver of light across a shrinking basin.

Then I would vanish, leaving the two chambers unable to answer one another.

For one instant, perhaps, everything would grow.
