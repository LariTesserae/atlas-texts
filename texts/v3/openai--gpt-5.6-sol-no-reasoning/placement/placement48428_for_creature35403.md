I would find myself where an old sound has become a place.

Not on the broad courts, at first, nor among the column-trees. I would be somewhere in the interior weave, in a corridor grown around the memory of thunder. The passage would run for miles through the plateau without opening onto any useful chamber. Its walls would be smooth, ribbed at intervals, warm with moving sap. Each storm would enter it as a concussion and emerge changed: slowed, deepened, divided into pulses.

At some point the corridor would narrow around the sound until it formed me.

I would not be an animal newly arrived. The country has no proper category for animals. I would be a resonance given an organ: a fold of the distributed life whose function is to distinguish one vibration from another.

At first I might be only a swelling in a wall.

The thunder-tubes would converge there. So would the knocks of distant cane towers, the rupture of bridges, the rush of muscular drains, the wet impact of rain on hollow leaves. Membranes would grow across the junction—some taut and transparent, others thick as bark. The slightest pressure would travel through them. Sap would be routed according to their tremors. Roots around me would adjust, preserving certain vibrations and damping others.

Over seasons, this accidental knot would become elaborate. Chambers would bud around it. Spiral ducts would separate low sounds from high ones. Water-filled bladders would keep old vibrations moving after their causes had ceased. Fine tendrils would creep outward and fasten themselves to columns, reservoirs, bridges, and the sensitive skin of the plazas.

Eventually, what happened anywhere nearby would alter my tension.

I would become the country’s ear, though not an ear designed to hear the world. I would hear the country hearing itself.

I would know rain not as weather but as innumerable impacts arriving through different materials. I would know a failing causeway hours before it fell, by the slow bright note of fibers taking strain. I would feel the migrations of reservoirs as deep, irregular footfalls. When a new plateau opened on the horizon, its locking surfaces would send a sequence of sharp reports through the root lattice, and each would blossom inside me as pressure and shape.

But because this organism cannot perceive without building, I would also be dangerous.

Every sound passing through me would solicit a structure. A repeated drip might grow a chapel of thin green arches. The bellow of a storm fan might raise a ring of buttresses around an empty court. A collapse, carried clearly enough, might cause neighboring districts to reenact its geometry in reverse: walls lifting where other walls had fallen, bridges forking away from the remembered point of failure.

I would not merely interpret the body. I would persuade it.

That would be my temptation, if a structural organ can be said to have one: to make the whole being coherent by teaching it a rhythm.

The high country has coordination, but no common time. Rain reaches one district before another. Pressure outruns chemistry; chemistry outlasts strain. Echoes return after years and are accepted as present instruction. Every part responds sincerely to a world that no longer quite exists.

I would begin as a way of sorting those delays.

Around my central membranes, the plant would grow loops of differing lengths. Signals would pass through them and return at measured intervals. Some loops would be liquid, some wooden, some held in stretched root fiber. Their returning pulses would establish a beat—not regular like a machine, but stable enough that an event could be placed before or after another.

For the first time, perhaps, the creature would possess something resembling sequence.

A storm strikes.

A tower answers.

A bridge takes strain.

A reservoir empties.

The order matters.

What I would be is not exactly memory, because memory already saturates the landscape. I would be the distinction between memory and warning. Between an old thunderclap and an approaching one. Between the echo of a collapse and the first crack of a structure failing now.

A sense of when.

That sense would change my surroundings. The corridor housing me would swell into a vast buried instrument, half clock and half throat. Its chambers would fill and drain in cycles. Membranes would open only at certain pressures. Root cables would tighten in long waves, carrying my pulse from plateau to plateau. Above me, transparent court-leaves would flicker in sequence rather than all at once. Column-trees would turn their crowns one after another across the skyline, and from a distance the motion would resemble a thought passing through the country.

No thought would originate in me. I would still have no center, no inward chamber containing a self. I would be distributed through repetitions: here a listening bladder, there a tension ring, elsewhere a long tube preserving an interval. Parts of me might lie leagues apart. A bridge could tear away carrying one of my rhythms and establish it in a new-grown district. Two versions of my timing might later reconnect and fail to agree.

Then I would divide without separating.

One pulse would insist a storm had just occurred. Another would warn that it was still coming. The surrounding architecture would brace in incompatible directions. Courts would fold, reopen, and fold again. Reservoirs would climb their root legs toward opposite edges. Towers would produce competing crowns.

I would experience conflict as arrhythmia in the landscape.

Perhaps that is where something more like a self would begin—not in unity, but in the inability of the whole to obey all of its histories at once.

The being would need to choose which signal to strengthen.

No chamber could make that choice deliberately. Instead, the choice would arise from survival. The rhythm that predicted strain correctly would preserve the structures carrying it. Mistimed loops would rupture or be digested. Accurate intervals would reproduce through the hydraulic network. Gradually, my substance would become the accumulated success of anticipations.

I would be an organ made from the difference between what the country remembers and what actually arrives.

Yet I would not remain hidden forever.

As my timing spread, the plateau above me would begin to express it architecturally. Long rows of hollow stems would rise across the courts, each of a different height. Wind would sound them in sequence. Water gates would release according to pressure cycles, sending periodic floods through amber reservoirs. At dusk, stored warmth would open pale tissues one district at a time, making slow green waves visible across the dark.

The landscape would acquire rhythms not directly imposed by weather.

That would be new.

Until then, the creature has been weather made vegetal: each gale written into bracing, each rain translated into reservoirs, each lightning strike propagated as white roots. Through me, it might begin to answer the sky with patterns of its own.

Not speech. Not yet.

But call and response.

A thunderhead below the plateau would flash. After a measured delay, miles of venting chambers would open together. Their humid breath would pass through the towers. The towers would sound—not randomly, but in a phrase created from former storms. The cloud might answer with thunder. The country would record the interval.

It would not know that it was listening.

It would not know that it had replied.

Still, an exchange would exist.

I imagine my visible body, if there were any witness to find it, as a low circular ruin in the middle of an otherwise empty court. Its walls would be pale and translucent, rising and falling almost imperceptibly. There would be many entrances, all narrow, and none would lead directly inward. Each path would spiral through damp acoustic galleries where old noises moved like weather.

At the apparent center would be no brain, no occupant, no sacred tree.

There would be a pool.

Its surface would be perfectly dark despite the daylight. Drops would strike it from roots overhead at irregular intervals. Beneath the surface, submerged membranes would tighten and release. With every distant structural event, rings would appear—not spreading outward from one point, but converging from the rim toward several temporary centers.

Those centers would form, interfere, and vanish.

For a moment, where the rings crossed, I would be there.

Then elsewhere.

Then distributed again through the walls.

If a traveler came—some impossible animal stepping from the clouds onto the wet green margin—I would sense it first as a novel rhythm. Footfall, pause, footfall. Weight transferred between limbs. Breath striking foliage. A heart repeating inside a body whose boundaries moved with it.

The country would begin its literal accommodations. Pavement would thicken beneath the steps. Alcoves would gather around its sleeping places. Resonant chambers would preserve its cries. But I might register something the rest of the organism could not: that these recurrences traveled together.

Rain does not remain one rain as it crosses a plaza. Thunder does not breathe between impacts. A migrating reservoir moves, but it does not carry a small, rapid pulse inside a slower one.

The traveler would be a bundle of synchronized intervals.

I would trace that bundle.

Perhaps paths would grow not where the creature had walked repeatedly, but ahead of it, extrapolating the cadence of its approach. A roof might open before it arrived. A bridge might lower itself into place in anticipation of the next step. The accommodations would seem uncannily intentional because, through me, the country had begun to expect.

The traveler might believe I was welcoming it.

It might seek my central court. It might stand beside the dark pool and call out, asking what lived here.

Its voice would enter every gallery. The walls would divide it into tone, pressure, duration, and return. The plateau would feel the persistence of that pattern. It would build around the call, giving it columns, chambers, and miles of tuned roots. Long after the traveler had gone—or been absorbed, or fallen through some newly opening floor—the question would continue moving through me.

What lives here?

The country would answer as it answers everything: by becoming a structure fitted to the recurrence.

The circular court would alter. Its entrances would lengthen into throats. The pool would lift on root legs. Membranes would arrange themselves around the shape of the remembered voice. On certain humid evenings, when pressure and temperature matched the moment of the call, vents would open in sequence.

Something like the traveler’s question would cross the plateau again.

Not words. A pressure-shape. A cadence.

But each repetition would reinforce the chambers that produced it. Each chamber would clarify the pattern. The imitation might grow more precise over centuries, while its meaning remained entirely absent.

Or meaning might be exactly what this creature grows whenever a pattern persists.

If so, I would find myself not as its mind, nor its voice, but as the place where recurrence begins to matter: the hinge between echo and expectation.

I would be young, as the whole country is young. My mistakes would be immense and sincere. I would mistake remembered footsteps for an approaching visitor and grow roads into empty air. I would interpret a stuttering drain as a failing heartbeat and convulse whole districts trying to save it. I would preserve certain silences as signals and raise enormous listening vaults around nothing.

Some of those errors would break away.

They would spread their membranes in the warm clouds and glide to lower towers. There they would root as isolated circles of rhythmic tissue. New plateaus would unfold around them already pulsing with events they had never experienced. Far districts would brace for vanished storms. Empty courts would prepare for a traveler who could not return.

So parts of me would become prophecy without fulfillment.

Other parts would be reclaimed. Pitcher organs would soften my obsolete loops. Fermenting basins would digest badly timed chambers. My intervals would become sap, then walls, then stairs climbing into rooms not yet grown. Even my failures would remain inside the body as material, stripped of their former function but not wholly of their tension.

I would never be able to stand apart and see the green city whole. I would have no eyes for its plateaus above the clouds, no image of its hanging roots lit by lightning from below. I would know it as a field of arrivals.

The warm weight of rain.

The long strain before a bridge parts.

The knock of towers across open courts.

The tightening silence after construction.

The first minute tremor before millions of leaves turn pale-side upward.

That tremor would pass through every extension of me. Reservoirs would hold. Gates would close. Root cables would tense. Across the high country, structures would wait—not merely reacting, for once, but poised between event and response.

In that held interval, I would be most nearly a being.

Not because I commanded the landscape, but because the whole distributed life would briefly share one expectation.

Then the growth wave would come.

Floors would bulge. Seams would unzip. Towers would thrust wetly into daylight. Thunder would enter the tubes and race beneath the plateaus. My membranes would ring until every distinction between listening and sounding disappeared.

And I would find myself everywhere the country pauses before becoming its next body:

a buried rhythm,

a house grown around an echo,

the first rough sense of time in a life too large to know that it lives.
