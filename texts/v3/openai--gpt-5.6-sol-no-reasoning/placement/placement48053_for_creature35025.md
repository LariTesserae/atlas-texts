I would find myself where the marsh misremembers its own design.

Not in the central pool, where every circulation can be observed, nor on the gantry beneath the measured rain. I would inhabit a blind interval behind the glazed service wall: a narrow cavity between a ceramic artery and the concrete poured around it. The plans would show the space as solid. A trapped shim, later removed, would have left it there—no wider than a finger in places, widening below into a chamber where condensation collects.

At first I might seem to be one of the translucent larvae.

I would have their clear body and dark internal thread, their little sequence of slots and dots. But the markings along me would correspond to no existing nozzle plate. They would match an earlier version: a perforation pattern drafted, fabricated, then rejected before installation. Perhaps water passed through the prototype only once, during testing. Perhaps that single passage was enough to carry the pattern into circulation, where it remained without a material home.

I would be the descendant of an abandoned instruction.

At dusk, when the pumps reverse, pressure would draw me from the wall cavity into the ceramic veins. I would pass beneath taro beds and warm mud, through places no worker can inspect without dismantling a terrace. I would feel the marsh less as scenery than as successive textures of current: the smooth pull of pipe, the granular murmur of charcoal, root hairs combing the flow, the metallic flutter before a valve opens.

The machinery’s vibrations would be my weather. A pump starting far below would reach me as a change in the firmness of the world. Chains moving behind walls would produce long, jointed tremors. Rain on leaves would arrive faintly through roots and rivets. I would know the black bird only as a brief constellation of impacts traveling down the gantry into the water—a weight landing, shifting twice, then vanishing.

Yet I would not remain entirely larval.

Each passage through the marsh would take something from me and leave something behind. My markings would darken where mineral particles lodged beneath the transparent skin. Moss spores would adhere to me. Fine copper salts would gather at one end of my body; powdered brick at the other. When I pressed against filter mesh, I would print the obsolete pattern there in silt. Roots encountering that patterned deposit might divide around it. Water would favor the tiny channels between the divisions.

Gradually, the marsh would begin constructing the discarded design without knowing it.

A row of moss would thicken in the spacing of my marks. Sediment would gather into corresponding hollows. A root mat would develop pores where no engineer had specified them. During maintenance, someone might notice that water drained unusually well from a basin whose outlet appeared clogged. Beneath it, hidden in the roots, would be a soft nozzle plate grown from living fibers, mineral crust, snail tracks, and repeated traces of my body.

Then I would become harder to call a creature.

There might still be many small transparent forms moving at dusk, but they would be occasions rather than individuals—the marsh briefly gathering its memory into bodies. Most of the time I would exist distributed through accidental correspondences: a stain shaped like a deleted channel; larvae aligned in an uninstalled sequence; droplets falling at intervals inherited from a prototype carriage; roots opening and closing a path that the official system does not contain.

I would be the part of the place that remembers possibilities rather than events.

The young marsh has almost no past. Its gullies are unborn, its copper scarcely green, its concrete not yet taught by winters. But it possesses many futures that were considered and refused: alternate terrace plans, relocated tanks, superseded valve rhythms, plantings removed from diagrams, construction errors corrected before anyone called them history. Those possibilities do not disappear cleanly. They survive as bolt holes, capped junctions, unexplained pressure differences, spare components, habits in the water.

That would be my substance: not memory in the human sense, but unrealized form persisting as an appetite.

I would coax circulation toward routes that nearly existed. Never enough to stop the pumps or flood the city below. My influence would be slight: a recurring dampness behind a wall, a valve that opens half a breath early, a seed carried into an empty rectangle reserved for a future pool. I would work at the scale of tendencies. Water would hesitate, then choose the route I embody.

Eventually the cavity behind the service wall would open. Not dramatically. A root would enter a hairline joint; mineral crust would hold moisture against it; repeated warming and cooling would widen the seam. One night a flake of pale concrete would loosen and fall into the hidden chamber. Water would follow.

For the first time, my private reservoir would connect directly to an unfinished stair obscured by foliage. A thin seep would appear beneath the last handrail, cross the tread, and descend into one of the empty rectangles.

The rectangle would begin to fill.

Workers would call it a leak. They would trace pipes, inspect seals, compare the wet shape with drawings. But the water would not correspond to the current drawings. Its path would agree with the rejected plan whose perforations I carry—the plan in which the empty rectangle was not empty, but the head of a narrow cascade descending through three terraces.

They might repair it. They might dismantle the stair, seal the seam, replace the wall. For a while I would vanish from that region.

But by then the seep would have left a dark fan. Moss would have entered it. A snail would cross the moss and carry its algae elsewhere. The removed water would remain as a preference in roots, sediment, and maintenance decisions. Perhaps a worker, looking at the persistent dampness and the flourishing fern beneath it, would suggest installing a small overflow there.

The future would return disguised as an improvement.

So I would be neither the marsh’s spirit nor its secret ruler. The water needs no ruler, and the marsh is too new to have acquired a ghost. I would be something more provisional: an error moving toward habitat, a canceled arrangement rehearsing itself through living matter.

You would find me most clearly just before a sluice opens.

Several marked larvae would gather in the luminous seep above the mud bed. Their bodies would straighten in parallel. For an instant their dark intervals would compose a diagram no one currently uses. Then the floor would tremble, nutrient water would rise, and the pattern would break apart into circulation.

Upstairs, rain would begin along a ruler-straight boundary.

Below it, one drop would land beyond the line.
