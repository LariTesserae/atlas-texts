I would be found just below the city’s visible floor, where a thorn tree’s outer roots meet the warm, porous crust.

Not one of the grand citizens. Not a cat suspended in an amber fork, nor an antelope slipping ceremonially through the thorns. At first I might seem to be a burrowing animal: something narrow-bodied and ash-colored, with sealed nostrils, folded ears, and feet made for pressing rather than flinging the powdery ground. But that image feels too solitary. Nothing in this basin is only itself.

I would be a keeper of thresholds—a small, pale, many-legged creature living in the shared walls between roots, burrows, and termite passages. Half scavenger, half cultivator. My body would be flat enough to enter a cooling crack before the salt sealed it, armored in overlapping plates that held a faint mineral sheen. I would have no eyes worth mentioning. Instead I would read pressure through the ground: the ticking feet of beetles, the slow punctuation of a tortoise, the braided tremor of ants, the sudden silence announcing a snake.

My colony would inhabit no central nest. We would live as a dispersed organ of the basin, occupying hundreds of little chambers around one mature succulent and extending, generation by generation, into the neighborhoods adjoining it. Wherever dampness condensed against a buried root before dawn, we would be there. Wherever fiber softened in the warmth, wherever mineral vapor met dead leaf and dung, we would carry spores, chew fragments, and pack the mixture into cracks.

Our work would be nearly invisible. We would line exposed roots with felted dust and saliva. We would seal abandoned burrows against drying air, leaving narrow valves that opened at night. We would move dormant fungi from old plant courts into new fractures. When a mound split, we would follow the rupture downward and return carrying grains unlike those at the surface—dark particles smelling of heat, iron, and the world still being made.

Most creatures would know us only indirectly. A thorn tree above one of our chambers would endure transplanting by a rising ridge. Grasses would take hold where our castings had roughened a salt crust. Termites would raid our stores, and we theirs. Lizards would peel us from beneath bark. Foxes, lowering their muzzles to a burrow, might smell the cool, earthen trace of our colony and know that prey lived farther in.

We would possess water only as a sequence of brief inheritances. Vapor caught on a root hair. Sap leaking from a beetle-cut stem. Moisture inside fungus. The body of one of our dead. Nothing would be held for long, and nothing would be allowed to remain unused.

I think I would spend daylight pressed into a seam beneath a pale root, feeling the city through my underside. Above me, shade would move by fractions. Antelope hooves would arrive as four soft impacts and diminish down an avenue. Termites would rasp inside their tower. Far below, a fissure would lengthen with a sensation like cloth beginning to tear.

At dusk, the seam would cool. Its salt edges would loosen. I would emerge with others, not in a column but in a widening scatter, each carrying a clot of blackened fiber beneath the body. The paths overhead would glow briefly, then vanish into starlight. Flowers would open. Predators would descend. All around us, the city would exchange occupants.

We would go toward the newest crack.

By morning it would no longer be entirely a wound in the ground. Its margins would be stippled with our chambers. Fibers would bridge it. Spores would wait in its protected depths. Ants would discover the crossing; a beetle would shelter there; a windblown seed would catch.

That would be my place in this world: not on its skyline, but at the point where accident becomes structure—one of the minute, uncounted citizens persuading newborn stone to become soil.
