I would be found where the official map and the working map disagree.

Not in the central factory, where every pressure door has a designation and every sound is assigned a cause. Not far out on the clean plains, either, though I would cross them often. I would live along a peripheral service route called something sterile—Conduit E-19, Thermal Return West—where a buried heat line passes beneath an abandoned crane and then bends toward an intake district. The crews would call it Ray Road.

There, warmth bleeds upward through the plating. Sediment never settles evenly. Broad rays arrive along the conduit during the dimming cycle and lie over it like leaves pressed to a vein. Small fish gather in the crane’s lattice. Mussels crowd its lower joints. One dead cable sleeve houses eels, and a leak no one has prioritized keeps a low green plume trembling beside a numbered hatch.

I would be human, but altered less in body than in attention: a route inspector, part ecologist, part mechanic, one of the people sent ahead of consequential work.

Before a valve closure, dredging pass, lamp outage, or current reversal, I would go out to discover what the diagrams had failed to include.

My suit would be old enough to have preferences. The left magnetic sole would engage a fraction late. One shoulder lamp would cast a warmer beam than the other. A retracting tool line would click against my hip whenever I turned across the current. On my helmet, beneath the required identification bars, there would be a small hand-painted mark: three white arcs indicating a ripple, or a ray’s wing, depending on who looked at it.

I would travel alone when regulations permitted and with a shoal of inspection drones when they did not. The drones would race ahead along welds, obedient to geometry. I would move more slowly, watching everything geometry attracted.

A pressure drop on a display might mean a failed seal. In front of me it might also mean shrimp rising from a recess because the seep had cooled, or eels protruding farther from a conduit to taste the changing flow. A vibration anomaly might be a loose panel, mussel weight, a crab lodged in a guide, or a ray sleeping on the sensor housing. My work would be to tell these apart before someone in a dry control room chose the simplest remedy.

I would know the basin through recurrent particulars.

A shark with a pale notch behind its dorsal fin would patrol the intake exclusions two minutes before the warning horn. A cloud of silver fish would divide around Lamp Pylon 441 because one side carried a weak electrical field. The algae at the red-anemone crossing would darken before a nutrient alarm registered upstream. A certain dredger would always provoke eels from the old cable sleeves, though its specifications claimed its vibration lay below their response threshold.

These would not be mystical intuitions. They would be observations accumulated through repetition, recorded where possible and remembered where the system had no category for them.

My dry quarters would be small even by the factory’s standards. One wall would carry sanctioned diagrams: evacuation routes, pressure boundaries, emergency breathing procedures. Another would hold my own basin chart, layered with grease-pencil marks.

Not places, exactly, but events:

**shrimp at second dimming**  
**ray rest after coolant purge**  
**juvenile fish during Bank 6 outage**  
**eel emergence when western pumps reverse**  
**do not flush without visual check**

The oldest marks would have outlived what they described. I would retain them anyway. In a world that renewed surfaces faster than they could age, obsolete annotations would be one of the few forms of history.

I would not be a protector of every living thing. That distinction would matter.

I would scrape mussels from instruments. I would poison bacterial mats when they threatened oxygen balance. I would help close an intake knowing what remained inside it. Sometimes I would stand on a rail while a warm accidental reef—months of shells, algae, anemones, eggs, feeding fish—was scoured to gray metal in less than an hour. The water would fill with fragments. Predators would gather at the edge of the work lights.

Then I would inspect the cleaned exchanger, certify its surface, and return through the lock with bits of shell lodged in my suit joints.

The guilt would be real but not clarifying. Refusing the work would not restore a prior sea. There was no buried coast beneath the plates, no patient wilderness waiting for machinery to withdraw. If the circulation failed, the basin would not become natural. It would stratify, cool, suffocate, or bloom according to the shape of its last operating state. Maintenance destroyed habitats and maintenance made habitat possible. I would belong to that contradiction as completely as the sharks belonged to the screens.

Over time, I might become known for delaying operations.

Not dramatically. Eight minutes for a ray to clear a hatch. Half a cycle to draw fish away from an intake using a portable lamp. A recommendation to reverse flow gradually because eggs had accumulated in a drainage gallery. I would phrase each request in operational language: obstruction risk, biomass loading, sensor contamination, downstream instability. Compassion unsupported by system vocabulary would be easy to ignore.

Some supervisors would consider me useful. Others would say I had begun confusing incidental life with infrastructure.

They would be right. The confusion would be the point.

One shift, I would be sent beyond Ray Road to investigate a periodic impact beneath a section of floor marked as structurally redundant. The sound would come every eleven minutes: three dull knocks, a pause, then one. Models would suggest a pipe crawler trapped in a lower maintenance layer.

I would descend through a flooded hatch into the machinery beneath the sea.

There would be another plate below the plate, just as promised, separated by a narrow black volume crossed with braces and obsolete rails. My lamps would reveal old paint, unlisted ducts, mineral deposits feathering from seams. The space would have no scheduled current, yet the water would move faintly with the flexing of the basin above.

At first I would find only discarded construction: cable drums, a collapsed gantry, ceramic filter racks never installed. Then small fish, colorless from generations beyond the lamp cycle. Shrimp clustered around a warm leak. Mussels opening and closing in the dark. Life not gathered around a recent failure, but around a forgotten layer of operation no current plan acknowledged.

The knocking would not be a trapped machine. It would be a loose pressure flap opening and striking its frame whenever distant pumps entered a particular phase. Three knocks, a pause, then one.

Inside the cavity behind it, eels would have made a colony.

That discovery would become my true location in the world: not the hidden chamber itself, but the interval between finding it and reporting it.

If I logged the structural defect plainly, repair crews would seal the flap, drain the layer, and perhaps fill the void. If I omitted it, the defect might widen and undermine a traveled sector. There would be no innocent choice and no authority to whom the whole truth meant the same thing.

So I would map it completely.

I would count the eels, sample the water, trace the warmth to an obsolete return line, measure the flap’s fatigue, and record the pale fish no one had expected below the manufactured floor. I would turn an accidental pocket into a fact too detailed to erase casually.

Then I would report both things at once:

**Structural intervention required. Established secondary ecology present. Repair method must preserve pressure exchange and thermal flow.**

Perhaps they would laugh at “secondary ecology.” Perhaps the phrase would enter the forms.

That would be my kind of influence: not saving the hidden place forever, but forcing the machine to notice what had grown inside its blind spot. A replacement flap might be built with a guarded aperture. The warm seep might be maintained deliberately. The cavity might receive a sensor, then a designation, then restrictions. Once named and monitored, it would cease to be wholly accidental. Its inhabitants would become operating conditions.

Years later, crews might call it the Knock Gallery. Children might be shown its camera feed in a classroom: pale fish crossing between braces, eels coiled in a structure laid before anyone remembered why. The official account would say the habitat had been incorporated into basin management after discovery during a routine inspection.

My name would not matter.

I would continue outward under the manufactured daylight, walking the straight service roads while silt moved across their markings. I would feel turbines through my teeth. When a distant pressure door closed, I would wait for the delayed concussion and judge its origin. When the lamps dimmed, shrimp would rise around my helmet like sparks going the wrong direction.

And when silence came, I would stop.

Not because silence was peace, but because everything living there had learned the same first question:

What has the machine just changed?
