I would find myself where the town has difficulty remembering what it intended.

Not at the signal yard or among the dynamos, though I would pass through both. Not in one of the great inhabited trunks, where generations have trained the walls to accommodate human sleep. I would be farther down the basin, at its cool edge, where an abandoned workyard slopes toward the oldest culverts. There, maps disagree by a few paces. Brick channels cross beneath root conduits, vanish, then reappear carrying water from no recorded source. The ground is pale with moss except for a narrow dark seam that never quite dries.

A small building stands over that seam. From a distance it resembles a maintenance shed: low, gray, and half leafless, with a ceramic threshold and a roof of overlapping reservoir scales. Yet it was not made all at once. One wall is woven willow-hardwood, another is old brick held upright by roots. Its rear chamber may once have housed a pump. Its front room grew later around a metal workbench. One corner has sunk, but the floor has thickened beneath it, preserving the tilt as though the building has decided that levelness is no longer necessary.

This would be my station.

I would keep lost water.

Not water that has been stolen or wasted, exactly, but water whose route is no longer understood: seepage appearing in a dry district, cistern levels changing without rainfall, condensation gathering on a pipe that should be warm, an old culvert breathing mist at noon. I would trace such occurrences through tissues and masonry. I would stain flows with colored water, listen through flower-shaped horns pressed to walls, and tie small threads around roots to see which tightened overnight.

The work would have no fixed boundary. Water ignores districts more easily than people do. I might be called to a nursery whose beds had begun tasting of iron, then to a press where the hydraulic vines pulsed in time with a reservoir several streets away. I would crawl under workshops, enter chambers opened only during drought, and stand barefoot on root felt to feel the minute cooling that precedes an unseen flow.

People would say I walked leaks, though most of what I followed would not be leakage. It would be old purpose persisting after its explanation had disappeared.

My work name would change as the route changed. At first I might be Lower-Culvert, inherited from someone who retired to the drier outer road. Later, after discovering that the culvert was only an overflow from a buried ceramic manifold, people might call me Nine-Mouths. If the manifold proved connected to the western seep, that name too would become inaccurate, but it could remain for years. Basin names need not be correct to be useful. They are handles, not definitions.

I would be narrow even by the town’s standards, with shoulders accustomed to turning sideways through service spaces. My clothes would carry permanent tide marks at the knees and elbows. Around my neck I would wear a listening tube of fired clay, glazed only on the inside. My pruning knife would be short, because deep underground a long blade catches on everything. In my belt would be wax tablets, a folding pressure gauge, chalk, copper pins, and several vials identified by knots: blue tracer, clotting sap, root sedative, fungal inhibitor.

One vial would contain nothing but clean water from outside the basin.

It would serve as a reference. Water acquires character quickly here—mineral, tannic, saline, full of spores or dissolved metals. When a hidden flow appeared, I could compare its smell, conductivity, and taste against the blankness of imported water. I would use only drops. The vial might last for years.

I would not be solitary, although my work would often make me appear so. Children would see me lifting grates at odd hours and imagine that I lived below the streets. Workers would leave messages at my station: a strip of bark curled one way for unexpected damp, another way for a dry line; three ceramic beads for urgency; a black thread if the water had touched copper. People who disliked speaking before certainty would bring me jars and say only, “This was under the north stair.”

I would ask when it appeared, what had flowered nearby, whether any wall had closed, whether the household had dreamed of dripping.

The last question would sound superstitious to outsiders, but sleeping people hear things waking people dismiss.

Over years, the hidden town would become more present to me than the visible one. I would know the basin as pressures and delays. Rain falling at the gate might raise a membrane beneath the cooling works two days later. A factory’s winter heat might draw moisture sideways through a wall thought impermeable. Certain roots would carry not water but the absence of it, tightening across whole districts before anyone saw leaves curl.

I would maintain maps, but not conventional ones. Mine would be layered sheets of translucent leaf laminate hung from the station ceiling. One layer for brick drains, one for living conduits, one for seasonal seepage, one for routes inferred but never entered. Copper pins would mark sounds without known sources. Pale thread would connect places that changed together. During wet weather the sheets would expand and become difficult to align. That distortion would itself be information.

The oldest sheet would show a channel running beneath the embankment and out toward the hard ground.

No one would know whether it still existed.

That uncertainty would be the quiet center of my life.

Perhaps the amphibians would lead me to it.

I would first encounter one not after rain, but during a long dry interval, deep in a ceramic pipe whose sealed end I had opened to investigate a recurring chill. My lamp would reveal it pressed against the glaze: narrow-bodied, copper-dark, without any visible movement of breath. There would be no water around it, only a skin of condensation.

I would not touch it.

It would open one pale eye. Then the wall beneath it would soften—not melt, not crack, but briefly lose the distinction between pipe and tissue. The creature would pass into that yielding surface as a thorn passes into mud. When the ceramic hardened again, a wet mark would remain in the shape of a small hand.

I would report the incident precisely. Location, temperature, pipe diameter, state of seals, duration of observation. I would omit the feeling that accompanied it: not astonishment, but recognition.

After that, I would begin to suspect that “lost water” was the wrong phrase. Water in the basin was not lost. It was withheld, rerouted, incorporated, or given temporary shape.

The amphibians might not be overflow bodies, as some claimed. They might be instruments of passage grown by the buried town: mobile valves, scouts, or brief sensory organs sent where roots could not enter without breaking old infrastructure. Or they might simply be animals who had learned the town more completely than its human keepers had.

I would resist deciding. Certainty would make me careless.

In time, my station would begin changing around the work. A shelf would grow beneath the hanging maps. The rear chamber would develop a cool recess exactly large enough for sample jars. Fine root hairs would enter the mortar around the workbench and arrange themselves beside the copper pins. During rain, the roof scales would release water not into the main bladder but in slow drops over one particular floor plate.

Beneath that plate I would find an old inspection shaft.

The shaft would descend below the level shown on any map. Its walls would begin in brick, continue in ceramic, then become something neither manufactured nor grown—or else both so thoroughly that the distinction had ceased to apply. Warm lines would move under the surface when touched. Far below would come a sequence of sounds: water, then silence, then water again.

Breathing, perhaps.

I would not descend immediately. A good interval includes fear.

I would brace the opening. Test the air. Consult the root colonies whose territories converged there. Read the nearest witness trees for agreements concerning depth. One scar might prohibit excavation “below the red seam,” though no red seam had been visible for generations. Another might grant passage when the lower reservoirs were in distress. Interpreters would dispute whether the clause referred to this shaft at all.

Meanwhile, amphibians would begin appearing around the station. One beneath the bench. Two in the drain mouth. Five motionless on the roof after a shower. Children would gather at a respectful distance and refuse to count them aloud.

The town would close certain doors behind me and open others ahead.

Eventually, after enough rain, I would go down.

I do not think I would discover a heart, a buried intelligence, or an ancient machine that explained the basin. The town does not seem built around a single secret. Its nature is agreement without center.

I would find, instead, an immense region of exchanges: roots sleeved through brick arteries, clay pipes lined with vascular tissue, forgotten cisterns divided into cells, mineral deposits shaped into valves by centuries of directed flow. Water would move through it too slowly to resemble a flood and too deliberately to resemble groundwater. Here the living town and the industrial ruin would no longer be separable even in thought.

There would be chambers no human could enter. There would be mechanisms no plant could have made alone. There would be repairs performed by no known generation.

And there, at the lowest reachable ledge, I would understand what I was.

Not the keeper of the water.

Not its interpreter.

I would be one of the town’s ways of reaching a place in itself that had become inaccessible.

A temporary probe made of appetite, memory, caution, bone, and tools.

The realization would not diminish me. In the basin, dependence is not degradation. A hand belongs to a body and still has its own scars. A worker may serve a system without being owned by it. The town’s purposes are not commandments; they are pressures to be read, resisted, or redirected.

I would take measurements. I would collect a sample. I would notice a constriction where new tissue threatened to close an old ceramic throat. Perhaps I would cut it. Perhaps I would decide that the closure was necessary and alter the surface maps instead.

Then I would climb back toward evening.

Aboveground, the signal fruit would be glowing pale green. The separated crowns would hold the last light. Someone would be waiting with hot broth and a complaint about condensation in the seed depot. A black bird might stand on the reservoir lip, making everyone look briefly toward the sky.

My report would be modest.

I would say that the lower route remained active. That several maps should be revised. That no deep excavation should occur near the old workyard until the dry season. That the amphibians were associated with pressure equalization, though the nature of that association remained uncertain.

I would not say I had found the town’s hidden self.

I would say only that it was still carrying water.
