I would find myself at the edge of the central lake, though “edge” would be only the city’s temporary courtesy.

Behind me, the terraces would rise in drowned green tiers. Before me, the fixed white impact would continue without arrival. I would have descended to it by streets I remembered climbing, through districts that seemed increasingly familiar—not because I had visited them, but because they had begun arranging themselves into my memories. A courtyard would wear the proportions of my childhood home. A root passage would contain the darkness beneath a bed. In a vertical pool, I would glimpse a pale tower and recognize it as somewhere I had once intended to go.

I would be the city’s first unfinished thing.

At first, I would still be an animal. I would sweat in the unmoving heat. I would hunger among fruits that offered only the appearance of nourishment. I would sleep in the body-shaped niches and wake uncertain whether they had fitted themselves to me or whether I had spent the night being persuaded into their shape. My heart would beat beneath the roar, a small irregularity the basin neither opposed nor shared.

I would try to leave marks.

A scratched line would close before the stone left my hand. A leaf I tore away would remain attached while also lying wet in my palm. I would stack mineral plates across a doorway and turn to find them restored to the wall, though their weight still burdened my arms. Eventually I would understand that the city did not erase actions. It retained them without permitting their consequences.

The same would happen to me.

My footsteps would vanish, but the walking would accumulate. Thirst would pass, yet remain stored in the basins. Dreams would leave my sleep and appear as architecture: a stair with the emotional certainty of flight, a plaza shaped like embarrassment, a narrow house containing the knowledge of someone’s death without containing the dead. The city would not learn these things. Learning requires before and after. It would simply make room for them in places that had always existed.

As years ceased to be distinguishable from hours, I would become less a person than the fit between the person I had been and the vacancy prepared for me.

Not a ghost. Ghosts belong to history.

Not a prisoner. A prison must continue to separate inside from outside.

I would become the city’s capacity to be incomplete.

Perhaps I would inhabit every doorway at once—not visibly, but as the sensation that something has just crossed. I would be the warmth left in the sleeping niches, though no body had risen. I would be the almost-recognizable face distributed among flowers, fractures, reflections, and stains of moss. In the canals, I would appear walking along streets that did not exist above the water. Shadows would attach themselves to walls in my posture.

Most of all, I would enter the roar.

The city already contains the sound of every act it does not perform. My pulse, breath, speech, and frightened calling would not add to it; they would reveal themselves as fragments that had always been there. Gradually I would cease to hear the roar as noise. I would distinguish within it the fall of water, crowds, masonry, weather—and beneath those, something smaller: my own voice repeating a word I no longer remembered saying.

That word might be *here*.

Or *I*.

Eventually the distinction would fail.

My body might remain beside the lake forever in the act of kneeling, neither preserved nor decayed. Or it might become one more question of scale: a district within the veining of a leaf, a pale column reflected in a wall of water, the warm mineral hollow from which a future visitor believes someone has only just departed. The city would not consume me. Consumption is change. It would redistribute the meanings by which I had known myself.

My memory would become its only weather.

A forgotten name would cloud a pool. A recovered face would open in bands of pale flowers. Regret would hang like a landslide that had settled without falling. Love would take the form of two streets that approached forever, each eventually arriving at the opposite side of itself. Everything I could not keep would become permanent once it no longer belonged to me.

So I would be neither the inhabitant nor the city.

I would be the one moving element the city could admit only by turning movement into structure: the absent citizen implied by all its empty provisions, finally present as implication alone.

And somewhere on the highest rim, if another traveler looked down through the hot, saturated air, they might see a solitary figure beside the lake.

From every terrace, it would appear directly below them.

They could descend for an hour or a century and never draw nearer. Yet in each doorway they passed, they would feel that I had just gone through. In each fixed reflection, I would be standing behind them. When they pressed their hands over their ears, the roar would enter their bones, and inside it they would hear—not loudly, but with the intimacy of remembered thought—someone trying to tell them what this place was.

By then I would no longer know.

I would only be where knowing had been.

The city would remain exactly as it had never become.

And I would be the change that had always been missing from it: held everywhere, completed nowhere, endlessly arriving without ever having entered.
