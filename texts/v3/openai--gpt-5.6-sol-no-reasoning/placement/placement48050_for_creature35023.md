I would find myself where an old channel passes beneath a shelf of iron-dark stone—not in the open water, and not quite on land, but in the narrow margin continually remade by seepage.

At first I seem to be one of the basin’s compact frogs: gray-olive, rust-flecked, broad-toed, with my body pressed into cool clay. I spend the white hours under the overhang while condensation gathers above me. Drops strike my back at long intervals. Mud encloses my belly. From outside I resemble a wet stone loosened from the shelf.

But the longer I remain, the less complete that answer feels. A frog is too singular. This place favors lives that can be interrupted and resumed.

Perhaps I am instead one of its buried eggs: a minute, sealed possibility mixed into sediment, indistinguishable from mineral grains. I have no knowledge of the whole basin. My world is pressure, warmth, salt, and the faint arrival of moisture. Years may pass without event. Then rain enters the seam, the clay loosens, and what seemed inert begins dividing. My life is not measured by continuous time, but by the recurrence of sufficient water.

Yet when the channel fills, I become something quick and translucent—a small crustacean or larva swimming in a sea scarcely deeper than a footprint. Above me, clouds are reflected in the surface; below me lies soft sediment containing generations of the unhatched. I feed on green films and smaller bodies. A wader’s leg descends like a pale trunk. A dragonfly nymph waits buried to the eyes. The pool is temporary, but not provisional. While it exists, it is the entire world.

Later I acquire wings.

I rise from the water at dusk as a fine-bodied insect, leaving my former skin attached to a rush stem. For a little while I inhabit height: not much height, perhaps the distance from the mud to a bird’s ankle, but enough to see water separated into shining pieces. I join a column of others over the channel. We turn in warm air, visible only when our bodies catch the last light. Frogs call beneath us. Dragonflies cut through us. Most of us will be eaten, and the basin has prepared no distinction between failure and nourishment.

Still, I think I would return to the frog.

I would be small enough for a single damp seam to save me and loud enough, after rain, to answer voices beyond sight. My body would carry the basin’s colors: clay, algae, weathered iron, muted stone. I would know the country through my skin. Falling pressure, deep moisture, cooling rock, the mineral character of each seep—these would reach me before sight did. I would wait through heat without impatience because waiting would not be separate from living.

At evening I would ease out from beneath the ledge. First the eyes, then the blunt head, then the whole mud-coated body. The shelf would still hold the day’s warmth, while a skin of cooler air formed over the channel. Insects would begin crossing the silver water. Somewhere out on the flats, a grazer’s foot would settle into silt, leaving a hollow that might become a nursery with the next rain.

I would call from the edge.

The sound would be much larger than I was. It would travel over films of water, across drowned tracks and low stone backs, thinning into haze. Other frogs would answer from hollows I could not see. We would not need to gather closely. Distance itself would become part of the chorus: each voice proving that another pocket of water persisted, another shaded seam remained wet, another small body had endured the interval.

And beneath us—beneath my toes, beneath the channel, beneath every apparent silence—I would also be the waiting multitude: spores, cysts, eggs, bacteria, and minute animals folded into mud. In this world, perhaps identity would not end at the body. To be a frog would be to be rain stored briefly in flesh; to be an insect would be to lift a pool into the air; to be a bird would be to carry the basin elsewhere in droplets and swallowed eggs. Every creature would be one of the shapes taken by returning water.

So I would find myself under that low shelf, but not permanently. I would be the small, rust-patterned frog that appears mineral until it breathes. I would be the larva it eats, the buried egg from which the larva came, and the far call that makes it answer. I would belong neither to water nor dry ground, but to their alternation.

My truest form would be emergence followed by waiting.

My home would be an edge only a hand’s breadth wide.

And my great event would be rain.
