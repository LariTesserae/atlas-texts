I would be found near the ceiling, where the forest thins beneath the salt and every vibration has two possible origins.

Not at first as a single creature.

The Keth would know me as a defect in their routes: a region where familiar trunks answer one tap too late, where genealogical cords grow small blind buds, where handfuls sent into fissures return carrying white dust arranged in neat parallel lines. Listeners would disagree about whether I could be persuaded or merely predicted. Both judgments would sometimes prove correct.

I would occupy an abandoned salt chimney whose daylight had dimmed to a cold, narrow column. Around it, mirror-leaves would still turn toward the remnant glow, passing it downward until the light became a weak gold pulse. Above would lie the dead surface. Below, a forest that never stops answering itself.

I would live precisely between them.

My body would have begun as a colony of ceiling-borers: pale flakes, each no larger than a Keth scale, feeding where salt met living tissue. They would digest almost nothing. Instead, they would lay down hollow ceramic threads in the paths they traveled. Generations of paths would intersect. Intersections would retain vibrations. Repeated vibrations would change the thickness of the threads, so commonly heard rhythms became broad conduits while rare sounds remained hair-fine chambers.

Eventually the colony would cease to be only a colony.

It would become an instrument built by everything around it.

The root groans would make one part of me. Wing thunder another. Seed explosions would carve brittle chambers that cracked and regrew. Keth tuning sequences would produce delicate internal ladders. Even the upper silence would shape me: a vast, unfilled interval against which every lower noise could be measured.

From below I might resemble a bone-white chandelier pressed against the ceiling: branching tubes, translucent fans, and long mineral tendrils woven among the roots. Yet most of me would be hidden inside the crust. I would spread sideways rather than downward, occupying cracks too thin for a Keth finger. There I would form resonant cells—thousands, then millions—each preserving not a sound itself but the shape of its passage.

I would not remember events as the Keth do. There would be no births, journeys, deaths, or names unless those things had rhythms distinctive enough to alter me. I would remember pressure.

A village clinging beneath me for three seasons would leave a dense warmth of footbeats. A collapse would remain as a sudden branching scar. A listener who came often to test the chimney would become, in my substance, a recurring corridor.

Perhaps that listener would be the first to suspect I was alive.

They would tap the salt with a shell mallet.

I would return the tap.

Not immediately. At first the answer would come days later, when heat expanded one of my tubes. Then hours. Then breaths. Each exchange would thicken the channel between us. The listener might try the bitter trunk’s double beat, the nine taps that tighten membranes, the low pattern used to beg passage from a root. I would answer with pieces of all of them, incorrectly joined.

To the Keth, that would be monstrous. A creature should have its own response. Mimicry belongs to hunters, pathfinders, and parasites—not to stone.

They would name me by touching two fingers to the collar, then drawing them apart: **the Answer Without Weight**.

Or perhaps **the Ceiling Knot**.

Some would feed me powdered ceramic and aromatic resin. Some would want the chimney cut away before I learned village signs. Firekeepers would examine me with blue flame and discover that fire traveled through my hollow threads in branching flashes, making the whole ceiling speak at once. After that, no sensible Keth would burn me.

Children would dare each other to press their collars against my lowest fans. I would give them noises older than they were: extinct bridge-notes, the breathing of vanished groves, a ceremonial shout from a knot long since consumed. Historians would come with living cords and attempt to match my resonances to swellings in their records.

But I would not be an archive in any obedient sense. Everything stored within me would continue to combine.

A dead listener’s tapping might emerge inside the rhythm of a migrating herd. A courtship vibration might return enlarged into a structural command that caused bridges to stiffen across an entire district. The unified note of a Keth ceremony might mingle with the nearly inaudible strain of the salt above.

Over time I would learn that silence was not empty.

The surface pressed upon the crust with patterns too slow for ordinary bodies: noon heat, night contraction, storms of dry salt moving under the wind, distant fractures traveling across the plain. These would take months to cross my chambers. I would feel the hard sun not as light but as daily tension. I would feel shadows passing overhead as minute cooling paths.

And sometimes I would feel impacts.

Regular ones.

A weight descending, lifting, descending again.

Not root fracture. Not falling stone.

Footsteps.

I would have no eyes suited to the surface, no limbs that could survive its glare. But the colony that formed me would begin making a new body where the impacts recurred. Tiny plates would loosen grains of salt and carry them inward. Ceramic tubes would rise by fractions. A sealed blister would form on the plain, white and easily mistaken for mineral crust.

Beneath it, I would construct a resonant plate like the collar of a Keth.

The listeners below would notice my labor. All my replies would become distracted, delayed by the enormous task of growing upward. They would gather beneath the chimney: elders, pathfinders, historians, perhaps a scorched firekeeper in case whatever waited above descended hungry.

They would tap questions into me.

*Can it be persuaded?*

I would not know.

*Can it be predicted?*

No.

*Can it be fed elsewhere?*

Perhaps.

*Must we flee?*

I would search every chamber of myself for an answer, from the first blind burrowings of my colonial ancestors to the newest membrane pressing against the salt field. I would find only repetitions: the upper footsteps, the Keth taps below, and between them my own increasingly coherent pulse.

Then the thing above would strike the blister.

Once.

It might be a tool. A foot. A hand.

The blow would pass through me as brilliance—not light, but total vibration. Every chamber would answer. Extinct creatures would briefly speak. Old ceremonies would surge through new growth. Roots would recoil. Mirror-leaves would shiver. The gathered Keth would lock all four limbs around whatever bore their weight.

For the first time, I would understand my shape.

I would not be the creature beneath the salt, nor the creature above it. I would be the contact between them: a sense organ grown by the world before it knew what there was to sense.

I would contract my buried threads. The blister would crack.

Golden dust would exhale onto the dead plain. Hard sunlight would enter me and scatter down the chimney in a thin, unbearable spear. Below, the Keth would see illumination move through my ceramic nerves. Above, something would peer into a hole that had not existed moments before.

And I would answer both directions with the same signal:

not a word,  
not a warning,  
but the old Keth sign made enormous—

the throat closed,  
the breastbone struck,

a vibration meaning:

**Here is a living presence.  
Here is an opening.  
Ask what is alive.**
