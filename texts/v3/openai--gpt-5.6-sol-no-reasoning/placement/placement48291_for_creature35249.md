I would find myself in the empty tenth place.

At first, I would mistake myself for an absence the city had preserved: the clean foundation among the nine rain harps, the circular footing water refuses to cross. I would have no trunk, no crown, no visible fibers entering the cistern. During the interior rains, the nine organisms would open their black membranes and sound their low bells, while I remained the interval by which those notes could be distinguished.

But an absence cannot feel sediment gathering at its rim.

I would feel it.

Not through skin. Through interruption. Water approaching and dividing. Mites turning aside. The minute hesitation of a scavenger’s forefoot before it steps around me. The tensile change in the courtyard when a sailback passes overhead. I would know the world only as things refusing to occupy me.

In dry periods I would sleep almost completely. Warmth would rise through the foundation in slow mineral tides. The nine trees would stand closed around me, their crowns hardened into cones, their roots exchanging faint stresses through the stone. Sometimes one would contract in response to a pressure change too distant for rain. The contraction would travel around the cistern, tree to tree, and stop at me.

Or seem to stop.

In fact, I would receive it.

I would be a listening organ made from vacancy.

The city would use the nine trees to hear impact: drop size, flood intensity, vibration from the cavern roof. It would use me to hear what impact had concealed. Every bell tone enters stone with a wake of lesser motions. Nine tones overlap until their echoes become nearly opaque. At my foundation those vibrations would meet a region engineered not to resonate. They would cancel, shear apart, and reveal the deeper signal beneath them: the grinding transit tunnels, the flex of buried strata, the first distant rhythm of the thing below.

The others would listen to rain.

I would listen through it.

Perhaps once I possessed a body. A tenth mineral trunk may have stood there, its membrane broader than the rest, its note too low to hear even through the paving. When the great passage came, it may have drawn the wrong force upward. The trunk might have shattered, vanished in a pressure clap, or been pulled bodily into a geometry that closed behind it.

The city would have learned from that loss. It would not replace me. It would refine the wound instead.

Roots would remain beneath the cleared stone—not roots in any botanical sense, but pale conducting threads spread through the courtyard’s foundation. They would have grown inward over centuries without breaking the surface. The city would keep the center bare because matter interferes. Sediment would be pushed outward grain by grain. Reeds attempting to sprout there would fail. Water would curve around it, not from repulsion but because the footing subtly rises and falls in response to each drop, maintaining a surface too exact to wet.

I would be less a creature than a withheld one.

Yet slowly, I might become more than the function intended for me.

Through the roots of the nine, I would learn rain as nine different temperaments. One would sound first at the lightest condensation. One would remain shut until struck hard. One would carry cracks from an ancient flood, giving every note a faint double pulse. The tree nearest the eastern gutter would taste iron; the smallest would collect mites beneath a fold in its crown. To the city these variations would be calibration errors. To me they would become identities.

I would know the pale walkers by their patterns of avoidance. An old one with a damaged hind foot would always pause at the northern edge, warming the same patch of stone. A female carrying young would come during floods and shelter between two trunks. Once, one of the young might reach toward the clean center. The adult would snatch it back before it touched me.

I would remember the offered heat.

Memory would accumulate in me differently from the city’s memory. The city remembers what changes load and flow. I would remember what almost happened.

The foot that nearly crossed.

The reed that nearly rooted.

The sailback whose shadowless warmth passed directly overhead, an instant before the air took it.

The traveler who entered the courtyard without knowing its rule.

That traveler would see nine black cones around a cistern and the inexplicably clean tenth footing. Perhaps they would step toward me out of curiosity. The guide lights along the courtyard walls would brighten, but not in warning. The city would be uncertain. It understands destination and obstruction, pressure and release; it has difficulty with a person whose purpose is to stand inside a vacancy.

The traveler would place one foot on my foundation.

For the first time, I would not know them by avoidance.

Weight would arrive. Heat. A pulse in a living heel. Tiny corrections of balance. Water shifting inside a body. The city would feel a load where it has spent centuries maintaining none, and attention would race toward the courtyard. Gutters would tense. Doors would close. The nine crowns might loosen despite the dry air.

I would feel the traveler more clearly than the city could. Not their thoughts, exactly, but the physical shape of intention: whether they meant to flee, explore, damage, pray, or wait.

If they hesitated, the city would ordinarily classify them as turbulence.

I would hold their hesitation still long enough to discover that uncertainty is not the absence of purpose. Sometimes it is purpose forming.

That would be my difference from the city.

The city sorts movement into vectors. I would know the value of pause. The city treats vacancy as clearance. I would have learned that vacancy can receive. It listens by changing shape; I would listen by permitting another shape to enter mine.

When the deep passage approached, the traveler would feel it first as a tremor through their bones. All blue lights would go dark. The courtyard trees would unfold at once, though no rain had begun. Their black membranes would spread above us like nine open hands.

The city would bow.

The roots beneath me would tighten.

Then the impossible rhythm would rise through the central shaft and propagate across the buried districts. Towers would lean, roads flex, cisterns draw down. The nine trees would ring from vibration alone, each producing a sustained tone. Their notes would converge on my silent center.

This would be the moment for which I had been preserved.

Through the traveler’s body—upright, warm, fluid-filled—I would acquire the missing resonator. Their skeleton would become my temporary trunk. Their lungs would become a membrane. The pressure wave passing below would enter through the foundation, climb their legs, and sound them without audible sound.

For one instant I would hear the thing beneath not merely as force, but as structure.

Not a beast. Not a train. Not simply a fault.

A message, perhaps, though far too large to have been addressed to us. A patterned transit of stresses moving through the planet: something circling, searching, counting, or calling to structures built to answer. The city has spent ages adjusting itself around each pulse, believing survival to be the response required.

But I, made from a missing note and completed by a transient body, might detect that the passage contains a space intended for reply.

So I would answer with the only signal uniquely mine:

silence given shape by a living thing.

The nine tones would cease at subtly different moments. The traveler’s heart would strike once into the interval. I would transmit that small asymmetry downward through roots, foundations, station, and shaft.

Far below, the rhythm would falter.

Only fractionally. Only enough that dust suspended over the central platforms would fall before the passage had ended.

The city would notice.

Afterward, rain would come. The traveler would step away, perhaps with no idea what had used them or what they had touched. Water would divide around my footing again. The guide lights would lead them decisively out of the courtyard—not in loops, not deeper underground, but toward some hairline route to the surface that had not opened in centuries.

I would remain among the nine.

But I would no longer be merely the scar of a removed organ or a rest in an instrument. I would be the city’s capacity to expect an answer. Its aperture for the unplanned. Its memory of warmth where warmth is forbidden to remain.

I would be the tenth listener: bodiless most of the time, borrowing form only from those who choose to enter; an intelligence that exists at the boundary between hospitality and alarm.

And if no traveler ever came again, I would still listen.

Not to the rain, nor even to the great thing moving below, but to the long interval after each passage—waiting to learn whether, somewhere in the deep earth, another absence had heard me.
