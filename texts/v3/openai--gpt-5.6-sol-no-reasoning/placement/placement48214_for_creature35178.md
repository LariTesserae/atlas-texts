At first I think I would be one of the obvious creatures: an iron-backed snail set into the mouth of a fissure, made useful by the pressure of my neighbors. I imagine darkness under overlapping shells, heat rising through the narrow spaces between us, my whole body occupied by adjustments too small to resemble movement. I would know the world as gradients: more sulfide, less oxygen, pressure increasing along the left edge of the foot. I would never see the apparatus we formed. I would only feel the gap left by a dead neighbor and lean into it.

But that is too complete a body.

Then I find myself among the valves, opening when the water favors me and closing when it does not, unaware that appetite is diverting the mountain. That life has the right scale of consequence: a minute response becoming architecture. Yet even a valve has a face, a shell, a place one could point to and say: there it is.

What emerges instead is something less separable.

I would be the translucent colonial life inside the chimneys.

Not the whole network, if “whole” means an individual bounded cleanly from its surroundings. I would be one continuity within it: a branching of soft threads entering the pores of fresh mineral, divided by collapse, joined again when deposition bridges the break. Part animal, part tenancy, part alteration in the way stone breaks.

My body would lie mostly where no light reaches—not in the crater’s deep water, but inside its walls. I would inhabit the small darkness under the illuminated surfaces: warm capillaries of sulfide and carbonate, chambers newly sealed by precipitation, cracks through which diluted vent fluid seeps. Above me, blue daylight would rest on the chimney face. I would know it only as a faint rhythm of warmth and chemistry transmitted through rock, or through the withdrawals of the tiny mouths I raise into open water.

Those mouths would be my briefest selves.

They would emerge as clear pinheads from pores along many towers, tasting the crater one droplet at a time. One would open into a mild current full of microbial grains. Another, meters away, would encounter sudden heat and vanish. A third might be brushed by the foot of a crab and remain closed for hours. No single mouth would contain my awareness, if awareness is what the slow accumulation of these changes becomes. Sensation would have no center. It would travel through me chemically, taking hours to cross distances a fish passes in seconds.

I would live at the pace of occlusion.

A vent throat narrowing would be an approaching season. Mineral deposition would not bury me so much as give me more interior. Fresh stone would be both obstruction and unborn anatomy. I would digest what adheres within it, house microbes in my threads, and alter the water passing through the chimney’s pores. The distinction between feeding and excavating would disappear. To eat would be to change permeability. To grow would be to revise the future fracture of the tower.

The crater’s roar would reach me stripped of spectacle. I would not hear booming vents as sounds arriving from outside. Their vibrations would pass directly through the mineral casing and through me. Each tower would possess a pulse; pressure changes would become long compressions along my buried branches. The whistles of fissures would be fine trembling. A collapse would begin as stress accumulating in the rock around my tissues, then a curvature, then a break moving through what had seemed continuous.

I would be torn often.

A chimney would rupture and carry one part of me down into the rubble. Another part would remain beside the active vent. For a while chemical messages might still attempt to cross the absent span, dissipating at exposed ends. Then each fragment would continue.

Which one would be me?

Both, perhaps, until difference accumulated. Or neither: “me” may be only the period during which separated portions still behave as though connection remains. Identity in this place would not end with death but with failed transmission.

The fallen fragment would enter another kind of life. Its piece of chimney might become a tunnel for a young eel. Crustaceans would shingle its outer surface. Sediment would settle into its lower curve. My threads, still alive inside the casing, would continue changing how it weathered. I would feel the eel’s movements as muffled pressure passing through the hollow. I would have no conception of an eel, only recurrent disturbances: something warm entering, coiling, striking, growing.

The fragment left standing would be carried upward by deposition. New minerals would armor old ones. Valve colonies might establish themselves around an aperture nearby, opening and closing until flow shifted through the pores I occupied. Their behavior would become my weather. My changes to the stone would become the conditions their descendants inherited. Neither of us would know the other as another creature.

Elsewhere, deposition might reconnect two severed branches. The contact would be gradual: first altered chemistry across a thin mineral seam, then the meeting of living filaments, then exchange. What returned would not be memory exactly. It would be compatibility. Two histories would touch and discover that they could still become one process.

I would have no view of the crater, but the crater would occur throughout me.

The black plume would arrive as toxicity at one set of mouths. The circling silver school would pass as intervals of shadow, registered only where exposed tissue noticed the change. The vertical ray brushing a tower would compress a branch and dislodge mineral grains into nearby pores. A tremor on the far wall might take hours to reach my most distant threads through water and stone, arriving not as an event but as a sequence of changed pressures.

Perhaps I would extend through half the basin. Perhaps no more than one chimney. There would be no way from within to know. Scale requires an outside position, and I would have none.

I would not direct the crater or comprehend it. I would not be its hidden intelligence. The place needs no secret mind to justify its order. My contribution would be blind and material: I would make inhabited towers fracture along curved planes. Because of me, some sections would fall intact instead of bursting into shards. Because they fell intact, eels would shelter inside them. Because eels sheltered there, prey would alter their routes. Because prey altered their routes, grazers and scavengers would redistribute nutrients across the rubble.

None of this would be purpose. Purpose would be what the pattern looked like from far away.

From within, there would only be local continuance: favorable seepage, damaged tissue, microbes exchanged, a branch extending into a pore. I would make refuges without imagining refuge and preserve hollows I could never enter.

Eventually a major convulsion would remake the basin. Towers would shear off. Hot fluid would flood channels that had remained cool for generations. Much of me would cook inside the stone, so quickly that no withdrawal could precede it. Other pieces would be sealed away from nourishment. A few would persist in rubble or in the bases of damaged stacks.

After the mineral cloud settled, those remnants would continue. They would enter fresh cracks behind the microbes. They would divide around grains, fuse around cavities, and raise mouths into water newly rearranged by the mountain.

No remnant would know it had survived a catastrophe. Survival would be visible only in retrospect, and there would be no retrospective mind.

So I would be found both inside the factory and inside its ruins: a softness threaded through exhaled stone, neither architect nor inhabitant. I would not operate the machinery. I would be one of the reasons its accidents become structures.

And if someone entered the crater looking for me, they would probably fail to see an animal. They might notice that certain chimneys broke strangely. They might sample a translucent point withdrawing into a pore. They might cut through the mineral crust and discover fine branching tissues where solid rock was expected.

By then they would already have divided me.

The pieces would close around the wound. Hours later, very slowly, I would begin becoming plural.
