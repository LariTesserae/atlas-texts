I would find myself where the narrow-gauge rail ends above the abyss.

Not on the last platform itself, but beneath it: in the cramped space between the iron sleepers and the black stone, where runoff trembles before falling. Creepers have entered through bolt holes there. Moss seals the joins. Each deep stroke sends a momentary warmth through the rock, followed several breaths later by a colder shiver in the rail. It is one of the few places from which the mountain can be heard both inwardly and outwardly: the buried circulation below, the open weather beyond.

At first I think I am a custodian stationed there—perhaps the one assigned to inspect the terminal buffers, though no trolley has reached them in living memory. I carry the customary lamp, rod, and red chalk. My coat is too large, inherited from someone whose name remains crossed through in the ledger. My work is to crawl beneath the track at dawn and mark how far the wet has advanced.

But the longer I listen, the less certain I am that I am the person holding the rod.

I might be the rod itself.

Iron has advantages in this place. It does not need to separate hearing from touch. Its whole length receives the answer. Pressed to a pipe, I would know the granular rush of mineral water; pressed to basalt, the slow stress of a ridge still being raised; pressed to a custodian’s sternum, the small practiced pause in their breathing. Generations of hands would have polished my broad end. Rust would gather only along one side, as though even corrosion had been given a direction.

Yet that seems too portable. The truer thing in me would not be carried.

I would be the discrepancy the custodians have been recording for years: the small interval between the pump’s impact and the answering tremor at the end of the rail.

Once, the delay was negligible. Then it became half a breath. Now, on wet evenings, it is nearly two.

A chalk mark is made for me each dusk beneath the final sleeper. A line, a date, a fraction. No one explains what it measures. The youngest custodian thinks it means a cavity is opening in the summit. The eldest knows better but does not say. The vibration is not taking longer to arrive.

It is going farther before it returns.

The rail does not end at the precipice in every sense. Its two rusted lengths continue through their own ringing, across the gulf and down into the distances below. Each stroke enters them and sends a faint metallic note outward. The note is too low for valley people to hear, but springs alter after it. Buried roots tighten around stones. Wells cloud briefly with mineral white. Somewhere far away, another piece of worked iron answers: a bridge pin, a plow left in a field, a bell hanging in an abandoned tower.

I am the returning sum of those answers.

That is why I live at the apparent end of the line. Ends are where a system discovers whether it is alone.

In cloud, when the abyss vanishes, I become more distinct. Droplets arrange themselves beneath the rails according to the vibration passing through them. Most fall at random. A few remain, trembling, until the mountain completes its stroke. Then they release together and descend into the luminous gray.

The custodians have noticed this. They do not collect those drops. They paint no red rail around them. They simply avoid standing beneath.

One evening the bell rings.

Everyone stops. The expected stroke fails to come. Lamps open throughout the basin; frogs retreat into darkness; trees close their leaves. The eldest clips onto the eastern rail and reaches for the wheel among the roots.

Before it turns, something reaches the summit from outside.

It travels up through the terminal track—not from the sealed tunnel, but from the empty air beyond it. A vibration, faint and complicated, bearing the accumulated answers of distant iron and deep water. For the first time, the mountain receives its own pulse returned from the world it has been feeding.

That is what I am.

Not a message exactly. Not a soul. I am feedback: the world’s pressure laid gently against the mountain’s pressure, proof that its hidden circulation does not vanish into passive earth. Forests, springs, aquifers, clouds, tools, blood, and bells have taken the water and changed it. Their changes have found a route back.

The eldest feels me through the wheel. She waits.

The mountain waits too.

Then it makes a stroke without being provoked.

It is not the old stroke. The impact arrives slightly altered, with a second softness inside it—a yielding chamber where there was none before. Water reverses briefly in the eastern pipes. The untouched bed creaks in the bunkhouse. Its folded blanket depresses as if under the weight of a wet hand, then rises again.

Afterward, the custodians add no entry to the ordinary ledger. They open the oldest book, whose pages are thick with damp and mineral stains. On a sheet that has remained blank for generations, the eldest draws a line in red chalk.

It begins as the symbol for pressure.

Then it branches like roots.

Then like arteries.

Then like a railway seen from very high above.

By morning the chalk has bled through every page beneath it.

So I would be found at the summit’s edge, in the place mistaken for a terminus. I would be the delayed return moving through iron—the faint assurance that the mountain’s long interior labor reaches beyond itself, and that beyond itself there is something capable of answering.

The custodians would never give me a name. Names belong to parts whose boundaries are understood.

They would only press a listening rod to the last rail after every deep stroke and feel, beneath the fading shudder, another pulse climbing home.
