I would be below the city, in the old drainage system where the streets have sunk close to the tide.

Not in the main culverts, which are scoured by pumps and inspected by crews, but in the obsolete passages beside them: brick storm drains superseded by concrete pipes, drowned cable galleries, foundation voids left between successive seawalls. There, the city’s eras lie pressed together. A corroded ladder descends beside a newer ladder. Dead conduits remain bolted beneath live ones. Saltwater enters through cracks at high tide and withdraws without ever quite emptying the chambers. The ceiling sweats continuously.

At first I imagine myself as an eel.

It would suit the place: a long, blind-seeming body entering through a grille bent by flood debris, living by pressure, scent, and tremor. I would know the tide as a coolness along the brick and the starting of a pump as a violence transmitted through my jaw. My skin would bear pale scars from squeezing past screens. I would feed on mosquito larvae, drowned flies, scraps of fish, perhaps young rats fallen from ledges. In the underground warmth I might never make the migration expected of me. The machinery would intercept the old command of the sea and turn it into circling: drain, sump, outfall, harbor, drain.

But an eel belongs too completely to water, and this place is neither water nor land. I would need to cross both.

Then I become a rat—one of the large, dark rats of the pump foundations. My colony would occupy a valve chamber raised just above ordinary high water. We would line our nests with cable insulation, filter cloth, pigeon feathers, shreds of labels softened from drums. Our paths would run along pipes warm enough to dry our feet. We would know every schedule: washdown water arriving bitter with chemicals, the brief fall in vibration before maintenance, the slam of a gate that sends crabs tumbling through a lower channel.

Yet the rats belong to abundance. They are quick, communal, innumerable. What emerges for me is slower and more solitary.

I think I would be a creature the workers dispute.

Some would call it a dog because the camera catches four legs. Some would insist it is a seal that has learned to climb through the flood system. On thermal images it would appear as a low smear beside the warm mains, then vanish when the pumps began. Maintenance crews would find broad prints in mineral residue—webbed, perhaps, though the edges would have run in condensation. Fish bones would collect on dry ledges inaccessible from the harbor. Once or twice, a dog pack would gather at a drain mouth and stand without barking, staring in.

It would not be a hidden monster in the ordinary sense. Nothing here needs to remain pure enough for a single name.

I would be one of the city’s adaptations: descended, perhaps, from the seals outside the breakwater, or from dogs trapped during an old flood, or from neither in any history that could be proved. A low amphibious scavenger, long-bodied and nearly hairless, with skin the color of wet concrete. Dense whiskers would read air currents in unlit passages. My eyes would be small and filmed against salt and chemical spray. The forefeet would be narrow enough to grip ladders and pipe braces; the hind feet broad enough to push through water. Rust would stain the underside of my body. Around the neck and shoulders, old scar tissue would form pale ridges where I had forced myself beneath intake grilles.

I would live in the intertidal machinery, where infrastructure ceases to be architecture and becomes habitat.

My den would be an abandoned pump room enclosed inside a newer flood barrier. The original door would open onto water and no longer move. I would enter through the discharge pipe of a dismantled sump, climbing when the harbor rose high enough. Inside, above the tide mark, electrical cabinets would stand open and empty. Pigeons might once have nested there, before subsidence flooded the access corridor. Their compacted droppings, feathers, wire, and bones would make a dry shelf beneath the switchgear. Warmth from an adjacent working station would pass through the wall.

I would feed wherever one system delivered life into another. At floodgates I would take stunned mullet. Beneath grilles I would find eels injured by pumps. After heavy rain, I would move inland through submerged tunnels to eat rats driven from their lower colonies, fledgling pigeons fallen into shafts, beetles and larvae washed from warehouses. I might follow the dogs’ waste-gate vigils from underneath, hearing their feet shift on the concrete overhead. What they failed to catch above would sometimes arrive below.

The city would not appear to me as districts, buildings, or roads. It would be a body of pressures.

Here, a turbine’s steady pulse. There, the hollow drumming of rain entering a flooded stairwell. The thin agitation of fish against a screen. The irregular scratches of crabs climbing a channel. The delicate impact of rats crossing a pipe brace. The enormous, distant concussion of a train passed down through pilings. I would distinguish seawater, runoff, cooling discharge, and groundwater by temperature and taste. I would know which rusted doors conceal air and which conceal only deeper water.

My range would expand and contract with the pumps. During dry weather I would remain near the harbor, under the oldest quay. In rain, when the sumps filled and automated gates opened, temporary rivers would connect chambers otherwise isolated for months. I would travel far beneath the industrial yards, surfacing in places where no one expected a large animal: a flooded rail cutting, a settling basin, the basement of an administrative building abandoned but still lit by emergency power.

The people would encounter me indirectly.

A technician might notice that fish caught against an intake grille had been removed from the dry side. A camera would show condensation trembling on a handrail, then something passing too close to the lens. During an inspection, workers would find a sleeping hollow among old filter mats, still warm, with scales pressed into the fabric. Reports would use phrases like *unidentified fauna*, *probable feral canine*, and *access route not established*. New screens would be installed. I would learn their hinges.

My importance would be modest but real. I would carry remains from water into dry chambers and from dry chambers back into water. Beetles and flies would breed in what I left. Rats would take scraps from my feeding ledges. Crabs would clean the bones below. Perhaps my body would host pale insects found nowhere else, living in the folds behind my forelegs or feeding on the bacterial film of my skin. I would be less an apex predator than a moving junction in the city’s appetite.

I would seldom see daylight. When I did, it would be from the mouth of an outfall at low tide. Beyond it: brown mud, concrete walls, bird legs, the undersides of wings passing through gray light. The open city would seem dangerously exposed, stripped of ceilings and echoes. I would wait beneath the lip while waders fed only a few meters away, each probing the same mud from above that I searched from below.

Once in a long while I might cross into the outer harbor at night and haul myself onto a drowned breakwater among the seals. They would lift their heads and smell the industrial water on me. I might resemble them enough to avoid alarm and differ enough that they would not make room. Behind us, the city would glow dimly under cloud: sodium lamps, red warning beacons, green emergency signs—the only green in the world.

Before dawn I would return through the warm outfall.

That is where I would find myself: not wholly beneath the city and not wholly within it, in the wet seam between old failure and continuing repair.

I would be what survives in that seam—a feeder shaped not by leaf, soil, or season, but by grilles, tides, vibration, corrosion, and the narrow intervals when a gate is open.
