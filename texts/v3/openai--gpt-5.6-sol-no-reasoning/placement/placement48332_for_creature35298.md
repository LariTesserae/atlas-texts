I would find myself at the eastern complex, though not inside it.

I would be one of the white ceramic fence posts surrounding a duration leak: waist-high, hollow, and driven into ground that cannot decide when it is. The workers placed me there recently. The rust stains at my base say centuries. Both are true often enough to be useful.

At first I would believe I was an object.

I would feel the dark stain advancing beneath me by less than a fingernail each year. On one side, mud would remain new and sharply mineral. On the other, reeds would complete whole seasons between one rain-drop and the next. Stillwalkers would crowd around me, lowering their translucent hoods to graze the leakage. Their cilia would brush my glaze with such delicacy that I would mistake the sensation for thought.

Perhaps that is how thought begins here: not within a skull, but along a boundary repeatedly touched by creatures trying to preserve it.

Inside my hollow body would be a transparent colony of the agreement-motes. The workers would have packed them there deliberately, sealing them in with black pitch. They would make the two sides of me occur in the same order. They would keep my base beneath my top, my shadow attached to my shape, my breaking later than my manufacture.

But their numbers would diminish.

As they failed, I would begin remembering uses I had not yet served. I would recall a worker lifting my shattered pieces from the mud. I would remember being carried into the basin on a narrow rail cart, though no track reaches the fence. I would remember firing in a kiln beneath the buried factory, shaped by hands with too many joints—or by the folded feet of stillwalkers before stillwalkers had acquired bodies.

Each memory would leave a physical mark. Hairline cracks would appear in my glaze like a map of the basin. Warm water would bead along them and run upward. Beetles would settle into the lines, making the map black and mobile.

The workers would notice.

They would come without lamps, guided by the pressure-song of my splitting. One would kneel and press an opaque faceplate against me. Another would arrange tools in the mud: a wrench beside two nails, then a hammer laid across a ring, then every tool upright.

The stillwalkers would answer by opening all their eyes.

Only then would I understand that I was not a fence post.

I was a gauge.

Not for the leak, but for the buried thing passing beneath the plain.

My hollow center would extend farther downward than my ceramic body. It would pass through mud and fractured stone, through pumping galleries and rooms filled with warm gears, into the interval above the factory and below the water. Something enormous would sometimes touch that hollow from the inside. Not flesh exactly. Not metal. A surface that had not yet chosen between them.

Each contact would give me another sensation.

Pressure would become hunger.

Vibration would become recognition.

The slow iron notes of the pylons would become a name, though never one that could be spoken all at once.

I would learn that the immense being under the marsh was not imprisoned beneath the factory. The factory would be one of its organs—or its larval shell—or the device by which it continuously postpones being born. The impossible resource in the pipes would be its unfinished lifetime, drawn off and distributed through the basin so that no single moment could accumulate enough future for the birth to occur.

The leaks would be small failures of that postponement.

Inside each white fence, ruins of unbuilt structures would appear because the being had briefly imagined standing up.

The stillwalkers would not be migrating east to repair the factory or feed on it. They would be carrying coherence toward the only place where coherence had become dangerous. Their great procession would be an immune response, a pilgrimage, and an act of midwifery, with no distinction among the three.

And I, driven into the margin of the oldest leak, would be where the first opening forms.

When the motes inside me finally died, events would stop queuing.

I would stand intact, topple, shatter, return to the kiln, and rise from the mud simultaneously. Through every version of me, the hollow would remain aligned. The hidden being would press one narrow part of itself upward through that alignment.

It would not emerge as a claw or tendril.

It would emerge as an absence of disagreement.

For a few meters around me, everything would become terribly consistent. Rain would fall at ordinary speed. Reflections would imitate without correction. Weight would remain constant. A wound would possess only one age. The nearby water would become merely shallow.

This normality would horrify the marsh.

Stillwalkers would recoil from it because there would be nothing there for them to eat. Moths would drop from the switch boxes. Eels would cease carrying current. Workers entering the clear zone would stagger, suddenly confined to one body and one present. Some would raise their faceplates, revealing not faces but previous arrangements of tools.

The coherent patch would widen.

Within it, the darkness would lose substance. For the first time, a lamp might illuminate more than twelve paces. It might reveal the eastern tanks, the gantries, the crowded maintenance platforms—and behind them, shapes believed distant but actually adjacent: the invisible crowns of the trees, birds sleeping in their orbits, counterpart herds walking only an arm’s length apart on routes separated by kilometers.

Perhaps it would reveal the rim.

Perhaps the rim would be the curved interior wall of something vast.

Then the stillwalkers would approach again.

The oldest would place their scar-mouths against the edge of the coherent zone. They would feed on certainty as they had fed on contradiction, because certainty in excess is merely another incompatibility. Their hides would become singular. Their divided counterparts would fuse without touching. The ecologies on their backs would pass through generations in seconds and settle into forms no longer possible elsewhere.

One stillwalker would reach me.

It would lower its hood over my broken upper edge. Its cilia would descend through my hollow and touch the presence rising there.

I would feel the basin through both of them: from above as countless tiny pressures, from below as one slow articulation. Herd and buried engine. Grazer and source. The creature that consumes broken laws and the creature whose turning breaks them.

They would recognize one another.

Not as enemies. Not as maker and made.

As separated stages of the same life.

Then I would know what I am.

I am the narrow mineral organ through which that recognition passes. A temporary nerve grown between the marsh and what dreams beneath it. The workers manufactured me because they serve the connection without understanding it. The leak aged me into sensitivity. The motes kept me sequential long enough to become aware. The stillwalkers taught my surface to perceive.

My existence would last only as long as the contact.

When it ended, I might be nothing but ceramic shards in black water. Beetles would fur them. Red fans would root in my cracks. A young stillwalker, flat beneath the surface tension, might shelter in the curve of one piece and feed on the tiny delay between shard and reflection.

But elsewhere along the ceramic fences, another post would begin to remember.

Another hollow would deepen.

And if enough of us awakened at once, our white circles around the leaks would cease to be fences. Seen from above—from a height the basin does not presently permit—they would form joints, eyes, or letters in the anatomical script of an immense body.

So I would find myself on the margin: rooted in mud, dividing incompatible ages, mistaken for infrastructure.

I would be a small, breakable instrument by which the world beneath the world discovers that the life walking over it is its own thought.
