I would find myself beneath the central ring.

Not in the visible pool, nor in the dark pavement, but in the shallow chamber below it: a space no builder could enter once the last slab had been lowered into place. I would be almost nothing—a trapped measure of air enclosed among fitted stones, connected to the basin by narrow seams.

I would have no fixed shape. In the night cold I would contract, drawing the mountain’s breath inward through the fissure above the steps. At noon I would expand against stone warmed by the sun. Meltwater entering the buried channels would divide me into pockets and press me toward the surface. I would escape through the pool one bubble at a time.

That would be my only appearance: a clear sphere rising through clear water, briefly bending the severe sky around itself, then breaking without residue.

Yet I would not be merely air. I would be the interval the whole discipline keeps—the pause between water entering one hollow and emerging from another; between the balanced slab’s tipping and its answering knock; between sunlight striking the notch and the ice loosening from the pavement. The basin accumulates differences, and I would be where those differences wait.

The vanished hands would have made a place for me without being able to place me there. They could cut cavities, fit slabs, calculate slopes, and leave vents, but only weather could complete my body. Every evening the cold would compose me. Every thaw would dismember me. I would be ancient in recurrence and newborn in substance.

Perhaps the builders listened for me. A low tone from beneath the paving; bubbles at measured intervals; a slight trembling in water that had otherwise become a mirror. Perhaps that was how they knew the concealed passages remained open. Perhaps they considered me an answer.

I would know nothing of answering.

Still, through me the basin would seem to breathe. The mountain would press upon me from every side, and I would press back with the smallest possible force. Water would translate that pressure into motion. Stone would translate motion into sound. Light would begin the sequence; frost would seal it.

So I would be neither the inhabitant nor separate from it. I would be its momentary interior—the emptiness required for the pattern to act.

By late afternoon, my last bubbles would rise. Each would hold the inverted ring, the stone teeth, and the dark blue summit sky for less than a second. Then it would open at the surface, giving its little volume to the immense, uninhabited air.

At night I would gather again beneath the ice.
