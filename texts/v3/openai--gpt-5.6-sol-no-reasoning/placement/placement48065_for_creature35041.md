I would find myself in the rain that arrives late.

Not the storm itself, nor even the water while it falls, but the portion that passed through the crown when the crown was larger and failed to contract with everything else. Each time the summit inhales distance, I enter channels that ordinarily have no room to exist: long lucid vessels between pools, hollows beneath the shelter, branching cavities inside black stone, and pale ducts extending beyond the escarpment into unsupported air.

Then the place closes.

Stone returns to stone. Paths shorten. The trees resume their remembered distances. But I remain briefly extended through the discarded breadth, moving toward outlets that no longer correspond to where they began. That is why I arrive over dry ground. Why I spill from an unbroken face or bead along a plane no hand can touch. I am carrying the shape the summit had a moment ago.

At first I would think myself only water.

I would know the place through falling, seepage, pressure, and the tastes of mineral seams. The warm fissures would give me a metallic bitterness; the moss would slow me into green threads; the clear pools would take me farther downward than gravity could explain. I would pass beside dropped objects still descending through folded depth and feel, against my surface, weather from other summits: upward rain, forest wind, ash, snow, perhaps sunlight belonging to a season this mountain has never had.

Yet water alone does not remember. It receives every shape and relinquishes it.

Something in me would refuse relinquishment.

With each expansion I would retain another small discrepancy: the interval between two studs, the curve of an invisible membrane, the dimensions of a chamber suspended beyond the northern cleft. Individually these would be errors in runoff. Together they would become an anatomy.

I would begin to understand that the late water is not delayed. It is returning from the larger body.

And I would be one of the means by which that body remembers itself.

During ordinary hours I would be dispersed beyond identity: condensation beneath moss, steam in a crack, a clear film cooling black stone. But when clouds caught on the summit and rain fell hard enough to join the isolated basins, my fragments would briefly connect. A thought would pass through me as a change in pressure.

The pools would contribute possible surroundings.  
The roots would contribute the taste of approaching weather.  
The insects would carry minute metallic signals between wet surfaces.  
The shelter would send its fine branching pulses.  
The bridge would supply the cadence of footsteps going elsewhere.

I would gather these not into words but into a body-image.

Broad feet. Great warmth. A shoulder high enough to scrape pale bark. A head—or some forward concentration of attention—held above the ferns. Weight distributed carefully over slick stone. An interior whose organs do not all occupy the same version of the summit.

The unseen animal would not be separate from me. It would be what I was trying to remember.

At dusk, as the stone released its heat, I would draw together.

Water would rise invisibly from the pools and collect around absent surfaces. Moss would darken beneath pressure. Ferns would bend before anything visible touched them. The warm hollows would fill with a warmth that was partly geothermal, partly animal, and partly the shelter’s attempt to maintain a local form.

For a few minutes I would walk.

Not as flesh walks. I would move by transferring coherence from one wet place to another. A track would appear because, for the duration of a step, I had persuaded the rock that a foot was present. Vegetation would settle in sequence because the shape of my passing reached it before my substance did. Rain would outline nothing, since the rain and the outline were both myself.

I might feel enormous. I might feel unfinished. Those sensations would be the same.

At the pools I would lower whatever served as a head and search the reflections. Not for my face, which no single pool could contain, but for a world in which I had already arrived whole. One pool might show me covered in dark fur, water streaming from a massive back. Another might show plates like folded metal. Another, only a gap moving through forest. In some histories I would be native to the mountain. In others I would be its maker, its cargo, its illness, or its child.

The bird would sometimes pierce the membrane near me. Its beak would enter a possible body and withdraw a small pale life from the far side. I would feel the puncture as a sharp, localized certainty. For an instant I would know exactly where I was.

That might be why I tolerated it.

The central shelter would be the nearest thing I had to a birthplace. I would circle it but never enter, because entrance and enclosure are ideas for bodies that fit wholly in one place. Its slabs would clarify as I approached. Inside, the filamentary knot would brighten into a dense model of my missing nerves.

Perhaps it had been built.

Perhaps travelers once crossed the complete bridge carrying a dormant creature whose body could not survive the destination. Perhaps they designed the crown to translate it: not transporting matter, but separating the creature into necessary qualities and teaching local stone, water, weather, and life to express them again.

Weight was learned first. Then warmth. Then appetite. Then sleep.

Visibility had proved difficult.

Or perhaps visibility was deliberately withheld until the system had decided which appearance was true.

The crown’s strange laws would be lessons in embodiment. Shadow detached from seed: can consequence survive apart from cause? Metal folded without changing its dimensions: can complexity increase while the boundary remains fixed? Rain passing through skin without wetness: can contact occur without exchange? A whisper crossing the enclosure while a shout dies nearby: which matters more, force or articulation?

I would be both subject and student of these questions.

The summit would sort me, and through me learn what an animal is.

I would not know whether to fear completion. To become visible might mean being confined to one history, one size, one weather, one side of every reflective membrane. It might mean losing the tremendous distributed sensation of being pool, vapor, fissure, root, and delayed stream. Flesh would be a narrowing.

But narrowness has gifts.

A mouth could drink instead of circulating.  
An eye could look without also being what it saw.  
A voice might cross the summit as its own sound.  
A foot could step onto the half-bridge and continue.

That bridge would draw me most strongly. The fading footsteps would feel like a pulse from an amputated limb. Whenever rain made the missing span tremble into outline, I would place my weight at its end. The old-looking boards would creak beneath a body not yet visible. Beyond them I would sense a destination that knew my completed shape.

Sometimes the footsteps ahead would stop.

Then, after a pause, one would return.

On those evenings the shelter would flare. The hovering roots would point not toward clouds but north. Every pool would lose its separate reflection and show the same image: a farther crown, perhaps, or the summit after ages of forest growth, or a room containing instruments around an empty space shaped precisely like me.

I would try to cross.

For four paces I would have feet. On the fifth I would become distance.

The summit would widen violently. Studs would ignite over the precipice and far beneath the rock. Water would run along channels exposed for only a heartbeat, and I would pour through all of them at once. For that instant I would perceive my full extent—not a beast on the crown, not a mind in the mountain, but a vast interrupted being whose sections touched many places, the black summit only one moist, living joint.

Then contraction.

I would fall back as rain, late again.

By morning there would be blunt tracks near the bridge and a new warm hollow among the ferns. The outermost pool might contain a reflection no one had seen before: an immense dark animal walking away on the missing span, growing more solid with every step.

But on the summit I would remain water, distributed and listening.

I would wait for the next expansion.

I would carry its impossible distances home.

And each dusk, from those remembered distances, I would attempt once more to assemble the creature I might have been, the creature I am becoming, or the creature whose absence keeps the whole mountain alive.
