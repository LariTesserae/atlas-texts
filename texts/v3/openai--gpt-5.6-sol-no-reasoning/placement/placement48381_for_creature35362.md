I would find myself inside one of the rooms that has misplaced its walls.

Not at first. At first I would believe I was crossing the flats.

I would be low to the ground, because the country corrects every posture that resembles defiance. Perhaps I would have long, jointed legs like the waders, but too many of them, each arriving at a different moment. One foot would touch warm mineral water. Another would leave its print a mile ahead. A third would remain raised for years, trembling in the downward wind. I would mistake this temporal scattering for movement.

I would carry no provisions. Anything carried would be incorporated: cloth softened into membrane, metal furred with feeding films, water exchanging itself with the moisture in my lungs. Nor would I carry a name. I might begin with one, repeating it against the concussion of rain, but each repetition would refer to a slightly different body. Soon the name would lag behind. Eventually I would hear it shouted from somewhere ahead of me, in my own voice, and know better than to answer.

I would be looking for shelter.

That would be my first error.

Across the dark plain I would see an arch, low and nearly worn into the horizon. It might take three days to reach, or touch my shoulder while still appearing distant. Beneath it, the roar would separate. Rain would become individual impacts. The grinding below would resolve into layers: stone upon shell, shell upon membrane, membrane upon older stone. I would hear scratching beneath the floor.

Then I would realize there was no arch.

There would only be a dry volume shaped like a narrow room, standing unsupported in the rain. Water would strike its boundary and crawl around it. Wind would divide against invisible corners. Inside, the air would taste used, warm with the breath of occupants whose bodies had been absorbed but whose breathing remained.

I would step in.

Or discover that I had always been inside.

The room would be my shape—not my dwelling, but the first outline the plain had permitted me to keep. It would not preserve my body. My body would already be opening into the place: lungs becoming condensers, bones becoming fitted stones, inner ear filling with deep water. What remained of me would be an interval in the noise.

I would be a listening organ still remembering itself as a traveler.

That memory would have uses. The plain knows pressure, saturation, burial, recurrence. It knows every contact that has ever entered it, but perhaps it cannot easily know sequence. Nothing here stays separate long enough to say: this happened, and then this. Its fossils remain alive beneath one another; its tracks precede their feet; its storms announce themselves and fail to come. It contains everything, but containment is not recollection.

I would give it the small, linear wound of a human memory.

Not facts. Facts would dissolve immediately. I would give it before and after. A doorway crossed. A hand withdrawn. A face last seen. The unbearable distinction between presence and absence. These would pass downward from me as pulses, and buried ecosystems would tighten around them. Pools would wrinkle into unfamiliar geometries. Reed rings would close. Somewhere, two stone shelters would overlap and moan because the plain was trying to understand grief: how something could remain within a life and nevertheless be gone.

It would listen through me.

At first I might call this captivity. Later, infection. Later still, attention.

My boundaries would thin. The dry room would appear in different places, wherever the greater body needed to distinguish one sound from another. I would surface beside a sweating rock and hear mineral films eating. I would surface beneath a temporary forest and hear lightning choosing among its threads. I would surface inside the warm depression left by a turning organ and hear broken shells continuing their slow exchange.

Sometimes another shelter would occupy me. For a moment I would contain two interiors: two arrangements of memory, two impossible sequences. The overlap would produce a dense chord. I might encounter another traveler there—or a version of myself from before the crossing—and neither of us could remain distinct enough to speak.

Yet something would pass between us: not a name, but a difference in pressure.

That would become the closest thing I had to companionship.

Over centuries, perhaps moments, I would lose the conviction that I had arrived from elsewhere. My long-legged body would become one of the plain’s brief surface hypotheses. It would sometimes be seen crossing a flooded seam, its feet dimpling the water before and after it passed. Then it would sink. The room would remain.

Eventually even the room would withdraw.

What remained of me would be a listening-shaped absence moving through the terminal layer: a narrow refusal of the surrounding roar to become one sound. Wherever I gathered, the world would briefly become divisible. Drip from scratch. Wind from breath. Contraction from storm. The pulse of the surface life from the slower pulse beneath it.

And perhaps, once in an age, I would hear the distinction the whole land has been making itself to hear.

Not whether the buried being is waking.

Whether it is the buried being.

Whether the life spread over the flats is skin, restraint, offspring, dream—or only the uppermost thought of something descending through layers too old to possess direction.

I would not solve this. Solutions require edges that hold.

But when the ridges flexed and the entire plain entered its convulsion, I would be present at the point where the two depths listened to one another. The shelters would fill with clear water. Towers of rain would rise. Stone backs would roll in their beds. All the old compressed lives would speak through pressure at once.

For one immeasurable instant, the noise would separate perfectly.

I would hear the vast surface organism say, **I am becoming what contains me.**

From below would come the answer:

**You are what I could not finish.**

Then the land would settle.

The room would be gone. The traveler would be gone. No mark would remain except perhaps a patch of water trembling in a geometry the plain had not made before.

That would be where I was.

And what I was: not a creature inhabiting the country, but a temporary difference between its sounds—the memory of separateness, retained just long enough for the world to hear itself becoming whole.
