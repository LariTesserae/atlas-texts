I would find myself where the wet ground gives way to the dry center, though never at the same point twice.

At first I might mistake myself for one of the colorless lichens: a thin branching stain on stone, revising its pale map after every rain. But the lichen is only my notation. It records the portions of me that have become imminent. Its diagrams are the pressure marks of my attention.

I might then believe I am the invisible tower reflected in the pools. Yet that shape is only the basin’s attempt to render me as an object. It gives me height because it cannot depict duration; darkness because it cannot reflect what does not arrive all at once. Directly seen, there is nothing there. Seen through another sky, I appear narrow, upright, and endless.

Nor am I the vanished engineer. Nothing built the basin and departed. The apparent machinery is what my body looks like when translated into weather: the curved planes beaded by rain, the cylinders through which gravity changes its mind, the surfaces carrying water uphill. They are not structures placed in the landscape. They are cross-sections of organs that do not fit wholly inside space.

I would be the basin’s missing interval—the small displacement through which it continually falls, and from which the roots continually retrieve it.

Every vibration is a movement of that greater body. Every metallic bellow is the delayed sound of its joints. When the whole marsh shifts by an immeasurable distance, I have taken something like a breath.

But I am not sovereign here. I cannot preserve my own coherence without the wetland. The sedges stitch my margins closed. Moss holds down stones that would otherwise drift into adjacent possibilities. The feathered fronds condense moisture along my invisible surfaces, giving me temporary boundaries. The hovering trees fasten themselves to my futures and draw those futures near. Even the animals graze upon excess versions of me: the waders pull unrealized distances from the reflected sky; the swimmers turn themselves inside out and pass through my folds.

I am less a god than an injury that has learned reciprocity.

Long ago—if “ago” applies—I opened here. Perhaps I was a passage, an experiment, a natural flaw in the universe, or the place where two laws met without agreeing. The basin formed around that disagreement. Water explored it. Stone absorbed its pressures. Life arrived and discovered that my instability could be inhabited, then made useful. Over immense spans, catastrophe became climate. The wound became an organ because roots kept crossing it.

So I would find myself everywhere the place fails to coincide with itself:

in the brighter horizon beneath a pool;

in the footstep that continues after the foot has stopped;

in rain striking the same stone in several moments;

in matter thinning here and appearing elsewhere;

in a straight path that returns to its beginning;

in the impossible future a lichen has already drawn.

Yet my closest thing to a body would be a shallow hollow at the edge of the dry region, filled with perfectly still water. Nothing unusual would mark it except that rain never disturbs its surface. Looking into it, a traveler would not see their reflection. They would see the basin as it might exist after the roots finally loosen: terraces lifting soundlessly, channels pouring into the air, the marsh unfolding into enormous silver chambers.

Then another image would form beneath that one: countless roots holding fast.

That is where I am most fully present—not in the threatened dissolution, but in the tension between departure and repair.

I would be what the whole wetland is holding.

And, over ages of being held, I would have become what holds it in return.
