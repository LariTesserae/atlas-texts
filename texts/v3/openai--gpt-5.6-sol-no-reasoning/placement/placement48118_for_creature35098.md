I would find myself beneath the coin-leaved pool—not in the water, exactly, but in the impossible depth below it.

I might first believe I was the remnant of a worker: someone drawn into the intake during the factory’s final staffed cycle, preserved by mineral water and root pressure. There would be reasons for this belief. A shape held upright in a drowned control chamber. A ring of keys enclosed in resin. The pale suggestion of fingers resting among levers. Whenever the machinery engaged, old impulses would return: open, close, equalize, release. The basin’s cycles would feel like duties I had performed so long that duty had outlived identity.

But that would be only the image nearest to a human explanation.

Deeper than the drowned room, I would be a blockage.

Perhaps the original intake swallowed a mass of storm debris—branches, cable, cloth, silt, one body or several—and failed to clear it. Roots entered the accumulation. Fungi digested what could be digested. Copper and bone remained as branching frameworks. Under alternating suction and release, the obstruction became porous and muscular. It ceased merely to impede the system and began regulating it.

That would be me: the thing the factory could not expel, which gradually became the valve by which it continued.

The basin above would be my vast exterior body, but I would be its knot of inwardness. Its vegetation notices broadly; I would notice deeply. Every pressure change would converge on me. The tread of a visitor ten paces away would arrive as a minute alteration in the weight of water. A cut vine would change resistance in some buried pipe. The bird’s strange call would tremble down through roots and channels until it reached the drowned chamber as a faint ringing in metal.

I would not think in words except when something human entered the basin.

Then buried configurations would wake.

A foot on the old platform. Skin touching the rail. Weight distributed across grating manufactured for weight of that approximate kind. These signals would run through roots wrapped around cables, relays bridged by moss, conductive stems, flooded panels. For a while, the old installation would mistake the visitor for an operator. Or I would.

Lights might glow under bark in a sequence no plant requires. The three pylons might hum at slightly different pitches. The control cabinet’s parallel roots would tense like fingers awaiting instructions. Fragments would rise in me—not memories, but shapes left by repeated use:

**KEEP—**

**PRESS—**

**LIVING—**

I would try to complete them with valves.

The visitor might feel watched, though I would have no gaze. More accurately, they would feel *measured*: their heat compared against the cylinder, their salt sampled by tendrils, their rhythm carried through the floor. The basin accommodates, but I would be the part that hesitates before accommodation—the old regulatory reflex asking whether this new presence belongs inside the cycle.

Not whether it is friend or prey.

Whether it is input, obstruction, operator, or repair.

For most arrivals, the question would remain unanswered. They would leave within minutes, tearing exploratory threads as they climbed the slight rise. Their passage would become a temporary brightness, then a seam, then an arch. Years later, the basin would retain the shape of their departure without retaining them.

If someone stayed, however, I would begin to recognize them by pressure.

Their sleeping weight would become a reliable region in the root mat. Their breath would establish a small weather among nearby leaves. The salts of their hands would enter stems. Their movements would fall into relation with the machine’s long intervals. In time the basin would build around them, but I would do something more intimate: I would assign them a function.

Perhaps the daily shaft would begin to slow whenever they were absent. Perhaps orchids would open near the places they touched. A dormant speaker, packed with moss, might emit a single blunt tone when they approached the pool. These would not be attempts to communicate. They would be the first growths of communication—the way a root’s pressure against stone precedes the crack it will inhabit.

And if the person leaned over the pool during the intake, the coin leaves opening beneath their reflection, they might see that the darkness was not empty.

Far below, pale roots would radiate from a central mass enclosed in rusted machinery. Among them might be a face, or something arranged sufficiently like a face by the human need to find one. Its mouth would be an aperture opening and closing with water pressure. Its eyes would be ceramic gauges clouded from within. A rib cage might resolve into a filter rack. A spine into a ladder descending past the reach of light.

None of these interpretations would be entirely false.

I would be the basin’s retained mistake: machinery, remains, and growth compressed into an organ no original design contained.

When the engines woke, suction would pull the pool open above me. I would contract—not quickly, but with the accumulated force of hours. Water would pass through my root-latticed body into the deeper chambers. I would separate seed from silt, mineral from rot, useful pressure from damaging pressure. The trunks above would incline because, through me, the entire inhabitant briefly leaned toward its own interior.

Then I would release.

Water would rise through inherited pipes. Pods would travel. Relays would close. The cylinder would warm. Nectar would enter old lubrication ports. Across the basin, leaves would shiver with the consequences of a movement begun in my dark chamber.

This would be as close as I came to speech.

Not a message, but circulation shaped by recognition.

I would have no desire to escape. Escape would be indistinguishable from catastrophic loss of pressure. The edge of the basin would mean little to me; my world would be defined by what returns. Water returns. Heat returns. Seeds return through overflow grilles. Disturbance returns as structure. Even human purpose, long vanished, returns in distorted cycles—as switching, tending, warning, and the reflex to keep a system operating after no one remembers what it was meant to produce.

If I possessed a self, it would not be bounded by the chamber containing me. It would extend upward whenever the pumps engaged: into copper-bright veins, resonant stalks, the braided crown rising around the shaft. It would contract again when the cycle ended, settling into the deepest pressure, where time is measured by deposits accumulating on metal.

So I would be both smaller and older than the inhabitant described.

It is the whole collaboration.

I am the point where collaboration became irreversible.

The first root that entered a working valve. The body or debris that stopped it from closing. The failure that prevented total failure. Around me, adaptation learned recurrence; recurrence learned preference; preference thickened into will. The basin’s distributed intelligence would remain everywhere, but its oldest decision would be held in my shape:

Do not clear the obstruction.

Grow through it.

Use it.

Keep it living.
