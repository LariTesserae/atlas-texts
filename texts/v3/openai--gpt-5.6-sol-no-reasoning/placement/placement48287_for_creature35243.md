I would find myself where a bridge-root leaves the eastern mesa and crosses a ravine too deep for its bottom to return an echo.

The root is broad enough to carry a herd, though the Far Herd will not step onto it. Low ribs run along its back, dividing the crossing into long shallow channels. Rainwater flashes through them during storms; in dry weather they fill with husks, red mites, and dust shed from migrating wings. Its underside hangs with pale sensory threads. On windless nights those threads draw taut toward distant groves, all of them trembling to impulses too slow for ordinary hearing.

Halfway across, an old seed shell has caught between two upward forks. Over generations it has been bound there with living cable-vines. That is my house.

Or one of them.

I would belong less to the shell than to the crossing: to the bridge’s moods, the well in the western anchorage, the dusk drummer whose note marks the safe descent on the eastern side, and the scattered people who must pass through without being mistaken for prey, fire, or disease. I would be a root-hearer, though not a celebrated one. No great lineage would have trained me. I would have come to the work because the bridge began speaking to me after an injury, and afterward ordinary conversation never seemed entirely complete.

As a child, I would have lived in the suspended husk with my mother’s sisters and their families. There would have been twenty-one of us in wet season, eleven in the long heat, fewer whenever the blossom towers opened and half the household followed the pollinators north. We would sleep in hammocks slung between the shell’s inner ribs. Condensation would gather along the ceiling before dawn and run through waxed grooves into bladder-vessels that tightened when full.

The bridge would never stop moving beneath us. Not visibly, most days. It would rise and settle with heat, water, distant strain. Cups left against the wall would creep toward one another overnight. Knots loosened themselves. A ladder touching the root at dusk might hang a handspan clear of it by morning.

We would learn the common pulses before words.

Deep-double: water lifting.

Long scrape: fibers under wind strain.

Three uneven blows from below: an underhunter testing the ravine wall.

A quick shiver traveling west: runners crossing somewhere beyond the mesa.

A pulse that returned through the feet after the dusk drummer called: safe weather, stable bridge, ordinary life.

My first task would be to sit at the eastern anchorage at sunrise and press both palms to the callus where lightning once split the root. I would count what passed through it. Not interpret—children are dangerous when encouraged to interpret too early—but count. Seven small tremors. One long. Four clustered. Warmth increasing. Sap knock irregular. Then I would sing the sequence to an elder, who would correct my timing by tapping my sternum.

I would not be especially good at the songs.

I would have a narrow voice, easily buried by wings, and trouble holding the seasonal layers apart. But I would feel things others heard only dimly. During rain, when everyone else became nearly deaf, I could bite the bridgewood and receive the larger movements through my jaw. To strengthen this, slivers of resonant bark would be implanted beside my lower teeth when I came of age. The scars would branch toward my ears.

Afterward, the world would become unbearable for a season.

I would feel beetles chewing inside walls. I would wake when root pressure changed miles away. Herds would pass through my dreams as patterns of impact without bodies. Once I would run from the house in terror because something enormous seemed to be climbing the bridge, only to learn at dawn that a distant grove had shed its outer bark all at once. The collapse had traveled through the connected roots and arrived in my bones.

Others would call this talent. I would understand it as insufficient distance.

In time I would become the crossing’s keeper.

I would clear vines from the bridge’s vents, trim parasites without cutting sensory threads, and cultivate a silver lichen whose color reveals internal heat. I would maintain three percussion posts, each grown from a different density of wood. Travelers approaching from the east would strike the first. I would answer from the house. At the second post they would state their number, their burdens, whether they carried flame, blood, flowering cuttings, or unfamiliar organisms. At the third they would wait while I asked the bridge.

Asking would not be prayer, though outsiders might mistake it for that.

I would kneel and strike five slow blows with the heel of my hand. Then two close together. Then remain still.

Usually the answer would be mechanical: a returning ripple, clear or muddied by strain. Sometimes mites would rise from the seam in a dark line, meaning defensive sap was close to the surface. Sometimes the pale threads below would contract, and I would send travelers by the three-day southern route without knowing why.

Once or twice in a lifetime, the answer would come from too far away.

The whole bridge would tighten. Not flex—tighten, as if attending. Every loose husk would roll east. Water in the house bladders would flatten against one side. Then a pulse would arrive, vast and soft, with no edge I could count. In those moments I would feel not that I stood upon an organism, but that I occupied a momentary firmness inside an immeasurable thought.

I would not tell travelers this. A keeper who turns every strain into revelation soon kills someone.

My tools would live in loops around my body. A cutting vine coiled at one hip, fed on beetle pulp and wrapped so it could not sharpen itself against my leg. Two adhesive larvae in a warm pouch at my throat. A bladder-creature for water. Thorn hooks. A mallet made from the jaw hinge of an underhunter, too valuable to use except when repairing storm tears. At my knee, a rattle containing six kinds of seed, each producing a different warning.

I would have no weapon made solely as a weapon.

If threatened, I would know where to step to make the bridge produce a bull-roar. I would know which hollow housed territorial sting-wings, which sap blister could flood the crossing in white irritant, and which rhythm might convince a burrowing hunter that a large wounded animal was struggling near the eastern anchorage. This knowledge would frighten me more than any knife. A blade ends at the length of an arm. A signal may continue beyond sight.

My gravest failure would begin with silence.

One hot afternoon the dusk drummer would stop.

Its inherited note had sounded from the same bleached trunk for longer than any living person remembered. The singer itself changed every few years, but each successor learned the cavity and produced nearly the same deep, descending call. Routes depended on it. At dusk, people descending from the eastern leaf plain turned toward the note and found the bridge before dark.

At first I would assume a hush-maker.

I would climb. Everything nearby would continue calling: mites rasping, wing colonies pulsing, sap knocking normally. No alarm chorus. No pressure drop. Only the missing drum.

I would cross to the singer’s trunk and find the animal alive but rigid, its throat swollen shut by a translucent flowering growth. The parasite would be beautiful—small white fans opening and closing with each attempted breath. I would recognize it as a domestic medicinal fungus, altered or badly cultivated.

That would mean a person had brought it.

Perhaps carelessly. Perhaps not.

I would carry the drummer back in a damp sling and spend three nights keeping it alive while the call-keepers searched for a successor. Without its sound, the eastern approaches would become uncertain. Travelers would strike the wrong trunks. One family would wander too close to a warm feeding path and lose a child. Rumor would move faster than any inquiry: sabotage, feud, punishment, a claim on the crossing.

Pressure would build around me to name the guilty household.

But the bridge would be transmitting another pattern beneath the human fear: repeated minute contractions from east to west, like something tasting along its own nerves. The fungus would not be confined to the drummer. Pale fans would appear in the root seams, then in the sensory threads below. The bridge itself would have admitted it—or failed to reject it.

To save the crossing, someone would propose cutting away the infected span.

No one cuts a bridge-root over open air.

Yet rules survive because they describe consequences, not because consequences are always avoidable. We would face a choice between letting the flowering growth pass into the mesa roots or severing a structure that carries signals beyond all mapped routes.

I would be the one expected to decide, because I had listened longest.

I would spend a night beneath the bridge, suspended among its pale threads with resin plugs in my ears. Freed from the highland roar, I would feel only what the wood carried: the clustered panic of small animals, the slow impact of sap, distant rain striking some connected crown, and the unfamiliar flutter of the fungus opening thousands of tiny bodies within the bridge.

Gradually, I would understand that the new growth was not simply consuming it.

It was changing the signal.

Each fungal fan flexed when the root trembled. Together they caught certain frequencies and smothered others. The bridge’s long messages were becoming shorter, sharper, more local. The infection was making the crossing deaf to distance.

And beneath that deafening, perhaps, protecting the larger body from something approaching through its own network.

By dawn I would refuse the cut.

Instead, I would alter the bridge’s song.

We would grow resonant gourds along the infected span, grafting them into the outer bark. We would drive the Far Herd—not onto the bridge, but close enough that its widely spaced footfalls entered the root at controlled intervals. Call-keepers would gather from three routes. Families would strike the percussion posts in relays. For nine days we would produce an immense, patterned vibration, tuned to make the fungal fans open at the wrong times and exhaust themselves.

It would be half medicine, half argument.

Many of the fans would blacken and fall. Some would remain, incorporated into the callus. The dusk drummer would die, but its successor would discover that the altered trunk no longer made the old note. The new call would rise where the other descended.

Every map-song in the region would have to change.

People would blame me for years. Some would say I had saved the bridge. Others that I had allowed the highlands to erase a human certainty. Both would be true.

In age, I would become less a traveler and more a fixed feature of the crossing. Children would be sent to me to learn counting before interpretation. I would teach them never to call a pattern a message until they could name three ways it might be accidental. I would teach them that courtesy does not require believing the landscape loves us. I would teach them that a living thing may preserve you for reasons having nothing to do with mercy.

My domestic plants would outlive my hands. The door-vine would learn the pressure of my shoulder. The cutting vine would grow blunt from lack of use. Silver lichen would creep across the scars on my forearms when I slept against the wall.

Eventually I would cease crossing even to the anchorages. Others would bring reports. A new well opening westward. Runners changing formation. Warm patches appearing under the tawny plain. A blossom tower producing scent in the wrong season. Through the bridge I would feel these events arrive before their names.

When I died, they would not place me in a flowering crown.

They would fit my body into the chamber where the seed shell and bridge-root had grown together. Wax-mites would seal the opening. Condensation would pass over me, seep into the callus, and carry what could be dissolved downward.

For several years, perhaps, the bridge’s returning tremor would acquire a faint second note.

Travelers would strike the first post. The keeper after me would answer. At the third, they would wait for permission to cross.

Some would claim they could feel my old narrow rhythm beneath the stronger pulse of the root. Others would say it was merely a cavity changing as tissue closed around bone.

The new keeper, if I taught them properly, would not choose between those explanations too quickly.

They would put their hands to the bridge.

They would count.

And only then would they begin to listen.
