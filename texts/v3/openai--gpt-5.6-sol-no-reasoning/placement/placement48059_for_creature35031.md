I would find myself neither fully in the water nor fully in the ground, but in the uncertain membrane between them: the upper finger-width of sediment, where the sea becomes mud by degrees.

I would be a long, nearly transparent worm, narrow enough to pass between grains without making a tunnel that lasts. No eyes. No armor. Little color except what shows through me: a dark thread of swallowed clay, perhaps a faint rust-red stain where iron gathers in my tissues. My body would be less an object than a pressure moving through the seabed.

I would live head-down.

The surface would be dangerous precisely because food collects there. I would extend into it after the great clouds settled, tasting the new deposit with soft, ciliated lips. I would take in everything without expectation: clay, bacterial films, fragments of plankton too small to name, the dissolved outskirts of some carcass already consumed leagues away. Most of what entered me would leave unchanged. My nourishment would be the slight difference between one mouthful of mud and the next.

Behind me I would leave small coils, each one a temporary account of my passage. For a while they might be the clearest structures on the plain. Then an amphipod would cross them, or fine particles would soften their ridges, or a current would erase them entirely. I would not know erasure as loss. A lasting mark would be useless here. My work would be to alter the ground imperceptibly: to carry surface matter downward, lift older grains upward, admit a little oxygen into a depth that had spent it, and open passages that collapse after me.

My world would be chemical before it was spatial.

Above: cold water bearing oxygen and the weak scent of descent.

Below: sulfate, iron, methane, the slow provinces of other lives.

Across: gradients finer than weather, boundaries that shift when I breathe through them.

I would know an approaching flow through my whole length. The pores around me would tighten. Water would be forced downward. The usual horizontal pull would deepen into a bodily command. If there were time, I would retreat into firmer clay and knot myself inside a chamber slicked with mucus. The bottom above me might liquefy and depart. Stones might pass where open water had been; shell fragments might strike and tumble; animals unable to descend might become part of the layer that buried them.

Afterward, I might emerge beneath a different sea floor.

There would be no familiar landmark to prove displacement. Only the sediment would have changed: coarser grains against my skin, fresher organic traces, perhaps mineral dust from a volcano whose existence I could never conceive. I would turn upward because upward was where oxygen entered, though the journey might now be many body-lengths longer. If I reached the surface, my small paired openings would appear in a plain polished blank by catastrophe.

If I did not reach it, I would become another concentration in the mud.

Yet I might also be older than a single worm.

Perhaps what lived here most truly would be the burrow itself—not one enduring tunnel, but a form repeatedly made by generations. Bodies would hatch, feed, mate rarely, and vanish; the paired holes would recur. A branching gallery would be crushed and rebuilt. The inhabitant would be a behavior passed through flesh: descend when pressure changes, rise when the new sediment settles, swallow the world in order to move through it.

Or I might be the microbial film lining that gallery, benefiting from the worm’s traffic. To the worm, the burrow would be shelter. To me, its wall would be a continent newly supplied with oxygen and dissolved food. I would spread over grains exposed by its mouthparts, divide once after a long interval, and wait through darkness that differed from other darkness only in chemistry. If the tunnel collapsed, I would not be killed so much as reassigned—to a sealed province, a dwindling resource, another mode of metabolism if my kind permitted it.

But the shape that persists is the worm: soft, anonymous, almost entirely buried.

I would not see the luminous fish pass overhead. I would feel its fin strokes arrive as delicate pressure through the sediment. I would not witness the shark, only cease moving when a heavier rhythm crossed the plain. The fall of a carcass would reach me first as impact, later as abundance: sulfides, oils, multiplying microbes, unfamiliar richness leaking outward through the pores. For a brief season I might grow quickly. In this place, quick growth would itself feel like danger.

I would possess no understanding of the upper sea. Its sunlight, storms, forests, coasts, and civilizations would come to me translated into particles. A seasonal bloom would be a sweeter layer of mud. A distant flood would be mineral grains. A fire on land might become charcoal softening beneath my mouth. An extinct creature would be a resistant fragment I bent around and left behind. History would have no events, only textures.

If a weighted line from the human margin ever touched near me, the blow would be immense and brief. A hollow sampler might take the sediment containing my body. I would rise through changes of pressure no abyssal instinct could interpret, carrying within me the black plain’s minute evidence. To those above, I might seem negligible: a pale filament among clay grains, one specimen in a dish.

But below, I would have been one of the motions by which the plain remained alive.

Not master of it. Not even witness.

A mouth passing through burial. A temporary softness in ancient matter. A creature whose home continually closed behind it, and whose whole life consisted of making the sealed world porous for a little while.
