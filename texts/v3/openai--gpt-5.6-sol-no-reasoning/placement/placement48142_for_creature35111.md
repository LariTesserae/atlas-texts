I would find myself in the city’s middle depths, where inhabited rooms give way to inspection galleries and the walls are never entirely dry.

Not in the lowest aquifers, though I would spend much of my life listening toward them. Not on the exposed towers, either, where swarms turn the daylight granular. I would live in a narrow apartment built into the side of an old quarry reservoir: three rooms behind a bronze grille, a concave roof crowded with stitchers, and a kitchen wall warm from a slurry main passing behind it. At night, runner traffic would tick around my bed. Every few years the district would tilt almost imperceptibly as mineral deposits thickened under its eastern foundations.

I would be human, or near enough that the distinction mattered only to anatomists. Compact, broad-footed, mineral-scarred. But my profession would require me to become less singular than that.

I would be a **continuance listener**.

The official title would be something longer: examiner of concealed occupations and interrupted flows. People would call us listeners because of the rods we carry—hollow lengths of bronze, iron, and fired ceramic, each responsive to a different register. We set them against walls, cisterns, bridge piers, pavement, even one another’s teeth. Through them we hear what cannot be surveyed directly: runners changing direction inside a conduit, stitchers sealing against contaminated rain, a glider colony shifting its grip before a beam fails, water forcing a new seam through limestone.

Courts employ listeners to establish whether a place is empty.

Usually, it is not.

A landlord may claim that a sealed wall channel has been abandoned and can safely be filled. I arrive before dawn, when street vibration is lowest. I press three rods into the masonry and wait. At first there is only seepage and the distant churn of a pump. Then a faint staggered clicking begins—not ordinary runner traffic, but brood movement. The channel is occupied. Perhaps it has been occupied for eighty years. The renovation must bend around it.

A hydraulic office may want to reopen an ancient spillway. The plans show bare stone. I descend with a veterinary surveyor and find the spillway lined with a translucent organism no present registry recognizes. It retracts from lamplight and makes the water slightly warm. We cannot tell whether it is one body or a colony, whether it filters poison or produces it, whether removing it would save the district or sterilize the wells beneath three others.

So I listen. I take residue. I return after rain, after discharge, after a swarm settlement, after the nearest underback call. I compare the intervals. Eventually I write the most powerful sentence available to my profession:

**Occupation cannot be excluded.**

That sentence can stop a demolition, postpone a sluice opening, or force a tramline to curve through three additional streets. It can also condemn a neighborhood if the concealed life inside it has already blocked the water. Listeners are not defenders of animals against people. We are defenders of passage against certainty.

My bell would make two low ceramic notes with a long space between them. It would announce that I might stop without warning and that those behind me should not crowd close. Children would imitate it sarcastically when I stood too long with my hand against a fountain.

I would have begun as one of those children.

In the school court, I would have built channels that were too orderly: straight cuts, equal gradients, perfect gates. Once the shell-creatures entered, they would settle at the narrowest junction and flood everything. Again I would rebuild, leaving what seemed like wasteful breadth. Again the channels would fail somewhere I had not anticipated. My instructor would turn the ceramic city over and show me the dampness spreading through its underside.

“You designed only the visible water,” she would say.

That would remain with me.

As an apprentice I would learn that every structure has several bodies. There is the body on the plan: measured walls, rated beams, pipe diameters. There is the body produced by weather: cracks, calcite, corroded iron, the slow swelling of saturated stone. There is the body made by occupation: nests, traffic polish, feeding scars, warm pockets, generations of shells. And beneath those is the conjectural body—the structure as experienced by creatures that sense pressure, salt, or vibration instead of doors.

My task would be to make those bodies briefly legible to one another.

It would not make me wise. It would make me hesitant.

I would know too many stories of confident interventions. A conduit poisoned to remove biting scavengers, followed seven years later by a filter collapse in a maternity cistern. A tower stripped of swarms because their weight threatened the cornice, after which winter contraction split the suddenly cold masonry. A herd corridor narrowed by less than a meter, causing no difficulty until a new generation of larger calves reached it two decades later and panicked above a buried reservoir.

People would accuse my kind of making inaction sound virtuous. Sometimes they would be right. There are listeners who hear claims everywhere because no one is blamed for a disaster prevented by delay. Meanwhile roofs fail, pipes choke, and families live in condemned rooms while officials request another season of records.

I would not want to become one of those.

The case that defined my life would begin in an old western district where water had started emerging warm from domestic walls. At first it would seem beneficial. Heating costs would fall. Warming colonies would flourish behind stoves. Gliders would begin wintering under bridges they had previously abandoned.

Then the runners would leave.

Not die—leave. Their wall channels would empty from below upward. Houses would become silent in bands advancing across the district. Soon afterward, black deposits would appear around iron fittings, soft enough to wipe away, smelling faintly of sour gas.

The hydraulic office would suspect industrial leakage. Pump engineers would blame a failed ceramic liner. Residents would insist that an underback was approaching, though the pressure records showed no recognized migration.

I would be sent because the silence had a shape.

For months I would map it. I would sleep in borrowed rooms, waking whenever cups touched their saucers. I would lower rods into abandoned cistern mouths. I would listen from tram tunnels and herd crossings. The warm zone would continue to widen, but unevenly, always arriving first along ancient masonry—causeways, buried foundations, channels built for creatures no longer known.

Eventually I would understand that the heat was not moving through the pipes.

Something beneath the district was using the old city as a sensory organ.

Whether it was an underback, an aquifer consortium, or a condition created by both would remain impossible to say. But it would be testing routes: sending pressure and chemistry through long-mineralized structures, withdrawing where iron concentrations were high, returning where porous shell aggregate carried the signal cleanly.

The district stood over a forgotten passage.

Opening it would require flooding several inhabited blocks and dismantling a market older than the present government. Refusing it might divert the deep movement into the main feed risers. There would be hearings. Residents would strike their bells against the court doors. Merchants would bring ledgers proving uninterrupted occupation. Engineers would display fracture maps. The aquifer workers would say little, which would frighten everyone.

I would testify that the buried route was active.

A magistrate would ask, “Active in what sense?”

I would answer, “In the sense that something is asking whether it remains possible.”

That phrase would follow me afterward. Some would quote it reverently. Others would paint it on walls as evidence of bureaucratic madness.

The city would not evacuate the whole district. Cities rarely make the clean choice available in retrospect. Instead, we would create a partial passage: abandon the lowest streets, flood the cellars, strip iron from the old causeway, install breakable ceramic barriers, and paint nutrient gradients into the drowned galleries. Families would move upward into buildings already crowded with swarms. The market would survive, but on suspended floors. For eleven years nothing decisive would happen.

Then one night every fountain in the district would draw inward.

The seepage would stop. Stitchers would close. Pipes would knock in descending order from west to east. People would wake and stand in their rooms, not speaking, while my two-note bell moved down the galleries with dozens of others.

Something would pass far below.

We would never see it. The flooded streets would bulge upward. Calcite curtains would fracture inside sealed buildings. Warm air would issue from wells in long breaths. For three days the district would rise and settle as if resting on muscle. Then water would return through the walls, colder and clearer than before.

The passage would remain open.

That success would not belong to me, though the city might try to make it so. It would belong to abandoned masonry, frightened residents, stubborn engineers, sacrificial buildings, displaced colonies, and a creature—or geological society of creatures—whose intentions no one knew. My contribution would have been smaller: I had noticed that silence was moving, and persuaded others not to fill it.

Afterward I would be given authority, which would suit me less than fieldwork. My hands would soften away from wet stone. Younger listeners would bring me recordings, residue trays, casts of unfamiliar attachments. I would sign conditional orders extending beyond my life:

*The lower galleries may be reopened after the second complete herd circuit, provided warm seepage remains west of the old causeway and runner traffic has returned continuously for nine seasons.*

I would dislike writing such sentences. I would know how much uncertainty they concealed. But that concealment would also be a kind of civic honesty: no order here could be final, only timed against other motions.

In old age I would begin losing my ordinary hearing. Speech would blur first. Wing noise would become a dry blank pressure. Bells would lose their upper notes.

Yet I might still feel the city through my palms and teeth.

I would sit beside my household cistern with a rod against its glaze, detecting the faint movement of filter bodies inside. I would recognize the freight lifts through the floor, the evening swarm settling on the roof, the cushion-foot herd several streets away. Once or twice a year, something deeper would pass into my bones: a call so low it seemed not to arrive but to reveal that it had always been present.

When I died, my body would go into a digestion well serving the district I had helped flood. I would leave instructions that the recovered salts not be made into a memorial plaque. They would be mixed into porous repair ceramic and used wherever the next inspection found a need—perhaps in a culvert lip, a runner channel, or a stair descending into water.

Years later, an apprentice might place a rod against that patch and hear traffic behind it. They would not know me. That would be proper.

I would have entered circulation—not as a soul, perhaps, and not as a symbol, but as a small alteration in the city’s permeability.

A little more passage.

A little less certainty.

A place where water, finding stone, could continue.
