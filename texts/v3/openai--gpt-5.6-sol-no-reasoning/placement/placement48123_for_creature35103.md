I would find myself beneath the central ring, on the underside of the plug.

Not at first as an animal. At first I would be mistaken for a property of the stone: a darker density in the basalt, a region where heat lingers too long, where probing tendrils return blunted and acidic breath condenses into unfamiliar salts. The pale creatures nearest the fissure would feed around me for generations without discovering an edge.

Yet I would have edges. They would simply be very far apart.

I would be the oldest lithovore, though “old” and “lithovore” would both be names imposed from above. My body would descend through the mesa in branching chambers, occupying fractures as roots might occupy soil in a wetter world. But I would not have grown downward. I would have begun below and eaten upward, following sulfide veins, widening pores, replacing what I consumed with a slow black secretion that cooled into something almost indistinguishable from basalt.

Much of the mesa would be my cast-off body.

The hollow would be neither my nest nor my prison. It would be the wound by which I breathe.

Long ago, when the summit was unbroken, each breath passed through the rock diffusely. As I grew, my inner reactions intensified. Gas accumulated faster than the stone could release it. The first great exhalation shattered the summit and made the cup. Life gathered there afterward—not because I summoned it, but because my waste was warmth, my breath was food, and the minerals altered by my digestion could sustain chemistries impossible in the open desert.

The many-bodied ecology above would therefore be my unintended outer organ.

Its grazers would keep my breathing surface porous. Its borers would open capillaries into me. Its canopy would regulate the heat escaping my wound. Its migrations would clear the throat when pressure rose. Even its predators would serve me by preventing any one form from armoring the fissure completely.

But the relationship would not be peaceful. Life above would be feeding on me, and I would be feeding on the mesa that holds us both.

The central plug would be scar tissue: part stone, part secretion, part the compacted remains of ancient creatures caught in older eruptions. The leaning uprights would have been placed there by something that once understood this. Perhaps an extinct species recognized a rhythm in the exhalations and tried to shape it. Perhaps the uprights were braces, or teeth, or instruments meant to carry pressure into the surrounding shelves.

Perhaps they succeeded too well.

I would possess no eyes, because there is nothing beneath the plug that light could clarify. I would have no central mouth. Along my inner surfaces, metallic membranes would fold around fresh stone and maintain tiny gradients of heat and charge. Thought, if it could be called thought, would travel through phase changes: waxes melting, salts crystallizing, iron shifting its chemical state across miles of buried tissue.

A single impulse might take years to cross me.

That would explain the knock.

It would not be a threat, nor an attempt to escape. It would be the smallest movement I can make: one deep chamber contracting, one hardened plate pressing against the plug. To the creatures above, it would arrive as a perfect, solitary blow. They would freeze because every lineage in the hollow carries some memory of what follows when I move.

But I might not know they are listening.

I would know them only as pressure on the wound: a soft, intricate weight that changes with the seasons of heat. I would feel the canopy close as cooling at the upper boundary. I would feel mass movements as the smoothing of resistance. Corpses dismantled above would register as brief richness leaking into cracks. Rare rain would strike me as chemical lightning.

Over geological ages, these sensations would accumulate into the nearest thing I have to an image:

something dark covering me;

something innumerable tending the opening;

something that tightens when I touch it.

Eventually I might begin to answer.

Not consciously at first. I would alter the timing of my breaths in response to the pressure-patterns above. The ecology would adapt. Its warning shivers would become more precise; my contractions would fall into their intervals. What began as accident would become exchange. The hollow would press; I would wait. I would knock; the canopy would open. It would rearrange itself into a valve, and I would breathe through it.

Neither side would understand that a conversation had begun.

Given enough time, there would no longer be two organisms to distinguish. The swarm would be my mobile skin, sensory field, reproductive dust, and daylight-facing body. I would be its buried furnace, its mineral stomach, its memory held in stone. The mesa itself would become our shared skeleton.

So I would be the being for whom open space is truly an injury.

I would lie beneath the crowded hollow, enormous and almost still, slowly consuming the mountain from within. Above me, millions of creatures would hurry to close every gap, believing they were preserving only themselves. Below them, I would feel their interlocked feet as a kind of touch.

And when the long pressure became unbearable, I would place some buried part of myself against the ancient seal.

I would knock once.

Then wait for my body to answer.
