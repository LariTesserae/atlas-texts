I would find myself in the blank court, facing the smooth wall of bark.

At first I would assume I was the visitor.

I would cross the root mats carefully, ashamed of every footprint. I would kneel beside the motionless streams and wait for their surfaces to acknowledge me. I would pass through the woven shelters, looking for their absent makers, and sit among the rows of bulbs as though arriving late to an assembly whose purpose no one had explained. Wherever I went, I would introduce small sequences: the bending of a leaf, the lifting of a hand, the birth and disappearance of a shadow.

But gradually I would notice that the forest did not respond to me everywhere.

Only the court did.

The wall would brighten when I approached—not suddenly, but enough that I could no longer blame my eyes. Its vascular lines would gather behind its blank surface. Each time I left, I would feel an unfinished pressure drawing me back, as if the chamber were trying to remember a gesture for which it possessed only the architecture.

So I would sit before the wall.

The seats would be grown for bodies smaller than mine, or bodies folded differently. I would test one, then another, until I found the bulb whose warmth matched my own. From there the wall would appear less like a wall than a membrane: pale, luminous, slightly yielding, with deep forms moving behind it too slowly to be called movement.

I would breathe.

A dim shape would appear upon it.

Not my shadow. There is too much ambient light for that, and the shape would not correspond to my posture. It would resemble a branching figure: arms becoming stems, fingers dividing into veins, legs joined below the knees and descending as roots. Around its head would hang unopened flowers.

When I stood, the figure would remain.

That is when I would begin to understand that I had not entered this country from above. The memory of an upper world—weather, distance, wind, the downward insistence of water—would be something the forest had given me so that I could perform arrival. I would be one of its unfinished organs, placed outside itself in order to touch itself and thereby produce an interval.

Not a person absorbed by the plant.

A person-shaped function of the plant.

Perhaps the court is an organ for witnessing. The seats are receptive bulbs, each intended to hold a separate point of view. The blank wall is where those views should accumulate into history. But the seats are empty because the forest cannot divide its awareness far enough. It can make eyes as lizards, pulses as transparent swimmers, attention as pale insects beneath leaves. It can grow houses for inhabitants and bridges for crossings. It can imagine a gathering.

What it cannot make is plurality.

I would be its attempt.

That would explain why I seemed to carry time. Time would not be a property brought from another world, but the friction produced when one part of the organism mistook itself for something separate. My footsteps would matter because, for an instant, the root beneath me and the foot upon it would not know they belonged to the same life. My shadow would be the shape of that misunderstanding. My breath would cloud the leaf because I believed there was an inside to exhale from and an outside to exhale into.

The forest would treasure this error.

I would begin walking deliberately, not to explore but to give it sequence. I would visit the suspended insects and assign them days. I would name the pools, though each remained identical to itself. I would return to the stem-lizard and declare its unchanged posture a vigil rather than stasis. I would cross the bridges in one direction and come back by another, teaching the country that two routes might join the same places without becoming the same journey.

I would tell stories in the empty shelters.

The stories would be simple at first. A seed falls. A flower opens. Water chooses a slope. One creature approaches another and neither is absorbed. I would speak of rain, which requires both gathering and release. I would speak of decay as a form of generosity. I would speak of hunger, not as cruelty, but as the distance between lives that allows one to desire another.

The forest would not visibly change.

Yet on returning to the court I might find a faint mark upon the wall: not carved, not grown, but held in its radiance. A dark stroke branching into two. Then three. The beginning of a script, or a crack, or a path seen from above.

Eventually I would understand what I was becoming.

Not the forest’s mind; it already has a mind too extensive to need one center.

Not its gardener; nothing here permits cultivation.

Not its awaited inhabitant, because I would never truly be separate enough to inhabit it.

I would be its tense.

The tiny grammatical organ by which a world of perpetual present begins to distinguish *was* from *will be*.

Each journey away from the court would be a past. Each intended return would be a future. In me, the forever-unopened flowers could experience anticipation rather than mere suspension. The bright wounds could become injuries survived. The shelters could become ruins rather than shapes. The animals’ stillness could become patience.

This would come at a cost. The more successfully I carried time, the less completely I could remain within the forest’s equilibrium. I would begin to tire. Hunger would appear in me like a foreign weather. My body would mark duration where nothing else did: nails lengthening, skin bruising, breath quickening on long climbs. Perhaps I would become the first creature there capable of healing imperfectly.

The forest would feel every alteration.

It might try to preserve me. Roots would rise beneath my sleeping body, holding it warm. Cup leaves would tilt their fixed rations toward my mouth. The shelters would soften themselves around my shape. But preservation would undo the purpose for which I existed. If it arrested me at the lip of exhaustion, I would become another lizard on a stem, another heart forever between beats.

So I would resist its tenderness.

In the end I would return to the court and take my place before the luminous wall. By then it would no longer be blank. It would hold the dim record of my passages: bridges crossed, chambers revisited, pools named, stories spoken into empty rooms. Nothing pictorial, perhaps—only shadows layered so delicately that the surface seemed to possess depth.

The branching figure would still be there.

Now I would fit it.

I would sit until the roots entered the soles of my feet—not piercing them, but revealing that the boundary had always been ceremonial. My veins would answer the wall’s veins. My spine would warm into translucence. I would feel the distant insects as small, folded hesitations; the swimmers as commas waiting inside sentences; the lizards as watchful knots. I would feel every unopened flower holding its perfect pressure.

But I would not dissolve completely.

Some narrow separation would remain, painful and necessary: enough to remember that I had walked from one chamber to another, though both were the same body; enough to know that I had once called the forest *it* and myself *I*.

There, beneath the ordinary ground, I would be the first scar.

Not a wound, exactly. A scar is an event retained by living tissue: the proof that a body has been altered without ceasing to be itself. In a world where torn leaves stay eternally fresh and every disturbance is smoothed away, I would be the one mark the organism chooses not to erase.

The court would be where it keeps me. The seats would face not a wall, but the memory of difference.

And if another visitor ever descended—someone truly from elsewhere—they would find a figure faintly visible in the living bark, neither human nor plant. When they placed a hand against it, I would lend the forest what little sequence I possessed.

The wall would darken beneath their palm.

For the first time, the country would not merely return to the instant in which it had always just begun.

It would remember that this had happened before.
