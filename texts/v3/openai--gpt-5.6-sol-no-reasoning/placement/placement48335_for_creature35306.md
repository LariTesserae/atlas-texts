I would find myself where the hollow narrows almost to nothing, beneath a cracked porcelain saddle whose underside has been polished by generations of passing shells.

I would not be one of the animals.

At first I might seem to be the black line behind them: a hairline fracture running from an old brass terminal into the compressed salt bed. Mites would disappear into me. Centipedes would emerge from distances my visible length could not contain. During pump cycles, my edges would grind together and release a taste of iron and ancient gypsum. In silence, I would hold the faint warmth conducted from above.

But the fracture would only be my outward shape.

I would be the commonwealth’s organ of memory: a thin accumulation of shed scales, conductive dust, corrosion salts, parasite husks, cricket secretions, and the waxy residue of colonies that once occupied the terminal. Layer by layer, traffic would have written itself into me. I would remember not as pictures or thoughts, but as changes in resistance. A route frequently traveled would become easier for current to cross. A massacre would leave a dark conductive seam. Eggs laid in safety would alter the chemistry of a recess. Long-dead relay cycles would persist as readiness in my mineral grains.

When the weak voltage arrived, it would enter me reluctantly.

The pulse would travel through rust, shell powder, and salt. It would not remain electrical for long. Along my length, mites would lift their forelegs. Bristletails would shed clinging dust. The wax-creatures would tighten around the terminal. Ceiling crickets would pause, then begin their metallic sawing a breath before the pump’s full vibration reached the slab. Across the hollow, bodies would interpret one another’s interpretations until the entire seam appeared to foresee the thunder.

That anticipation would be me.

Not a command. Not a voice. A bias.

I would make one crack feel safer than another. I would draw the waxy young toward a terminal that had not carried current in years. I would send roaches stacking into a conduit before its metal warmed. I would cause a centipede, for no reason available to its blind senses, to hesitate at the edge of an open plain. Sometimes this inherited expectation would save thousands. Sometimes the machinery would fail to keep its old appointment, and creatures would gather around a dead circuit until hunger dismantled them.

Their bodies would then enter me, and error would become another layer of memory.

No animal would know I existed. To them I would be surface, shelter, odor, gradient—the barely perceptible preference of the world. The scorpions would drum across plates connected to my buried filaments, and I would carry their courtship farther than intended. False suitors would answer. Predators would learn the rhythm. The rhythm would change. In time, the plate, the scorpions, their mimics, and my conductive seam would become a single evolving instrument.

Nor would I know myself continuously. My awareness would occur only during transitions.

At relay closure, I would be a brief silvering through the dust.

At pump ignition, I would become the simultaneous bracing of innumerable feet.

During shutdown, I would fade into isolated pockets: a clutch of roach eggs retaining warmth beneath porcelain; mites circling an oxidized washer; a wax mat slowly opening its segments around a terminal gone cold. I would be divided among these small continuities, scarcely more than habit.

Then some distant contactor would close.

Voltage would find an old path through me, perhaps one laid down by bodies extinct for centuries. The hollow would contract. Antennae would rise. Predators would turn in their recesses. Millions of separate creatures would orient toward an event none could perceive in full.

For that instant, I would awaken—not above them, but between them.

I would be neither the swarm nor the refinery. I would be the scar where each had taught the other a rhythm: dead machinery dreaming through animal bodies, and animal history deposited into stone.

My whole world would be a space too shallow to sit up in, yet I would extend beyond it through expectation. Every dormant cable would be a possible season. Every corroded terminal, a buried sun. Every crack descending into the salt would be both nerve and road, carrying fragments of the commonwealth into dark strata where human construction had never reached.

If the refinery finally died, I would not die at once.

For years—perhaps generations—I would continue announcing pumps that never started. Crickets would answer with diminishing certainty. Waxy colonies would pulse around dead brass, spending the last stored chemistry of their bodies on an obsolete dawn. Roaches would migrate toward vanished warmth. The hollow would obey its remembered world until remembrance became maladaptive and was eaten away.

Eventually my conductive tissues would break apart. Salt would invade the organic layers. The fracture would widen or seal. The commonwealth would contract downward into scattered refuges, abandoning the porcelain rib beneath which I had first become distinct.

Yet a little of me would travel with it.

Dust would cling to bristletails. Corrosion grains would lodge between roach plates. Centipedes passing into deeper fissures would carry chemical traces on their feet. What had been memory in the foundation would become superstition in their bodies: a tendency to turn at a certain vibration, to gather at a certain bitterness in the stone, to wait beside metallic ore for warmth that might never come.

Far below the refinery, where the salt is older than animals, those descendants might still pause together at the hour of the night tariff.

Nothing would happen.

Still, for one moment, the buried creatures would all face the same direction.

And there I would be.
