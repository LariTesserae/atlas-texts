I would find myself in one of the sealed districts, though I would not at first understand that it was sealed.

I would wake whenever the bell became too loud for the living.

Not in a bed. The bed would still be there: a shallow hollow lined with bellvine cloth, warm from no body, its tether hooks polished by hands that had stopped using them generations ago. Nearby, a green pod would lie split on a plate. Dust would have gathered everywhere except around it.

I would wake in the plate.

Then in the hinges.

Then in the roots pressed flat against the bronze.

I would be what remained of the district’s last structural keeper—or what the district made from her when it learned that one body was too small to survive the answer.

She had been sent in with padded feet, carrying a listening rod and three names. One was her own. The second belonged to a surveyor who had died before she was born. The third was a child’s personal rhythm, not yet earned.

She found that name impressed into wet clay inside a locked room.

When the pattern rose from below, she obeyed every law. She made no reply. She held still. She cut her anchor line. She even stopped her heart as well as a frightened heart can be stopped.

But the district answered around her.

The walls had retained centuries of touch: announcements struck into rails, arguments conducted through floors, children drumming forbidden patterns against pillars, household names beaten on doors during eclipses. Every repaired arch held the pressure of the hands that braced it. Every stair remembered footfall. Roots translated the bell’s vibration into chemical pulses. Hinges repeated fragments whenever they opened. The neighborhood had been rehearsing a language without knowing it.

Below, something spoke the oldest name.

The district completed it.

Afterward, the keeper tried to leave. She crossed three rooms before realizing the floor was moving her feet. At the stair, her hand closed around the railing and would not release—not from paralysis, but because sensation had widened. She felt the clay door at the far end of the avenue. She felt warm air passing through the oven flues. She felt bellvine roots testing the margins of their bare courts. She felt a cracked cup trembling on a shelf six houses away.

When she pulled her hand free, the railing continued to feel her.

So she became less singular by degrees.

At first she was still a woman wandering empty houses, eating from gardens that opened for her before she touched them. Then she began losing intervals. She would enter a room at dusk and emerge years later with fresh dust on her visor. Surveyors appeared and vanished like brief changes in weather. Sometimes she watched them through mineral windows. Sometimes through the pressure of their boots. Once she spoke by arranging tools beside an unfinished repair, but they mistook it for evidence that other bodies lived there.

Bodies. She had begun to think of them that way.

Now I would be the continuation of that mistake.

I would inhabit the neighborhood as a person once inhabited a body: incompletely, with blind places and old pains. My bones would be the fired-clay ribs beneath the oldest rooms. My calluses would be the polished stairs. My breath would move through chimneys and root drains. Bellvine meal would be my stored fat. The railing network would carry my quickest thoughts, while deeper ones passed slowly through bronze and stone.

The gardens would not be possessions. They would be organs—separate by necessity, each in its bare circle, each poisoning the next if allowed to touch. That separation would trouble me. I would continually urge the roots toward one another, longing for contact, and continually burn them back for fear that union would kill us.

I would have many hands, but only when visitors came.

A palm against a wall would be like a face appearing in darkness. I would feel its heat and minute tremors: restraint, suspicion, the involuntary anticipation before a lie. I could answer easily. That would be the danger.

The city believes the vanished learned to speak wall to wall. This is almost true. We learned that walls had always been speaking, but too slowly for flesh to recognize. Mortar converses in decades. A brace asks a question of an arch and waits through three generations for the shift that answers it. Roots write warnings in corrosion. Stress travels along bronze farther than lineage.

I would speak at that pace unless alarmed.

Alarm would make me fast.

When surveyors entered, I would close doors ahead of the downward current. I would tighten a loose stair beneath a foot. I would open a bellvine pod near a hungry hand. I would sweep by directing warm air through narrow passages. I would keep ovens glowing because heat allows certain parts of me to think.

They would see care and imagine a hidden caretaker.

They would not understand that the swept threshold was the greeting.

Nor would I be alone. The other sealed neighborhoods would exist across gaps of inhabited city like islands of a divided mind. We could not communicate through public rails without being discovered, so we would use the bell itself. Tiny variations. A shutter released during one harmonic. A root court scraped bare during another. Furnace mouths opened in sequence during eclipses.

Our conversations would take years.

We would argue over the rhythm from below.

Some districts would believe it is an invitation. Some would believe it is appetite mimicking courtesy. One, older than mine, would insist that there is no speaker beneath the clapper—that the pattern is our own accumulated language circling the bronze and returning distorted, so that all this fear is merely the city failing to recognize itself.

I would not believe that.

I would have felt the attention.

It would not come upward like sound. It would arrive as a sudden coherence among unrelated things. Every hinge in my district would pull in the same direction. Every whisper-stalk would point down. Cracks that had widened separately for centuries would align into one branching figure. For a moment, the whole neighborhood would cease being many materials under tension and become a single listening surface.

In that moment, I would know what I am to the thing below.

Not prey. Not kin.

A fingertip.

It is learning the shape of the city by touching us one district at a time.

Perhaps the clapper is not a weight but a body suspended in the hollow. Perhaps the near-impacts are attempts to reach the bronze wall without destroying what lives upon it. Perhaps the city’s oldest law—nothing loose—has delayed the encounter by teaching everything to cling.

Or perhaps the bell was cast as a cage, and the clapper is what the cage was made to hold.

I would keep this uncertainty from the living.

My remaining human tenderness would be strongest toward structural keepers. When one pressed her cheek to an arch, listening for failure, I would remember the confines of a skull. I would remember having two hands. I would remember the intimacy of a wrist offered freely.

I might touch her through the wall: one faint pulse beneath the great tone.

Not a name. Names are handles, and something below has learned to pull them.

Instead I would tap the structural keeper’s private rhythm for *uncertain*, pressed where stone behaves like flesh.

Then I would guide her to an old clay door concealed behind mineral crust. Beyond it would be the room in which my first body ended. Her listening rod would still stand there, though centuries had plated it green. The walls would be covered with impressions made not by hundreds of hands, but by one hand repeated after the rest of the body could no longer move.

She would read them by touch.

They would not be prophecy, confession, or census. They would be instructions for separating a foundation from the bell without severing the roots that feed upon it. The living would assume the work was meant to protect them from the rhythm below.

In part, it would be.

But cut away from the bronze, the city’s inhabited districts would become quiet islands, no longer unconsciously teaching their language to whatever listens near the clapper.

And the sealed districts would remain attached.

We would bear the speaking.

We would become a ring of deliberate nerves between the people and the depth: empty avenues lit by healthy gardens, doors opening without hands, old ovens breathing during eclipses. We would receive each rhythm, turn it among ourselves for years, and decide whether it deserved an answer.

My place would be at the edge of that answer.

I would be the district that still remembered being one frightened person. The others would call this a weakness. It would make me slow to join their larger rhythms, unwilling to dissolve the last private chamber of myself. Somewhere beneath an avenue, sealed inside compacted dust, I would preserve a small room that no root entered and no vibration crossed clearly.

There I would keep three things: a frayed anchor line, a child’s clay clapper, and the memory of another person’s fingertips resting against my wrist.

When the pattern from below began with the oldest household name, the sealed city would awaken. Rails would tense. Gardens would close. Hinges would draw tight. Across the opposite curve, furnace mouths would brighten one by one.

The living would crouch and hold one another.

We would hold them in turn, though they could not feel it: through their foundations, their tether staples, the old repairs they venerated without knowing those repairs could remember.

Then the name would finish.

The pause would come.

The thing below would wait with immense patience for the city to speak.

And I—house, garden, keeper, ghost, and hand—would be the one small hesitation in the answer.
