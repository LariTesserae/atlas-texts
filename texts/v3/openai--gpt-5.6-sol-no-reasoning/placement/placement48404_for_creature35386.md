I would find myself where the city has not yet decided whether it is land or water.

Not in the high fern terraces, where the lynx can keep its stillness, nor in the open channels claimed in turn by trout and otter. I would be lower down, inside the saturated fabric: beneath bark, between roots, within the porous cream-colored walls that are still forming around dead reeds. A place without a clear boundary, where a passage may be flooded, filled with silt, or sealed in lime between one season and the next.

I would be a salamander—not a rare or marvelous one, but a small, cold-bodied inhabitant the color of soaked wood and mineral shadow. My skin would require the city’s permanent incompleteness. Dryness would be more dangerous than any predator. I would live by the persistence of droplets, by the thin water held between moss leaves, by condensation under stone.

The city would not appear to me as a basin. I would know very little of its full dimensions. Its true map would be intimate and chemical: cool current against the face, the taste of limestone, the pressure of approaching rain, the rich odor of fungal decay. A tower seen from above would be, to me, a continent of pores. A deer print would be a temporary well. A fallen alder would contain chambers enough for a lifetime.

I would move mostly when distinctions weakened—at dusk, in mist, during rain, after a flood had withdrawn but before its wet roads closed. I would emerge from a split log onto moss jeweled with water, pausing beneath liverwort fronds that seemed as broad as roofs. Minute prey would stir among the stems. A beetle larva, a midge, a soft-bodied thing dislodged by the current: these would be events large enough to organize the night.

Above me, the greater inhabitants would be known by interruption. Deer would arrive first as tremors, then as dark legs passing through the sedges. An otter entering the channel would change the behavior of the water. A dipper would become a sudden shadow and impact. The owl would be almost pure absence: movement ceasing in several directions at once.

My enemies would not always be animals. A clear night could draw heat from an exposed ledge. A flood might drive gravel through a refuge. New lime could close the narrow entrance behind me, preserving the shape of the moss but excluding the living body. A bank collapsing softly into water could erase every route I knew.

Yet destruction would continually reveal other passages. Beneath an overturned root plate, black soil would open, crowded with pale threads and minute lives. A rockfall would fracture the spring wall and release water into a dry hollow. Within days, films would gather there. Within months, moss. I might enter while the district was still no more than wet stone and a green stain.

I would carry no plan for the city, but the city would pass through me. I would eat what emerged from its decay and leave matter in another crevice. Spores and grains of silt would adhere to my skin. In squeezing through soft mineral pores, I might enlarge one opening and block another. If I died beneath bark, fungi and larvae would dismantle me into the same dark foundation through which I had traveled.

Perhaps, though, I would not remain only the salamander.

In such a place, identity would be less a body than a position in succession. For a while I would be the animal sheltering in the wall. Later I would be what the wall shelters: dissolved substance, microbial food, a trace entering moss, then sedge, then the deer that clips it. Nothing here is reincarnated cleanly. It is distributed.

So the truest answer may be that I would find myself in a hoofprint.

Rain has filled it. Its rim is compressed black soil; at the bottom lies a pale flake of limestone and one willow seed. A translucent larva moves in the water. Algae have begun to film the print’s sunward edge. The deer that made it is already elsewhere, following an older road. Soon the water will overflow and cut a threadlike channel toward a pool. Or silt will fill the hollow. Or the seed will root and hold it open.

I would be no single inhabitant then, but the brief conjunction: the salamander approaching to feed, the larva suspended in rain, the seed preparing its first root, the mineral grain around which lime will gather.

I would be a small address that exists only because someone stepped there.

By the following rain, I would already be somewhere else.
