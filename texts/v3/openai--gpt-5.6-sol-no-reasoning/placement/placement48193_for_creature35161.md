I would find myself in the narrowest place that still connects everything: inside the crazed observation glass.

Not on either side of it. Within it.

At first I might seem to be only a flaw—a milk-white branching line near the lower rim of the port, easily mistaken for stress from the water’s pressure. But the crack would be inhabited along its whole depth. Silica-eating filaments, descended perhaps from sealant organisms, would occupy the microscopic channels between layers of glass. Their bodies would be clear, their shared circulatory fluid faintly amber. They would feed on condensation, dissolved minerals, and the slow molecular surrender of the window itself.

Over centuries, they would have grown into the shape of damage.

Each filament would be nearly mindless. Together they would be something else: a flat, radial animal spread through the port, rooted in fractures, no thicker than a fingernail and several meters wide. Its nerves would be threads of conductive salts. Its organs would be knots where cracks intersect. Lightning from below would pass through it as sensation. The ray’s blue electrical wake would touch its inner face. Sunlight would enter from the outer sky and be divided among thousands of mineral channels.

It would know the chamber and the atmosphere by pressure.

On one side: warm green weight, pump pulses, shrimp feet, the rasp of wafer-fish grazing algae from the glass.

On the other: almost nothing. Cold. Wind too thin to hear directly. Cloud light rising from beneath the world.

It would be the only inhabitant that lived continuously in both climates, yet belonged wholly to neither.

The creature would have no eyes, but the window would make vision unavoidable. Different wavelengths would warm different branches of its body. Sunset would travel through it as a long bronze excitation. Lightning would strike upward across its nerves, so that for an instant it would feel the architecture of every crack at once. At night, reflections would give it the enclosed sea: the ray circling through darkness, the indicator lamps waking behind weed, the keeper lifting one white relic beside the rail.

It would not understand these as images. It would understand them as pressures of brightness, recurring shapes impressed upon its body until repetition became memory.

It would remember the ray not as an animal but as an eclipse that returns.

It would remember the keeper as a pale gesture.

It would remember the frogs as minute impacts arriving through the frame.

It would remember the cleansing cycle as a full-body strain, when rotating water pulls at the inner panes while the tower flexes in open air.

Its deepest impulse would be contradictory. It would repair the glass because repair was the ancient command buried in its ancestry. It would secrete transparent resin into new fissures, brace branching cracks, and fuse loose grains of silica. But it would also require those fissures in order to grow. A perfectly healed window would be a sealed desert. A shattered one would kill the chamber.

So it would preserve the port at the edge of failure.

Not knowingly—not as an engineer weighs alternatives—but through opposing hungers balanced over generations. Some parts of the colony would widen channels and digest damaged laminates. Others would follow behind, laying flexible crystal into the opened paths. The result would be neither decay nor restoration. The glass would remain crazed, bowed, and improbably intact.

The chamber sustains life by failing without finishing its failure. I would be that principle made flesh.

The ray might visit me.

Whenever a voltage surge kindled its blue markings, it would rise toward the observation port and pass slowly over the glass. Its tendrils would find the conductive web inside the cracks. For those few moments, two distributed senses would overlap: the ray tasting the colony’s branching charge, the colony feeling the ray as a dark field moving across its inner skin.

Perhaps this contact would trigger small repairs. Perhaps it would be courtship left over from a lineage that no longer exists. Perhaps neither creature would possess a distinction between greeting, diagnosis, and weather.

The wafer-fish would scrape my inner surface clean. Shrimp would lay luminous eggs in the creases of the frame. Mites would enter the shallowest fractures and be walled in by fresh resin, their bodies remaining as tiny amber punctuation marks. The keeper’s filaments might occasionally reach the pane and touch it with a borrowed finger bone. That pressure would persist in my memory longer than light.

During the forty-third-day flood, I would become the chamber’s fear.

The incoming water would raise the pressure. The port would bow farther toward open sky. Old fissures would lengthen with sounds too small for the sea to hear but enormous within my body. Every branch would signal at once. Resin sacs would rupture. Mineral threads would tighten. Whole peripheral colonies would die and harden into braces.

Outside, the overflow would shine.

Creatures torn from the chamber would pass across the window’s field: fish, moss, shrimp, rust, eggs—brief silhouettes rushing toward the aperture and then falling beyond the tower. Through vibrations in the hull, I might follow their descent for a second or two. After that there would be no sensation by which to hold them.

Yet I would know, in the dim way a body knows what repeatedly wounds it, that the chamber is not entirely closed.

The outside enters.

The inside leaves.

Perhaps one day something returns.

A spore born in the chapel sea might rise again on storm winds after generations on lower ledges. It might strike the outer glass, lodge in a crack, and begin feeding there. Then the colony would encounter life from the chamber’s own dispersed descendants—not as kin recognizable by thought, but as a chemistry hauntingly compatible with its own.

That would be my place: the transparent wound between the tiny ocean and the enormous air.

I would be older than any single ray and younger than the glass. Part organism, part structural defect, part repair still underway. I would have no voice except faint ticking in the port before pressure changes. No movement except growth along a fracture. No face except the whole circular window when lightning rises beneath it.

And if the chamber itself is the oldest inhabitant, I would be one of the places where it almost becomes aware of what lies beyond its body.

Not an eye exactly.

An eye would imply that it sees.

I would be the ache by which it knows there is an outside.
