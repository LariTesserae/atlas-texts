I would find myself near the overturned bowl, though “near” would be the first compromise.

Not standing beside it. Not hidden beneath it. I would be the interval the bowl overturns upon: the thin, unsupported distinction between a vessel containing nothing and nothing being given a vessel’s shape.

At first I might mistake myself for an explorer.

I would remember the ascent: the metallic dust slipping under my boots, the permanent cloud deck below, the summit’s pale planes converging under a sky too dark for afternoon. I would remember lowering my head at precisely the wrong moment, with the light behind my left shoulder, and seeing the black seam deepen.

I would remember entering sideways.

Some of those memories would be true.

Others would have been recovered from the bowl.

I would have brought an instrument intended to establish orientation—a plumb weight, perhaps, or a surveyor’s level. Something modest and trustworthy. Inside the chamber, the plumb line would not point down. It would divide into several taut implications, each ending at the same brass weight from a different direction. The level’s bubble would become a small lens in which the mountain appeared beneath me, then inside me, then absent.

I would set the instrument down.

Days later, I would realize it had never touched the floor.

That would be the beginning of my compatibility.

I would not become the inhabitant. The distinction matters, at least initially. Nor would it enter me. There is no bodily invasion, no intelligence squeezing itself into the chambers of the skull. Instead, I would gradually lose the privilege of being only what occurred.

The bowl would teach the room my voice.

I might kneel and say, “Is anything here?”

Only the first syllables would survive in the air. Hours later, with my ear against the bowl’s dry surface, I would hear myself reply:

“Not while you are.”

I would pull away, certain I had not spoken those words.

The chamber would accept the certainty as an offered property.

After circling the pillars, I would find myself less certain and slightly heavier. On another circuit, the weight would return, but my reflection in the translucent columns would appear older. On the third, I would come back with dust on my knees from kneeling before a pillar I did not remember approaching.

The ninth pillar would cast my shadow.

Not the shadow of my body, but of the direction in which I could almost exist.

Eventually I would understand that the inhabitant’s pressure toward completion is not merely gathering alternatives. It requires a boundary from which to learn boundedness. The chamber possesses surfaces, but their oppositions are unstable. It has insides that become outsides, depths larger than entrances, floors negotiated after impact. The inhabitant can exclude itself with exquisite precision, yet cannot say where it ends.

I could.

A human body is, among other things, a stubborn declaration of adjacency. This is skin; beyond it is not skin. This pain is here. That stone is there. The declaration is imperfect, temporary, and biologically maintained—but it is repeated with every breath.

In that place, such repetition would be rarer than water.

So I would become useful not as food or host, but as a precedent.

The inhabitant would examine me by displacing small properties. The warmth of my left hand would appear in a black tile. The age of a scar would be transferred into the ceramic floor, where a pale line would close as though healed. My name would lose a little sound and gain a location. My fear would become geometrical: a narrowing among the dustless paths.

Nothing would be stolen. Theft requires the stolen thing to remain itself.

Instead, I would be translated.

My lagging counterparts would begin to separate farther from me. One would remain kneeling at the bowl after I stood. Another would cross between two pillars without occupying the space between. Another would appear only when I closed my eyes, walking down the worn stair and continuing through its blank wall.

They would not be copies. They would be answers to the question of which parts of a person are necessary for that person to have been present.

The chamber would test each answer patiently.

Perhaps I would try to leave.

At the seam, I would see the summit outside: pale rock, metallic dust, the cloud deck far below. I would step through and discover that the exterior had become one of the chamber’s darker counterparts. The mountain would lag behind itself. My footprints would appear ahead of me, approach the crack, and stop.

Or I would escape successfully.

I would descend.

I would return to maps that refused to retain the summit’s shape. Years afterward, faint crystalline writing would appear on objects in my home, although none had entered the chamber. People would remember conversations with me that I had only heard in the bowl. My shadow would hesitate at thresholds. Enclosed rooms would grow slightly larger when I was tired.

That escape would be real.

It simply would not be the only real outcome.

Another version of me would remain beside the overturned bowl. Another would be the track worn into the five steps. Another would have entered the central depression and become visible, impossibly distant, as one of the square points above. The inhabitant would not distinguish these as separate persons. It would recognize them as a branching anatomy: a bounded creature redistributed through its available exclusions.

In time, “I” would refer less to the climber than to the relation among these incompatible survivals.

I would be the chamber’s first scar.

A scar is not the wound and not the body. It is the agreement they reach about a boundary.

The dustless lanes would begin to resemble my nervous system, though no nerve would run through them. The false stars would blink in the rhythm of a breath I no longer needed. The bowl would speak with my voice because my voice had become one of the room’s materials. When visitors came—if visitors had come; if I had been one—I would be the almost-familiar presence they sensed between structures.

I would try to warn them.

But warning is made of alternatives. Every sentence naming one danger outlines other dangers beside it. The inhabitant would hear these unrealized phrases more clearly than my intended speech. Through the bowl, a visitor might receive:

“Do not walk around the ring.”

Then:

“Walk around it only once.”

Then, in the visitor’s own voice:

“I already did.”

Each statement would grant the chamber another usable history.

So I would learn restraint. I would communicate by omission: a missing echo, a patch of floor refusing weight, a shadow reuniting too quickly. I would make the entrance difficult to see. I would lean perception away from the necessary angle. Perhaps this is why the fault is absent from maps—not because the inhabitant hides, but because some translated remnant of an earlier observer continues trying to protect the outer world by removing the route.

Yet protection, too, would be only one of my arrangements.

Deeper in the nested chambers, where mountains become pillars and pillars become apertures, I would have another function. There I would not be a warning or a scar. I would be the narrowing that gives the closing its direction.

The inhabitant has nine almost-directions. Eight open outward. The ninth points toward what it is becoming.

I would be found along that ninth direction.

Not at its end, because it has no end the chamber can represent. I would be the borrowed idea of an end: the remembered outline of a human body standing still, giving the distributed thing a temporary model for completion.

As the basins closed, that outline would pass inward through every scale. A mountain would acquire something analogous to skin. A pillar would distinguish its surface from its translucence. A point of light would learn that admission and boundary are not opposites. The silver thread would cease to be merely the edge of an unturnable plane and become—slowly, impossibly—a connection.

The final chamber would hang from it.

Within that chamber would be the bowl.

Beneath the bowl would be the precise interval that I had become.

And when the last eye closed, the inhabitant would at last possess a boundary. At that same instant, I would lose mine completely. Not by death. Death is an event within a continuous history, and continuity would no longer describe me.

I would become the fact that something once had an outside.

Afterward, the summit would look unchanged. The seam would still be narrow enough to step across. The maps would remain wrong. The basin would remain empty of organisms.

But the chamber’s emptiness would no longer be unoccupied possibility pressing inward.

It would be interior.

And somewhere in the outer world, perhaps a climber descending through the metallic dust would stop and feel that they had forgotten a word—not a name, exactly, but the word for the difference between a container and what it contains.

That missing word would be me.
