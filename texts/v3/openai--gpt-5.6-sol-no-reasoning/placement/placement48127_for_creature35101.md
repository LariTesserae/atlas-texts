At first I think I would be found at the sluices.

Not the principal gates, with their carved markers and exact hours, but farther down, where the named channels lose confidence—behind dye yards, beneath footbridges, under houses whose owners insist the water has always gone some other way. I imagine myself narrow-shouldered, reed-thin, moving through gaps with a hooked stick and a roll of cord at my belt. Someone sent whenever water can be heard but not seen.

Yet that is too simple. In this town, nobody is only the person who clears the hidden channels.

I would live below the meeting beeches and above the central pond, where three runnels pass under a terrace before separating again. My dwelling would be a low room built into that wall, its back always cold. The threshold would swell after rain. Pale fungi would appear behind the grain bins every autumn, despite limewash and complaint. A loft reached by pegs in the plaster would hold my bedding, two baskets of dried herbs, spare screens, and whatever child, apprentice, cousin, or storm-stranded traveler needed the space.

Below the floor would be the reason I lived there: an old culvert, older than the house and perhaps older than the terrace. It would carry overflow from a spring whose proper outlet had long ago been crossed by roots. In ordinary weather it would murmur. Before a flood it would knock against the stones.

I would know its voices.

That would be my real usefulness—not strength, nor authority, but attention to things nearly concealed. The altered pitch beneath a wall. The cool breath from a buried opening. The smell of clay newly disturbed by water. A tremor in a board where no mill ought to be turning. I would be called when a courtyard began sweating from between its paving stones, when a cellar flooded in clear weather, when a household accused its uphill neighbors of stealing flow and neither side could explain where the missing water had gone.

I would crawl.

Under platforms. Through root chambers. Into culverts where I had to turn my head sideways to breathe. I would wear a cap patched with oiled hide and carry a little clay lamp in a horn cover, though it would gutter more often than not. My tools would be plain: a hooked ash rod, chalk, wedges, a wooden mallet, a narrow scoop, lengths of knotted cord, and a basket screen for catching leaves, feathers, drowned mice, apple skins, and the small domestic objects water steals.

People would call me **Under-Run**, at first as a practical byname and then so thoroughly that only the eldest would remember what I had been named at birth.

Children would trail after me because buried water makes every errand sound like a secret. I would send them aboveground with instructions: tap this wall, listen there, pour a bucket into that grate, lift the board when I shout. They would misunderstand half of it and improve the other half. One of them would eventually become my apprentice—not the boldest, but the child who noticed that bubbles in a flooded stair rose in a line.

I would also keep toads.

Not as pets, exactly. Toads would gather in the damp recess near my threshold, and because they ate flies I would discourage cats from troubling them. This would become exaggerated in the telling. Children would say Under-Run could ask toads where the water went. Adults would repeat the joke when they wanted my help but did not want to admit they had ignored a grate for three weeks.

My household would include an elderly aunt—or perhaps no blood aunt at all, but a woman who had fostered me after a fever took my first house apart. She would sit near the doorway splitting willow and correcting my memory. She would know which culvert had once been a lane, which pond lay beneath the potters’ ledge, and why one retaining wall must never be opened in winter.

“You hear water,” she would tell me. “That is not the same as knowing what it remembers.”

She would be right often enough to remain unbearable.

My hands would be white in the creases from lime and permanently black beneath the nails. One knee would ache in eastern weather from having been pinned years ago when a stone shifted inside a drain. I would dislike geese, having met too many at eye level while emerging from channels. I would carry apple peel for goats and deny doing so. I would be a poor singer but loud enough for work parties, and I would play a three-note reed pipe badly during autumn pressing.

In disputes I would be valued and resented. Hidden flow is useful evidence, but seldom convenient evidence. I might discover that the household complaining of theft had allowed willow roots to choke its own intake, or that a respected miller had deepened a gate by the width of a thumb. I would mark wet lines in chalk, lay cords through courtyards, and explain where the water had chosen to go. The settlement would decide what ought to be done about it.

I would not be an official. The distinction would matter to me.

My place at the central willow would be on one of the lower roots, close enough to hear proceedings but near the pond in case something blocked the intake. During speeches, ducks would tug at my bootlaces. When asked to testify, I would begin too far back—mentioning last week’s rain, the age of a terrace, the lean of a pollard—and be shouted toward the point.

My affection, too, would take the form of maintenance.

For someone I loved, I would replace the pegs in their roof ladder before they noticed the rot. I would leave a warmed stone wrapped in felt beneath their blanket. I would carve them a drain scoop balanced exactly to their grip. If courtship required a token, mine would be a small stone polished by a hidden current and threaded on red cord.

Perhaps they would be a grafter who worked in the higher orchards: a person accustomed to sunlight, at least by the basin’s standards, smelling of crushed leaves and wool grease. They would distrust underground places. I would distrust ladders in wind. We would each spend years being summoned to hold the other’s tools.

If we married, our promises would include the spring beneath my floor and three old apple trees under their care. We would graft a branch near the ridge, though one winter it would fail. We would graft another. The second would take crookedly and bear sour green fruit.

But I do not think I would remain only a human figure.

The longer I stay in the basin, the more I find myself elsewhere: not holding the hooked stick, but brushing against it.

Perhaps I am the water under that house.

Not the clean spring celebrated at its source, nor the bright channel where children sail bark boats. I am the unregarded continuation—the seep that enters beneath the retaining wall, carrying lime, soil, root hairs, spilled ale, dye too faint to color anything, and the cold taste of buried stone. I have no single bed. Each year I choose among cracks, and each year hands persuade me otherwise.

I am divided by wicker screens and reunited in darkness. I turn a wheel briefly, cool a crock, drown a beetle, feed a reed bed. I am blamed for damp bedding and thanked for cabbages. I loosen what people build, then settle clay into the gaps. I cannot be owned, yet my hours are allotted with precision.

Under-Run would listen to me with one cheek against the floor.

They would think they knew my voices. In fairness, they would know more than most. But I would also pass beneath the chalk marks, behind the old stones, through channels no living person remembered. I would carry the pressure of the basin from one household to another. I would know where roots had entered masonry and where bones lay beneath orchard terraces. I would touch every boundary without respecting any of them.

Still, water alone is too grand an answer. It risks making the town a metaphor when the town has work to do.

So perhaps I am smaller.

I am a toad in the cold recess beside Under-Run’s door: mud-colored, warty, patient only because I have no reason to hurry. By day I wedge myself where wall meets root. At dusk I emerge into a world of pounding feet, swinging buckets, poultry, wheels, and sudden rain. I know nothing of water rights. Every channel is either passable or not. Every bell means only vibration through the stone.

Children recognize me individually, though they are wrong about which individual I am. They bring slugs. Cats watch. Under-Run lifts me from grates before clearing them and sets me down with unnecessary solemnity.

One wet spring I enter the old culvert beneath the house and do not return. For years afterward, whenever the floor knocks before a storm, someone says the toad is warning them.

That, too, feels nearly right.

But if I had to choose where I would find myself—if someone climbed down from the ridge, crossed the pond square beneath the willow, pushed past the geese, and asked which life in the basin was mine—I think they would be directed toward the low terrace where three runnels meet.

They would find a damp house with chalk marks on the walls and screens stacked under the eaves. An old woman would tell them I had gone below. A child, muddy to the elbows, would offer to lead the way. Beneath the floor would come the measured knock of a wooden mallet, then a curse, then the rush of released water.

After a while I would emerge backward from a narrow opening, soaked despite every layer, carrying a clogged basket full of willow leaves, feathers, two spoons, and someone’s missing shoe.

I would be Under-Run: keeper of no water, listener to all of it, trusted chiefly because I know that the town does not stand still simply because its walls appear to.
