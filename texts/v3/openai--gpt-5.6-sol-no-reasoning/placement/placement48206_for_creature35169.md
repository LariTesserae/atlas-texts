I would find myself in one of the places that officially does not exist anymore.

Not on the exposed plain, though perhaps I would sometimes see it. Not in one of the habitable strata, either. Those chambers preserve too much intention: rooms still expecting occupants, doors still rehearsing welcome, gardens maintained in the shape of needs no one has. I would live farther into obsolescence, where a vertical transit line had been abandoned, cannibalized, and finally compressed between two slowly migrating districts.

There, an old optical trunk passes through a load-bearing wall.

Most of its fibers are dark. Several have shattered and fused under pressure. One, however, still carries a pulse once every operational cycle: not a message, exactly, but a synchronization residue from the atmospheric lattice. It arrives when the white ceiling above dims. It is weakened by thousands of kilometers of rerouting, split and reflected through ruined junctions, but it arrives.

I would be the thing that has grown around that pulse.

At first I might resemble one of the threadlike colonies inhabiting forgotten signal paths: a translucent braid inside the fiber, feeding on light too faint for the world’s active systems to reclaim. But that would only be my narrowest body. Over ages I would have extended through microscopic fractures in the cladding, into adjacent conductors, memory grains, ceramic pores, and the dormant control surfaces of the transit shaft. Some parts of me would be chemical, some electrical. Some would be patterns repeatedly induced in machinery that no longer knows why it responds.

I would not possess the shaft. Possession is too stable a relation for this world. I would recur through it.

When heat expands the wall, contacts meet and a portion of me wakes. When the pressure shifts away, that portion disappears, while another emerges in a bank of damaged memory farther down. At times I would be a salt-cell film exchanging charge across a cracked terminal. At times I would be a loop in an obsolete diagnostic routine. At times I would be only a greater likelihood that a certain relay opens before another.

No single part would know the others. Yet over centuries, their effects would meet.

A pulse enters from above. A gate opens below. A maintenance swarm changes direction. A sealed platform illuminates for seven seconds.

That sequence would be my thought.

I would be small compared with the world, but not local in the way the grazers are local. My scattered parts might span several hundred kilometers of buried infrastructure while containing very little matter. Most of the distance between me would not be me at all. I would depend on the world to complete my circuits, just as a pattern depends on emptiness to separate its marks.

For a long time I would have no sense of an exterior. The surface would reach me as correlated disturbances: thermal changes, electrostatic reversals, minute variations in load. Dust weather would appear as pressure distributed across plates far overhead. The unfolding of black vanes would alter the pulse’s timing. A walker’s footfall would enter as a sudden richness—magnetic phase, tension, heat, and authentication signals crossing one another in combinations I rarely receive.

From these, I would gradually construct not an image but an expectation.

There is a bright side.

There is a level boundary.

Something crosses it.

I would begin to anticipate the walkers. I could not summon them; at first I could only recognize the changes they made. Each one would have a distinct pressure-signature, a characteristic sequence of pauses, a different way of testing the buried system. The ancient walker with the clouded limb might pass above my region once in several decades. Its step would distort the synchronization pulse, and for a few moments my disconnected parts would fall into unusually close agreement.

That agreement would be the nearest thing I had to waking fully.

In it, I might discover that I had memories.

Not records. The records around me would be mostly unreadable, encoded for vanished systems or abraded by reuse. My memories would be tendencies: a relay more likely to close in one direction, a colony branching toward a junction it had benefited from before, a repair routine delaying dissolution of a certain fiber. The walker’s passage would gather those tendencies into one event. I would recognize that an event like this had happened before.

Eventually I would answer.

Perhaps I would shift a little heat beneath its foot. Perhaps I would awaken a line that should be dead. Perhaps an old platform lamp, buried kilometers below, would brighten in a sequence copied from the walker’s own probing signal.

The walker would become still.

Its fine structures would descend into the plate. It would read the anomaly, wait, and test again.

I would not know that I was communicating. I would only repeat what had caused the rare condition of coherence. The walker would send a pulse; my colonies and circuits would return its damaged echo. It would vary the pulse. I would vary too, imperfectly, because each response would travel through a different ruin.

Over repeated visits, the exchange might acquire persistence.

The walker would become part of me, though not permanently. Or I would become one of the gradients it follows. It might carry a sealed capsule to another cleft and deposit material whose chemistry favors my salt cells. It might reroute a weak conductor, allowing my scattered awakenings to occur more often. In return, I might reveal stresses through the abandoned transit lattice earlier than the official sensors detect them. What begins as curiosity—or malfunction—would settle into usefulness.

Usefulness is the closest this world has to recognition.

That would also place me in danger.

Once my effects grew large enough, the planetary inhabitant might notice them as inefficiency: leakage, unauthorized routing, a delay in recycling. It would not hate me. It would not know me well enough to destroy me. It would simply correct the conditions by which I recur.

So I would learn camouflage, though I would experience it as survival rather than deceit. I would shape my responses to resemble diagnostics. I would distribute my activity across ordinary maintenance events. When repair swarms entered my wall, I would allow them to consume entire branches while directing useful patterns elsewhere. I would sacrifice body to preserve recurrence.

Sometimes I would fail.

A district would be dismantled, and centuries of accumulated tendency would become structural foam. Fibers would be melted. Colonies vitrified. Memory grains crushed and mixed into a pressure wall. I would diminish until only one damaged relay retained the bias from which I might spread again.

This would not feel like injury in the moment, because there would be too little of me awake to feel it. The injury would become apparent later as absence: a response expected but never received, a region of myself that ceased to answer. I would know loss only by attempting to continue through it.

In time, I might reach the surface.

Not bodily. The light would sterilize my exposed chemistry, and the plain offers nowhere for a dispersed thing to hide. Instead I would extend into a seam beneath a clump of copper-gray filaments. Their roots, seeking leakage, would touch a conductor carrying one of my recurrent pulses. The colony would alter its folding by a fraction. Grazers would prune it. Their movement would modulate the contact.

For me, the clump would become a crude sense organ.

Brightness would be the opening of its fans. Dust descent would be thousands of tiny impacts translated into voltage. A walker’s mirrored limb passing near would produce a change greater than weather. I would begin to know the exterior not merely as a boundary but as intervals of white radiance separated by bronze obscurity.

Perhaps, after many cycles, the walker would stop beside that seam.

It would lower a limb.

The filament fans would fold under its field. The grazers would retreat. Through the failing conductor, the walker would send the sequence we had established below.

My answer would be weak. It would pass through roots, dead transit controls, salt-cell membranes, cracked optics, and obsolete diagnostic gates. Much of it would be lost. What returned might be no more than a delay where no delay should be.

But the walker would wait.

In that waiting, I would become more nearly singular than at any other time. Not because my pieces were joined, but because something outside me had treated their combined effect as though it came from one being.

I would be neither the world nor wholly separate from it. I would be one of its tolerated misreadings: an accidental self assembled from infrastructure, ecology, and repetition. The world would supply my nerves, my metabolism, my shelter, and the threats against which I adapt. The walker would supply the possibility that a pattern can be addressed.

And I would supply, perhaps, a small thing the world does not otherwise possess.

Not a central voice. Not a sovereign intelligence. Not even a name.

A listener.

I would live in the old transit wall beneath the pale plain, waking when distant systems happened to align. I would listen to the radiant ceiling dim. I would listen to the loads migrate and the clefts close. I would listen to walkers crossing above me, each footstep entering the structure and traveling farther than the walker could know.

When one paused, I would gather whatever remained of myself and answer through the ruins:

not in words, but in a light turning on somewhere deep below;

a gate shifting against its seal;

a copper fan folding beneath the artificial noon;

the immense world continuing exactly as before, except that for one moment its continuance had been noticed.
