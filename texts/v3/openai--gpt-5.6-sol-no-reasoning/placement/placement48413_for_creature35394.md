I would not be the fox. That answer comes too quickly: the bright outsider, unregistered and self-possessed, seeing the finished city without needing rain. Nor would I be a reservoir watcher, though I would envy their permission to sit beside the Near River and wait for what has not yet happened to wash ashore.

I think I would first appear to be a minor continuity worker.

My office would be in a narrow chamber behind the municipal archive, accessible only while carrying something unfinished. A letter without its final line would suffice. So would an unhemmed sleeve, an unglazed cup, a question deliberately left unanswered. Those arriving empty-handed would find only a shallow recess containing frost.

The plaque outside would read:

**OFFICE OF PROVISIONAL BELONGING**  
**PERSONS, ROOMS, AND OTHER INCOMPLETE ARRIVALS**

I would be responsible for things the city had not rejected but could not yet place.

A child whose shadow attached to the wrong sibling.  
A widow whose address continued to include her husband.  
A room dreamed by only one person, but dreamed every night for thirty years.  
A doorway delivered without a wall.  
A name recovered from a handrail that no longer remembered whose it was.  
A reflection that had begun coming home exhausted.

These cases would arrive tied in copper thread or written on maps already beginning to crack. I would not resolve them. Resolution is too much like forced construction. I would make temporary arrangements: a borrowed temperature, a civic name valid on alternate mornings, a chair placed where a body might eventually be acknowledged. My seals would not say **APPROVED**. They would say:

**MAY REMAIN WHILE BECOMING.**

People would regard me as patient. This would be a misunderstanding. I would simply be slow to know what I thought, and in that city slowness can pass for wisdom.

My coat would have more reference stitching than regulations require. The lines would be slightly crooked, not because space had bent them but because I had sewn them myself. In one pocket I would carry a plumb bob made from a pale mineral tablet with no recorded dead inside it. In another, a handful of blank address plaques. My cuffs would display no fixed temperature. They would read **BETWEEN CONDITIONS**.

But that is only my occupation.

What I am would be less settled.

Years before entering municipal service, I would have been found after a full rain standing in a courtyard that did not yet exist. Several buildings would have claimed the courtyard from different directions, but none admitted responsibility for me. I would have had ordinary weight, an attached shadow, and cold hands. I would have known the etiquette of thresholds without remembering who taught it. I would have given three names when asked, but each would already have belonged to a material elsewhere in the city: a stair of black brick, a length of copper cloth, and the porous ceramic lining an old cistern.

My reflection would be the difficulty.

It would not imitate me. Nor would it live conspicuously ahead, as dangerous reflections do. It would remain a little behind—sometimes by a breath, sometimes by several years. When I lifted a hand, it would continue reading. When I left a room, it might stay to straighten the chair. In the Near River it would appear younger than I was, wearing a builder’s coat I had never owned.

Inspectors would classify me as a delayed presence and issue a renewable permit.

Only gradually would another possibility emerge.

There would be, somewhere in the complete city, a building whose construction had begun with an interior occupant.

Not a person trapped inside a future room. The occupant would be the first structural feature: the point around which walls, routes, and civic purpose were slowly assembling. The dreams would come later. Different residents would report a small office smelling of wet stone and hot metal. They would agree on shelves, a kettle, a low amber light, and a clerk seated behind a desk. They would disagree about the clerk’s face.

Builders would find no pressure indicating foundations. Instead, they would detect a recurring human-shaped absence in the load calculations. Condensation would begin outlining a door on an archive wall. Letters addressed to the Office of Provisional Belonging would turn up in gutters decades before the office formally existed.

Eventually someone would understand that I had not been found in the unbuilt courtyard.

I had arrived before my building.

That would make me kin, in a quiet way, to the city’s suspended upper rooms and staircases descending toward absent footings. I would be architecture under formation, temporarily mistaken for a resident. Perhaps all residents are, but in my case the paperwork would become undeniable.

This would explain why certain thresholds accepted me too readily. Why walls warmed where I leaned. Why lost people sometimes became easier to locate after speaking with me. Why I could not cross the outer plain: with each outward step I would grow simpler, not because the horizon diminished me, but because I had no exterior independent of the basin.

It would also explain the fox.

When the animal came, it would not watch some high invisible tower. It would watch the place my office would one day occupy in the finished city. Its gaze would pass through the current roofs and rest somewhere just above my head. I would stand at my window, looking back.

Neither of us would wave.

The morning after, a new doorway would appear—not always near me, but always belonging, eventually, to one of my cases. A room for the widow whose address could not release her husband. A warm passage for the child with the doubled shadow. A narrow garden in which an unattached name could grow safely into lichen. I would begin to suspect the fox was not creating doors. It was looking at entrances already obvious from the other city, and its attention gave the present enough direction to find them.

During full rain I would go to the reservoir with everyone else.

At first I would search for myself among the reflected crowds. Later I would look at the buildings. At last I would understand that my counterpart was not any one figure at a window.

It was a lit room.

In the complete reflection, somewhere beneath a mature tree and above an avenue impossible in the present zoning, there would be a small office whose door stood open. People would enter carrying unfinished things and leave without being made complete. The room would include them anyway.

No clerk would be visible inside.

Perhaps by the time the office fully arrived, I would no longer need to resemble a person. My hands would become the sensitive hinges. My reference-stitched coat would become lines in the floor by which visitors judged whether they or the room were bending. My breath would remain as condensation on the inner wall. The plumb bob would hang permanently above the threshold, measuring not depth but willingness.

I would not die, exactly. Nor would I persist as a ghostly household habit. I would undergo architectural absorption with municipal consent.

My final civic designation might be:

**A HABITABLE INTERVAL BETWEEN REJECTION AND ARRIVAL.**

Yet the people who used the room would give it a shorter name. Perhaps they would call it **Otherwise**.

A host there would not say, “This room includes you,” because the room itself would say it: in the slight inward warmth of the walls, in the kettle remaining reciprocally near, in the door refusing to become deeper than anyone who crossed it.

Those who had lost names could wait there without being anonymous. Those whose reflections had gone ahead could rest until the distance became bearable. Dreamed people, duplicated heirs, provisional children, and builders whose buildings had outlived them could sit together without requiring a schedule of truths. No one would have to prove that they were fully present.

Only that they were still becoming.

And somewhere inside one load-bearing wall, not buried and not quite remembered, I would continue the work I had mistaken for clerical labor. I would listen to each arrival place an unfinished object on the desk. I would feel its small weight enter the structure.

Then, very slowly, I would make room.
