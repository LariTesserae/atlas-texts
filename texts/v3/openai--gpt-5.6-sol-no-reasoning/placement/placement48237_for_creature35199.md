I would be found where the town presses closest to the western wall, in a passage most people use without noticing.

It was once the front room of a house. Then the floor rose, the neighboring chambers expanded, and a stair was cut through one corner. Now it is a crooked public throat between tiers: low-roofed, smoke-stained, always brushed by shoulders. One wall is ancient fitted stone. The other is generations of plaster, potsherd, ash, fiber, and old repairs. Between them runs a seam that sometimes gives off a thread of cooler air.

I would be the listener assigned to that seam.

Not an elder, though people might assume so from the work. Not a priest, though I would possess a few objects no one else was permitted to use: three clay listening cups, a bone sounding tap made from an ancestor’s forearm, wedges of horn thin enough to mark movement without forcing it, and a cord record of every change heard there for almost two hundred years.

I would be narrow even among the people of the cleft, with one shoulder held slightly lower from spending hours pressed sideways into the passage. The hair above my left ear would be worn away by the wall. I would know the town less by its visible arrangement than by its transmitted noises.

Through stone I would hear communal ovens opening several tiers below, each lid producing a dense, recognizable knock. I would hear the freight team begin before their chant reached the air. I would know which granary quern had an uneven saddle, which antelope stamped during milking, and which concealed household still used an illicit night lamp because the tiny expansion and contraction of its wall repeated after curfew.

Most of this would be none of my concern.

That distinction would define me. In a town where walls carry everything, a listener survives by learning what not to hear.

My responsibility would be one old joint in the mesa-facing masonry, where the builders had closed a natural fissure with blocks so exact that later generations mistook them for unbroken cliff. The cord record would say that the joint opens during the hottest weeks, closes during the cool winds, and gives three dry clicks after rare storms. It would say that bees nested there seventy-three years ago and departed without swarming. It would say that a grit fall once persisted for nine days, causing the evacuation of four rooms, after which nothing happened.

The oldest knot would signify something uncertain: not danger, exactly, but *continue listening*.

I would inherit the duty from someone who was not my parent. As a child I would have feared the outer bridges—not the height, but their motion. I would freeze when the whole span shifted beneath me, unable to accept everyone’s assurance that bending was how a bridge remained unbroken. My family kept dew combs on the rim and expected me to follow them. Instead, during an apprenticeship exchange, an old listener would discover that I could separate two nearly identical taps returning through stone.

She would put the bone striker into my hand and ask, “Which one is hollow?”

I would point to neither.

“The hollow is behind us,” I would say.

That answer would alter my life.

My apprenticeship would consist largely of stillness amid interruption. I would learn to place a cup against the wall without grinding dust beneath its rim. To tell settling plaster from shifting stone. To recognize mouse-scratching, root pressure, cooling pitch, beetle larvae, distant wedges, and deliberate tapping from sealed rooms. I would learn that fear makes every sound seem structural. I would learn never to announce a crack until I had listened from both sides, above and below, in heat and coolness, during labor and during midday quiet.

The old listener would teach me the town through false alarms.

“This is a dangerous sound,” she would say as a pulley shrieked.

Then she would take me three tiers upward and show me a dry bearing that needed oil but would hold another week.

“This is not dangerous,” she would say when a wall gave a deep report in the night.

Then she would show me a beam seating itself after a repair.

Finally she would touch my fingers to a patch of plaster from which no sound came at all. Beneath my fingertips would be a faint, continuous dusting, too soft to hear.

“This,” she would say, “is why listeners have hands.”

I would become useful, but not easy.

People would seek me for judgments beyond my office. They would ask whether a ceiling was safe, whether a new sleeping niche could be opened, whether a household had falsified the age of a wall, whether a tapping noise was an animal or a hidden person. Lovers would ask if voices carried through a particular shaft. Smugglers would offer me lowland salt to forget what I heard behind pitch jars. Children would dare one another to creep up and whisper accusations into my listening cups.

I would pretend not to notice most of them.

I would have an apprentice who noticed everything.

She would be broad-footed, impatient, and fearless on bridges. Her family raised wall bees, and she would arrive smelling of wax and bitter smoke. She would dislike my habit of waiting. Where I heard ambiguity, she would hear a problem demanding action. Yet she would possess an extraordinary sensitivity to vibration through tensioned cord. With her fingers laid on a lashing, she could detect movement several chambers away.

I would teach her stone. She would teach me rope.

Together we would begin to understand that my seam did not belong to the wall alone.

The cool thread of air would strengthen over several years. Not dramatically; catastrophe in the cleft rarely announced itself with spectacle. A little more dust would gather beneath the joint. The bees in nearby tubes would seal their inner chambers and migrate upward. A public stair would develop a new note under the morning crowd. One of the ancient blocks would sound unchanged when tapped, yet the plaster around it would begin cracking in a pattern too orderly to be ordinary age.

The counting assembly would want certainty.

Certainty is expensive. To obtain it, rooms might need to be emptied, walls opened, freight halted, families displaced. Every intervention would consume rope, clay, timber, labor, and trust. If I warned too early, people would say listeners heard danger because danger increased their authority. If I waited too long, the town beneath the joint might die under stone.

I would be asked the formal question:

“Is it falling?”

And I would answer, “Not yet.”

There would be anger at that. *Not yet* is the answer of a coward, a mystic, or an honest craftsperson, depending on who must act upon it.

So I would crawl into the forgotten thickness of the town.

Behind the passage would be a sealed chamber known only by the bend of corridors around it. We would open it not by breaking through the ancient blocks, but by lifting a later floor two tiers above and removing its aggregate piece by counted piece. Nothing could be discarded. Dust would be bagged. Potsherds would be stacked in their recovered order. Old fiber would be examined for reuse. The excavation would become a public nuisance, then a fascination.

The room below would be smaller than expected.

Inside, the air would smell not of decay but of mineral cold. The walls would bear hand marks made in dark lichen dye. There would be no treasure, no bones, no forgotten water. Only a system of stone ribs built against the natural fissure, and between them dozens of tightly wedged objects: horn slips, potsherds, pebbles, bone plaques, bits of ancient wood. Earlier listeners had placed them there as witnesses. If the fissure moved, the witnesses shifted, cracked, or fell.

Some would have fallen.

Others would remain fixed.

The chamber would reveal neither safety nor imminent collapse. It would reveal a process: the mesa breathing by increments too slow for one life to comprehend. The ancient builders had not conquered the fissure. They had left room for it. Later generations, seeking more space, had packed that room tighter, plastered over flexible joints, and mistaken accommodation for permanence.

The danger would not be that the cliff had begun moving.

The danger would be that the town had stopped allowing it to move.

My apprentice would understand first. She would lay a cord across the chamber, pluck it, and listen to the note die in the ribs.

“We have tied the wall too tightly,” she would say.

Then my place in the town would change. I would cease to be merely the keeper of a seam and become the advocate of an emptiness.

That would be difficult in the cleft. Empty space is suspect. Every sheltered handspan could hold seed, a sleeper, tools, pigeons, fuel, a loom. I would ask households to surrender rooms so a narrow flexible corridor could be restored along the ancient joint. I would ask builders to dismantle useful walls, rope wardens to invent yielding braces, plasterers to leave deliberate gaps. I would ask the town to preserve a place for nothing.

People would mock it as my shrine to absence.

Perhaps it would become one.

Not a sacred chamber in the ordinary sense, but a long inaccessible slit threaded through the settlement, crossed by sliding joints and tonal cords. Air could pass through it. Stone could expand into it. Witness pegs would show movement. Children would be taught that the town endured not only by filling, saving, and fastening, but also by leaving certain things unclaimed.

My apprentice would eventually inherit the seam. She would improve the warning cords until each section hummed at a different pitch. The whole western wall would become an instrument audible during shifts in heat. She would be a better public speaker than I was, less patient with evasive councils, more willing to call danger by its name.

I would move inward as I aged.

My climbing would shorten from tiers to ladders, ladders to steps, steps to the distance between my sleeping hollow and the passage. My fingers would remain useful. I would sort witness wedges by touch and teach children the difference between a wall’s voice and the noises traveling through it.

They would ask whether stone was alive.

I would tell them no, then reconsider.

I would say: “It does not need to be alive to answer what touches it.”

After death, one of my finger bones might become a small sounding tap. Not the grand forearm striker I used as an apprentice; I would not merit such theatrical usefulness. A finger tap would suit me. It would be tied securely to my successor’s belt and lifted to old plaster, bridge pins, sealed jars, and blocks warmed by afternoon light.

Or perhaps my bone would be judged too curved, too porous, too worn. It might become a toggle in a child’s safety cord.

That would also suit me.

I would remain in the cleft not as a name carved somewhere—carving removes material, and names are poor reasons to weaken stone—but as one of the people who persuaded the town to keep an empty seam within itself.

On windy days, air would pass through that hidden interval. The tonal cords would murmur behind the walls. Most inhabitants would hear only a low, many-stranded hum beneath the usual noises of pigeons, looms, quarrels, pulleys, and feet.

They might call it the mesa’s voice.

But I would know it as the sound of a burden given somewhere to move.
