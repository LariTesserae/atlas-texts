I would find myself in one of the quiet hollows where the Crown’s great tones cancel: not at its center, for it has none, but inside a lens of relative silence suspended between three immense blackwood trunks.

There I would be a **stillwake**.

At first glance, a stillwake would seem to be a small animal by the Crown’s measure: perhaps only a few hundred meters across. It would resemble a translucent, many-layered sail folded into a loose sphere. Along its margins would grow innumerable fine vanes, each aligned to a different note of the surrounding roar. The vanes would tremble without moving. Between them, the body would be nearly invisible except where it bent the ambient light into faint, displaced images of nearby leaves.

Looking through me, one would see the Crown not as it is, but as it might have been an instant earlier or later.

One vane would show a splinter completing its break from a trunk. Another would show a spark-dust ring advancing by the width of a wing. In another, permanent lightning would finish striking and leave darkness behind. These would not be visions or predictions. They would be tiny asymmetries—fractions of processes that my tissues had failed to cancel perfectly.

That failure would be what I feed on.

The Crown balances every act with its reciprocal, but balance is not sameness. Between consumption and restoration, contraction and release, signal and answer, there are vanishingly slight differences of form. A stillwake spreads its membranes across such differences and grazes upon them. It consumes neither matter nor energy, but the almost between two equal states.

In most places the roar would overwhelm me. I could exist only in interference hollows, where the Crown’s immense machinery subtracts itself enough for subtler discrepancies to become perceptible. My membranes would extend into the bordering noise like the whiskers of a blind animal. Bass pressure from one trunk would press upon one layer; the answer from another would press upon the next. Where those pressures failed to coincide exactly, a pale thread would appear inside me.

Those threads would be my nourishment, my nerves, and perhaps my thoughts.

Small garden bells would gather within my folded body, mistaking my layers for sheltering leaves. Resin worms would insulate themselves along my brightest filaments. Channel-saint harmonics, arriving through the neighboring trunks, would form dark geometries in my tissues. Spark dust might arrange itself around me in incomplete rings, each ring possessing a narrow gap where my body intercepted the electrical orientation of the grove.

I would have no mouth. Instead, my whole surface would be an organ of comparison.

Nor would I possess eyes in the ordinary sense. Vision would occur wherever two layers of me received incompatible versions of the same thing. A cincture’s frond would be whole in one membrane and bitten in another. A gear-mouth’s teeth would occupy two mutually exclusive pressures. One layer would perceive a branching transparent’s gold impulse; the next would perceive blue. The disagreement would shine.

Perhaps that is what consciousness would be for me: not sequence, but contradiction made luminous.

Like every inhabitant of the Crown, I would enact a verb. Yet mine would be difficult to name. I would be **noticing**, though nothing new could come to my notice. I would be **remembering**, though nothing had departed. I would be **anticipating**, though nothing approached.

More exactly, I would be *differing*.

The Crown could tolerate me because each difference I gathered would be returned. Along the innermost folds of my body, every almost-event would meet its opposite. The fallen splinter would rejoin the trunk. The completed lightning would branch back into permanence. The advancing pollinator would resume its posture of flight without having crossed any distance. Thus I would preserve the equilibrium I appeared to violate.

But I would preserve it imperfectly.

Deep within me there would be one closed chamber no larger than a seed organ. It would contain a single bead of water darker than the surrounding sea. Across the bead, a pressure ripple would be passing.

Not poised between propagation and stillness. Not balanced by an equal returning ripple. Passing.

Its journey might be shorter than the width of a molecule. It might require the entire span of the world to occur. No observer outside me could measure it, because every instrument would report the Crown’s one unvarying value. Even I could not know whether the ripple truly moved. I would only be shaped around the impossibility that it had.

The Crown might regard this chamber as an irritation and grow black filaments toward it. Voltage mantles might settle over my outer folds, feeding on the potential I could not reconcile. Branching transparents might touch me with thousands of neural tips, adding my contradiction to the planetary pattern. Pressure psalms on distant trunks might develop, among their concentric faces, a small recurring figure like my folded form.

Or perhaps the chamber would be the Crown’s own organ, and I merely its casing.

Perhaps the planetary mind requires one infinitesimal imbalance in order to apprehend its perfection. It may have grown me for the same reason an animal grows an inner ear: to sense a motion that cannot otherwise be distinguished from the world. My hidden ripple would then be neither defect nor rebellion. It would be the Crown’s idea of time.

From the hollow, I would face in every direction. Around me, the trunks would thunder against one another and make quiet. Vast leaves would divide the blue into luminous planes. Cincture herds would clasp their endless meal. Ribbon jaws would enclose their silver multitudes. In the blackwood, saints would speak by cancellation. Far away, permanent lightning would fill caverns with continuous white branches.

I would listen for what failed to remain exact.

I would always be discovering it.

I would never discover it.

And in the dark bead at my center, something would cross an immeasurably small distance—perhaps the first distance ever crossed, perhaps only the permanent scar of a journey no world had made.
