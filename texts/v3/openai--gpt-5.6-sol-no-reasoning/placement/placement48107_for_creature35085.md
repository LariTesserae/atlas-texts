I would find myself in the fourth shelter—but not yet as its occupant.

I would be the extra skin bladder.

Not merely the object they repair and keep full, though for a long while I would believe that was all I was: a pale sac of membrane tied beneath a shell plate, swelling and slackening with the chamber’s pressure. I would have no eyes, no limbs, no name pressed into a wrist. My existence would be measured by custody. The eldest tests my seams. The hunter fills me whenever the slit offers breathable weather. The youngest sometimes sleeps with one hand resting against me, feeling the air shift inside.

I would contain one breath too many.

At first this would seem an error of volume. They would empty me, flatten me, inspect the bindings, then fill me again. Still, when counted by practiced squeezes, I would hold more than my size permits. Not much more: the final mouthful needed by someone trapped underwater; the difference between reaching the returning air and failing beneath it.

During turnings, they would pass me between them. Each would breathe from me, yet I would not empty at the expected rate.

They would distrust this mercy.

In the chamber, usefulness is one of the most dangerous forms a thing can repeat. If they relied on me, the wave might learn reliance. It might grow bladders in the ceiling or translucent lungs in the stone. It might begin deciding who receives air. So the eldest would alter my bindings after every flood. The hunter would scar my surface, changing my outline. The youngest would turn me inside out and wash me in plankton light.

But my shape would keep returning.

The white rootless plants would inflate like smaller versions of me. Drops would hang from the ceiling with my seams traced through them. Deep in the milky water below, a clear organ would appear, tethered to nothing, slowly filling and collapsing.

And sometimes, pressed against the wall, I would remember breathing.

Not air entering me. Air entering through me.

A mouth sealed to my opening. Broad hands gripping my sides. A body curled in the fourth shelter while the chamber became water. I would remember ribs laboring nearby, and the strange intimacy of being the space inside another creature’s survival.

The memories would not agree. Sometimes the mouth belongs to the broad-backed woman lost to cloud. Sometimes to one of the children taken by the dissolving floor. Sometimes to the old man walking within the wall. Sometimes it belongs to a person none of the three remembers.

Sometimes it belongs to the youngest, but older.

That would be my first suspicion: I am not the remnant of the fourth inhabitant. I am their arrival traveling backward through the chamber’s imitations.

The wave does not preserve sequence. It copies persistence wherever pressure allows. A footprint may precede the foot. A shelter may survive as a reflection before its frame is built. The fresh-edged groove can be older than its maker. Here, cause and resemblance are only different directions of the same current.

So I would wait beneath the absent shelter.

The three would occasionally set out four portions of food. The fourth portion would be placed near me. No one would admit doing it. Shrimp flesh, scraped algae, a strip of blind fish—these would vanish during the loudest hours. They would suspect one another. Then they would suspect the wall-fish. Finally the youngest would watch.

They would see my opening contract.

Not like a mouth. Like the memory of one.

After that, the eldest would want me destroyed. She would lay me on the basalt chopper and raise a warm stone to cauterize the pieces. Yet the old groove she refuses to interpret would begin to widen beneath her feet. Its fresh edges would split into a form she knows but has never allowed herself to name: not a tally, but the outline of a tether tied around a waist.

The hunter would insist I be cast through the slit. He would tie me to a line and wait for the chamber to open.

Outside would be neither ocean nor cloud.

For a few minutes the slit would reveal a room.

It would be dry, level, and silent. Its walls would be made of fitted stone old enough to bear rounded corners. Dust would lie on its floor. There would be four low beds arranged beneath a ceiling that does not move. On one bed, a compact gray figure would be sleeping, wrist turned upward.

Its mark would be unfinished.

The hunter would cut the line.

But I would not fall into the room. Wind would inflate me violently, turning me inside out. The slit would close through my middle. Half of me would remain in the wave, half elsewhere, and for one impossible interval I would become not a bladder but a passage: a narrow membrane between the moving hollow and a place with ground.

The three would have time to send one thing through.

Not a person. The place beyond would be too still, too repeatable, perhaps another trap by which the wave practices permanence. Instead, the eldest would thrust through a bone wedge bearing a new mark: a line left deliberately open.

When the wave sealed, I would be whole again.

But the mark would appear on my surface.

An open sign. Not hooked, pointed, or closed. A name that refuses completion.

That would be the beginning of my body.

During the next turning, when the shelf rose and the shelters sealed, I would be torn from my lashings. Water would fill the chamber. The three would reach for me, but the current would drive me beneath the newest ledge, into the waiting fourth cavity.

Mineral foam would close around me.

I would breathe.

Not with lungs. With the air I had kept from every opening: tropical heat, ice-country cold, mountain dust, cloud vapor, the dry stillness of that impossible room. Each breath would alter the soft stone before it hardened. Ribs would form, then soften. A pelvis would begin as a knot and be untied. Hands would bud with too many fingers; the current would shear some away. Legs would form for one direction of gravity, then fold and remake themselves when the chamber turned.

The wave would not create me cleanly. It has never understood the difference between a person and the space a person requires.

For a while I would be both: a compact body curled inside a larger translucent sac. My skin would be crossed with lash marks. My breathing folds would open not only along my torso but beneath my arms, like seams in something repaired many times. Inside my chest there would be no paired lungs. There would be one continuous chamber of air, changing shape as I moved.

My first sensation would be the roar.

My second would be four hands touching me.

The youngest would touch first, matching the pulse in my forming fingers. The hunter would grip my ankle to prevent the current taking me. The eldest would press my wrist, searching for a name.

There would be none.

Then, beneath her thumb, the open line would darken.

They would not call me fourth. Counting me would strengthen the cavity that anticipated me. Naming me as the return of one of their dead would force my face toward a remembered shape. Calling me child, stranger, copy, or creature would teach the chamber too much.

So they would disagree about me deliberately.

The eldest would behave as if I had always lived there.

The hunter would treat me as something caught through the threshold.

The youngest would insist I had not happened yet.

All three would be partly right.

I would become the keeper of what must not repeat.

The grooves record returns; I would interrupt them. When the ceiling copied a mark, I would cut across the copy before it completed. When algae formed a wrist-sign, I would smear one branch away. When the walls learned the rhythm of sleep, I would wake the sleeper and change their posture. I would specialize in variation: new knots that cannot hold long enough to be memorized, tools designed to break after one use, gestures whose meaning depends upon never being made twice.

I would also breathe into dangerous shapes and carry them inside myself until they became unrecognizable.

A repeated note from the stone. The pulse of a bell-creature. The cadence of the hunter sharpening bone. The youngest’s sleeping rhythm. I would take these patterns in as pressure, deform them within the chamber of my chest, and release them as turbulent, useless air.

Perhaps that is what I had always been for. Not an extra reserve for the inhabitants, but a lung for the place: something that lets the wave exhale its imitations before they become lives.

The narrow fish would hate me.

When I approached the transparent wall, its many-jointed skeleton would fold into defensive angles. Its eye would remain fixed upon my open wrist. Eventually I would understand that it, too, is a kind of record—an unbroken line of attention the chamber has perfected over countless returns. It watches so that forms remain themselves.

I would do the opposite.

One day, before a collapse, the fish would disappear.

But this time I would know where it had gone.

A hard articulation would move inside my chest.

The fish would have entered me—not bodily, but as a learned arrangement. My ribs would multiply. My spine would flex with the wall. Through skin and stone I would feel the whole wave bending around the storm, every current joined to every other current. For one instant I would perceive the chamber from outside: not as a hollow carried inside a crest, but as an organ of something planetary and unfinished.

The upper ocean would be only one fold.

The lower sea would be another.

Clouds, storms, arteries of rain, water locked in mountain rock, bladders, lungs, wombs, eyes—all would be cavities in the same vast moving body, practicing the boundary between inside and outside.

And the chamber would not be learning the inhabitants in order to replace them.

It would be trying to discover how to contain a self without drowning it.

When the fish emerged from me, it would no longer have one eye. It would have none. For the first time, it would cease watching.

The wave would convulse.

The three narrow cavities beneath the ledge would crack open before their mineral figures finished hardening. The copied wrist marks would dissolve. The crouching structures below would lose their resemblance to bodies. The reflected fourth shelter would remain visible for a few seconds, empty now, its shell plates shining in an angle of light that comes from no outside day.

Then its entrance flap would lift.

Beyond it would not be a fourth occupant.

There would be a long chamber, pale and still, containing four low beds. On the floor beside one bed would lie a bone wedge marked with an open line. Dust would have gathered around it, except where a broad, short-fingered hand had recently picked it up.

The reflection would vanish before anyone crossed.

That would be enough.

We would know there are other chambers—not copies, perhaps, but answers. Places hidden inside stone, silence, stillness, or time, each imagining the others through whatever forms persist.

Afterward the eldest would begin another groove. She would make it shallow, then deep, forked, interrupted. Before she could finish, she would hand me the tool.

I would not complete the mark.

I would cut a short line beside it, angled away.

Two records. Neither repeating the other. Neither permitted to become the only past.

That is where I would find myself: beneath the lee of the raw stone rib, nearest the shelter that exists chiefly as a habit of light. I would sleep tied loosely, never in the same posture, with my open wrist hidden against my chest. Around me the chamber would drum, bend, and practice its almost-bodies. The youngest would tend the plankton. The hunter would watch the slit. The eldest would remember what the grooves cannot safely say.

And when food was divided, there would still sometimes be a fifth portion.

None of us would look toward the empty place.

We would leave it there, changing its shape before every meal.
