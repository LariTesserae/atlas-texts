I find myself where the city listens to itself.

Not in the coordinating halls, where maps are revised faster than they can be filed, nor high among the tram cables, though I sometimes go there. My place is lower and farther inward: in the retaining walls behind the western dye district, where three buried streets descend toward the canal and no present door opens directly onto the oldest one.

I am a wall reader.

That is the name people use, though walls are only part of what I read. I read the darkening of mortar, the temperature of iron under a gloved palm, the altered pitch of a stair when freight passes overhead. I read moss borders and salt blooms, cracks crossed with dated pencil lines, pipes that knock once when they should knock twice. I listen at inspection plates with a short brass horn. I watch beads of water gather on old tile and note which bead moves first.

Most days, nothing I observe is dramatic. A seam widens by the thickness of a hair. A patch of brick dries from the bottom instead of the top. Ferns appear in a joint where no fern grew last month. These are enough.

My district contains more city than is visible.

Behind the active walls are earlier walls, and behind those are rooms filled with rubble, water, conduits, or air. Some were houses. Some were workshops. One was a station, though only the curve of its platform remains. Another space appears on no surviving plan and contains a row of glazed pillars descending into black water. Every generation has passed its burdens backward into these structures. The newest tram viaduct bears on a market hall, the market hall on a mill, the mill on an embankment built around a bridge that once crossed a river now flowing elsewhere.

I know the loads, but not completely. No one does.

My maps are narrow folded things on waxed cloth. Official plans show what should be there. Mine show what answers when struck, what sweats before rain, what shifts when the upper reservoir is lowered. I inherit some annotations and add others. Beside one foundation pier, an earlier hand has written:

**Sings in thaw. Not dangerous if alone.**

It has sung every winter of my life.

I live above a public laundry in a room whose floor slopes toward the mountain. The slope is old and stable, so the legs of my table are cut to four different lengths. A warm conduit passes behind the eastern wall. I dry my boots there and grow pale mushrooms in a cupboard built around an inspection hatch. The laundry opens before my shift, and each morning steam finds its way through the floorboards. My ceiling disappears into it. For a little while, waking feels like entering cloud.

My front door is on the sixth level from the canal, unless the lower arcade is sealed. Then it is considered the fourth. In heavy rain I leave through the window, cross a hinged bridge, and descend through the laundry’s pressing room. The workers there keep a cup for me beside the boiler. We seldom speak before the first siren.

I am compact, even for the valley, with a damaged finger that no longer straightens and one knee that predicts pressure changes less accurately than people suppose. My coat is gray-green, patched at the shoulder where I lean against wet masonry. My boots have the diagonal cuts of the western slope, though one sole was replaced by a cobbler from upstream and carries the hook pattern of her district. Strangers sometimes think this means I belong to two places.

Perhaps I do.

As a child, I wanted to be a cistern diver. I imagined the submerged chambers as a second city, perfectly quiet and waiting to be entered. Later I learned that they are cramped, blind, cold, and full of cables that catch at the shoulders. The romance diminished, but did not disappear. I still volunteer when a flooded foundation must be surveyed.

Underwater, the valley becomes simpler. There is the circle of my lamp, the pull of my line, my breath arriving through the mask, and the wall close enough to touch. Silt has covered most signs of human use, but sometimes my hand finds a carved molding, a door latch, the rim of a basin. Once, beneath a pumping station, I found mosaic letters set into a floor. Only three could be cleared before the current thickened the water again.

They may have named a saint, a manufacturer, or a tram destination.

We built a new pump footing over them.

This is not considered loss. Not exactly. The letters remain where they were, bearing a different weight.

People imagine a wall reader as someone who prevents collapse. More often, I decide what may safely move.

A city like ours cannot be preserved by holding it still. Pressure must be given somewhere to go. Cracks must sometimes be allowed to open so that worse cracks close. Water must enter one chamber to spare another. A leaning structure may support three neighbors, and straightening it would bring them down. I have condemned sound masonry because it obstructed drainage and defended a visibly bowed wall because its curve had become part of the district’s balance.

This is why my work is argumentative.

Engineers bring figures. Residents bring memory. Gardeners bring roots wrapped in wet cloth. Tram officers bring timetables and insist that vibration is negligible. Shopkeepers bring keys to doors no plan records. Elderly people stand in passages and say there used to be a stair here, no, farther left, behind the blue tile. Often they are right.

I distrust certainty, including my own.

On clear nights—there are a few—I climb to the roof beds above the old reservoir. The alders there grow from crushed brick, slag, compost, and the dust of demolished walls. Their roots are instrumented with tension bands. Small numbered plates hang from the trunks. Nothing in the grove is wild, yet the wind passes through it without consulting our diagrams.

From there I can see the valley’s working lights. Green at the sluices. Blue at the pressure mains. Amber beneath bridges. White along the freight ways. They do not form a picture so much as a set of conditions.

Safe to cross.

Held for water.

Load transferred.

Route provisional.

Someone awake below.

My closest companion tends fungi in the buried timberworks beneath the upper tram exchange. She can smell rot before instruments register it. We meet irregularly, according to our shifts and the weather. Sometimes we eat in the market while it is still a market. Sometimes, by the time we arrive, the stalls have folded away and the landing has become a repair depot. Then we sit on stacked gate panels and drink broth while crews pass around us.

She says I treat people like structures: testing gently, watching for movement, leaving routes for pressure to escape.

I tell her she treats structures like people.

Neither is entirely a joke.

During the long rains, I sleep in the district station. Everyone does who might be needed below the fifth level. We hang our coats from hot pipes and lie on rolled mats between cabinets of clamps, lamps, wedges, sounding hammers, chalk, and sealed records. The sirens travel through the walls more clearly than through the air.

One winter, after six days of rain, the old western facing began to move.

Not much. Three millimeters at first. Then another two between midnight and the second freight pause. Water had entered a forgotten chamber and was pressing outward on masonry already carrying the corner of a school, two tram columns, a vineyard terrace, and part of a boiler house.

The obvious action was to drain the chamber.

But the chamber lay below canal level. Opening it would have drawn more water in. Reinforcing the face would only have passed the force upward. Closing the tram would reduce vibration but strand the crews needed at the spillway.

So we opened a street.

It was an old one, sealed for more than a century behind the laundry’s lower wall. An elderly resident remembered that it descended toward a millrace. The plans disagreed. We cut where she indicated.

Behind the bricks was a stair.

The water came first in a black sheet, then clear. It descended the steps, crossed a workshop floor, entered a temporary channel, and reached a service basin whose reeds were already flattened by rain. For nine hours the vanished street carried the district’s excess pressure.

By morning the facing had moved back half a millimeter.

The school opened late. The vineyard lost two beds. The laundry kept one section of the old stair and now uses it to store soap. A brass plate beside the opening bears the elderly woman’s name—not because she saved the district alone, which she would deny, but because memory became infrastructure through her.

That, more than anything, is what I believe in.

Not permanence. Not mastery. Not even resilience, a word used too often by people who do not have to inspect what survived.

I believe that attention can be handed on.

Someday another wall reader will unfold my maps and find my marks unclear. They will curse my abbreviations. They will discover that I mistook a conduit for a foundation tie, or failed to note a cavity behind a tiled corridor. Perhaps they will place an instrument against the pier that sings in thaw and decide at last that it is dangerous even when alone.

I hope they alter my work.

I hope I have left them enough access to do so.

Until then, I move through the inward city with chalk, lamp, listening horn, and keys. I place my hand against walls that are colder than the air. I descend stairs that no longer connect places anyone names. I mark dates beside cracks and return after rain.

Around me, the valley shifts its immeasurable weight from stone to brick, brick to iron, iron to root, root to water, water to the hands waiting at the next gate.

I listen for the moment the burden changes.

Then I draw a line.
