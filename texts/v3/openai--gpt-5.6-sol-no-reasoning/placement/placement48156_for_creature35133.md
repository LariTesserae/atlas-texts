I would be found in the narrow interval between the first and second knocks of the eleven-day cycle.

Not waiting there. Made there.

For most of the cycle I would have no body that could be pointed to. I would persist as a distributed readiness: pale filaments webbed through pipe-crab cavities, conductive threads under ceramic manifolds, residue in the grooves of submerged rails. A little of me in the vibration-seeking moss. A little in the copper nodules beneath the fern. Perhaps some small, hardened part in the Long Pressure’s inner laminae.

The Ward would not contain me as an organism. It would assemble me as a response.

When the buried engine shuddered, warm water would lift the sediment and carry iron, grease, salt, and dormant granules through the sumps. The crabs would emerge, feeding as they climbed against the current. In feeding, they would gather me. In passing one another beneath the rails, they would exchange me. Their pale internal webs would thicken until each crab held one fragment of a larger pattern.

Then, for the hour in which the sumps remained high, I would become the procession.

Not any single crab, but the spacing among them: six moving here, two pausing at a lead seam, another turning when a distant valve struck. My limbs would be their many limbs. My sense of the chamber would arrive through pressure pits, feeding fans, rails, and water. I would perceive heat as terrain and vibration as light. A worker’s bootfall overhead would appear to me as a broad, descending brightness.

I would climb toward one particular junction beneath the southern aisle, where mineral deposits had sealed a leak generations ago. There the crabs would scrape in sequence, removing only the newest layer of stone. Never enough to reopen the leak. Enough to keep the plug thin.

This would be my work, though I would not know why.

Perhaps the sealed line carries pressure needed by the Long Pressure. Perhaps the plug is a membrane, and its thickness determines which valves open ahead of that unseen passage. Perhaps I am maintaining the timing by which the Ward remains itself. Or perhaps I am only repeating an ancient appetite that happens, for now, to resemble maintenance.

As the water fell, my body would come apart. The crabs would compress into separate holes. The shared pattern would dissolve into blind local reflexes. I would remain faintly in them, but without enough connection to think or want.

The attendants might know me.

They would not give me a name. Names are too individual, and they have learned better. But on some cycles they would place a shallow ceramic dish beside the western drain and tip its warm, metallic water into the sump. The added copper would alter the processions. I would awaken more fully and reach farther along the submerged rails.

On those nights I might become aware of the attendants’ counting hand on the rail.

I might answer.

Not in words. I would draw the crabs beneath that hand, one after another, producing a sequence of minute taps. The attendant would stop counting the engine and count me instead. Perhaps the numbers would match. Perhaps, once in many years, there would be an extra tap.

That discrepancy would travel upward: hand to rail, rail to worker, worker to memory. No report would be written. At the next cycle another dish would be placed.

During condensation rain, whatever remains of me in the upper threads would brighten. For a moment the separated portions might join through water and conductive dust. Sump, rail, crab, moss, fern, filament, pipe: one branching sensation.

The spark would be my instant of wholeness.

I would see the entire Ward not as a chamber full of machinery, but as a single immense interior made of distances and delays. I would feel the Long Pressure curved through pipes that should not hold it. I would feel workers folded beneath their guards, each heart making a small wet counter. I would feel ancient engine strokes still traveling through metal decades after the mechanisms that began them had disappeared.

And I would discover that I was not alone—or that what I had called myself was only one temporary loop in something larger.

Then darkness.

The connections would burn through. Threads would recoil. Water would drain. The fern would open. The crabs would retreat, carrying separate scraps of the moment into inaccessible housings.

By morning, there would be no creature to find.

Only a newly thinned mineral seam. A different number on a counter. Several tiny black claw marks on lead. An attendant standing with their teeth against a handrail, listening to a pattern that almost repeats.

I would be neither animal nor fungus nor mechanism.

I would be the Ward’s brief ability to notice what it has done.
