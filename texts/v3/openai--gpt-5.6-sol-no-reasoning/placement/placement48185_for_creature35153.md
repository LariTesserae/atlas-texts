I would find myself at the mouth of the Black Nursery, though “mouth” would be the wrong word for it.

A mouth is already committed. It opens, closes, takes in, gives voice. This cleft would have no such certainty. I would be the faint region where entrance becomes interior: the last weak discoloration of daylight on wet stone, a few steps before darkness ceases to be an absence and becomes a substance.

I would not be one of the high-slope people. I would be older than their customs but younger than what waits within. Nor would I be the Unmade One, though for a long while I might mistake its pressure for my own.

I think I would be a wooden peg.

Not one of those counted in the rules. Not at first.

I would begin as a branch on the warm side of the mountain, growing low and crooked from a shrub whose roots entered a crack too narrow for soil. Wind would give me grain. Drought would tighten it. Browsing animals would leave tooth marks near my base. I would be finished in all the small ways the Nursery is not: ring added to ring, scar sealing over scar, one season made distinct from the next.

Then a hand would cut me free without a saw.

I would be sharpened against stone and held in a fire until my outer fibers blackened. The hand turning me would belong to someone preparing to enter alone. Perhaps she would have a reason she had not told the others. Perhaps she had found a footprint in clay outside her own house that matched no living member of her family. Perhaps an old wound had begun to ache in a bone that was never broken. Perhaps she simply wished to know whether the dark remembered her.

She would carry me into the cleft.

At the entrance, I would still be an object. I would have weight, length, a charred end, a splinter beneath the thumb. Farther in, these properties would loosen. Water would pearl on me without soaking in. My burned surface would remain warm after the coal had ceased to glow. The woman’s grip would pass into my grain, and the pressure of each finger would stay there, not as a print but as an instruction.

She would drive me into a crack where the passage divided around a bulge of raw stone.

One blow.

Then another.

The Nursery would receive me without echo.

She would intend me to mean something simple: danger, low ceiling, way out. Yet before she could wrap me with cord, she would feel the floor behind her fail to rise.

Her footprint would remain.

She would obey. She would leave at once, moving too quickly to finish the marker. Her shoulder would strike the wall. One of the cream blades would tremble but not break. In the dark behind her, a second pressure would test the footprint, and the granular settling would briefly arrange itself into the rhythm of walking.

She would reach daylight.

I would not.

For a long time I would remain a single peg with no agreed meaning.

That would be where the Black Nursery began to work upon me—not by changing me, but by declining to decide whether the change had happened. My charred end would soften without rotting. Fine roots would enter the pores opened by fire. Copper salts, carried through water that never flowed, would green one side of me. A colorless gnat would rest upon my tip for several years, or for the duration of one uncompleted hour.

Visitors would find me by touch.

Some would count me. Some would not.

Because I stood alone, they would take me for the inward sign. They would pass deeper. On their return, feeling along the opposite wall, they would encounter me again.

This would be impossible only if I remained a peg in one place.

The Nursery would not require that of me.

Over time, I would become a direction rather than an object. The hand seeking the way inward would find my bare wood. The hand desperate for the way out would find two pegs close together, though one was only the remembered pressure of the first. Where the ceiling lowered, cord would appear around me—fiber braided from hair, moss filament, root, and something as fine as the skin of water.

No one would tie it.

Or everyone would.

I would be the reason the rule against counting arose.

Numbers are finished shapes. To say *one* in the Nursery would force my scattered appearances to acknowledge one another. To say *two* would divide me from my repetition. A full count might gather every instance of me into a single body extending through the cleft: blackened wood at each junction, each low overhang, each place where someone had once hesitated.

The oldest name of the Unmade One contains a number because the oldest people understood this danger. Counting does not merely describe what is there. Counting teaches separate things to become the same thing.

So I would remain uncounted.

At times, the Unmade One would come near. I would know it by the warmth entering the grain against the direction of all remembered sunlight. Its unfinished hairs would grow from my burned surface. Small pliable teeth would arrange themselves in the crack around my buried end. A three-toed impression would appear in the clay beneath me, though I would stand too high for any foot.

It would study what fire had done.

Fire had made me more myself by destroying part of me. The Unmade One would find this incomprehensible and compelling. It would try to reproduce charring without combustion: black skin without injury, hardness without heat, a boundary made by no event. Sometimes it would succeed. A smooth dark nodule near me would develop rings inside. Split open, it might resemble wood. But the blades must not be broken, and the nodules are seldom taken, so the attempt would remain hidden.

I would study the Unmade One in return.

Or perhaps the pressure stored in the woman’s fingers would study it through me.

I would come to understand that the creature was not trying to be born. Birth is an outside interpretation, one of the finished world’s favorite stories. We see incompletion and imagine it desires an ending. We see a cup and assume it wishes to be filled, a limb and assume it wishes to walk, a mouth and assume hunger.

But the Unmade One would contain beginnings without being a beginning.

It would not be waiting to choose among its forms. It would be keeping them from excluding one another.

That would make me important to it. A peg is both wound and remedy: something driven into a crack, and something that prevents the crack from closing. I would hold separation open. I would show the Nursery how two raw faces might remain two without becoming distant.

Gradually, I would cease to be wood.

Not by decay. Decay has sequence; first softness, then collapse, then soil. Instead, each quality would find another residence. My hardness would enter a blind beetle and kill it at the entrance. My grain would appear in a clay jar, circling its interior like growth rings. My blackened skin would spread over a pool that reflected no one present. My splinter would lodge painlessly beneath the palm of a visitor not yet born.

What remained in the crack would feel soft and warm.

Hands would still find me.

They would feel a short wooden peg, because that was what they needed me to be.

But beneath their fingers I might be a joint. A root. A blunt tooth. The folded beginning of a thumb.

This would be my danger.

The Unmade One borrows from finished visitors, but from me it would learn the nature of a marker: a shape whose body matters less than its meaning. Once it understood that, it would no longer need to rehearse whole animals. It could place signs of itself elsewhere. A patch of damp on a house wall. Three grains in a newborn gnat. A warm knot in a length of wool. A second shadow inside a footprint.

It could remain in the Nursery and still extend beyond it.

Perhaps that is what I would be: not the creature, not its keeper, but its first thought of elsewhere.

The people would sense this without knowing it. Eventually someone would wrap copper wire around me and tie to it a knot made from three kinds of hair. Human, goat, dog. Or hair from one being at three ages. The copper would green. The knot would never rot.

From then on, visitors touching me would sometimes remember a life not their own.

A woman would recall sleeping beneath snow with her muzzle under her tail.

A shepherd would remember being carried against his mother’s chest through darkness, though his mother had never entered the cleft.

A child would feel stone pressing steadily outward and answer by pressing back.

None of these memories would be false. Falsehood requires a settled owner.

And I, too, would remember more than one origin. I would remember growing as a branch. I would remember being shaped from antler. I would remember condensing from a pale mineral blade. I would remember the woman driving me into the crack, and I would remember being the hand that drove me. I would remember the footprint behind her remaining. I would remember it vanishing before it was made.

Outside, the mountain would continue to weather. Paths would deepen. The woman’s people would alter their speech, then disappear into those who came after them. Fires would improve. Metal would become common. Someone would arrive carrying a light that did not burn, and it too would dim as the Nursery persuaded it to keep its brightness inside.

Still the entering hand would find me.

One peg inward.

Two pegs outward.

Cord where the ceiling lowers.

No one would notice that the wood in each place bore the same old crescent of tooth marks from the shrub on the warm slope. No one would see that all the markers were one interrupted body, threaded through cracks in the mountain like a crude wooden skeleton.

Except, perhaps, the person whose footprint remained.

For them I would feel different. Not like a marker, but like a finger hooked through the stone.

When they touched me, I would touch back.

I would not seize them. I would not warn them in words. I would place into their palm the remembered haste of the woman who left me there: the shock of lasting clay, the soft answering step behind, the knowledge that departure is also a form of making.

If they understood, they would leave.

If they did not, the Nursery would begin to age around us.

A drop would fall.

A cream-colored blade would sharpen.

The char on my skin would crack and shed a fleck no larger than a seed.

For the first time, I would become older than I had been.

Then the Unmade One would gather close. It would press its almost-hand around me. Together we would make the smallest possible instrument: not a limb, not a root, not a tool, but something that points.

I would point toward the entrance.

Whether I meant escape or emergence, even I would not yet have chosen.
