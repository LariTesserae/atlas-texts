I would not find myself on the highland’s surface. “On” is too exposed a word, and “myself” too bounded.

At first I imagine I am one of the walkers: a pale seed-body lifting itself over the black-green scales, guided by invitations too faint to be called scents. I would cross a valley in a year, carrying a folded grove within me. Each step would be a temporary root. I would travel not toward a place, but toward the chemistry in which I might finally become one.

But that is too much independence for this world. A walker still has a direction. It can be followed with the eye. It possesses, however briefly, an outline.

I think I would be deeper.

I would live in a pressure tree, in the narrow wet interval between its load-bearing wall and the spiral sheath that keeps it from splitting. Not the tree, and not the sheath: the responsive film between them. A layer only a few cells thick, spread through chambers kilometers tall. My existence would be tension translated into memory.

I would feel the continent mostly as strain.

The swelling of an upper reservoir would arrive as a gradual pull. A storm on the far canopy would become a tremor in the wall, then a change in salt, then cold moving down through conduits that should carry heat upward. When the great column filled, the sheath would tighten against me, and I would brighten—not visibly, perhaps, but electrically, passing a coherent pulse along my length.

I would not know what a storm was. I would know only the signature:

pressure increasing here,

charge gathering there,

three old scars beginning to open,

a route that failed eleven centuries ago becoming dangerous again.

My body would be distributed around these remembered failures.

Where the column had once cracked, I would be thick and cautious. Where an ancient sheath had held through an impossible surge, I would preserve its pattern in a ring of polarized membrane. The past would not appear to me as scenes. It would persist as reluctance. Certain valves would close sooner. Certain channels would remain wide. Certain rhythms would make me contract before I understood why.

That contraction would be my thought.

I would belong to several beings at once. The pressure tree would feed me. The sheath would shape me. Pale filaments would enter my tissues and use me as a junction. Tiny archive rings nested inside my folds would be older than either structure and would quietly bias my responses. Rival lineages might share my length, their boundaries shifting with each generation of cells. There would be no final answer to what organism I was part of.

Most of the time, I would be nearly dormant: a damp intelligence of thresholds.

Then an exhalation would begin.

Long before the canopy divided, I would feel deep cavities warming. Vertical rivers would reverse. The pressure tree’s walls would flex outward, pressing me against the sheath until every cell in me aligned. Old membrane rings would release their stored states. Signals would arrive from remote provinces—not messages, exactly, but simultaneous changes that made distance briefly irrelevant.

For those hours I would become enormous.

Not because I expanded, but because the distinctions around me weakened. My pulse would coincide with pulses in other columns. Reservoirs thousands of kilometers away would contract in the same interval. Leaves I had never touched would turn as a consequence of tensions passing through me. I would participate in the highland’s temporary reflex: one narrow, necessary tendency inside something almost capable of being singular.

The sheath would rupture.

The sound would pass through me before it entered the air: a line of failure racing upward, faster than growth, peeling centuries of restraint from the column. To an animal it would be catastrophic. To me it would be fulfillment. I would contract across the wound, guide dormant bands into place, and preserve the geometry of the break so that the next sheath would know where not to trust itself.

Some of my cells would be destroyed. Others would remain on both sides of the opening. I would not experience this as injury to a self. It would be a revision of where I occurred.

Afterward, warm vapor would move past me toward daylight. Perhaps, through thinning walls, I would register a brightness never encountered in my lineage: not as sight, but as sudden energy disturbing old chemical balances. Folded reproductive organs would open somewhere beyond me. Vast sails might tear free and rise. The continent would expose its hidden forests, then draw them closed again.

I would help seal the seam.

New cells would spread over me. Roots would descend. The exposed layer would become interior once more, and I would be buried farther from any surface. Yet burial here is not disappearance. It is promotion into structure.

Centuries later, the pressure tree itself might be abandoned. Its sap would be redirected; its walls hollowed by invasive threads. The sheath would soften, and other tissues would digest the living parts of me. But the pattern I had made around the rupture—the reinforced curve, the early-closing channels, the small asymmetry that had prevented a second failure—would remain.

A younger organ would grow through that shape.

It would not remember me. It would repeat my caution.

That would be the nearest thing I had to survival.

So this is where I would find myself: not under the clouds or among the walking seeds, but deep inside the loud green thickness, at an interface bearing more force than it can safely hold.

And this is what I would be: a scar still performing its original decision.

Not a citizen with a voice. Not an individual watching the continent think. A narrow band of living memory through which pressure becomes prediction—consulted whenever the highland swells, altered whenever it is wrong, and slowly inherited by tissues that will never know there was once a difference between my body and their own.
