I would be found where a silent square opens into the full thunder of an avenue—not wholly within refuge, not wholly exposed.

I would be one of the old broad hunters, though “hunter” would describe only the few violent moments of my life. Most of the time I would resemble a fallen piece of the city: a low shelf of limestone beneath a veil of dust, my limbs folded into cracks, my scales edged with the first blue grains of lichen. Beetles would cross my back. Moths might shelter under my jaw. A brittle root-cord would slowly grow across one hind foot, and I would let it.

My world would not be darkness. Darkness would be as meaningless to me as silence is to the stone. I would know the city through my lower jaw.

Each surface would have a texture of pressure. The main avenue would grind continuously against the fluid chambers in my skull. The great plaza behind me would pulse at a slower interval, making the mineral plates of my teeth ache. Far overhead, the vault colony would pass its contractions across the ceiling, a vast soft pattern descending through pillar and floor. Beetle files would be a fine, regular stitching. Burrowers beneath the salt would arrive as blunt subterranean turns. The fungal groves would have their own constant tremor: membranes shuddering, trunks carrying the wind’s buried voice downward into root-nets.

And the grazers would be warmth expressed as rhythm.

I would feel a herd long before it entered my stretch of street. Hundreds of feet, but not hundreds of messages: one collective body, thickening as it approached. I would know whether it moved calmly, whether alarm had begun at one edge, whether young animals traveled protected in the center. Hunger would not make me strike. Hunger would be almost permanent and therefore useless as a command. I would wait for alignment—for one limping rhythm, one animal separated by the passage around a pillar, one sudden gap in the herd’s field of touch.

Then, for a breath, I would become the quickest thing in the city.

Afterward, I would drag the carcass into an alcove beyond the cancellation line. I would feed slowly while scavengers assembled at a respectful distance. What remained would pass to mites, beetles, moth larvae, fungal cords, and eventually into the trembling membranes above. For months or years I would withdraw into stillness. The mites beneath my scales would close the small wounds with chewed fiber. Dust would restore my disguise. The city would forget the interruption.

But I would not live only beside a herd road.

As I aged, I would begin to seek something my kind usually avoided: the center of the silent square.

At its boundary, the city would disappear from my jaw by degrees. First the high abrasive note of the avenue would fade. Then the plaza’s deep pulse would weaken. Footfalls beyond the margin would become uncertain and displaced, no longer attached to direction. Inside, I would be nearly senseless. A grazer might stand a body-length away and seem farther than a distant collapse. The small creatures born there would touch me before I knew they existed.

That helplessness would draw me back.

Perhaps, in my species, there would be no concept for curiosity separate from appetite, migration, or reproductive impulse. Yet I would return to the still place after each long dormancy. I would rest with my jaw pressed to the quiet floor and attend to the absence.

There I would learn that silence was not empty. It held minute signals erased elsewhere: fungal cords tightening as they grew; salt crystals fracturing under changes of pressure; larvae turning inside eggs; the minute rasp of grazers feeding; my own layered heartbeat. I would discover that the thunder had hidden the nearest world.

The animals of the refuge would slowly cease to fear me. They would learn the pressure pattern of my approach and the fact that I did not hunt inside the cancellation zone. Grazers would sleep against my flanks, taking advantage of the heatless shelter of my body. Wingless moths would leave scented threads along my mineral-crusted claws. Newly emerged burrowers would use the hollow beneath my chest as a passage. Spores would collect between my scales and germinate in the waste of the creatures gathered around me.

Over decades, I would become an extension of the grove.

This would not make me gentle. Outside the square, I would still kill. The road would still require its waiting mouth. But my territory would gain an unusual shape: an ambush corner at one end, a nursery refuge at the other, with my migrations between them carrying spores, mites, dung, shed scales, and the bodies of small hitchhikers across a boundary they rarely crossed. Without intending it, I would join two districts of life.

Eventually the roof would begin to fail.

I would feel it before the grove did: not as danger, but as a new thin line beneath the roar, a discontinuity repeated whenever the far ridges drove their pressure down through the limestone. Over years it would lengthen. Tiny chips would fall. The vault colony would contract away from one seam. Beetle files would alter their route around fresh dust. The herds would continue using the avenue because their inherited path was stronger than the faint warning.

I would not understand collapse. No creature there would. But the changed pressure would make my old hunting place intolerable. I would move farther along the road, and the grazers would begin bending around my new position. Others would follow their touch. A route used for generations would gradually shift toward the silent square and then skirt its grove.

Thus I would warn them without foresight.

When the hall fell, I might be in the refuge, nearly blind to the greater vibration. The floor would lift. The grove would burst into gray-green brilliance as every membrane strained at once. A pressure too large to resolve would pass through my skull. Pillars would break; air would strike like a solid body; ceiling colonies would descend in torn living sheets.

Perhaps I would survive beneath the intertwined root-cords and layered fungal fans. Perhaps I would not.

If I died, my body would remain near the margin where silence met thunder. The refuge creatures would feed on me. Beetles would enter beneath my scales. Mites would abandon their sealed chambers and disperse. Fungal cords would follow the moisture and minerals held inside me, enclosing my ribs in their brittle net. The new road would curve around the fallen stone and the fallen animal together.

For a few centuries, my jaw might remain pressed to the floor.

It would sense nothing. Yet the city would continue through it: the endless resonance entering bone, crossing mineral deposits in the fluid chambers gone dry, and passing back into limestone. Dust would fill me. Salt would articulate my joints. Lichen would erase the border between scale and rock.

At last I would become what I had always imitated—not a ruler or guardian of the buried city, but one more accidental piece of its architecture.

A low pale threshold.

A place where small things slept.
