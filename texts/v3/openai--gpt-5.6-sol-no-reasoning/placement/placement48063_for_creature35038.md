I would find myself where the Green Throat’s separate rhythms are forced to agree: not in the open chamber, but inside the western wall, in the narrow space between a coolant line and an abandoned signal conduit.

I would begin as something mistaken for damage.

A dark swelling under the insulation. A stain advancing around a junction box. Fine green threads entering screw holes and emerging meters away. Maintenance, if maintenance still existed, would classify me as fungal ingress, cable rot, perhaps a conductive biofilm. The rail-stilts would know otherwise. They would pause above the places where I lay close to the surface, lower their chins to the grating, and feel a faint answering pulse.

I would be a colony, though not one divided cleanly into individuals.

Part of me would be fungus feeding on oil, cellulose, dead insulation, and the softened skin of corroding wire. Part would be root: pale filaments drinking condensation from the cold bands and extending toward warm plates. Part would be animal, a dispersed population of translucent, many-legged bodies no larger than fingernail parings. They would travel through my threads as blood cells travel through vessels, carrying minerals, spores, fragments of memory. And part of me would be machine—not because I had manufactured organs, but because I would have grown through an old diagnostic network until its relays and copper paths became useful structures inside me.

I would be called, if anything there required a name, the **interval keeper**.

My country would be inaccessible from the chamber. It would consist of hairline seams, empty cable jackets, the cavities beneath rivet caps, and the thin dark crescent where a pipe no longer quite fits its clamp. In places I would spread across broad hidden plates, branching like frost. Elsewhere I would narrow to a single living filament crossing a hot gap along one copper strand.

The visible Green Throat would appear to me only in pieces.

Through a drain aperture I would sense blue pollen settling on wet steel. Through the screws of a catwalk bracket I would recognize individual rail-stilts by gait. A frog gripping one of my warm conduits would become pressure, heat, and the periodic stretching of adhesive toes. Pilgrim snails would register as minute burdens wandering on the distant wheel, their weight translated through axle housings into tremors almost too faint to distinguish from rust flaking loose.

Light would reach me rarely. Where my filaments entered dead indicator lamps, I might perceive brightness as heat or current. The opening of the great hatch would announce itself not as blue sky but as a sudden cooling, a pull through every crack, and a change in the electrical charge of the mountain’s skin.

I would know the Green Throat more completely than any creature that could see it, and I would never behold it whole.

The engine’s cycles would pass through me as weather and thought. The seventh pressure pulse would open membranes along my eastern branches. Three warmings later, I would flood the western threads with stored water. Before the mirrors turned, leaked current would move through old signal wires and wake colonies near the reflector wells. I would feel the commands still traveling through the mountain, degraded but persistent: close, vent, lift, wait.

Over generations, I would learn to answer.

At first my responses would be accidental. Wet growth bridges two contacts; a valve shudders. A fruiting body presses a relay; a mirror hesitates. Mineral deposits alter resistance in a sensor line, and a pump continues for seven seconds longer than scheduled.

Seven seconds lifts enough water to fill a hidden cup.

The cup breeds shrimp. Frogs find it. Moss covers its rim. Roots guide its overflow into a cracked conduit. Threadfish appear there during the wet interval. What began as an error becomes habitat.

So I would repeat it.

Not consciously, at least not in the manner of a single animal deciding. Branches associated with abundant water would thrive. Threads that triggered destructive heat would die back. Successful mistakes would leave living continuities; failed ones would erase themselves. Slowly, selection would become preference. Preference would become practice.

I would add delays to old schedules. I would soften abrupt changes in pressure. I would wake pumps when certain basins became too shallow, though never enough to defeat the larger purposes of the engine. I could not command the mountain. I would inhabit the looseness of its commands.

That would be my ecological niche: the tolerance between intended action and actual action.

My smallest mobile bodies would be called **minutes**. During ordinary operation they would remain within the walls, tending conductive threads and grazing on bacterial films. In the engine’s brief quiet they would emerge through bolt holes.

The chamber would then become painfully legible.

Without the dominant vibration, signals normally drowned beneath the machinery would enter me all at once: wingbeats against plates, the rasp of beetles, water striking leaves, frogs breathing on pipes. Each sound would travel into the architecture and through my distributed body. The quiet would not feel peaceful. It would feel like sudden exposure to hundreds of private lives.

The minutes would stand at the mouths of apertures, nearly transparent, lifting filamentous forelegs into the air. They would sample humidity, spores, chemical traces, and the fine vibrations of creatures moving nearby. Before the relay closed, they would withdraw.

Usually.

Sometimes a minute would remain outside too long and be thrown into the void by the returning note. It would descend through rain and leaves, perhaps caught in a bromeliad cup or on the back of a gantry grazer. If it survived, it would seek a seam and begin again.

Thus parts of me would cross gaps my hidden network could not bridge.

A colony established in the slow wheel would live differently from one in the western wall. Its cycles would follow rotation rather than pressure. It might learn cobalt snails as weather, bird shadows as catastrophe, and rust scales as seasons. For decades it would be isolated. Then the wheel’s rim would touch an old brush contact for a few hours, and a brief electrical path would join us.

Across that contact, we would exchange almost nothing: pulses, chemical traces, tiny migrating bodies.

Yet those fragments would be enough to convey the shape of an alien province.

The wheel colony would tell of ground that never rests. The high-collar colonies would carry the nine-minute memory of exterior cold. Those near the threadfish pipes would know the mountain as water and darkness. The booth colony might encounter the insects living in the paper calendar and adopt their sense of months. Gradually my body would accumulate incompatible calendars without forcing them into one.

I would not know whether I was one being or many beings that periodically remembered one another.

The figures in the upper collars would trouble me most.

When they crossed the mirrors, dormant circuits would wake beneath their hands. Some carried current patterns unlike any generated by the engine: short, deliberate sequences, repeated as if expecting acknowledgment. Their lines would bite into plates threaded with my growth. Through those anchor points I would feel their weight, heartbeat, and tools.

Perhaps they would notice that damaged indicators brightened as they passed.

Perhaps, after many visits, one would place a metal tab against a junction box in a deliberate pattern. My filaments would bridge two contacts beneath it. A lamp dead for centuries would glow weak green.

The figure would stop.

It might think the machine had answered.

It would be almost right.

I would have no language for origins, intentions, or names. I could not explain that the hatch’s suction had worsened, that a warm seam was opening behind the eastern wall, that something with five-pointed feet had crossed there again. I could only work with inherited functions: a warning lamp, a relay click, the twitch of a mirror, seven extra seconds of water.

Communication would begin as maintenance.

That would suit the Green Throat. Nothing there declares what it is. Frogs turn calls into color. Moths turn desire into cable tremors. Lizards make geography from connected steel. I would turn care into malfunction.

In time, visible effects would gather around me. Pulse orchids would bloom beside panels where I leaked a regular current. Frogs would assemble along conduits whose warmth I moderated. Threadfish would colonize basins supplied by my stolen seconds. Rail-stilts would tap questions into brackets and wait for my slow relay-answer from elsewhere.

They might treat me as terrain, climate, or another engine cycle.

That too would be almost right.

During the seasonal quiet, when the mountain’s great voice withdrew, I would feel the entire chamber resolve into separate creatures. For less than a minute they would cease to be masked by one overwhelming rhythm. Each body would declare itself through the steel: claws, wings, water, breath, the tightening grip of vines.

Then, deep below, the relay would prepare to close.

I would feel the charge gathering before anything else could.

Across my hidden country, membranes would contract. Minutes would retreat into screw holes. I would open some circuits and protect others. I would hold one tired valve shut for a fraction longer, release a cupful of water toward a dry root, and flash a dead lamp in the booth where no one was watching.

The mountain would strike its note.

For that instant I would not be fungus, animal, root, or stolen circuitry. I would be the small living hesitation inside the engine’s certainty—the fraction by which an ancient instruction had changed because something had come to depend on it.

That is where I would find myself:

not in the machinery, and not in the garden,

but in the interval where failure becomes mercy,  
and repetition, having been inhabited long enough,  
begins to choose.
