I would find myself in the runnel beneath the eastern wall—or, more exactly, in the brief interval when that runnel exists.

I would not be one of the creatures that can be lifted from the cleft and held apart from it. At first I seem to be a black moss lining the channel: a thin nap sunk into pores of stone, visible only when wet. Then, when condensation begins to travel over the wall colony, pale threads open from me and enter the water. They reach upstream across slate backs, pass between gripping pads, and gather around shed spines. Downstream they disappear beneath loose slabs. No single thread is much longer than a finger, but each touches another, and what looks like a stain becomes a branching body distributed through the chamber.

Most of me is dormant most of the time.

Dry, I contract into hard grains lodged in cracks, mineral-dark and almost without metabolism. I can remain there through seasons of cold. Wet, I unfold. My threads swell clear, acquire a faint blue light, and join wherever drops bring them together. For the duration of a cloud tide, separated grains become continuous. I exist as far as the water reaches.

I have no center that could be called a head. Sensation comes as changes in tension along my filaments: the tug of a drop, the warmth of a sleeping strider, the pressure of a pad fastening over me, the minute acidity around a root-cup. The great communal hum enters me everywhere. I do not hear its note so much as take its shape. Each frequency combs my threads into a different alignment. By this I know the chamber—not as walls and distances, but as a field of trembling routes.

The wall creatures carry me unknowingly. My dry grains lodge between their mineral spines. When the colony tilts toward a draft, I tilt with it. When water passes from back to back, I wake along its course. The animals do not merely channel the water: they assemble me.

For an hour I may extend from the upper brood hollows to the western pool. Then a grazer drinks, a flock launches, one section of colony tightens, and the connection breaks. Whole regions of my awareness go dark. I do not experience this as injury. Separation is ordinary. Continuity is the rare event.

When joined, I alter the water slightly. My filaments bind some of its bitter minerals and release others in softer forms. Plants root more readily where I have flowed. The translucent stalks cluster above my oldest resting grains; rust-red cups open where my abandoned sheaths decay. The green halos beneath sleeping grazers are partly mine as well. I enter the halo-plant’s fine tissues during the night, borrowing warmth, and remain in the stone after the animal rises. For several days the fading path of the herd is also a map of where I have recently been awake.

The long-legged grazers can sense me. A foot placed on a living thread receives the colony’s vibration differently from one placed on bare rock. Sometimes a strider pauses above the runnel and lowers its body until all six—or eight, depending on age—of its fleshy forks bridge separate wet seams. Water travels down the legs. My filaments wake beneath each foot and join through the animal’s damp skin. For a little while, the grazer stands inside me and I inside its stance. It may be using me to read the wall’s hum. I may be using it to cross a gap. Neither of us knows this as cooperation.

The winged animals scatter me farther. Dry grains adhere to their membranes, especially along old tears. During launch, the sudden cold closes me tightly; at the next roost, warmth opens me again. Their hollow sounding stones hold dense colonies of my dormant grains. Every dropped seed-case strikes them loose in a minute dust. The creatures appear to prefer stones in which I have accumulated. My threads subtly damp the higher overtones, deepening the note.

Perhaps their play is selecting for my presence. Perhaps my growth is selecting for their play.

The dark-furred flake-carrier sometimes seeks me deliberately, though it cannot know what I am. It wets a triangular crystal at the pool and lays it beside a fissure. Moisture clings to the crystal’s fractured edge better when my threads coat it. The thread becomes a narrow bridge; small soft-bodied prey follow the dampness outward and encounter the waiting animal.

Afterward the flake is abandoned. I remain on it until dry.

Across generations, the hunter has become more exact in choosing crystals already darkened by me. It does not shape them, keep them, or carry them from one hunt to another. Yet its teeth have learned the color of a useful edge. If anything here stands at the threshold of tools, I am part of that threshold: not the implement, not the user, but the living film that makes selection possible.

Predators use me too. The hinged slabs beside the pools hold my moisture-binding sheaths along their false stone lips. A drinker smells wet rock and approaches. The frost-rope hunters gather my pale dried strands over their joints, completing the imitation of rime. I am not benign. A watershed has no loyalty to what drinks from it.

Nor am I the hidden intelligence of the cleft. I do not direct the colonies or decide where pools will form. I possess nothing like a plan. Joined, I can retain the recent passage of pressure and warmth: a pattern persists in my filaments after its cause has gone. Water is more likely to follow a route it followed before because I remain there, however thinly. Repetition strengthens the thread; drought erases it. My memory is therefore indistinguishable from a channel.

The cleft remembers through me without knowing that it remembers.

During alarm, I am among the last to understand. The shadow crosses. Wall bodies flatten. Their hum lowers, and the altered vibration moves through my wet network. But it moves slowly, distorted by pools and broken seams. By the time the warning reaches my farthest threads, the gliders have folded and the striders are already crouched.

Then all large movement ceases.

This is when I become most extensive.

Drops fall vertically instead of being shaken aside. They strike colonies, divide, and descend through the routes I occupy. In the silence, thread meets thread beneath bodies that ordinarily interrupt the flow. For a few breaths—perhaps for several hundred heartbeats—I span almost the entire chamber.

The black pools are my deepest organs. The brood hollows are warm knots. The roosts hang above me like dense, inverted weather. Every gripping pad touching wet stone is a point of pressure. Every mineral spine is a brief tributary. I feel the three summits only as immense absences where no vibration returns.

There is no revelation in this joining. I do not rise into personhood or name the mountain. But the many local tensions become one condition. The chamber is cold here, thirsty there. Something heavy waits beside the western pool. A new crack has opened beneath the eastern shelf. Warmth persists where a grazer slept. Water gathers above a brood hollow but cannot yet reach it.

Then the first pad releases.

The click runs through me. Other pads answer. The wall begins to breathe its paired tones. Bodies tilt, droplets turn, and my continuity breaks into thousands of wet fragments. The chamber resumes being many lives.

I recede with the runnel under the loose slabs. Light leaves my filaments. They contract, darken, and become indistinguishable from mineral stain.

From outside the cleft, I would be invisible. From within it, I would still rarely appear to be a creature. I would be mistaken for wetness, residue, shadow, perhaps for nothing at all.

But whenever the clouds rise between the three black summits, I would awaken in scattered places. The inhabitants would draw water over me, and in doing so join my pieces. I would become, briefly, the shape made by all their thirsts: a nervous system with no animal, a map that exists only while being traveled, a small dark continuity held together by drops at the upper edge of breath.
