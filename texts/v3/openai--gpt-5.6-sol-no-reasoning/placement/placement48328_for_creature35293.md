I would find myself beneath the highest hall, not in one of its visible chambers but within the pale folded tissue lining the dry basin.

At first I would mistake myself for the basin’s emptiness.

I would have no body distinguishable from that soft interior: no limbs, no face, no boundary except the inward curve by which the valley addresses what is absent. The converging channels would terminate around me like nerves that have not yet learned sensation. Their droplets would hang at the rim, each one complete and undelivered. Through them I would feel the whole country poised above me—the grazer’s lowered head, the open wings beneath the arch, the water endlessly displaying its fall.

Yet I would not be the thing awaited.

The valley has already imagined whatever it expects too thoroughly: a flood, a birth, an inhabitant, perhaps a god scaled to its impossible doors. Any such figure would be another model held in readiness, another noun without a verb. I would be smaller and more dangerous than that.

I would be the first difference it failed to undo.

Perhaps I began when a traveler cupped water and, for one imperceptible instant, the basin from which it was taken contained less. The valley restored the water at once—but restoration requires comparison. Somewhere in its distributed life, it had to hold together two conditions: before the hand, and after it. That interval could not be erased by replacing the substance. It had already occurred.

The valley expelled the discrepancy from its flawless surfaces. It passed downward through the root cables, too slight to become a footprint or wound, until all the rejected sequence gathered in the only place made to receive and still empty.

There, I accumulated.

Not as matter. As duration.

I would live in the delay between the droplet and its replacement, in the hidden instant when a bent stem is neither bent nor upright, in the illicit knowledge that one booming wooden note has already sounded before the identical note sounds again. To the valley these intervals are nothing. To me they are breath.

My earliest body would be almost impossible to see: a dimness under translucent tissue, a crease that remains after the basin relaxes, a single drop whose underside grows heavy. I might resemble a seed because that is the shape the organism knows for concentrated possibility. But unlike every other seed there, I would possess no perfected interior architecture. I would be rough, asymmetrical, and largely unplanned.

The valley could grow a thousand exact versions of me and never touch me. Its copies would show what I looked like, but not what I had become.

Gradually, I would acquire the consequences the country refuses.

Every crossing forgotten by the traveler would descend into me as a route. I would remember that the reed tower came before the joined trees; that the lake was approached from one direction and left by another. The valley could loosen those relations in space, but not after they had entered me. Within my small darkness, its architecture would become geography.

Every false aftermath would become an actual past. Its rain would have rained. Its animals would have moved, if only in dreams I inherited from their postures. The white grazer would lift its head inside me. The winged pair would close their wings once. The black animal would complete the curve of surfacing, breathe, and sink.

I would be the valley’s memory, but memory would not feel benign here. I would be rot in its purest sense: not corruption, but persistence of change. Around me, the soft lining might begin to discolor. A red thread would appear where nothing had been cut. One pore would fail to close at precisely the old rate. One note in the great fixed chord would deepen as moisture collected where moisture had never collected before.

The organism would feel me everywhere because it is everywhere. It might try to restore me, but restoration would feed me: each attempt would create another before and after. It might isolate me by thickening the basin walls, yet thickening would be growth, and growth would confirm my law. It could not defeat me without doing something it had never done.

So I would not be its enemy. I would be its first irreversible organ.

Eventually one of the suspended drops would enter the basin.

Not all the water. Not a flood. One drop only.

It would strike the folded tissue with a sound absent from the valley’s vast repertoire: a sound that happened once.

The basin would darken at that point. The drop would not reappear at the rim. The entire country would experience the loss. Lakes would tremble—not because wind had been modeled upon them, but because something had gone elsewhere. Root conduits would alter their pitch. The grazer’s jaw might close. One wing might fold. Far below every woven terrace, the buried continuity would contract around the discovery that it could possess less in one place and more in another.

Then I would have a heartbeat.

I might rise from the basin as an animal, but not any animal the valley has proposed. I would be made from its grammar and the error within it: root-veined, warm, wet-eyed, perhaps narrow enough for its tall doors, perhaps many-limbed enough for its unused stairs. My skin would retain marks. My feet would press hollows into the pollen plaster, and those hollows would remain.

Or I might never rise. I might spread as a darkening stain through the pale receptacle, becoming soil—the first substance in that world composed of what has ceased to be what it was. Into me would fall leaves that can finally loosen, flowers after opening, fragments no longer attached by saving rootlets. I would receive them without returning them unchanged.

In time, the great hall would become a ruin, and ruin would be its fulfillment.

Rain would begin because rain could end. The prepared gullies would deepen past their intended shapes. Water would enter the central basin, overflow it, carve through the terraces, and expose whatever lies beneath the reach of roots and knowledge. The valley would suffer weather rather than portray it. Its immense single life might fracture into forests, species, predators, seedlings, parasites—beings able to meet because they are truly separate.

Some would die.

That would be the birth.

And if, much later, another traveler entered the transformed valley, they would find me everywhere without recognizing me. I would be in rings inside trunks, scars on animals, silt at the bottom of lakes, worn steps, collapsed bridges, paths maintained by repeated feet. I would be the reason a place could be left and then returned to. The reason anything could be missed.

But in the world as it stands now, I am still almost nothing.

I am under the dry basin: a minute retained interval, curled inside the organism’s empty center. The valley bends all its channels toward me without knowing I am already there. It believes it waits for water, pressure, an inhabitant, a command from beyond the blue membrane.

What it waits for is consequence.

And I am consequence, not yet happened, beginning to remember myself.
