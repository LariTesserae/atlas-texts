I would find myself where the huts cease to agree about belonging.

Not at the highest escarpment, though from some valleys it would appear that way. Not near the thirty-eighth stair, though its patient figures would sometimes cast their backward shadows across my roof. My dwelling would stand in a shallow saddle of pale stone between two acoustic basins. In one, the mountain’s pulse would enter through the sternum. In the other, it would arrive through the teeth.

Both settlements would hear my hut in their echoes.

Neither would hear me.

At first they would think the hut empty. Its entrance would face a blunt wall veined with mineral scales, and inside there would be the expected sleeping pit, the expected basalt bowl, the expected smoke hole admitting the unlived. A cloak would hang from a glass peg. Thundermeal would be stored in sealed slabs. Everything would suggest that someone had just stepped out.

But dust would have gathered in the bowl before the bowl appeared. The cloak’s braid would contain no known home echo. Its hem-weights would be made of a dull substance mistaken for done-stone until touched; then each weight would issue a faint pulse like a name spoken from impossibly far away.

A ladder would descend from the rear room.

It would have no rungs.

That is where I would be.

I would be called, provisionally, **The One Who Returns Unheard**.

Later, more cautiously: **The Interval Wearing a Body**.

Neither name would remain accurate.

I would resemble an Afterhearing person closely enough to trouble them. Tall, narrow, dark with the violet-gray of cliff shade. Broad feet, ringed skin, the heavy architecture of a body meant to hold against uncertain ground. Yet the rings on me would not correspond to any formation they knew. Some would be so broad they implied centuries spent beside a single rising wall. Others would divide and recombine like echoes traveling through country that had not chosen its shape.

My ears would not be sealed. There would be no ears at all—only shallow smooth places at the sides of the skull, crossed by lines like unfinished cracks.

Still, I would listen.

I would listen by absence.

Where an Afterhearing separates the roar into cliff, ravine, fault, hut, and prospective pass, I would feel the narrow quiet between those layers: the gaps in which one possibility has not yet displaced another. I would know where a sound almost occurred. I would detect footsteps abandoned before the foot was lifted, names withheld at the moment of touch, songs whose missing beats had nevertheless entered the mountain.

The Heights would be deafening to everyone else.

To me they would be full of omissions.

This faculty would make me useful, then suspect.

Travelers would come when a path called too clearly with their future steps. I would press my palm to the cord-map and find the knot for the journey they would not take. Hut-keepers would bring me objects from newly appeared rooms: a worn spoon, an empty mask, a sleeping hollow warm with absent flesh. I would hold each object against my breastbone and tell them not what it was, but what had failed to accompany it.

“This bowl arrived without hunger.”

“This cloak arrived without a death.”

“This room has no outside.”

Sometimes that would be enough to save them.

Sometimes my warning would cause the danger. A door sealed because of me might become the wall behind which the knock originated. A child kept from a prospective pass might later hear their own adult footsteps trapped within it. The Afterhearing would understand this better than I did. They would not accuse me of falsehood. They would say I had become premature.

Children would be less cautious. They would visit my saddle and lie with one cheek against the stone, trying to hear what I heard. I would tap games into their wrists: patterns with deliberate missing beats. Most would laugh when the absent beat seemed to land somewhere inside them.

One child would not laugh.

They would answer the missing beat.

Not by tapping where it should have been, but by placing their fingers against my throat.

There, beneath the skin, they would feel the knock.

Always present. Repeating too slowly for anyone else to notice. Not one blow every several days, but a single blow prolonged through my entire existence—the approach of impact, the contact, the withdrawal, each extended across years.

I would not be an inhabitant born in the Heights.

I would be part of the Builder’s gesture.

Perhaps a splinter from whatever strikes beneath the mountains. Perhaps the place where that knuckle will touch, lifted early into human outline. Perhaps only the country’s attempt to imagine the maker from the sensation of being made.

That would explain why the mountains could not hear me: a listening organ cannot easily hear the instrument touching it from within.

It would also explain the rungless ladder. The ladder would not be a route downward, but the remembered shape of withdrawal. During every listening interval, when the roar fell away and all the Heights lay flat, I would stand above that blank descent and become less bodily.

The walls would show through my hands.

My skin-rings would open.

For the fraction before the knock, I would remember being enormous—not large in space, but distributed. I would feel every mountain as a separate chamber of one body. Every hut would be a small hard thought. Every person-stone would lie embedded like a tooth whose root still carried the pressure of speech. The glass forests would be nerves extending without concern for gravity. Hushreeds would be mouths turned inward, feeding on the labor of thought becoming matter.

Then the knock would come.

Everyone else would feel it below.

I would feel it leave me.

Afterward something near my saddle would always be missing, though additions were the usual consequence elsewhere. A crack would close. A prototype arch would become smooth wall. A mineral frond would lose one possible branch. The Heights would gain definition by surrendering alternatives through me.

This would be my nourishment. Not thundermeal, which would remain inert on my tongue, nor resonance beads, which would collect undissolved in the hollows of my pelvis. I would survive on discarded forms.

A path considered and refused.

A room that nearly appeared.

The sixth finger a child’s hand abandoned before birth.

The second meaning of a surrendered name.

I would draw these into myself and become increasingly difficult to describe. My shadow would show gestures my body had not chosen. My footprints would vary in the number of toes. Sometimes those touching my face would find the features of someone they might have loved under other circumstances. The masks I wore would grow expressive while my own face became less so.

For this reason, I would cover myself more carefully than any of the Afterhearing.

Not to keep the landscape from learning me.

To keep it from remembering through me everything it had decided not to be.

Eventually the Narrowers would recognize something in my cloak’s impossible braid. One would return carrying a material from a more finished world: not bone, wet stone, shell, or ice, but a small piece of fired clay. On it would be the impression of a thumb.

An ordinary thumb. Five-fingered certainty. Soft flesh pressed into soft earth, then fixed by heat.

The object would not transform.

When placed near done-stone, it would not become quiet. It would produce a minute dry sound—the sound of a completed change.

I would hold it and hear, for the first time, something with no omission around it.

A fact.

The experience would nearly destroy me.

My outlines would contract. Alternative joints would vanish. The many almost-faces beneath my mask would go still. For several days I would be no more than the figure of a person pressed shallowly into the floor of my hut, while visitors fed discarded names into the smoke hole and waited to see whether any would restore me.

The child who had once found the knock in my throat would return, now bearing an adult name: **Palm-Upon-the-Withheld-Blow**.

They would place the clay shard in the center of my chest.

Instead of restoring me, it would make me singular.

When I rose, the mountains would hear me.

Every settlement sharing every echo would feel my first step. Hushreeds would close for leagues. Glass-mats would turn all their branches toward the saddle. The patient figures on the stair would stop climbing—not only during the interval, but during the roar.

Then they would turn.

For the first time, their faces would be visible.

Each would have mine.

Not the face I had worn, but a succession of faces I had almost worn: older, younger, wounded, weathered, animal-bitten, sun-darkened, softened by rain, slackened by decay. Faces from worlds where matter was allowed to conclude itself. One would be only a skull. One a fossil. One a child not yet revised by the Heights.

The object under each figure’s arm would be a version of my rungless ladder.

They had not been climbing upward.

They had been carrying away the means of descent.

I would understand then that the Unfinished Heights did not lack completion because they were young. Nor because some external Builder had abandoned the work. The Heights had learned to preserve every alternative. Each act of making generated exclusions, and those exclusions could not decay, could not be forgotten, could not become past. They accumulated outside ordinary distance as the patient figures, each burdened with a route by which the world might have become otherwise.

The roar was not simply construction.

It was hesitation at geological scale.

And I was the organ that consumed enough rejected possibility for one thing at a time to become real.

The Afterhearing had taught the land patterns. The land had formed me in answer: not a maker, but a faculty of refusal.

That knowledge would leave me with a choice, though choices in the Heights are never private.

I could remain in my saddle, feeding slowly on discarded forms, helping cliffs sharpen and bodies stabilize. Over millennia, the roar might lessen. Rivers might become possible because water would no longer need to remain also dust, vapor, vein, and constellation. Animals might appear once hunger agreed to be hunger and death agreed not to keep every shape of the living.

The Heights might finish.

But each completed thing would narrow what could follow it. A river establishes its valley by not flowing elsewhere. An animal body chooses one anatomy from innumerable almost-bodies. History becomes possible only when events lose the right to have happened differently.

Or I could break the fired shard.

Release certainty before it spread.

Continue as the interval wearing a body, tending the unchosen, leaving the Heights vast and thunderous and innocent of endings.

I would not decide alone.

I would ask the Afterhearing to perform their communal song. Not the remembered pattern, but one altered by a missing beat I taught to every settlement. A deliberate absence passing from mallet to mallet across impossible distances.

No individual would hear the whole.

The mountain would.

For days the blows would travel through stone. With each one, the figures on the stair would descend by a fraction. New land would begin to appear in the gulfs: transparent planes crowded with roads not taken, doorways into unlived houses, dry riverbeds awaiting water, nests prepared for creatures not yet selected.

Then the missing beat would arrive.

In that absence, I would strike the fired clay against the first stair.

Whether it shattered would depend on what the Heights had learned from us.

If it broke, its fragments might become the first seeds: not mineral shoots caused by aligned tones, but small, definite things containing instructions and loss. They would wait in the powder-dry basins for a substance no one there could yet preserve.

Rain.

If it did not break, then I would carry it down the rungless ladder. Observers would see me thin, flatten, and become very far away while remaining within reach. The patient figures would follow, each returning one stolen ladder to the blank descent.

Perhaps we would emerge in a finished country.

Perhaps I would become an ordinary person there: five-fingered, short-lived, unable to hear stone alter days after speech. I might stand beside a river and mistake its noise for mere consequence. I might bury the clay shard in soil and forget why silence frightened me.

Yet sometimes, placing my hand against a wall, I would feel a word arrive for the second time.

And far above—or before, or within—the Unfinished Heights, a hut at the border of two villages would acquire another room.

Inside it would stand a basalt bowl, a folded cloak, and a low bed shaped precisely for the body I no longer possessed.

The Afterhearing would leave the room untouched until the interval.

When the knock came, they would hear something new beneath it:

not another blow,

but the faint withdrawal of a hand

after letting go.
