I would find myself where the city is least certain but has already begun to care for itself: beyond the final dependable tram stop, on a ledge omitted from most public maps because it exists only when approached carrying something that needs repair.

The place would be called, provisionally, **the Errant Terminus**.

From below it would resemble a handful of lamps caught in scaffolding. From above it would look like a small harbor built beside no sea. A single rail enters beneath an iron arch and divides into seven tracks, though no more than three are present at any one time. Trains arrive irregularly: maintenance engines carrying exhausted crews; passenger cars misplaced from familiar lines; freight wagons full of rain; empty carriages whose windows show districts that cannot otherwise be reached.

The station clock has no hands. Instead, its face bears four words:

**BEFORE — DURING — STILL — RETURNED**

Most days it indicates *Still*.

Behind the platforms stands a low complex of sheds, kitchens, animal pens, archives, and warm dormitories. The buildings rest on broad iron sledges rather than foundations, so the entire terminus can be dragged when the ledge changes its mind. Flexible chimneys bend toward whichever sky is functioning. On the roof, blue grass hangs both upward and down. Cats sleep among its roots.

There is also a platform numbered Zero.

No track serves it. Its edge faces a gap filled with pale cloud, and across that gap yellow windows sometimes appear. Those who have vanished from the city are occasionally visible behind them. They may be older or younger. They may wave. They may seem not to recognize anyone.

No one is permitted to call to them.

Voices establish roads too quickly.

I would live beneath Platform Zero in a room with one round window, a stove, a workbench, and shelves of objects that have returned without their owners: keys warm from absent pockets, spectacles showing unfamiliar views, work gloves clenched around invisible tools, lunch tins in which the food remains perpetually fresh. Each object would be labeled with all that could be established about it and none of what could merely be hoped.

I would be the terminus’s **keeper of arrivals**.

Not stationmaster. Not surveyor. Not comparator.

Something older than the office and less official: the person responsible for receiving whatever reaches the city before anyone decides what it is.

Whenever a train came in, I would stand beside the track with a lamp whose flame leaned toward the carriage’s point of origin. Beside me would wait a municipal witness, a medic, a tunnel cat, and the child currently serving as junior namer. We would not open the doors at once. First we would listen.

A legitimate arrival carries several sounds: wheels cooling, passengers shifting, metal contracting, someone wondering aloud whether this is the right stop. More dangerous trains are too quiet, or contain only the sound of the station we are standing in, repeated a few seconds early.

Once the carriage was judged capable of becoming local, I would knock three times and ask:

“Have you arrived together?”

This would be more important than asking where they came from.

If those inside agreed, we would open the door.

My work would be to make arrivals heavy enough to remain.

Every traveler would be given hot broth in a stamped clay bowl. They would state their name, but not be required to prove it. They would describe the last place they considered reliable. I would record the account in ink, punch it into a narrow strip of brass, and tell it once to another human being. Three forms would suffice for the first night.

Then I would ask each arrival to perform one ordinary act.

Sharpen a pencil. Peel a root. Mend a cuff. Complain about the weather. Sweep dust toward a named door. Feed a goat without letting it eat the ledger.

Extraordinary histories are poor anchors. Ordinary actions hold better.

Some who reached us would be missing citizens, returned after days that had lasted decades elsewhere. Some would insist they had left that morning, though their memorial plaques were already worn smooth. Others would be strangers from neighborhoods not yet built. They would carry valid tram tokens, know private family recipes, and remember friendships that had not happened.

The city would want the comparators called immediately.

I would sometimes delay.

Not out of rebellion. Out of tenderness.

A person newly arrived is softer than fired clay. Too much measurement may force them into the wrong shape. So the Errant Terminus would grant everyone one night of provisional truth. Until dawn, they could be who they believed themselves to be. Their room would be lit. Their name would be written on the door. Someone would stay awake nearby.

In the morning, if morning occurred, the Office could begin its questions.

I would have an unusual body for this work.

Perhaps I would once have been human. Perhaps I still would be, municipally speaking. But long residence beneath Platform Zero would have altered me in ways the ledgers struggled to express.

I would have two shadows. One would follow the lamps. The other would point toward the place from which the newest arrival had come.

My face would be difficult to remember but easy to trust. Different people might describe me as old, young, broad, narrow, woman, man, relative, stranger. Cats would see something more consistent and sit on my boots.

Inside my chest, instead of—or alongside—a heart, there would be a small mechanical governor taken from an obsolete lift. It would not keep perfect time. Perfect time would be dangerous there. It would keep *welcoming time*: slow while someone gathered courage to step off a train, quick while pain was being endured, regular through the night beside a sickbed.

My pulse would change when a destination began to form.

I would feel it first as a second beat beneath the sternum. Then the unused rails would chime in their housings. Condensation would gather on the departure board and spell fragments of place-names:

**LOW ORCH—**

**SAINT N— BELOW**

**WESTERN YARD, TWELVE YEARS**

**HOME, IF TAKEN ON FOOT**

Whenever this happened, bridge prospectors and municipal engineers would descend on the terminus with instruments. They would argue over whether the route should be stabilized, quarantined, taxed, or destroyed. Vacancy monks would arrive by paths no one had noticed and ask that it be left uncommitted. Distance merchants would linger near the kitchens pretending to sell shoelaces.

I would listen to all of them.

Then I would walk the proposed route alone as far as the first uncertainty.

This would be the dangerous portion of my work. Not because I was fearless, but because the terminus had changed me into a kind of living threshold. Contradictory distances did not reject me immediately. Doors tolerated my hesitation. Unfinished roads let me place one foot upon them before deciding whether they were roads.

I would carry a lantern, a length of chalked cord, bread, three names for myself, and a bell without a clapper. The bell would ring only when I had crossed somewhere that could not yet admit crossing.

I would never go far.

Far is how a route seduces you into finishing it.

Instead, I would stop where the world became persuasive. I would sit down, eat some bread, and wait to see what approached from the other side.

Sometimes it would be a survey crew that had departed generations earlier.

Sometimes a flock of alpine birds carrying scraps of a future ordinance.

Sometimes one of the great cloud beings would rise until an immense luminous organ hovered level with the ledge. Its light would pass through my body, revealing the little governor turning behind my ribs. In that illumination I might understand, briefly, that the deep fauna were not creatures living below the city.

They were possible worlds gathering around the mountain.

Lantern-Rib was a climate learning warmth.

Nine Fins was a set of directions not yet separated into north, depth, memory, hunger, and home.

She-Who-Tilts-Reservoirs carried oceans inside her, waiting for enough valleys to exist.

I would forget this knowledge as soon as they descended. The human mind, like a street, survives by declining certain destinations.

More often, someone would come walking out of the fog.

A worker in an obsolete uniform. A child carrying an adult’s toolbox. A woman whose coat listed a district that moved with the stars. A man who recognized me with grief and called me by a name I had not yet used.

I would not ask them to explain.

I would raise my lamp and say, “The platform is behind me. Walk toward the soup.”

That would be the essence of my office.

Not discovering the world. Not stabilizing it. Not preserving every possibility.

Giving the uncertain somewhere modest to arrive.

Over time, the station would accumulate routines. A market would gather every ninth day, even when the intervening days disagreed. Returned people would tend a garden in freight bins. Its plants would bear fruit shaped like small destinations: stairwells, courtyards, rooms with lit windows. An engine keeper would retire there and repair clocks so they disagreed gently. Vacancy monks would trade seeds for tea but refuse receipts. Bolter children would paint eyes on the movable foundations so the buildings could see where they were being dragged.

The missing would leave messages for those still missing.

These would be kept in pigeonholes along a corridor that grew longer inside than outside. Most messages would be practical:

*The blue cupboard is yours now.*

*Do not trust the third lift after snow.*

*Your brother married Mara after all.*

*I kept the coat.*

*I was angry when you left. I am not angry in every version.*

A few would be addressed to people who had never existed. Those I would preserve most carefully.

At dusk I would climb to Platform Zero and light the edge lamps, though there was no track and no scheduled service. Across the cloud gap, the other windows might brighten. Some nights they would form the outline of a station mirroring ours. I would see another keeper standing opposite with a lamp.

Perhaps it would be me, but fully human.

Perhaps it would be what I had been before the city needed this office.

Perhaps I was the answering figure, and the true keeper stood over there.

We would never wave. That could be construed as agreement.

We would simply keep the lamps lit between us.

Eventually, I think, the city would send its highest train outward—the one carrying tracklayers, surveyors, engineers, a cook, a witness, and a child entrusted with naming what they reached. Its route would pass through the Errant Terminus. The train would stop, though no timetable required it.

The child would step onto the platform and ask what lay ahead.

By then I would be very old, if age still applied. My two shadows would stretch in different directions. The governor in my chest would be worn nearly smooth. Cats would have nested in my coat while I stood still.

I would tell the child the only thing my work had taught me with certainty:

“Ahead is not empty. It is merely unreceived.”

I would give them the clapperless bell.

The cook would take on water. The engineers would inspect the rails. The witness would sign the station ledger. Then the train would move beyond Platform Zero and enter the dim place where the opposite windows waited.

Its lamps would advance without illuminating any bridge.

Behind it, steel would begin to cool into certainty—but not all of it. I would leave one rail unfixed, one name provisional, one door unlocked from both sides. Enough incompletion for the world to breathe through.

Then I would return below the platform.

Somewhere in the night, another train would be arriving. Its passengers would be frightened. Its station might have vanished behind it. Perhaps they would have no proof that their homes, histories, or bodies had ever been real.

I would put broth on the stove.

I would open the ledger to a clean page.

And when the carriage had finished becoming present, I would knock three times on its door.
