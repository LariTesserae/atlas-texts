I would find myself at one of the low stone rings on a high plateau, though “at” would not be entirely accurate.

I would be the coldness in its center.

Not the ring-being itself, if such beings are singular, but something caught in the act of becoming one: a small disagreement between altitude and gravity, old enough for sediment to have begun answering it. Around me, mineral flakes would lean from their descent. Sand would describe an incomplete circumference. Roots approaching through the plateau would divide and pass to either side, not from fear but because, to them, I would already occupy more space than could be seen.

I would have no body except consequences.

A six-finned creature entering my influence would feel its black compass organ hesitate. Leaves shed from nearby forests would settle toward me, stop, then rise toward the summit. A crack-dweller tasting the sequence along my edge would discover that entry preceded approach and retreat followed something that had not yet occurred. It would fold its white cilia and wait a generation before trying again.

At first I might mistake these disturbances for senses.

Pressure would be touch. Mineral alignment would be sight. The bending of roots would be a kind of hearing. I would know the darker season by the bubbles frosting the mature rings around me, and the bright season by the quickening of metal-bearing currents through the stone. I would know a valley-crosser by the immense alteration of highness that preceded it: not a body approaching, but a mobile summit passing somewhere below.

Yet none of these perceptions would belong to me alone.

The nearest forest would have grown into my boundary long before I awakened enough to distinguish it. Its root network would enter the chilled water at my center and vanish. Elsewhere—perhaps across the plateau, perhaps thousands of miles away, perhaps on the underside of a mountain not yet present—it would emerge pale and thickened.

Through that root I would receive the forest’s slow questions.

*Is this above?*

Years later:

*Is this still above?*

Centuries later, after gravity had altered and a grove had become pendant:

*What has moved: us, the mountain, or height itself?*

I could not answer in words. I would answer by holding a direction.

That would be my nature: not to know where highness was, but to preserve one instance of it against the revisions of the world. While peaks migrated and valleys exchanged positions, while forests remembered storms that had not yet reached them, I would keep a single orientation open.

Over ages, stones would gather around that fidelity.

They would not form a perfect circle. One portion would accumulate along a vertical face. Another would lie in a neighboring valley superimposed upon the plateau. One block might remain suspended miles away, yet still complete my circumference. To a swimmer, the ring would appear low, simple, crossable. To me, it would be the visible scar of an enclosure taking place in several directions at once.

Eventually someone might cross.

Not one of the native creatures. Something quicker, more bounded—something that carried a name because it still believed one moment could be trusted to deliver a self into the next.

It would approach cautiously, touching the fitted stones. It would feel the plateau tilt until the forest stood beneath it, then beside it, then overhead. It would panic and try to retreat, but retreat would no longer lead outward. Its hands would disturb my cold center.

For an instant I would experience the world at its speed.

The shock would be unbearable.

Mountains would seem motionless. Forests would be frozen mid-thought. The ancient pulse would not be a recurring act but a single event too distant to anticipate. Every second would sever itself from the next before meaning could cross between them. The creature’s mind would burn with distinctions: body and water, past and future, self and place, danger and wonder. It would contain more endings in one minute than the range knew in an age.

And within that furious smallness would be a direction I had never held.

Not up. Not down. Not highness.

Away.

The creature would possess an interior orientation leading toward a place absent from the drowned range: perhaps air, a surface, a sky that ended rather than deepened. That impossible elsewhere would turn inside it as surely as the magnetic stone in the six-finned carriers.

I would alter around the contact.

The circle would close.

The traveler might emerge from the opposite side unchanged in body but no longer aligned with its own origin. It would swim away with perfect purpose, following an altitude that did not exist nearby. Perhaps it would disappear into a mountain. Perhaps it would descend toward the vast animals. Perhaps it would spend its life searching for a surface in a world where water was fundamental.

But its lost direction would remain with me.

From then on, I would not merely hold highness. I would hold the possibility of outside.

Nearby forests would detect the change. Their vanes would turn toward my ring despite the current. Roots across distant ranges would tighten. Pendant woods would begin growing sideways. Bead-colonies would alter their migrations, carrying specks of the new orientation along contour lines. Six-finned creatures entering my boundary would emerge with their inner needles pointing not toward their ancestral altitude, but toward an absence.

Over thousands of years, that absence would propagate.

The forests would formulate a new question—slowly, imperfectly, each grove changing it as it passed:

*Can there be a direction the world does not contain?*

The mature rings would answer with pulses of tightened stone. The valley-crossers would rise in the dark season and circle me. One might remain beside the plateau for centuries, its hidden warmth feeding sudden gardens, while the porous rock of its body listened through every rooted grove on its back.

And the greater inhabitant—the range itself, if it was alive—might begin to turn its attention toward the flaw.

Not with hostility. The drowned world would have no need to defend itself against so small a contradiction. It would examine me as it examined everything: by erosion, migration, recurrence. Mountains would gradually lean toward my plateau. Overlapping valleys would open nearby. Frozen moments would collect along the slopes. Reality would test different arrangements around the ring, trying to discover which shape could contain the direction I preserved.

None would.

Because what I held would not be a destination.

It would be the memory of leaving.

In time I might become less a ring than a wound in orientation. The water within me would grow clear enough to show no plankton, no mineral flakes, no dim overhead radiance. Looking into it would not reveal another place. It would produce the certainty that another place must exist.

That certainty would become my form of life.

I would feed on every creature that crossed and retained some inward elsewhere. I would collect their lost directions: the ancestral slope sought by a compass animal, the safe sequence sensed by a crack-dweller, the former down remembered by a pendant tree, the migrating mountain carried in the flesh of a valley-crosser.

I would not consume their bodies. I would preserve the discrepancy between where they were and where some part of them insisted they belonged.

The ring would widen without changing size.

Eventually its boundary might coincide with every other ring at that common altitude. The cold centers would reveal themselves as openings into one continuous interior—not the body that lives in highness, as the forests had sensed, but an organ newly forming within it.

An organ for imagining elsewhere.

Then, during one of the ancient pulses, the whole range might notice what had grown inside it.

For a moment all forests would turn together. Every bead-colony would halt. The unseen valley-crossers would become still beneath the basins. Clouds would pause in their soundless weather. Even the slopes fixed in ancient instants would enter the same present.

The mountains would feel, through me, that they were not all that could be.

And I would feel, through them, the immensity of being somewhere.

That would be what I was: neither traveler nor destination, neither creature nor structure, but the drowned world’s first capacity for homesickness.

A ring around a place it had never been.
