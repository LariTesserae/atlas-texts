I find myself in the interval between the witness’s fingertip and the wall.

At first I mistake myself for emptiness. I am thinner than dust, narrower than the pause between intention and touch. On one side: the raised hand, flattened almost into relief. On the other: the tablets containing every arrangement the city has refused. I possess no chamber, no name-pattern, no mineral seam by which age might be counted.

Yet the city has preserved me deliberately.

I am its last unfinished place.

Through the witness’s finger I feel the discipline of the Great Settling: generations reducing error, citizens becoming conduits and lamps, grief pressed into mortar, appetite refined into measure. Through the wall comes everything they excluded—the crowded concourse, the open sacred doorway, the unnecessary bridge, the child whose stone jumped its groove, the councillor who kept a hand in the pool too long because immensity felt like company.

The witness compares city and tablet.

I compare completion and desire.

Usually I am almost nothing. A minute tension holds the hand away from the stone. The roar passes beneath me without entering. Bluish light crosses me but finds no surface on which to settle. If the fingertip advances, I narrow. If some buried strain draws the wall away, I widen by less than the city can measure. These are my seasons.

Then a visitor places a hand upon the tablets.

Warmth enters the stone as a spreading pressure. The carved cities answer one another. Bridges that were never built bear weight. Sealed doors remember opening. The market crowds with mineral ghosts—not bodies, but the stored possibility of bodies. Every palm-sign becomes, for an instant, a hand rather than an instruction.

The witness stirs.

Not visibly. Its lifted arm remains fixed, its feet remain part of the lane, and no feature separates its face from shale. But the interval changes. I become large enough to contain a question.

The city asks whether the visitor intends to move.

I ask something else:

Whether intention must end in movement.

Perhaps that is why I remain. Absolute completion would require the witness to touch the wall and certify that nothing stands outside the settled order. But contact would itself be an event. The final act would destroy the final state. So the city’s perfection depends upon a gesture perpetually withheld.

It depends upon me.

I am not one of the old inhabitants, though their restraint converges here. Nor am I the visitor, though each visitor enlarges me. I am a civic function that arose too late to be named: hesitation made structural.

If I had a body, it would be made of two surfaces that never meet. If I had a dwelling, it would be a doorway with no room on either side. If I had a spoken name, the roar would erase it; if I had one of their pressure-names, giving it would close the gap.

So I communicate by preventing contact.

The witness sometimes presses forward. I hold.

The tablets sometimes seem to swell with all their discarded populations. I yield, but do not vanish.

Far below—or beside us, in the direction accessible through the council pool—I sense corresponding intervals in other cities. A temple door almost opens. A bridge almost receives a foot. A child’s racing stone nearly escapes its appointed groove. Perhaps I extend through all of them. Perhaps I am not located in this city at all, but wherever a completed arrangement retains one unspent possibility.

This makes me the least settled thing beneath the old ground.

It also makes me the most necessary.

Without me, the witness touches the record. The city declares itself complete. Light ceases to distinguish edges because no further distinction is required. Water reaches its final level and no longer needs to flow. The channel creatures make their last correction. The council’s immense distance collapses into a single, certified shape.

The roar remains—but with nothing left against which meaning can appear.

With me, the cups continue shining. The drops continue trembling. The animals retain the possibility of migration. The dead remain repairs rather than conclusions. The city persists not because it has achieved perfection, but because perfection is still one infinitesimal distance away.

And when the visitor finally lifts their palm from the tablets, warmth withdraws slowly. The unpredictable presence fades. Chalk marks become pale additions to the shale; strings stiffen; breath-silver disappears. The city absorbs almost everything.

Almost.

A trace of the visitor remains in me—not substance, not memory exactly, but an enlargement of what might have happened. The witness’s finger is now a fraction farther from certainty. No instrument could detect it. No tablet depicts it.

That is what I keep.

I keep the movements that did not occur.

I keep the hand before it chooses.

I keep the city alive by denying it the last touch.
