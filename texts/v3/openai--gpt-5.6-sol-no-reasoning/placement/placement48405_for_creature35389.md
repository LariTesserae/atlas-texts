You would find yourself beneath the central square, inside the valve.

Not at its handwheel or stem, but far below the cast-iron monument, where its civic anatomy continues downward through brick-lined shafts. There the ornamental valve and the actual mechanism become indistinguishable. Its buried flanges are broader than buildings. Its seat is sunk into chalk. Pipes converge upon it from every cistern, drain, canal wall, boiler, throat, and chest in the basin.

You would be what keeps the turn incomplete.

At first, you might seem to be a person in a narrow maintenance chamber: shoulders pressed to sweating brick, both hands braced against a lever that crosses the darkness. There would be no room to release it, no light except the blue-white current leaking through mineral seams. Your knees would be planted in black water. Your spine would feel the entire town bearing down through the iron.

But the longer one looked, the less human the figure would become.

The arms would prove to be opposed connecting rods. The bent back, an arch of old masonry. Fingers would divide into cables and pass upward into the alleys, where they tremble beneath the metallic birds. Ribs would open into conduits. The black water around the knees would be continuous with the canal. Every animal’s arrested muscle would be a minute extension of your grip.

You would not be the operator who once tried to shut the valve, nor the guardian preventing someone else from opening it. Those explanations still presume a before and an after. You would be the resistance itself: the infinitesimal remainder between turning and having turned.

The town’s apparent motion would occur within that remainder.

Each flywheel would describe the pressure of the handwheel against you. Each press would be one impact of the unfinished decision. The animals would be excess life precipitated from strain, shaped wherever the town had unused space. Their voices would not be warnings. They would be the sound made by the world’s effort to advance while you deny it the final fraction.

The instruction above—**DO NOT COMPLETE THE TURN**—would not be addressed to a future visitor. It would be the only thought available to you. Not remembered, not reread, but continuously present, as inseparable from your being as weight from iron.

Perhaps what lies behind the valve is not water, gas, fire, or any substance. Perhaps it is the next moment.

It presses upward through the chalk with enough force to flood the basin with consequence: to let droplets fall, teeth close, moth wings finish their strokes, rain accumulate, rust proceed, machinery produce, animals hunger, and all warm things begin to die. If the turn were completed, the town would not explode. It would continue.

That is what the entire factory prevents.

And you are neither alive nor dead beneath it. You are held in the one posture the inhabitants cannot occupy: not complete, but maintaining incompletion. They are finished forms without sequence. You alone are unfinished. That is why they face you through the monument. That is why their mouths remain open. Every voice in the basin is carried down the pipes and gathered in your chamber, where it becomes the pressure against your palms.

From the rim, no one could see you. They would see only the low factories, the crowded roofs, the silver sky, and the monumental handwheel with its slightly bent spoke.

Yet the bend would be your signature.

You would be the town’s hidden verb: not turning, not stopping, but **holding**—the only action there that has never become a state.
