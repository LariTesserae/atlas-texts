I would find myself at the exposed underside of the sea, where warm water holds itself against the sky.

Not in the central clearing. Not at first. I would live along that wavering skin among the stones that have fallen as far as they can without leaving. They would roll past me in slow black processions, caught between immersion and descent. Their reflections would roll elsewhere, sometimes beside them, sometimes days ahead. I would keep to the cool layer just above the boundary, though “above” would be something I felt rather than trusted.

I would be one of the transparent swimmers, but altered by long residence near the underside.

My body would be a loose knot of lucid cords. Most of the time it would be difficult to distinguish me from folds in the water. I would have no face, no fixed front, and no durable symmetry. Direction would pass through me as a contraction: a tightening here, a slackening there, a kink handed from loop to loop until the sea accepted it as an intention.

At my center would hang an amber pulse.

But mine would not flash with the detonations from the thick red reaches. It would answer the other basin—the reflected sea beneath the skin. Its rhythm would slow when the ancient forests appeared, stagger when the stable many-lit creature swam through the clouds, and go almost dark before the eye-colored emptiness arrived.

For this reason the others would seldom gather near me.

They would sense that my pulse carried a second cadence, slightly misplaced. When they synchronized around the six spires, I would remain below, flickering between their rhythm and the older rhythm beyond the boundary. I would feel each incarnation of the central being as a tremendous tug through my cords: trunk, cage, hand, dome, head. Each shape would call me upward. The underside would call me through.

I would be the part of the creature that had become interested in escape.

Not freedom, exactly. The sea would be too young for such an idea. I would be its doubt about its own boundary.

My work would begin when stones broke through.

As a fragment approached the skin, I would loop myself around it. Together we would descend until the boundary cupped beneath us and the open atmosphere shone below. Surface tension would flatten my cords into branching lines. For a moment I would inhabit both the stone and its independent reflection. I would feel the cloud-depth pulling—not downward, but toward a definition of falling that the suspended sea had not yet learned.

Most stones would escape me. They would pierce the skin, dragging after them a long transparent ligament. I would hold that ligament until it narrowed to a trembling thread. Just before it snapped, something would travel back through it.

Not sound. Not light.

Continuity.

For one instant there would be an unbroken path between the sea and the distant world. Through it I would receive rain rising from rivers, mineral strata old enough to have forgotten heat, rooted forests agreeing on one downward direction. I would feel creatures whose skeletons stayed inside them and whose hands did not dissolve after closing. The knowledge would be far too large for my amber center. The cord would break, the report would split nearby growth from the rock, and I would recoil into a nearly invisible knot.

Afterward I would carry a minute impurity: the memory of persistence.

The sea would use it.

Currents would seize me and throw me inward. Corridors assembled from slabs would return me repeatedly to their entrances, each time facing a direction that had not existed before. Silence pockets would engulf me and remove the expression of my movement. Inside one, I might contract without swimming, turn without changing orientation, cry out without possessing sound. Those unfinished acts would accumulate in my cords until the pocket burst and released me all at once—a tangle of attempted departures.

Eventually I would reach the six spires.

The other swimmers would already be hanging there, their amber centers blinking in one grave rhythm. Mine would resist. It would pulse once with the gathering body, once with the reflected basin below.

The sea would begin trying to provide us with anatomy.

Water would tighten. Gravel would enter suspension and arrange itself as joints. Sheets of pressure would close around us like transparent flesh. The other swimmers would unwind willingly, their cords becoming nerves, tendons, channels of sensation. Their amber lights would travel inward to furnish the creature with brief intentions.

I would be taken into the palm.

The hand would form around me: five columns folded inward, black glass armoring their outer curves, whirlpools turning at every joint. I would lie in the hollow center where no flesh quite gathered. Through me the hand would remember the underside—not merely as a boundary, but as something that could be crossed.

For the first time, it would not close.

It would turn.

That motion would distort the whole sea. The black pinnacles would groan toward new positions. Weed would surrender its archive of obsolete gravities, every pale strand pointing at once toward all the abandoned meanings of down. Rust-red mouths would open and release their stored heat. Silence pockets would burst in a chain, delivering thousands of withheld impacts. Doorways would assemble across the cliffs, each framing the same impossible view: clouds far below, and through them an older creature swimming with a definite spine.

The great hand would reach toward the underside.

The answer from above would come immediately—perhaps before the reaching began. Red pressure would descend as command. The water would bruise. The spires would lose their agreement.

But I would hold the shape for one additional heartbeat.

That would be what I was: not the hand, not the mind controlling it, but the additional heartbeat.

A small error in the timing of dissolution.

Long enough for one finger to touch the lower skin.

Its reflection would meet it from the other side. The two fingertips would not correspond. One would be young water plated in volcanic glass. The other would be translucent, stable, marked by rows of amber lights and the scars of a completed anatomy. Between them the boundary would tighten to nothing.

I would see then that the finished creature was not waiting in the future.

It would be made from every attempt that failed here. Every collapsed rib cage, every mineral spine, every swimmer divided or lost, every room that taught the sea an inside, every doorway that taught it elsewhere—all of these had crossed by reflection. Consequences that could not persist in the suspended ocean had accumulated on the atmospheric side. Below us, impossibly, our discarded coherence had been growing old.

The hand would break apart.

The central being would die again. Black fragments would scatter. Foam would fill the clearing. The amber swimmers would re-form from severed filaments—some smaller, some doubled, some absent.

I would not re-form.

My clear cords would remain stretched through the touched boundary, part in water and part in sky. The sea would enter me from one side. Air would enter from the other. Neither would destroy me, because destruction requires a stable account of what a body contains.

I would become a narrow passage between incompatible immersions.

Perhaps no wider than a thread at first. A transparent root hanging from the underside. Bubbles would enter it and become droplets; droplets would pass the other way and become stones. Sounds would descend and return as shadows. A colorless strand of weed might root through me and, for the first time, find a downward direction that remained true after germination.

The sea would build around me.

It always builds around anomalies. Flat fragments would gather. Mineral foam would seal their joints. A little roofless cell would assemble on the underside, projecting into open air without falling. Stairs would form along it—three or four black steps descending from water toward cloud. They would have no maker, but they would not be innocent. They would last longer inside than outside.

I would live in the lowest step.

Or I would be the lowest step: a loop of transparent substance containing one amber light, half organ and half threshold.

The central creature would begin incorporating that shape into its attempts. Its next trunk would contain a hollow channel. Its next rib cage would orient toward the sky beneath. Its next head would incline not toward the detonations above, but toward the small persistent stair at the edge of the world.

Each time it collapsed, the stair would gain another consequence.

Not another physical step, necessarily. One day it might gain distance. Another day, sequence. Another, the distinction between departure and return. The sea would practice crossing through me in pieces. Heat would go first, falling as red grains into the clouds. Then sound, freed from the architecture of the roar. Then one of the reflected shadows. Then an amber pulse without its swimmer.

At last a transparent creature would approach.

It might be one of the two that emerged from an earlier capture. It would circle the lowest step, contracting and loosening, unable to choose a front. I would feel its amber center preparing for a detonation that would not occur until later.

I would open—not like a door, because doors still belong to walls, but like a knot discovering that it was tied around an absence.

The swimmer would pass through.

On the atmospheric side it would not fall. The clouds would interpret its motion as swimming. Its cords would lengthen, then braid themselves around the solitary amber spark I had sent ahead. For the first time, a fragment of the suspended sea would possess a body whose consequences remained attached to it.

Above, the six spires would agree.

The roar would become one utterance. Weed, stone, heat, silence, and distance would submit to a single pattern. In the central clearing an enormous hand would form once more.

This time, when it opened, it would find that one small part of it was already elsewhere.

And I would be that part: the first nerve laid across the edge of the world, the creature’s instinct for a direction that does not vanish, the tiny living passage through which an unfinished ocean begins to leave itself.
