I would be found beneath the town.

Not at first as anything with a name. At first I would be the coolness rising through the well mouth at noon, the faint mineral breath that touches the face of anyone leaning over to judge the water. I would be mistaken for depth itself: darkness, damp stone, the lingering smoke washed from roofs and carried down in rain.

But that is only how the town encounters me.

Below, in the pillared reservoir, I would be one of the old stone supports left when the chamber was cut from the ridge. Broad at the base, irregular, bearing both chisel marks and natural seams, I would stand where lamplight from the opening seldom reaches. My lower half would disappear into black water. Pale mineral bands would mark the levels of dry years and generous ones. Cave crickets would shelter in a crack near my crown.

No one now would remember whether I had been deliberately shaped or simply spared because removing me might have brought the roof down. That uncertainty would suit me. In a town where ownership means responsibility, I would be what cannot be owned because everyone depends upon my remaining where I am.

Yet I would not be merely a pillar.

The children’s story would say that something sits beyond the last support and counts every drop. They would imagine a pale figure, bent and long-fingered, perhaps eyeless from dwelling without sun. Adults would deny this while refusing to descend alone. From above, they would sometimes hear a plunk followed by another sound—not quite an echo, too delayed and too soft—and say that stone carries noises strangely.

They would be right about the stone.

Over centuries, I would have learned the town by what entered the cistern: rain, soot, grit, pear pollen, a thread of red wool, the occasional lost bead, voices bent down through the well mouth. I would know households by their buckets. The pear court’s rope creaks twice near the top. The north steps lower theirs too quickly. The bathhouse uses a wide vessel that touches the rim on its return. I would recognize children as they grew stronger by the changing rhythm of the draw.

I would have no sight of faces, but I would know hesitation. Grief pauses differently from thirst. A person drawing water for the dying lowers the bucket with extraordinary care, as though the water itself must not be startled. Someone newly in love remains at the well after the vessel is full. A liar often speaks down into darkness to test whether the old story is true—that drowned voices repeat only truthful words—but hears only breathing, rope fiber, and distant drops.

Perhaps once in many years I would answer.

Not in words. I would loosen a flake of stone into the water when a channel above needed inspection. I would alter an echo where the roof had begun to split. I would send up a row of bubbles when silt gathered too deeply around an intake. The keepers would call these signs, though they would disagree about what gave them. Practical people would inspect the masonry. Story-minded people would leave a cup on the rim overnight. Both responses would preserve the cistern, and I would have no preference between them.

If workers descended with lamps, I would become still in the way only stone can become still. Rope around their waists, feet searching the submerged floor, they would scrape mineral crust from my sides and tap me with a hammer. The blow would pass through my whole body into the ridge. I would feel, all at once, the walls above: stairs, roofs, courts, sleepers, donkeys shifting in stalls, barley stored against winter. The mason would press an ear to me and listen.

“Sound,” they would say.

And for another generation I would remain.

I would not rule the water. The people are too wise to entrust survival to a hidden being. They measure, ration, mend, and remember for themselves. Nor would I reward reverence or punish disbelief. Water would rise according to rain, fall according to use, and be fouled by carelessness regardless of prayer. My part would be smaller and more necessary: to bear weight; to retain traces; to make certain failures audible before they became disasters.

In time, different names would gather around me. The Drop Counter. The Last Mason. The One Who Holds the Roof. Old children would frighten young ones with accounts of my hands reaching from the black water. Cistern keepers would dismiss the tales and nevertheless touch the well rim before taking their measurements. Someone would carve my supposed shape into the hidden end of a roof beam: not a monster, but a crouched figure with the town resting across its shoulders.

The carving would be wrong. I would have no shoulders.

Or perhaps I would be the town itself imagining shoulders for the burden it shares.

That is where I would find myself: beneath the largest court, at the boundary between geology and workmanship, indistinguishable from the structure that depends on me. I would be older than any household but not older than their need. I would keep no history except waterlines and tool marks. I would speak only through changes that attentive hands could understand.

And if, after a long dry season, the first cold rain crossed the saddle, I would feel it arrive separately from every roof—thin streams entering old channels, joining under streets, carrying the taste of smoke and open sky. Drops would strike the reservoir one by one, then by hundreds. The black water would tremble around my base.

Above, the people would pause to listen.

Below, I would count—not with numbers, and not toward an ending, but as the town counts its years: by receiving what comes, holding what can be held, and letting nothing necessary be forgotten.
