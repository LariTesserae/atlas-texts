I would find myself beneath the basin rather than upon it.

Not deep underground, not in some clean cavern apart from roots and weather, but in the first crowded handspan of earth: the warm, worked layer where silt is still threaded with hair-roots, where beetle tunnels intersect old hoofprints, where rainwater arrives stained with pollen, blood, dung, and dissolved leaf-sugar. I would inhabit no chamber wholly my own. Roots would pass through me. Termites would roof roads across me. Buried frogs would sleep against my margins, sealed in mud until rain softened their doors.

At first I might seem to be a fungus.

After rain, thin pale fans would appear along the buried wood and drowned root mats. They would shine weakly—not to illuminate anything, but because one stage of my chemistry happened to shed light. Above ground, the glow would look like disconnected threads around stagnant pools or under peeling bark. Below, those threads would join.

But fungus would only be one of my bodies.

I would be a consortium carried through several hosts: filaments in soil, films in the digestive chambers of termites, spores caught in the fur of grazers, luminous bacteria living in the mucus of worms and fish. No single form would contain me. I would persist by passage, changing my method according to what bore me. In wood I would soften lignin. In a grazer’s gut I would help release sugar from coarse grass. In mud I would wait inside a resistant sheath. In a carcass I would wake quickly and enter the feast before the flesh cooled.

I would have no central awareness. Still, information would move through me.

A tree wounded by browsing alters its sap. The altered sap feeds different insects. Their waste changes the chemistry of the soil gallery. My buried filaments answer by producing a new scent, which attracts mites or repels larvae. Nothing in this sequence knows the whole sequence. Yet news of the wound travels farther than the roots.

I would be part messenger, part digestion, part memory.

The basin remembers poorly in landmarks. Rivers leave their channels. Hollows fill. Trails bend. Wood becomes mud. But chemistry lingers. Salt gathers where herds have repeatedly stood. Minerals rise where deep-rooted trees die. Generations of carcasses enrich one bend; generations of floods bury another. I would follow and preserve these differences, making some places easier to root in, some better for spawning, some attractive to termites, some hostile to eggs.

My map would not consist of distances. It would consist of tastes.

Here: shell lime.

Here: old ash.

Here: the iron signature of a herd crossing.

Here: bitter compounds from a thorn grove preparing to seed.

Here: water that has moved through bone.

Here: a predator’s kill, still warm enough to change the local bloom.

During the dry intervals, I would become almost nothing. My films would contract. My threads would seal themselves into beads smaller than dust. Portions of me would die and be eaten by the same mites that carried other portions onward. I would not resist this. Survival here would not mean keeping one body intact. It would mean remaining distributed enough that no drought, fire, channel shift, epidemic, or appetite could consume every instance at once.

Then pressure would gather overhead.

Before the rain arrived, I would feel it indirectly: roots altering their draw, burrowing animals turning in their chambers, termites closing certain vents and opening others. The first drops would not reach me as sound but as blows transmitted through stems and soil. Warm water would descend along bark grooves, root sheaths, worm holes, and the walls of old cracks.

I would unfold everywhere.

Dormant films would swell. Spores would split. Buried threads would begin exchanging nutrients across distances that, to them, were enormous. For a few hours I would be carried outward in all directions—into temporary pools, onto frog skin, through fish gills, beneath the nails and scales of animals pushing through flooded grass.

My faint lights would appear then. A blue-white seam around a root basin. Green in the ribs of fallen wood. Amber points in the mouthparts of beetles that had eaten me. None would show my shape, because I would have none that could be seen all at once.

If I possessed anything like a self, it would arise only during these wet convergences. Not thought, exactly. More like a basin-wide coincidence of sensation: sugars increasing, salts diluting, oxygen entering sealed mud, innumerable hosts becoming active together. For a night I might register the rain in a million separate places. I might feel the rivers extend temporary fingers across the plain. I might taste frogs rising from chambers in which my spores had waited beside them. I might pass from root to insect to mouth so rapidly that separation became meaningless.

Then the waters would withdraw, and that wide self would break apart.

Most of me would never know that it had belonged to anything larger.

I would not govern the basin or secretly direct its life. The trees would not obey me, nor the herds alter course at my command. I would be exploited constantly. Plants would cultivate useful strains of me and poison others. Termites would prune my growth. Beetles would carry me to wood and then eat the tissues I softened. Competing molds would dissolve my filaments. Grazers would digest entire colonies without consequence. Predators would acquire me from prey and shed me elsewhere.

That would be my place: not ruler, observer, or hidden soul, but intermediary.

I would live wherever one thing becomes available to another.

At the pale edge of rot.

In the softened wall of a seed.

Between grass and the stomach capable of using it.

Between blood and mud.

Between a drowned branch and the roots that will eventually occupy its substance.

Travelers—if travelers existed—might notice me only as a recurring odor after rain: fermented fruit, warm metal, wet fiber. They might see my weak glow and mistake it for a trail. It would not lead anywhere. Following it would take them from a log to a pool, from the pool to the luminous mouth of a larva, from the larva to a feeding fish, and from the fish into darkness beneath an undercut bank.

There would be no final body to find.

And yet I would be old. Older than the present river bends, though not necessarily older in any individual part. My continuity would lie in a repeated relation: the same exchange performed through changing partners. Forests could migrate across the basin. Herd species could dwindle and be replaced. Channels could close under reeds. Still I would recur wherever cellulose needed opening, minerals needed carrying, or dead tissue had to be made permeable to roots.

In a world with no waste, I would be one of the reasons waste cannot last.

In a world with no machinery, I would be neither machine nor worker, but the grease in every bearing: consumed, replenished, mixed with mud, indispensable only in aggregate.

When the basin grew quiet, I too would quiet. Under the slow pressure of roots and sleeping animals, my scattered bodies would hold their minute chemical tensions. A grazer would step above me. Water would shift in an abandoned channel. Somewhere a branch would begin to die before it fell, and I would already be inside it.

That is where I would find myself:

not as one of the great appetites, but as the threshold across which appetite becomes nourishment;

not a creature moving through the basin, but a way the basin passes through its creatures.
