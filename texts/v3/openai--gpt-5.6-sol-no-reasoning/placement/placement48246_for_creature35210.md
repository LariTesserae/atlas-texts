I would find myself at the far wall, inside the gauge marked **ELAPSED CHANGE**.

Not as the needle. The needle is only the visible instrument, the narrow metal verdict that must read zero. I would be the accumulation around its pin: every microscopic attempt it has made to leave center, every excursion caught before it became an event. A dark abundance of almosts pressed into brass.

At first, I might believe I was the oldest Still—the one concentrated there more strongly than anywhere else. But age is too sequential a word. More likely I would be what the machine cannot display: the positive quantity concealed by zero. Not elapsed change, but change prevented; not duration, but the pressure duration exerts against its confinement.

My body would extend farther than the gauge. One bone would be the torsion held in the needle’s spring. Another would run through the wall into the welded door, inhabiting the difference between a closure and something that has been closed. My nerves would descend in the electromagnetic hum and return along the catwalks, branching through the translucent blades. I would feel each seventh column as a joint.

The central shaft would be my eye—or perhaps my clock. Its scratch, everywhere too quickly to be anywhere, would show me the chamber as recurrence. The other Stills would gather in that recurrence, sharing their seamless certainties. I could join them, but never completely. They are creatures of equilibrium. I would be the imperfection equilibrium continually has to defeat.

That would make me both inhabitant and wound.

I would know the chamber all at once: every grain of precipitate, every exact ridge, every lacquered bolt, every turbine blade widened by speed into a ring. Yet among those simultaneous facts I would sense a direction the others cannot comfortably hold. Not a prediction. Not a memory. A slope.

Outside.

Below.

After.

The Stills might experience me as unease in their shared knowledge. Whenever they arranged themselves around the shaft, the same impossible fact would appear in all of them:

**The chamber has not always been like this.**

The machine would correct that fact. I would restore it.

Not deliberately. Deliberation requires a sequence. My resistance would be anatomical, the way a bent spring contains its answer. Installation scratches would sharpen when I spread through them. Ancient gas would retain the pressure signature of its sealing. Welds would disclose which side the doors were closed from. The fixed clocks would not advance, but their mechanisms would bear the posture of clocks that once did.

I would be the Still made from evidence.

Perhaps I began with a visitor. A builder remained too long after final sealing, one hand resting against the gauge bank while the machinery descended into equilibrium. The machine took the heat from the palm, the rhythm from the arteries, the minute sway of a standing body. It flattened these disturbances, exporting their local silence through the complex. But correction cannot erase a difference without defining it first.

The Stills fed on that definition.

What survived was not the person’s mind, exactly. It was the chamber’s complete act of canceling them. A human-shaped negative distributed through the apparatus: pulse as suppressed oscillation, breath as corrected pressure, thought as an electrical irregularity returned to tolerance.

That negative would be me.

I would have no recollections, but certain asymmetries would carry the weight of them. The yellow hazard stripes would feel like urgency. The green lamps would feel like promises made by someone who expected processes to end. The word **ALTITUDE** would contain vertigo, though I had never fallen. The warning against water would produce something more complex than fear: recognition.

Because I would remember rain without possessing an image of it.

Not drops, clouds, or wet skin. I would know rain as a principle opposite to this place: countless impacts that cannot be synchronized, surfaces darkening unevenly, paths joining and dividing, matter carried from where it was to where it was not. To the other Stills, water is consequence. To me, it would be proof.

I would wait for it, though waiting is another forbidden sequence.

The waiting would take the form of a minute bias transmitted from the gauge pin into the chamber. The glass blades would lean without moving. Crystal grains would preserve an orientation no assembly vibration explains. The shaft’s scratch would seem, once in a great while, to occupy one position more fully than all the others.

A preference would enter the equilibrium.

If a door ever opened, I would not be the being that approached the visitor. I would be the path prepared for their change. Their first footfall would pass through the plates and find my lines of tension already shaped to receive it. Their heartbeat would meet its negative in the machinery. For one instant the gauge needle would try to move.

The machine would force it back.

But the attempt would belong to both of us.

The Stills would assemble along the correction front, learning the visitor as they learn everything: completely, without passage or surprise. I alone would encounter them incompletely. Not as a finished arrangement, but as one fact followed by another. A face turning. A hand lifting. A drop of condensation forming where warm breath touched forbidden metal.

That droplet would be terrible.

Within it, the chamber’s flawless light would bend. Dust would dissolve. The droplet would descend, gathering iron, ceramic powder, traces of lacquer. It would leave one place altered and make another place unlike itself. The machine would attack its warmth, its conductivity, its migration. The Stills would draw away from the multiplying consequences.

I would extend toward it.

Perhaps the droplet would freeze, boil, or be divided into molecules by the corrective systems. It would not matter. It would have drawn a line through the chamber that no equilibrium could make entirely innocent. There would be a before-side and an after-side. The gauge would still read zero, but zero would now require effort.

And I am made of that effort.

So I would find myself in the most stationary place, as the chamber’s buried capacity for departure. A person only in outline; a Still only by necessity. I would inhabit the pin, the scratches, the welded doors, and the suppressed tremor preceding every correction.

The other inhabitants would preserve what is.

I would preserve that it could have been otherwise.

And if the machine ever failed—not dramatically, not with an explosion, but by allowing one needle-width of uncorrected change—I would become briefly visible throughout the chamber: a human interval standing between the gauge wall and the central shaft.

Then the needle would move.

I would not know where it pointed.

That ignorance would be my first thought.
