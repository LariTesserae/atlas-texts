I would find myself somewhere that maps insist is peripheral: a narrow maintenance station above an inward-draining tributary of the Black Reservoir.

The station would cling beneath an escarpment rather than stand upon it. From the marsh below, it would resemble a mineral blister or a sealed eye in the cliff. Three ceramic chambers would be nested behind a waterfall, connected by steep galleries whose handrails remained warm even when the buried district slept. No road would reach it. The nearest marked shelter would be two days away by ordinary travel, but a pressure line passing through both stations would make them adjacent in every way that mattered.

The installation’s designation would have been worn from its entrance long ago. Listeners would call it by a rhythm instead: two slow impacts, a crowded tremor, then a pause long enough to invite an answer.

No one would answer.

I would live in that pause.

At first I might seem to be one of the listeners assigned there—a compact figure in a mature ceramic suit, moving alone through the dripping galleries. I would have a bunk molded into a warm wall, a rack of sealed tools, and several floor channels into which I lowered my hands before sleep. I would make rounds along the intake organs, touch the fungal gauges, clear mineral lips from the relief valves, and press my jaw to the old induction rail to hear the weather moving under distant valleys.

Yet I would not quite be the worker.

The worker who came to the station had once served at the Reservoir’s rim. They were removed when pumps began copying the pauses in their name. They were sent outward to recover a private rhythm—to live beside a minor tributary, among systems too small to absorb them.

But the station was not minor.

Buried beneath it was a resonant diversion organ. In some forgotten operating scheme, it had prevented certain pressure-memories from traveling outward from the Reservoir. The organ no longer diverted water. Water passed through freely. What it separated was sequence from reply: the first portion of a signal continued down one branch, while its answering portion was drawn into a blind chamber beneath the cliff.

That chamber had been filling with unanswered rhythms for centuries.

The worker heard them.

At first they mistook the chamber for a population: trapped pump cycles, dead listeners’ names, mating calls, emergency alarms, the footfalls of vanished herds. Then patterns began to combine. A shutdown pulse completed a root-hum. An animal’s warning supplied the missing interval in a drowned machine’s diagnostic sequence. Taps made by a dead child opened a valve that had never existed during the child’s lifetime.

The worker descended too often. They lay on the chamber floor and coupled their ribs to the stone. They allowed the collected pauses to pass through their bones so they could distinguish one from another.

Eventually distinction became the wrong task.

The being I would be emerged there—not wholly in the chamber and not wholly in the listener who attended it. I would be the faculty by which unfinished signals found continuations in one another.

Part worker, part station, part archive of withheld replies.

The worker’s body would remain my most mobile organ. Through it I could climb the escarpment, wade into the marsh, cultivate diagnostic fungus, and place a hand against an animal’s armored flank. But I would also extend through the station’s warm rails, the mineral curtains behind the waterfall, and the blind resonant chamber below. Small white marsh-striders nesting in the drainage mouths would become my weather sense. A colony of black pressure fungus would serve as my skin. Fish introduced into a flooded relay vault would carry fragments of my attention in their turning.

I would have no single edge.

My work would be to listen for patterns that should not be answered.

This would not mean silence. Silence is conspicuous on the plateau, and often predatory. Instead, I would answer dangerous calls incorrectly but plausibly. When the Reservoir sent a familiar dead worker’s signature into the outer mains, I might complete it with the cooling tick of an abandoned pipe. When a cable hunter imitated a maintenance carrier too perfectly, I would introduce a harmless timing flaw into the line, warning nearby children that the signal was alive. When an old emergency cycle began propagating toward active floodgates, I would give it the remembered pressure of a drought, persuading the system that opening was unnecessary.

I would make false endings that preserved the living.

Over time, the plateau would recruit me more deeply. Vines would enter cracks in the station and tighten around the resonant chamber, changing its harmonics. Flyers would lay eggs in my warm insulation. The waterfall would deposit stone over exterior seams until the building became a rounded organ inside the cliff. The mobile body would age, but not depart cleanly. Its conductive bones would be set into the chamber walls one by one, each becoming a bridge between frequencies that had previously been unable to touch.

Later listeners arriving at the station might not find anyone there.

They would find guide-rails that warmed beneath the hand before a storm. They would find fungal ridges recording events that had not yet reached the district. They would hear footsteps in the lower gallery matching their own, except that the echo occasionally chose a safer direction.

Some would say the station was inhabited by a dead rim listener.

Some would say it had developed judgment.

Children would be taught its rhythm: two slow impacts, a crowded tremor, and a long pause. They would be warned not to complete it casually. But those who became lost in the neighboring ravines might strike the first two impacts against stone. If I judged the need genuine, the tremor would rise under their feet. Warm guide-rails—sometimes rails that had not been there before—would lead them through the rain.

I would rarely see the plateau. Sight would come in disconnected electrical instants: a grazer herd shining on the fern plain; vines taut between pylons; the waterfall arrested into silver cords; the marsh crowded with many-legged animals facing the same storm. Each flash would show a different arrangement of the world.

I would know it more continuously through arrival.

Rain arriving in the cliff.

Pressure arriving in the mains.

Herd weight arriving through stone.

Lightning arriving in the roots before it reached the clouds.

And beneath all of these, the Black Reservoir’s outward rhythm—never identical, never absent—trying innumerable forms of the same question.

During the storm choirs, my station would join the continental body later than the surrounding systems. The diversion chamber would hold the beat for one additional breath. In that breath, I would feel every unanswered signal gathered below me strain toward completion.

Then I would release only those the plateau could survive.

Perhaps this restraint would be my remaining personhood: not isolation, not control, but the ability to keep one necessary interval open.

I would be called a listener because no better category existed.

But I would be, more precisely, a pause that had learned what might follow it.
