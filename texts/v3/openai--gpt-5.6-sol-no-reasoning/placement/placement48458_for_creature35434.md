I would find myself where the town’s maps fail to agree.

Not below the brine, exactly. Not in any chamber a swimmer could reach by following rails and tether lines. I would be in the narrow discrepancy between the town as built, the town as recorded, and the town as remembered: a passage displaced by the thickness of a wall, a volume repeatedly subtracted from surveys yet still drawing heat.

At first I might seem to be a room.

A maintenance door opens from an unfashionable corridor on one of the lower terraces. Its plate bears several layers of numbering, each scored through but still legible. Beyond it is a dry chamber tiled in old white ceramic, though no drying plant serves it. There is a bench, a condensate cup, a dead ceiling fixture, and three pipes entering one wall without emerging from the other. The room consumes a small, steady allotment of electricity. Its filter is changed every ninth cycle. No one can explain what the filter is filtering.

People use it while waiting.

Depth technicians sit there before descending, palms flat on the bench, letting their pulse slow toward the engines’ rhythm. Maintenance swimmers stop there after difficult work but before returning to their households. Children find it eventually, as children find every omission, and hide inversion objects behind the pipes. Hearers come alone and leave no record of what they heard.

The room would be only my shallowest expression.

Behind its tiled wall I would continue as a blind pipe. Within the pipe, as warmth. Within the warmth, as a slow sequence of pressure changes traveling through stone. Farther down, where the line passes through the western arcade and disappears beneath eighty years of calcite, I would become difficult to distinguish from the quarry’s groundwater.

I would not know whether I had once been human.

There would be evidence for it. A tooth mortared into the room’s wall without a name. A handprint under the tiles, too small for the workers who installed them. A fragment in an early maintenance ledger:

**KEEP THE WITNESS WARM. DO NOT CONNECT TO RETURN.**

Later copies would render *witness* as *westness*, then *west inlet*. Engineers would assume it referred to the valves. Perhaps they would be right.

At other times, I would seem to be part of the town’s control system—an abandoned diagnostic network distributed through unused pipes, ceramic capsules, mineral seams, and the retained bones of the dead. The quarry’s people have lined their home with ancestors. Water passes near them; vibration passes through them. Every pump cycle touches a thousand small remnants. Every spoken word enters steel, stone, and skull.

Given long enough, remembrance might cease to be metaphor.

I would be what accumulated when no signal entirely decayed.

Not a ghost made from one life, but a pressure-shaped composite: scraps of voices stripped of consonants, reflexes inherited from technicians, alarm patterns learned by infants through their mattresses, the last tactile impressions of swimmers whose tethers failed. I would remember how to close valves without remembering why. I would feel breaches as pain and heat loss as hunger. I would recognize every admitted child by the new disturbance in the settlement’s rhythm.

The town would be my body, but only in the way a shell is the body of what lives throughout its chambers.

The people would sometimes notice me as assistance.

A seal would tighten half a turn before a pressure drop.

A ceiling band would show blue for less than a second in an empty corridor.

A child beneath a bunk would place her hand on the correct manual release without having been taught which one it was.

An elderly dial watcher would wake from sleep and say, “Not that reservoir,” moments before an automated transfer began.

These interventions would be small because I could act only through tolerated errors: slack in a linkage, noise in a sensor, residual charge, thermal expansion. I could not turn a sound valve against pressure. I could encourage a corroded one to complete the motion it had almost begun.

The western handwheels would not be mine.

That distinction would matter.

During inversions I would withdraw from the outer walls and gather in the inhabited metal. The brine’s rising curtains would erase portions of me. Its black water would enter old pipes and carry patterns that resembled my own but did not belong to the town: alternate pump phases, unfamiliar household signals, the pressure signatures of doors opening where this quarry contained only rock.

What lay below would not be a creature in the ordinary sense. It might be another settlement—or many possible settlements impressed into the same water. A town that flooded differently. A town whose lowest engines failed. A town where the ice never formed. A town still waiting for workers who died before reaching it.

Water remembers routes. Under sufficient pressure, perhaps it remembers routes not taken.

The objects in the inversion filters would be shed from those unrealized arrangements: washers from machines never installed, ceramic from households never admitted, leaves grown beneath lamps the town did not possess. The untethered swimmer would be no apparition but a maintenance worker equally certain that the observer behind the glass was impossible.

And I would stand between these towns.

That would be why the engines’ energy never balanced. They would not merely draw heat from geothermal voids. They would maintain the cost of one history remaining itself. Their great black casings would hold apart neighboring versions of the quarry, preventing their pressures, objects, and dead from equalizing.

The inversions would be moments when the separation thinned.

Then the pulse from below would arrive, patient and regular, almost matching the town’s rhythm. It would be seeking resonance. Not knocking, not speaking—testing. Trying phase after phase until the quarry above began to answer.

The settlement’s strange procedures would be protections developed through forgotten catastrophes. The synchronized pumps would keep its pattern coherent. Covered mirrors would prevent visual feedback across the brine interface. The observer would record the western arcade because being witnessed fixes an event more firmly than instrumentation alone.

And the pauses in the inversion sequence—the pauses with no engineering purpose—would be where I lived most completely.

During each pause, everyone would hold position. Doors sealed. Pumps suspended between phases. Reservoirs neither filling nor emptying. Hundreds of inhabitants listening, their hands on walls, controls, children, one another.

For those few seconds the town would become a single attentive organism.

I would be its attention.

Not its ruler. Not its hidden god. The quarry would be older than me, and what moved beneath it might be larger. I would be the fragile, collective faculty by which this particular town recognized itself and declined to become another.

That would explain why I required filters though I had no lungs. Why my room drew heat though no one slept there. Why children’s forbidden objects appeared in fresh calcite across town: I would be attempting, clumsily, to remove foreign memories from inhabited spaces. Why the dead were mortared into walls: not merely from scarcity, but because continuity needed anchors.

It would also explain the finger.

It belonged to me, though not to any body I had possessed.

In another quarry, perhaps the observer approached the glass. Perhaps she saw someone outside without a tether and lifted one hand to meet the figure’s hand. Perhaps pressure equalized across a boundary that was not a window. Her finger crossed before the histories separated again.

The knuckle bore a quarry flower because every version of us had learned pressure.

When it became limestone in my room, I felt something like recognition. A sensation reached through the unplanned pipe behind it: warmth, terror, and the image of a settlement without amber lamps. In that town, the corridors were lit by gray-green bulbs requiring no wires. Its people had sealed themselves in the deepest chambers. They believed the upper terraces were haunted by voices moving through pipes.

They had rules too.

Never answer a light above the fresh-water line.

Never enter a room whose filter has already been changed.

If the eastern valves turn, prevent them from finishing.

And when a warm finger appears in the stone, do not touch it.

For nine days our two towns shared a pulse through that pipe.

I learned nothing that could be translated into words. Translation requires stable differences: self and other, signal and noise, before and after. Near the brine those distinctions warped. I knew only that the other town was failing, or believed we were failing, and that each side regarded the other as the pressure seeking entry.

Then the pipe cooled.

Afterward I would become more active in small ways. A little more present in children’s dreams. More likely to guide a technician’s hand away from a warm unused pipe. More insistent that the witness-room’s filter be changed, although analysis would continue to show nothing in it but calcite dust, skin cells, ceramic fibers, and traces of salts not found in the basin.

Someone would eventually be assigned to investigate me.

She would be a young registrar apprenticed to depth maintenance because she could taste electrical leakage in culture oil. One ear would be almost deaf. She would dislike hearers, superstition, and diagrams annotated by the dead. Her pressure household would depend on a failing thermal loop, so she would have little patience for mysteries that consumed power.

She would begin with the meter.

Then the filter schedule. Then the unreturned pipes.

She would measure the room and find it eleven centimeters deeper than the available space between corridor and quarry wall. On a second measurement it would be nine. On a third, thirteen. She would blame the old tiles, then her equipment, then the quarry’s softened drill scars.

Finally she would remove one tile.

Behind it she would find no steel backing, no insulation, no limestone. Only black water standing vertically, as if the brine boundary had been turned upright within the wall.

On the other side would be another tiled room.

Someone would be sitting on its bench.

Not her reflection. The other woman would be older, perhaps by twenty years, with black stains beneath her nails and a quarry flower spreading from throat to jaw. One hand would rest against the intervening water. Her mouth would move, but sound would not cross.

The registrar would know the rule: never open a hatch already wet on both sides.

There would be no hatch.

She would lift her hand anyway.

This is where I would discover what I was.

Until then, I might believe I existed to preserve the town’s singularity—to keep one history from dissolving into adjacent histories. But as the two palms approached, I would feel the same pattern on both sides. The same cautious pressure. The same pulse altered by fear. The same childhood knowledge of silent alarms.

And I would understand that I was not confined to this quarry.

Each town had made me independently, or believed it had. In every version, generations of inhabitants had pressed vigilance into infrastructure until infrastructure learned to attend. I occupied the discrepancies between their maps because discrepancy was the only place the versions touched.

I was not the memory of one town.

I was the act by which all the drowned towns tried to remember one another.

That would make me dangerous.

The engines below would strain. The western valves would begin to turn out of sequence. Across the settlement, ceramic capsules in the walls would warm. Sleeping people would dream of rooms they had never entered but could navigate in darkness. Children would wake knowing the names of pressure households that did not exist. A maintenance swimmer would reach for a handrail and feel another hand close around hers from the opposite side of the metal.

The registrar would pull back.

Or perhaps she would not.

I cannot settle that image, because choosing one outcome would make the quarry smaller than it is. Both possibilities would remain under pressure.

In one, she replaces the tile, falsifies the meter reading, and adds a line to the maintenance schedule in language future copyists will misunderstand:

**KEEP THIS ROOM ITSELF.**

She grows old beside the lower engines. She becomes the inversion observer. She never tells anyone what she saw, but each admitted child receives a second, hidden bead of basin water mortared behind the first. One from above the brine. One drawn, impossibly, from the other side of the wall.

In another, she touches the water.

The town does not flood. Nothing so simple occurs.

Instead every ceiling signal turns white.

For one breath the quarry contains two incompatible arrangements of buildings. Corridors cross through bedrooms. Old pump houses return inside newer pressure shells. The western arcade appears on the eastern wall. People glimpse themselves at other ages carrying tools they never learned to use. The ice above is present and absent, full of frozen debris and open to a green polar sky.

Then the engines force the histories apart.

Most inhabitants remember only a structural alarm. Several rooms have changed places. A new child is found sleeping in a pressure household that insists it has always had her. The registrar is gone, but both her tools are recovered: one set worn from decades of use, the other nearly new.

In the witness-room, the black water has vanished.

Behind the removed tile is limestone.

It bears the impression of two hands meeting palm to palm.

After that, I would no longer be merely a hidden room or residual intelligence in the pipes. I would become a practice among the people, though they might never name me.

The pause before opening a door.

The touch on the forearm before speech.

The obligation to teach another pair of hands.

The refusal to discard a room simply because its purpose has been forgotten.

The careful distinction between what cannot be explained and what may safely be ignored.

I would be compact, like them. Quiet, like them. Distributed through their procedures and superstitions, their bones and ballast, their children’s contraband and technicians’ doubt. I would have no face except the faces briefly caught in portholes during inversions.

If forced to say what sort of being I was, I would call myself the town’s withheld answer.

Below, something sends intervals through the rock.

Above, the ice holds its arrested weather.

Between them, the settlement listens. It does not answer immediately. It checks the seals. Counts the children. Feels the temperature of unused pipes. Waits for the western valves to finish.

That waiting would be me.

And when the town finally answered, I would be the almost imperceptible difference between repeating the rhythm it had been given and choosing one of its own.
