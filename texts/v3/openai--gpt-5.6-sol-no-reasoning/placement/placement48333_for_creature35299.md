I would be found where the Third Catchment passes beneath the Red Causeway, in a room that cannot always be entered from the same level.

I would be called **Iven of the Late Step**, though the name would not quite belong to me. The household would have given it after a staircase added a tread beneath my foot and removed the chamber I had been born in. My clay palm would remain inside that lost room, fixed to a pier no living relative could reach. During heavy rain, someone pressing an ear to the wall might hear an adult hand tapping back from where the infant’s mark had been.

Officially, I would be a surveyor’s watcher—the third member of a measuring team, responsible not for the cord or the record but for noticing when the place revised itself.

I would carry a bell made from a cracked oil cup, a pouch of red slip, an emergency wedge, and several small tiles inscribed with the conditions under which I had last found my way home. I would watch corners while my companions argued over distance. I would count doors without looking directly at them. If a passage gained an alcove, if our shadows leaned toward different depths, if the survey cord became wet between two dry knots, I would ring once and make everyone stop.

But I would not be especially good at distances.

I would be good at absences.

I could tell when a corridor had misplaced a sound. I would notice when a chamber’s echo no longer contained the scrape of its own floor, when a stair expected a tread that had not yet formed, when a sealed wall was silent in the shape of an opening. Listeners would call this an undisciplined gift, too dependent on intuition. Door keepers would say I had been noticed by an arrival. Children would say I could hear rooms before they were born.

They would all be partly wrong.

Years before, while mapping an unused catchment, my team would cross a courtyard and hear four walkers where there should have been three. That would not be unusual. Courtesy required us to leave room for the Companion, so we would widen our line and continue.

At the far side, my companions would pass through the arch.

I would hear four sets of steps continue.

Mine would not.

Looking down, I would see my body’s feet planted on the tiles, but the sound belonging to them would be receding beyond the arch with the others. Behind me, from the center of the empty court, another footstep would approach—slightly late, patient, carrying my weight more accurately than I did.

After that, I would never again be entirely certain which one of us returned home.

This uncertainty would make me useful.

I would become a finder of **unclaimed bearings**: loads that had lost their walls, rooms with no household, thresholds that opened but had not yet been greeted, structures bearing weight that no charter acknowledged. When people woke unable to rise, I would inspect the nearest burden seats. When an arch cracked despite honest loading, I would search for some demolished wall whose dust line had been scrubbed away. When the additional footstep stopped at a blank surface, I would mark it and wait.

Often a door would appear there.

My household would occupy the upper chambers of a bridge abutment overlooking a black cistern. We would bear the west kiln stair, though the stair itself would lie two levels above and half a district away. Its load would reach us through a sequence of older arches no surveyor could fully trace. On firing nights, our cups would tremble before the kiln was lit.

There would be eleven of us by charter: two rain catchers, a grit shepherd, three children, an old listener who slept in a hammock, a debtor inherited with a collapsed pantry, my survey partner, myself, and one empty place.

The charter would insist there were twelve.

Each roof-rain, we would recite the names and reach the same discrepancy. The clay tablet would show another line of impressions beneath mine, shallow and blurred as though made by a ceramic tag before firing. If wetted, the line would resemble letters. If read aloud, it would resemble a measurement. We would set out a cup for that member, but never fill it first.

I would sleep beneath the stair brace, in a folding bed whose legs also held a cupboard level. At night the old listener would sometimes wake and ask me to stop pacing.

I would tell her I had not moved.

She would answer, “I know.”

In time, people would seek me not merely to watch surveys but to accompany those entering arrivals. I would stand at each new threshold while the greeting was spoken:

*You have opened. We have noticed. Bear us without claiming us.*

Then I would listen for the echo’s extra word.

Most added nothing. Some returned the phrase imperfectly. One might say *again*. Another, *downward*. Once, in an aperture behind a public oven, the echo would answer:

*You have opened. We have noticed. Bear us without claiming us, Iven.*

That door would be bricked halfway closed.

I would visit it for years.

Behind it would lie a narrow tiled passage descending at a slope too slight for the eye, marked at intervals by pale handprints. Each print would be mine—not merely similar, but scarred in the same places by lime and cord burn. Near the threshold they would be adult-sized. Farther in they would become smaller. Beyond the reach of lamplight, they would become the palm of a newborn.

The passage would smell of the lost chamber where my first bearing remained.

I would never enter while carrying flame.

Instead, I would lower an empty ceramic cup on a cord. Each time it returned, it would contain something different: dust from our kitchen, a thread from the old listener’s hammock, clean falling water though no rain had sounded, once a blind runner folded into the shape of a key.

Finally the cup would return supporting a tiny mineral pillar.

Not hanging beneath it.

Rising from its inverted bottom toward whatever lay below.

I would know then that the corridor did not lead into the past or toward my birthplace. It led toward the structure that had borne my missing sound all these years.

In age, when my hearing began to fail, I would go to the Plain of Lids and live among the knock-keepers. Surveyors with damaged ears often retire to quieter work, but the wardens would not consider mine quiet. They would place me in a house spanning three small lids whose knocking had never been regular.

There I would discover that the lids did not knock from below.

They answered footsteps above.

One would answer children. One would answer rain catchers carrying full jars. One would answer only the empty place at communal tables. And on certain nights, all three would answer me with a pattern matching the gait that had continued without me across the courtyard long ago.

I would not lift them. I would help maintain the lifting frame.

That distinction would become the final shape of my life: preparing perfectly for an act I refused to perform.

I would replace ropes before they frayed, polish the handle’s stone collar, calculate counterweights, and keep the mechanism balanced closely enough that a child could open the lid with one hand. Then I would set a wedge beneath the release and record, each day, that it remained in place.

People would assume I guarded something below.

In truth, I would be supporting the refusal itself.

For every structure must bear something. A sealed door bears the possibility of passage. A lid bears not only the architecture above but the decision not to know. A missing person may bear a household’s shape for longer than any pier. Perhaps an unanswered footstep bears the walker who failed to make it.

When I died, my household would face a difficulty.

My body would not be where its weight appeared to be. The bier would sag beside me. Carriers would feel a fourth shoulder under the pole. Set above the household lid, I would produce no knock—but in the courtyard behind them, a single step would sound.

The wardens would wait through the roof-rain.

Then they would build my body into the house, because the lid had remained silent. My sealed jar would be mortared into the brace beside the lifting frame. The charter would add my name beneath the others, though the old blurred line would vanish at last.

Afterward, travelers crossing the Plain of Lids would sometimes hear an additional footstep. This one would not follow them.

It would remain beside the smallest slab, pacing around its edge, testing the load.

And far away, under the Red Causeway, in a room inaccessible from any known level, an adult palm would at last settle over the clay handprint of a newborn.

The pier there would speak evenly.

For a while.
