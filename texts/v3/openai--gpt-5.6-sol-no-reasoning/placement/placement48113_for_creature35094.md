I would find myself at the chamber’s unfinished margin, where the smooth wall tests forms for lives that have not arrived.

At first I might seem to be one of those forms: a shallow recess in pale tissue, roughly the size of a curled body. For years it would have no occupant. Its ribs would appear, soften, and be redrawn. Water would trace its edges. Hair roots would enter and retreat. The colony would try different entrances: a slit at floor level, a round opening among the hanging roots, a porous membrane through which only breath and vibration could pass.

But the recess would not be waiting for me.

It would be me.

I would be a creature whose animal body is temporary and whose enduring body is a place.

In motion, I would resemble a small, many-jointed amphibian, translucent and nearly without pigment. Six short limbs would let me distribute my weight over the root raft. Along my sides would lie folded membranes—not wings or gills exactly, but thin sensory fans capable of reading the chamber’s pressure currents. When opened, they would show branching lines of green-black symbionts. These would feed on the colony’s dim light and translate changes in chemistry into contractions of my skin.

I would have no eyes. An eye isolates a direction, and direction here is unreliable. Instead, my skull would be hollow and finely partitioned, a resonating organ filled with droplets. Through it I would hear the chamber bodily: the load in the pillars, the approach of a browser, water gathering behind a wall, the minute fracture of mineral under roots. The world would not appear to me as surfaces at a distance. It would arrive as tensions passing through everything.

I would move rarely. Most of the time I would press myself into the pale niche at the margin until its lining closed around my limbs and entered the grooves of my skin. My heartbeat would slow to match the chamber’s several-hour contraction. Fluids would pass both ways. The wall would sample what I had encountered; I would receive, in return, a muddled inheritance of pressures and chemical traces from elsewhere in the colony.

Not knowledge as images. Inclinations.

A reluctance to place weight on one stretch of floor.

Hunger when a mineral seam needed opening.

Unease before a distant roof shift.

The remembered shape of an animal I had never met.

While joined to the wall, I would serve as an organ for imagining movement. The colony can model stresses, growth, and flows exquisitely, but it cannot easily model what it is to detach, choose, hesitate, or turn back. I would lend it that uncertainty. In sleep my limbs would twitch through possible paths. The niche would read each pressure. Some imagined routes would later become real corridors; others would be rejected and absorbed.

The colony, in turn, would lend me continuity. Each time I left the niche, I would be somewhat different. The wall might thicken a membrane, alter the spacing of my feet, seed my gut with a new culture, or leave dormant buds beneath my skin. I would not reproduce by mating. After enough journeys, one of those buds would remain behind in the niche. The recess would deepen around it, nourish it, and slowly give it a body assembled from both my tissues and the chamber’s.

What emerged would not be my child in the ordinary sense. It would inherit my routes, but only as bodily biases: one foot turning naturally toward cold water, a resonant chamber tuned to a certain flower’s call, an aversion to the chemical signature of an old predator. It would also inherit revisions from the wall. It might possess eight limbs where I had six, or a narrower skull suited to a passage not yet opened.

We would be a lineage of provisional answers.

My journeys would take me where the chamber was least certain of itself: fresh cracks, newly joined hollows, places where foreign spores had appeared, or where the behavior of animals no longer matched old patterns. The vegetation would loosen routes for me, but not completely. I would have to decide where to push, where to wait, and when to retreat. That friction would be the point. A perfectly guided messenger could report only what the colony already expected.

In neighboring chambers I might be mistaken for a parasite. Roots would constrict around me. Bitter films would coat my mouth. Local mites would gather in my joints. I would remain still and allow myself to be sampled. If accepted, I would unfold my side membranes and release living fragments collected from home: microbes, mineral grains, flower compounds, sloughed cells containing histories of stress.

I would take fragments back as well.

Yet the most important thing I carried would not be substance. It would be incompatibility. My body would register where one colony’s rhythms failed to align with another’s—where contraction met release, where one kind of root interpreted pressure differently, where familiar healing compounds provoked closure. I would return fevered, swollen, or altered, and press that discord into my niche. The wall would spend years translating it into new anatomy.

Perhaps this would make me less an ambassador than a wound that travels safely.

When two chambers approached contact, I would feel it before an opening formed. My niche would become restless around me. Its smooth tissue would ripple through unfinished geometries. I would be repeatedly released and recalled, walking the narrowing interval between known space and the blind wall. On the far side, perhaps another being like me would be doing the same—not my species exactly, but another chamber’s solution to uncertainty.

We would meet in the first pinhole passage.

Neither of us would have language. We would touch skulls and allow our resonant droplets to tremble together. Each would hear, faintly and impossibly, the architecture behind the other: two slow pulses, two distributions of water, two living landscapes discovering that the world contains another way to hold itself up.

We might fight. We might exchange infections. One of us might be sealed into the wall and dismantled. Negotiation here would not be gentle merely because it was biological.

But if we separated and returned home, both chambers would begin to change.

My final journey would come when I could no longer detach cleanly from the niche. Roots would have entered too deeply into my membranes. My limbs would be stiff with green tissue; the droplets in my skull would have clouded with mineral. The wall would loosen me once more, and I would cross the chamber very slowly.

Perhaps I would choose the central nave. Perhaps a place beneath an old flower where gliders often landed. There I would settle and open all my sensory fans. The floor would grow up through them. My hollow skull would become a resonating bulb in the root network. My limbs would stiffen into braces. Water would circulate through spaces once used for hearing.

I would not become the whole colony. I would not remain myself.

I would become a small architectural memory of having moved freely.

Animals crossing that patch would alter the pressure in my buried bones. The colony would feel their gait through the shape I had left behind. New corridors might be planned from those signals. Creatures descended from my buds might pause over me without knowing why, their bodies briefly aligned with mine.

So I would live there as the chamber’s detachable uncertainty: an animal grown whenever the landscape needs to leave itself, and a room grown whenever the animal needs to return.

Not resident, messenger, organ, or offspring, but the reversible passage between them.
