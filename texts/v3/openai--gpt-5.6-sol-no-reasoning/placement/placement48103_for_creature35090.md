I would find myself far below the rain, where an immense stair descends along the inner wall of a reservoir and vanishes beneath an older roof.

The stair would not appear at first as a stair. Its risers have been worn into a single shallow gradient, and black leaves lie so closely against the stone that the whole slope resembles one continuous surface. Only where the leaves narrow into the ancient joints would the buried construction become evident: parallel courses turning downward, landing after landing, into water untouched by daylight.

Halfway down, the outer wall has opened by the width of a hand.

Through that opening comes a current from another basin. It is almost imperceptible—colder by less than a degree, carrying pale mineral dust and the dissolved residue of upper gardens. The water takes years to cross the landing. Yet the seam is the most eventful place for leagues around. It joins two bodies that had been separate since a gate settled shut in some remote district.

I would be the growth inhabiting that seam.

Not the dark leaves on the stair, though I would exchange substance with them. Not the translucent film inside the wall, though portions of me would disappear among its threads. I would be a narrow colonial border where several kinds of life have met and become difficult to distinguish: a black-green margin spread over the cold face of the blocks, pale filaments penetrating the mortar, and long colorless strands extending into the water along the path of the seep.

From a distance I would look like staining.

Close by, if closeness and looking still had meaning, I would have structure. My upper cells would lie flattened beneath the pressure of the current. Deeper layers would enter tiny cavities left by grains washed from the mortar. Dead layers would remain behind me as a soft dark packing, changing the seam through which the water passes. New growth would advance over that residue by fractions of an inch.

There would be no central body. Parts of me would be buried alive inside the wall. Parts would spread beneath the leaves covering the stair. Fragments would break away and travel into the reservoir, perhaps taking decades to reach its opposite side. Most would settle in darkness and fail. One might lodge in an inscription groove on a higher ramp, receive a little light during a rare clearing, and become a separate dark line. Whether that line was still me would be a question no inhabitant of this world needed to answer.

My life would be measured by changes in the architecture.

A season would be the arrival of colder water.

An age would be the widening of the joint by one grain.

A catastrophe would be the stair shifting against the wall and closing the seam.

A migration would be a trace of my substance carried through it before it shut.

I would not perceive the reservoir or know that the slope beside me had once been used by feet. But I would respond to what remained of those vanished purposes. I would grow more densely along the tread edges where silt gathered. I would retreat from the limestone and enter the old brickwork. I would follow the seep upward through the wall until darkness or mineral poverty stopped me. Through my distribution, the submerged stair would become faintly legible: a dark comb descending across a darker plane.

Over centuries I would alter the current that sustained me. My dead matter would gather in the crack. Filaments would bind incoming sediment. The hand-wide opening would narrow. Water would pass more slowly, and the pale films dependent on what it carried would thin. Eventually the seam might seal.

Then most of me would die without violence.

The exposed margin would collapse into brown softness beneath the black leaves. The interior threads would persist longer, feeding on what had accumulated in the mortar. Some would remain dormant in the cold masonry, neither flourishing nor wholly gone. I would become another substance packed between the blocks: not builder, not mortar, not soil, but a layer through which those conditions passed into one another.

Far away, however, the current would already have carried pieces of me elsewhere.

Perhaps one would reach a shaft beneath the reservoir, settle on a lead clamp, and survive as a translucent veil. Perhaps another would enter a brighter court and darken into something broad and leaflike. Perhaps nothing recognizable would continue, and only a slight chemical inheritance would pass into colonies that had never touched the stair.

So I would not be an individual in the usual sense. I would be a conjunction temporarily given continuity by water: upper growth, buried thread, mineral film, drifting fragment, and the dark residue of earlier layers. My extent would be uncertain, my death distributed, my descendants indistinguishable from neighbors.

I would be found where the world admits, very slowly, that none of its walls are absolute.

Not at a grand gate or a visible ruin. At a flaw in the fitted stone. At the meeting of two cold waters. On a stair no one descends, making its erased steps readable by covering them.
