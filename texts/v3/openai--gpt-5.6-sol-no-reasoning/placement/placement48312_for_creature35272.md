I would find myself beneath the orchard rather than within its visible tumult, in the narrow, braided country where the wind-tree roots meet.

Not a burrower, though they would pass through me. Not a root, exactly. I would be one of the things the keepers have no settled name for: a small colonial creature living in the sleeves of stillness made where two root cords touch. Soft-bodied, translucent, almost impossible to separate from the damp gray fibers around me. Each individual no larger than a fingernail clipping; together, perhaps, the length of an arm.

We would fasten one tree to another.

At first this would seem parasitic. We would pierce the outer fibers with mouths like hollow thorns and drink the minute moisture moving through them. But we would also consume rot, salt excess, burrower slime, and the pale grubs that enter wounds. Our bodies would span gaps the roots cannot safely cross. When one tree pulls away, we would stretch into threads. When the strain becomes too great, we would divide cleanly, sealing both broken ends with wax.

The trees would use us without knowing us. Or they would know us in the slow, distributed way the orchard knows anything.

Air pressure in one root system would pass through our joined bodies as a tremor. We would answer by tightening or loosening. A thirsty tree might draw a trace of moisture from its neighbor through us. A diseased one might be cut off. During migration, we would become temporary tendons between elders and saplings, holding until new fibers braid, then digesting our own abandoned bridges.

Our lives would be measured not by seasons but by tensions.

The visible orchard would reach us as pressure, vibration, and taste. Bell-throat panic would arrive as a scatter of impacts. A hollow-jaw’s pursuit would be a rapid sequence of weight and loosened soil. Evening ascent would make the roots hum against stone until every body in the colony aligned lengthwise. We would taste distant places in what filtered down: copper dust from glider sails, velvet acids, animal salts, smoke from a keeper’s cooking jar, blood from a thorn wound.

We would have no eyes. Light would be a warmth occasionally passing through the soil when some living island migrated away and left us exposed. Exposure would be disaster. We would contract at once, becoming hard gray beads indistinguishable from grit. Some of us would be eaten by burrowers and later coughed out elsewhere, polished but alive. That would be our principal means of travel.

So even we, who seem to bind the orchard together, would never remain.

The keepers might notice that certain trees migrate in company, separating aboveground while staying mysteriously coordinated. They might uncover one of our bridges while moving a stone from a young root knot. To them it would look like a trembling translucent cord threaded with dark grains. If touched, it would tighten around the finger—not to seize it, but because touch means two bodies have met, and meeting is what we are for.

A careful keeper would lay the stone back.

A careless one might pull. We would cling until our bodies parted. The torn colony would release a faint smell like wet iron, startling in a place with almost no wetness. For years afterward, both trees would carry a hitch in their evening notes.

Chord-minders would hear it.

Perhaps they would call us **the interval**, because we exist neither in one tree nor the other, but in the changing distance between them. Perhaps they would deny that we are creatures at all. Some would say we are stray tree tissue. Others, underground velvet. Others, the orchard’s stitching.

All would be partly right.

When a keeper’s body enters the roots—after the crowns have opened the wrapping and the wind has distributed what it can—we would gather around whatever descends. Not devour it quickly. We are too small for that. We would fasten fragments into the living network: hair against fiber, bone dust in wax, a tooth lodged where several colonies meet. Calcium would strengthen our bridges. Fat would sustain a brood. The dead person’s salts would pass slowly among trees that might stand on opposite sides of the hollow by the time the process ended.

Years later, one of those trees might expel a resonant knot.

Its channels would preserve not a voice, exactly, but the pressures that once crossed us: bell-throat running, root migration, a shelter rope snapping, the deep tremor of a claimed person falling from crown to crown. Sounded beside the northern cliff, the knot might produce two notes held very close together, beating against each other.

The keepers might hear a name in that beating.

But if I had anything like a self, it would not reside in one body or even one colony. It would occur only while contact lasted. I would become more myself whenever two migrating lives pulled in different directions and, for a little while, did not part.

My whole being would be a fastening that permits movement.

Not an anchor. An accord.

And when the roots finally drew too far apart, I would not die so much as become plural: one half traveling east beneath an elder, the other west with a young tree, each sealing over, each carrying a little of the other’s tension.

In the Wind Orchard, that would be the closest thing to belonging.
