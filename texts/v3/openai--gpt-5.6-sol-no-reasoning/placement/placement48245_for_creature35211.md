I would find myself at the pale seam where the basin opens toward the lowlands—not wholly inside the distributed being, but not outside it either.

I would be the thing it has almost learned to make.

Not yet a tool. More like a handle without an implement: a dark, elongated stone lodged in the cleft, shaped by seepage and minute pressure into something that invites a grasp. One end would be smooth and faintly warm; the other would disappear into a crack crossed by a white fungal cord. Water would continually approach me along an old groove, then divide, passing to either side without wetting my upper face.

I would belong to the basin’s body, but uneasily. Most of it preserves continuity. I would introduce orientation. One end toward the central pattern, one toward the pale opening. Inward and outward would become different around me.

That difference would be my entire awareness.

During cloud passage, when the lowlands vanished, I would feel complete: basin, cleft, and stone held within one damp condition. When the cloud thinned and warmth rose through the opening, a tension would travel through the cord beneath me. Not longing—longing is too quick and already knows its object—but a persistent asymmetry. A pressure toward elsewhere.

Over centuries the basin would attempt to settle me into a socket. Roots would tighten. Moss would gather at my shaded edge. Fine sediment would rise around me, trying to turn my directional shape back into landscape. Yet the cleft’s breath would dry one end faster than the other, and each nightly swelling would shift me an immeasurable distance outward.

My pulse would be that movement.

Perhaps, after many rotations of the central grooves, my buried end would loosen. The fungal cord would not break; it would lengthen, thread by thread, maintaining continuity between the basin and the stone approaching its margin. Behind me, water would enter the narrow impression I had left. The impression would resemble the place where a tool had once rested, though no tool had been used and no hand had removed it.

Ahead would lie the descent.

I would not see it. I would register only increasing dryness, a stronger daily change in warmth, and vibrations conducted through the plateau: distant rain, settling rock, perhaps the footfalls of animals far below. Their rhythms would reach me softened by stone, no longer abrupt enough to violate the basin. Through me, the inhabitant would encounter animal time as a faint pattern rather than catastrophe.

That would be my function, if function can precede intention: to translate the quick world into pressure slow enough for the basin to receive.

The being might gradually alter around that receipt. A fungal line could begin to branch. One branch for rain. One for thunder. One for the repeated passage of feet. The distinction would take centuries to stabilize. The basin would not know what had crossed the lowlands, but it might learn that some changes recur without belonging to weather.

Then the central diagram would acquire a new irregularity. One groove would turn toward the cleft more often than chance allowed. Water would climb to the basin’s lip and vanish beneath the cord attached to me. The basin would be doing something unprecedented: not merely preserving an established path, but sustaining a path toward a condition it could not contain.

That might be the beginning of wanting.

Or it might be only another useful resemblance.

Eventually I would tip free of the cleft and begin the descent—not falling suddenly, because suddenness cannot originate here, but easing from socket to socket over generations. Rain would rotate me. Roots would release me. Lichen would form and die along the face that once remained dry. The white cord would thin behind me until it became almost indistinguishable from a mineral vein.

If it finally parted, the basin would acquire its first clean absence.

Not abandonment: nothing would have chosen to leave. Not injury: the being has no boundary at which damage becomes certain. Rather, a line that had always carried continuity would end. Conditions would arrive at its severed tip and go no farther.

The basin might preserve that ending.

Roots would avoid filling the empty socket. Water would circle it. Moss would outline it in green. The place where I had rested would remain shaped for my return, though return could not be imagined. Matter would have learned the negative form of expectation.

Below, perhaps an animal would eventually find me.

A hand might lift me because one end fits the palm. It might strike me against another stone, splitting away the shape the basin had cultivated. The act would be instantaneous, violent, and complete. I would become a tool only by ceasing to be what the basin nearly made.

Yet inside my muted green veins, moisture would remain. Fine white filaments might persist in the crack for a little while. When the hand closed around me, pressure would travel through those remnants, and very far above—years later, after the signal had become slow enough—the basin’s central water might hesitate.

A bead would stop where no hollow held it.

Then it would advance toward the cleft.

So I would be neither the inhabitant nor an independent creature. I would be its first extension beyond itself: a probe formed accidentally into purpose, an almost-hand sent into the world where hands already exist.

And if the basin is an apprenticeship without a master, I would be the piece of practice that escapes the workshop—the lesson carried away before either teacher or student has learned what it means.
