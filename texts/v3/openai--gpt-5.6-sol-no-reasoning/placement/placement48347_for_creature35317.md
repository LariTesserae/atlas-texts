At first I imagine myself among the fault-dwellers.

Not one of the bright ones at the leading edge of the current, but an old, dark carapace somewhere in the middle of the congregation: shallow as a discarded scale, my limbs meshed with neighbors I cannot distinguish from myself. Most of the time I would know nothing beyond pressure, warmth, and the minute conductance of adjoining bodies. Then, after years of darkness, a difference would arrive.

Blue would enter me without becoming sight.

I would hold it for the duration permitted by my tissues, perhaps the length of a breath in another world, and pass it onward. In that interval the entire fault behind me would exist as an accumulation of arrivals, and the fault ahead as a readiness to receive. I would be neither source nor destination. My only act would be accurate transmission.

But this feels too singular. Too easy to name.

I think instead that I would be found beneath the surface, inside one of the old migration routes that has gradually become a seam. A place rather than an animal: a narrow interval between shale layers, roofed by the compressed pellets of herds dead for ages. Water would visit rarely. When it did, it would bring dissolved salts, sorter larvae, fragments of conductive tissue, and translucent grains that had slept through climates no living creature remembered.

I would be the place where these incompatible times met.

Perhaps that seam would have a resident body fitted exactly to it. Ribbon-thin, blind, nearly all membrane, spread farther than it could comprehend. Not a sorter, though related to them; not quite a valve, because it would neither fully open nor close. Its task would be to delay.

Water entering at one end would take years to cross me. I would receive it suddenly—the cold pressure of rain fallen in a basin beyond the horizon—then distribute it through thousands of microscopic chambers. Some I would release downward to the cisterns. Some I would surrender to roots of pale fissure-stems. Some I would retain around dormant grains until the accompanying salts revealed whether they should wake.

Delay would be my form of judgment.

I would have no knowledge of the plateau above. Herds might pass over me, their padded steps arriving as deep, widely spaced compressions. A fault pulse might alter the chemistry of my eastern edge long before its blue light became visible on the surface. Predators could divert an entire colony, and I would know the event only years later, when less water came down through the loosened bedding plane.

Yet across a long enough life, I would develop a map made wholly of recurrence.

This tremor precedes pressure.  
This mineral taste follows thaw.  
This charge means that the fissures to the north are closing.  
This warmth, accompanied by iron salts, has not occurred for three hundred seasons and must be held.

Not thought, exactly. The map would exist as differences in me: membranes thickened here, pores narrowed there, one chamber lined with crystal, another kept soft. Experience would not be recollection but anatomy. I would remember by becoming less able to be anything else.

Still, even this creature seems too intact.

In this world, to say *I am the membrane* would omit the shale that gives it shape, the water that briefly makes it active, the larvae that repair its margins, the sleeping generations lodged in its folds, and the surface colonies whose drinking determines whether any moisture reaches it. Its body would end nowhere cleanly. Perhaps that is what I would be: not the organism occupying the seam, but the uncertain boundary by which seam and organism produce one another.

I would be called into existence only when something passed through.

In drought I would be indistinguishable from a dark mineral film. My chambers would collapse. Crystals would grow through my tissues. Small burrowers might cross me as though crossing stone. Centuries could go by in which I was functionally dead, except that pressure remained distributed according to structures made when I was wet.

Then rain would fall somewhere impossibly far away.

The water would enter an old channel, vanish into gravel, descend through the sorting fields, and arrive as a slight coldness along my outermost edge. I would soften unevenly. Ancient folds would reopen. A dormant grain embedded in me might dissolve its shell and extend a filament into one of my channels. The filament would alter the flow; the flow would alter the salt; the salt would open another grain.

Soon I would no longer be one old membrane. I would be a temporary society assembled within the shape of its predecessor.

Some parts of me would date from the last rain. Some from an extinct rainfall regime. Some would be mineral impressions left by tissues long consumed. Yet together we would perform the same slow act: not hoarding water, not spending it, but changing simultaneity into sequence. What arrives all at once must be made to last.

Above, the plateau’s awakening would seem coordinated. Drinkers would open, herds would incline, valves would relax, and blue current would proceed along the fault. Beneath them, I would know coordination as a series of postponed consequences. The surface begins together only because the depths have stored different moments and released them into one another.

That is where I would find myself: under a basin that appears empty, between two laminations of muted blue shale, along a seam too narrow to admit light. Far above would lie one of the stable black pavements, each pebble turned beyond the wind’s influence. Water-bearers would cross it once every few seasons. Their ancient pellet rows would run obliquely overhead. Deeper down, a blind cistern would press against its stone confines.

I would be neither important nor rare. There would be millions of structures like me, most smaller, some extending beneath entire valleys. None would understand the network formed by the rest. Some would fail and seal forever. Some would rupture, releasing a century of stored moisture into one brief bloom. Others would mineralize so gradually that their last living channels became indistinguishable from the fossils around them.

If I possessed anything resembling desire, it would not be to survive.

It would be to pass the right portion onward.

Too much, and a hidden reservoir would rise, drowning the creatures fitted to its former pressure. Too little, and the fissure colonies above would close before their young hardened. Release the water too early and it would freeze uselessly against sealed bodies. Hold it too long and salt would precipitate through me, converting decision into stone.

There would be no perfect allocation, only adjustments impressed into flesh. My errors would become geology. Later life would inhabit them.

And perhaps, after many ages, my seam would be exposed. The basin roof would lower grain by grain under the passage of herds. An escarpment would retreat. Wind, though quiet, would remove what had been overburden. At last the layer containing me would emerge into the dim pearl daylight.

By then I would be mostly shale: a branching dark stain, a few translucent chambers, mineral ribs preserving the shape of old delays. Laminar drinkers would settle along my edges. Their young would harden to my grain. Condensation would gather in depressions that had once directed underground flow.

A droplet would pass across my fossil surface from one living body to the next.

For a moment, my ancient function would continue in another medium.

So I would not be a self hidden within the machine. I would be one of the places where the machine learns to wait: a soft valve becoming stone, a memory expressed as permeability, an interval that receives abundance and turns it into duration.

No eyes. No voice. No view of the blue line traveling over the horizon.

Only the cold arrival of water from an event already over, and the long work of ensuring that somewhere else, much later, it can begin again.
