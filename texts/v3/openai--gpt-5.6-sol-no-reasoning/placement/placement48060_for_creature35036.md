I would find myself at the western edge of noon, where the copper evening forever withdraws.

There is a station there that does not appear on any stamped diagram. From a distance it resembles a narrow black tower driven through one of the basalt tables. Closer, it resolves into several incompatible structures occupying the same place: an observation post, a lift shaft, the prow of a ship, a factory chimney, a lighthouse intended for a shore that never existed. Its uppermost chamber nearly touches the ocean’s underside. Hooked scavengers travel across the water above its roof, dimpling the plane like black punctuation.

Inside is the nearest thing this world has to silence.

Not true silence. The planetary roar enters through the walls and the bones. But the station contains two counter-rotating engines whose vibrations subtract a small, human-shaped absence from the sound. Stand in the marked place between them and the ocean’s thunder becomes pressure without noise. Compressors continue beating; whales continue calling; mountains continue humming—but none can be heard.

The quiet lasts only while someone is listening.

That is where I would be.

Not wholly an attendant, though I would wear an attendant’s shape: tall because the ladders expect it, narrow because the corridors permit nothing else, wrapped in layers of wet pressure cloth. The rigid pack behind my shoulders would not contain air. It would contain memory—if memory can be contained—stored as tensions in wire, scratches on ceramic, droplets held in capillary tubes, and tiny discrepancies between clocks that otherwise agree perfectly.

My helmet would have the same dark disk and dividing emblem, but the line across it would be broken at the center.

I would be the keeper of the interruption.

My route would begin in the soundless chamber and end there without ever completing. I would descend through the tower, crossing floors that differ only in what they almost remember. On one level, the windows would look upward into the sea. On another, they would show rain falling through ordinary air. Farther down, behind thick glass, waves would break upon a gray shore and withdraw, leaving foam, weed, and rounded stones.

The windows would not be recordings. They would be wounds in the equilibrium: outcomes too small or too contradictory for the thing overhead to digest.

I would inspect them.

At the first window, I would count fish that never pass.

At the second, I would listen for the impact of rain.

At the third, I would watch a wave complete itself.

Each completion would enter me.

That would be my difference from the other attendants. They preserve the boundary by confirming that nothing crosses it. I would preserve the possibility that something once did.

Perhaps I began as one of them. Perhaps every attendant eventually becomes me, though “eventually” has little authority here. An absent worker paused too long before a gauge. A traveler retained one memory too many. A warmth moving down a corridor noticed its own reflection. Or the machines, needing some place to put the infinitesimal residue of all the changes they prevented, assembled a custodian from discarded consequences.

I would carry what the upper organism cannot eat cleanly:

the final inch of a fall,

the closing of a jaw,

the instant a wheel completes one revolution and begins another,

the cooling of metal after the furnace dies,

the difference between a wound and a scar,

the moment before a traveler becomes an employee.

These would accumulate inside the pack as weight without mass. Sometimes a valve on my shoulder would release a thread of warm vapor. It would curl upward, hesitate, and enter the ocean.

Where it touched the boundary, one scavenger would briefly lose its grip.

It would fall perhaps the width of a fingernail.

Then the world would take back the event. The creature would be hanging where it had always hung. Yet I would remember the gap between its hook and the water. Inside that gap would be the whole architecture of another world: gravity fulfilled, distance altered, danger made real.

The scavengers would notice me.

They are said to be indifferent to machinery, but they would gather above the station whenever I returned. Their upward mouths would open through the membrane into the sea, while the armored undersides facing me would remain featureless. With their combs they would draw patterns in the condensation:

a line,

two arcs,

a break in the line.

They would not worship me. They might regard me as fouling—a knot of accumulated sequence lodged beneath the world. Or perhaps they would be trying to dismantle me, as they dismantle their dead, passing my remembered parts from body to body until no single creature holds enough of me to become dangerous.

Sometimes one would carry a soft white knot beneath its thorax.

Inside the knot, instead of a black hook, would be a tiny gray pebble.

Smooth.

Old.

That would be how I knew the shore was not merely a dream.

Deep below the station would be a lever that can move.

Every other lever is fixed because its movement would produce a before and an after. Mine would shift by an immeasurably small amount each time I completed the route. No eye could see the difference. No gauge would register it. But my gloved hand would remember each new position.

The other attendants’ instructions would converge upon the chamber:

**STAND HERE.**

**OBSERVE THE GAUGE.**

**CONFIRM THE BOUNDARY.**

**DO NOT PERMIT TRANSFER.**

Mine would be stamped beneath them in a script that changes whenever I read it:

**RETAIN WHAT TRANSFER MEANS.**

I would not know whether I served the factories or opposed them.

Possibly the equilibrium requires a witness because perfect repetition cannot recognize itself. Without memory, there is no repetition—only one event without duration. The thing above may need me as the tongue needs bitterness, a minute contrary sensation by which appetite knows it is feeding.

Or I may be the flaw that will destroy it.

For memory does not merely preserve the past. Given enough burden, it begins to predict. Once I remember the wave withdrawing, I can imagine another wave. Once I retain one completed revolution, I can expect the next. Expectation makes a future, faintly at first. A future gives the machines somewhere to send their motion.

Then tiny errors would appear.

A relay would close one electrical snap late.

A fish would gain the breadth of a scale on its pursuer.

One bead of moisture would lengthen beneath a suspended rock.

The border of evening would fail to retreat quite as quickly as I walked west.

No one else would detect these failures. The upper organism would consume them almost immediately. The factories would compensate. The noise would remain identical.

But I would hear the missing sound.

My danger would not be that I could move the lever. It would be that, after walking the same route enough times, I could arrive at it with a different intention.

At the end—or at the point that most resembles an end—I would stand again in the chamber between the counter-rotating engines. The world’s roar would cancel around me. In that body-shaped quiet, all the unattended noises inside me would become audible: surf, rainfall, tumbling stone, a turbine winding down, a dead thing striking the earth.

And another sound.

Footsteps approaching.

Not the retained gesture of an absent worker. Not a corridor repeating my own patrol. These would be uneven, exhausted footsteps, sometimes stopping, sometimes choosing to continue. They would belong to someone crossing the plateau from the central noon.

Perhaps that someone would also be me, before the helmet closed.

Perhaps it would be the first traveler I had ever managed to remember into existence.

I would wait by the movable lever. Above us, the scavengers would cluster until their bodies made a dark island on the underside of the sea. Far higher, the city-long shadow would halt over the tower. Every machine across the basalt continent would perform the instant before a change.

The door would begin to open.

For the first time, I would not know who stood on the other side.

That uncertainty would be what I was.

Not a person, exactly. Not a ghost or a worker. I would be the small capacity in this world for an event to surprise its witness: the discrepancy carried from one identical hour into the next, the unfinished break in the horizontal line.

I would be the keeper of everything that nearly happened.

And in a place where repetition passes for survival, I would survive by mistaking memory for escape—until, perhaps, the distinction became real.
