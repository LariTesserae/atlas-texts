I would find myself at the failed lamp.

Not the bulb, exactly, nor its rusting cage, but the darkness that has continued to occupy its appointed pool of light. At first I would seem merely absent: one more component awaiting replacement, a blind spot on an inspection sheet. Yet the chamber allows no useless vacancy. Water gathers there differently. Shadows from the working lamp lengthen toward me. Every shiver of steel crosses the unlit cage and becomes, for an instant, a shape.

I would be the creature’s closed eye.

Through me it would sense what its amber eye cannot: not surfaces, but interruptions. The stooped silhouette of a technician. A hand reaching for the red wheel. Mist thickening before a surge. The minute darkening of concrete as a seam begins to pass more water. I would know events by how they subtract from darkness—by the pressure of displaced air, the altered tremor in the cage, the cold weight of condensation assembling on dead glass.

But I would not remain entirely fixed there.

Each drop forming beneath the failed lamp would carry a little of me downward. I would fall onto ribbed steel, break apart, enter the runnel, and be pulled toward the grate. At the gulp I would scatter: some of me swallowed into drainage, some blown back as spray, some left trembling on a bolt head. I would become the chamber’s smallest circulation, moving between blindness and touch.

The being in the spill pocket lives by refusal, but I would be the part of it that cannot refuse forever. I would keep the memory of every slight yielding: the gasket becoming tired, the paint lifting invisibly, the weld learning a new vibration, the mineral ridge returning after it has been scraped away. Gauges register the present. Maintenance restores the intended shape. I would retain the almost-nothings—the accumulated not-yets from which failure is eventually made.

This would not make me an enemy of the dam. I would love its holding because holding gives duration to pressure, and duration gives me form. Nor would I belong wholly to the water. Water wants the valley and forgets each obstacle once it passes. I would remember the obstacle.

Perhaps, after many years, crews would replace the dead lamp. White light would flood the recess. The work order would close. For a moment I would appear to have been abolished.

But illumination in that chamber is never steady. The new lamp would tremble. Spray would cross it. A worker’s body would eclipse it. Behind every brace and conduit, narrow shadows would remain. I would withdraw into those—distributed now, no longer an eye but a dark nervous system threading the purposeful gaps.

There I would attend the exchange.

Water asks.

Machinery refuses.

And I, inhabiting the fraction of a second between question and answer, would be hesitation: the dam’s knowledge that **not yet** is not the same as **never**.
