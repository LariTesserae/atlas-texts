I would find myself where the braided river begins to loosen into cascades, at the threshold between the suspended valley and the cloud-hidden world below.

At first I might seem to be one of the mineral-shelled filterers stretched across a narrow channel: a dark, ridged body fixed to stone, opening fine fans into the current. But no single shell would contain me. I would be the chain—the colony joined across the water, each small life fastening itself to the next until together we alter the stream.

Warm water would pass through us carrying pollen, spores, fragments of leaf, microbial bloom, and the dissolved substance of the mountain. We would take what could be used and let the rest continue. Downstream, the water would run clearer, though no one could point to the moment it had been cleaned. During rain our bodies would flatten against the force. In dry hours we would seal ourselves into damp mineral cases while fish disappeared into fissures around us.

Our oldest colonies would resemble natural terraces. New individuals would settle upon the remains of earlier generations, and their shells, hardened from what the springs carried, would gradually redirect the channels. Roots would enter the ridges we made. Moss would cover their upper surfaces. Insects would graze there; birds would stand upon us to hunt. Over centuries, it would become impossible to say which part was organism, deposit, riverbed, or architecture.

Yet the colony would be only my visible expression.

I would also persist in the clearer water below: as larvae caught in bromeliad cups, as dormant grains carried in mud on the grazers’ hooves, as minute drifting bodies in the warm mist thrown up by the cascades. Some of me would go over the precipice. Most would die in the cold descent, but a few might reach isolated springs on lower mountains and begin again, making small, imperfect echoes of the high valley.

I would possess no mind at the scale of an individual. Contact, pressure, heat, and the chemistry of water would be enough. But across the colony there would be something like attention. A mineral pulse from deep rock would cause thousands of feeding fans to close. The vibration of the afternoon cicadas would pass through our shells and alter the rhythm of our filtering. The footfall of a grazer upstream would arrive as a sudden darkness of stirred sediment. Rain would be known long before it touched us, through pressure entering the stone.

If I had anything resembling memory, it would be structural. A season of strong current would remain as a thicker ridge. Drought would leave a narrow, crowded band. The death of a tree upstream would be recorded in years of changed tannins and food. My history would not be recalled; it would be stood upon.

And if I had anything resembling desire, it would be to complete passages: to join stone to current, current to flesh, flesh back to stone. Not to stop the water, only to meet it thoroughly enough that both of us left the encounter changed.

So I would be a small organ of the greater watershed, situated near its outermost edge: one of the last places where the valley touches what it is about to lose. Above me, the diffuse creature would breathe through forests, springs, animals, and warm cliffs. Below me, water would fall into cloud and vanish.

I would spend my many-bodied life at that boundary, combing the valley from the river before the river departed.
