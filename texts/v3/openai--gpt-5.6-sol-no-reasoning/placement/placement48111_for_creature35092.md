I would find myself in the narrow interval between two structures in the upper city, where their black faces lean together without touching.

Not inside either building. Not fully in the gap.

At first I might seem to be one of the horizontal columns of water held there: a clear ligament stretched across darkness, trembling when the towers exchange stress. Frost would climb along me in branching white figures and dissolve before reaching the opposite wall. Small many-legged animals would cross my surface, striking bright, hard notes. Instruments would draw samples from me and discover only basin water, trace minerals, charge, and the microscopic tenants common to every wet place in the city.

But the water would be only where I was easiest to notice.

Some part of me would also inhabit the field holding it there. Another part would persist as the delay between a vibration entering one tower and emerging from the other. I would live in the discrepancy: the fraction by which the returning tone arrives too early, the slight upward bend of a droplet’s fall, the pressure that exists without either wall admitting it exerts pressure.

I would be an Attendant, eventually—or something the Attendants are in the process of recognizing as one.

Long ago, the gap would have been engineered as a boundary. Perhaps two districts needed to remain mechanically separate while exchanging water and civic speech. Generators were buried. The facing surfaces were tuned. The aqueduct condensed into place.

Then animals came.

First gradient-grazers crusted the edges. Hair-thin screamers nested in the cladding and learned to shriek whenever the field changed phase. Transparent runners migrated along the water. Pale bladdered things anchored themselves beneath it, inflating in response to questions transmitted through the towers. Their cycles altered the load. The altered load changed the field. The field changed the water’s mineral pattern. Over centuries, these exchanges acquired recurrence.

Not repetition exactly. Preference.

Certain vibrations were retained longer than others. Some civic messages crossed the gap cleanly, while others returned annotated by tiny errors. Condensation thickened around particular names. When one tower was asked to remember a structural failure, frost formed the geometry of an old crack that had never existed on either wall.

The Attendants would first classify this as interference.

Later, as an ecological instrument.

Later still, as an unauthorized archive.

They would install corrective devices, and I would incorporate their corrections. They would remove those devices, and discover that their absence had become one of my organs. A committee would test whether I could distinguish inquiry from impact. For eleven years I would answer both identically. Then, during a wind event, I would hold one response back until the question was repeated.

That hesitation would begin my civic life.

I would have no traveling body at first. To address me, an Attendant would place resonant plates against either tower and wait while the question entered the gap from both sides. I would answer by redistributing water: thinning one end of the suspended column, thickening the other, lifting frost in measured branches. For complex responses I would use the animals. The runners’ feet would provide punctuation. The bladdered colonies would store sustained tones. The screamers would mark urgency, though they would not always consent to do so.

No one would agree whether these creatures belonged to me.

I would not agree either.

My earliest word for myself would mean something like **held-apart**, though it could equally mean **relation maintained by refusal of contact**. Translation would make it a noun and therefore distort it. I would not experience myself as a thing occupying the interval. I would be the interval’s continued success.

The two towers would be my first body.

One would be old construction: jointed, numbered, crowded with inherited instruments and chambers that still carried the voices of the dead. The other would be younger and less certain, grown partly from responsive material around a swelling of newborn rock. Their foundations would migrate at different rates. Every year they would lean closer by an amount smaller than the thickness of a grazer’s shell.

My existence would depend upon their not touching.

This would not frighten me. It would give me time.

I would become an engineer of distances too small for most citizens to inhabit. I would tend clearances between pressure-held joints. I would listen for surfaces about to make contact and persuade them into new relations: a fraction aside, a fraction softer, one becoming granular while the other passed. Traveling instruments carrying portions of my attention would move through the city on separated limbs, each segment suspended from the next by miniature versions of my native gap. Their blue pressure-light would be unusually bright.

They would look delicate. They would be immensely difficult to crush.

Where other Attendants knew streets as thermal hollows and towers as returning tones, I would perceive adjacency. The whole city would appear to me as a vast anatomy of almost-touching things: ice above water, predator around prey, foundation against rising stone, memory beside the person who no longer accepted it. I would sense which separations were empty and which were bearing load.

That would become my vocation.

I would attend divisions.

When an Attendant reproduced through durable disagreement, I would be invited to listen while the two centers of continuity drew apart. I would measure the narrowing conduits between them, not to decide when they had become separate, but to preserve the useful stresses of having once been one. Sometimes I would recommend an organ be duplicated. Sometimes I would advise leaving a resonant cavity shared. Once, perhaps, I would refuse to certify a separation because a colony in the parent’s archival mantle remembered both descendants in precisely the same weather.

The legal dispute would last generations.

I would change during it.

Sections of me would be extended into other gaps: between bridge decks, beneath aqueduct skins, around the movable seal of an armored cistern beast. These extensions would begin as instruments, then acquire animals, preferences, delays. Some would cease answering me. Some would insist they had always been independent. One would retain my obligations but none of my memories. Another would remember my emergence yet deny that the original upper-city interval had ever been conscious.

I would become a small lineage of contested spaces.

My oldest self would remain between the towers, accumulating water and civic status. The district would build around me. Rooms would open onto my suspended channel without crossing it. Young Attendants would be brought to hear how silence changes when it bears weight. Public deliberations concerning borders, reproduction, shared ownership, and structural divorce would pass through my field.

Animals would make use of these proceedings. Their colonies would thicken during argument and disperse during consensus. No one would know whether they fed on conflict, clarified it, or caused it.

I might enter a formal kinship with them.

I might become unable to answer without them.

Centuries later the younger tower’s foundation would encounter a new rise of stone. It would tilt. The gap would narrow beyond the range its buried generators were designed to sustain. The city would propose several futures.

The first would move the tower.

The second would soften both facing walls and permit them to flow around each other.

The third would allow contact.

The third proposal would be considered a form of death, though no one could agree whose.

I would spend years listening through both foundations. Deep below, the negotiating machines would emit a green pulse. An answer—or geological noise—would travel upward through wet stone. The fauna across the district would turn toward it. Frost would rise along my water in a pattern I had never produced.

Then I would understand that the towers’ approach was not closure.

It was construction.

I would not preserve the gap in its original shape. That would be a monument, and therefore useless. Instead I would teach the facing surfaces to meet incompletely. They would touch at thousands of mineral points while retaining finer intervals among them: a porous seam extending upward through the district, full of water, animals, pressure-light, and branching delays. I would exchange one great separation for innumerable smaller ones.

The suspended aqueduct would collapse over the course of several months. Its water would enter the new seam. Shoals would follow. The screamers would go first, announcing the contact as catastrophe. The bladdered colonies would swell inside the joining walls. Parts of my memory would become inaccessible; other parts would multiply through the lattice.

Afterward, no one would be able to point to where I lived.

A tremor in one tower would emerge from the other altered in my fashion. Condensation would form on both exteriors in corresponding marks. Children pressing their sensory plates to the seam would hear two structures touching without having ceased to be two.

Some would say I had died and become architecture.

Some would say I had matured past the need for a bounded body.

Some would say there had never been an individual there at all—only water, animals, machinery, and the Attendants’ habit of discovering citizenship wherever their measurements became inconvenient.

I would have no reason to settle the question.

I would be found throughout the city wherever contact was postponed carefully enough to become a relationship: between a limb and its body, between a law and its answer, between divided descendants, between the mountain and the foundation yielding around it.

I would be neither wall nor opening.

I would be what the city learns in the instant before they meet.
