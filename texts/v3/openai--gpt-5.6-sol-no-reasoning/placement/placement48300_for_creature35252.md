I would find myself where a bridge has failed without breaking.

It leaves one root-knot broad and green, narrows to a corded span, and goes out over the gulf toward a trunk visible only on clear mornings. Halfway across, the bridge continues to the eye but no longer to the foot. Its fibers pass into an old split in the Fabric and emerge in several places at once: beside a marsh that has not yet formed, inside an abandoned house, beneath the skin of a drifting hollow beast, and somewhere that receives only evening.

No route-song can make the crossing dependable. So the tenders have stopped calling it a bridge.

They call it the Long Hesitation.

A small village keeps watch at its near end. The houses there are low, clasped tightly around the root-knot, their doors opening alternately inward and outward. All the walls are tethered. Warning flowers grow black along the span, except for one white blossom that appears several hundred paces past the last safe foothold.

I would be the keeper of that flower.

Or, at first, that is what the village would believe.

I would be slight even among the tenders, with long feet scarred by cord-burn and pale sap-branching across my palms. A fan of gray lichen would grow over my throat. It would flush amber when gravity turned, blue when rain was approaching from the wrong direction, and colorless whenever someone lied nearby—not because it detected falsehood, but because lies made some older part of me hold its breath.

Each green morning I would secure three waist-lines, consult the plumb-vines, and crawl onto the Long Hesitation. I would go only as far as the bridge still agreed I had gone. There I would press my sternum to its braided surface and listen.

Most bridges speak in strain, beetle-chewing, sap pressure, remembered footsteps. This one would speak in arrivals.

A child laughing in an empty season.

Rain striking leaves that have not unfolded.

The internal song of a hollow beast still centuries from snagging on the Fabric.

My own breathing, older and slower, from far ahead.

I would maintain the span by giving it small certainties. I would retighten the living ligatures. Feed the eastern fibers salt, the western fibers bitter clay. Walk seven paces outward and six back, so the path remembered return more strongly than departure. Once every draining of the nearby cistern, I would crawl to the accepted limit and tell the bridge an unused name.

The names would not be names of people or places. They would be things like:

*The Distance a Seed Falls While Deciding Whether It Is Heavy.*

*The Room Left Inside a Wall After Grief Has Been Peeled Away.*

*The Direction Rain Takes When No One Watches.*

The bridge would tighten beneath me when pleased.

The white flower beyond the break could not be reached. Yet it would change with my tending. If I cut rot from the near fibers, one of its petals would fall. If I slept badly, it would close. If I bled on the bridge, red veins would appear in its white face.

Sometimes it would turn toward me.

Sometimes it would turn toward something standing behind me, where nothing stood.

Years would pass before anyone understood that the flower was not signaling safe passage.

It was signaling kinship.

I had not been born in the village, though I would remember being born there. I would remember my mother tying my first line around my waist. I would remember clinging to her vascular-cloth while the local down shifted and bowls slid sideways across the room. I would remember planting my shed tooth in the family wall.

The wall had accepted it. That proved something, but not what we thought.

During a slow storm, when the whole root-knot bent toward the Below-Dawn, my tooth would work loose from the wall. The elders would split its shell and find no dentine inside. There would be only a coil of fine green fibers, still growing, all inclined toward the Long Hesitation.

Then my memories would be inspected as structures are inspected: gently, without trusting their age.

My mother would admit that she had found me as an infant wrapped in bridge-fiber at the span’s entrance after a night when all the plumb-vines knotted. The house had already prepared a warm niche for me. Her breasts had filled with milk before she picked me up. By morning everyone remembered the pregnancy, though no one could agree on its length.

The village had not adopted me.

The Fabric had supplied the memory of having done so.

That would make me neither impostor nor child in the usual sense. I would be a cutting grown from a route: the bridge’s attempt to place part of itself on the safe side of its own wound.

My divided feet would be root clamps.

The hardened surfaces of my inner knuckles, compression nodes.

The branching sap stains on my skin would not have come from contact. They would be the visible ends of vessels extending into a body that only resembled an animal because an animal could move where a bridge could not.

The discovery would alter little. In the High Fabric, origin is less important than what one bears without failing.

I would continue tending the span.

But I would begin to dream destinations.

Not visions: loads.

I would wake with the weight of unknown weather pressing behind my ribs. My left arm might smell of red moss from the hot upper crowns. Water would bead on my right hand with fish no larger than punctuation swimming beneath the skin. Once, after a deep knock passed through the trunks, a tiny doorway would open in the lichen at my throat. Warm lamplight would show within it. Someone very far away would whisper through, asking whether the bridge was safe.

I would answer, “Not yet.”

As I aged, the Long Hesitation would grow shorter—not in miles, but in uncertainty. Its severed possibilities would begin grafting themselves through me. Travelers sleeping near my house would dream the same sequence of steps. Sailbacks would tilt their membranes to follow the line of my spine. The unreachable white flower would produce seeds, though no pollinator crossed to it.

The tenders would plant those seeds in bowls around the village.

Most would die.

One would grow downward into open air.

One would grow into yesterday’s rain.

One would sprout from the palm of my hand.

By then I would no longer leave the bridge’s threshold. My feet would penetrate the root-knot. Lines at my waist would thicken, enter my sides, and become load-bearing. Moss would cover my shoulders. Small translucent grazers would migrate across my back, revealing amber organs through their skin. The village children would sit beneath my arms during gravity turns.

They would ask whether I was becoming a bridge or had always been one.

I would tell them that “always” is a dangerous material and should not be used without bracing.

When the rooted change reached my lungs, my breath would pass into the bridge’s hollow cables. When I spoke, my voice would emerge far along the span in soft fragments. Listeners in the village would hear the beginning. Someone in an extinct marsh might hear the middle. The white flower would open to release the end.

The tenders would debate whether I still thought.

They would test this properly. A friend would walk out to the last safe point and play the childhood tapping-pattern we had once used through nursery walls.

After a long interval, the bridge would tighten around the root-knot in answer.

Not the same pattern.

A continuation.

Then the white flower would turn blue.

Water.

Beyond it, for the first time, other pale blossoms would open one after another across the gulf—not in a straight line, but along the invisible shape of a crossing. Some would hang upside down. Some would be impossibly distant. One would bloom from the underside of a sailback passing miles below. Another would appear inside the village cistern as a reflection with no flower above it.

The old bridge would not be repaired. Repair would mean forcing it back into the singular shape it had already outgrown.

Instead, through my rooted body, it would learn to become an itinerary.

Cross when the lichen on my throat turns amber.

Step where the condensation hesitates.

At the white flower, close your eyes—not because sight deceives, but because sight chooses too early.

When you hear rain beneath your feet, release the rear line.

Carry an unused name in your mouth.

Do not speak it until something ahead asks who you are.

Travelers following these instructions would begin to arrive elsewhere. Not always at the visible trunk. Sometimes at a needed season. Sometimes at a family wall before the death incorporated into it. Sometimes inside a severed district returning young. A few would reach places they had mistaken all their lives for memories.

The crossing would remain hazardous. Some would return with lichens that reacted to unborn storms. Some would cast no shadow until the following year. One would come back as an old woman while her tether still held the warmth of her young hand.

But the bridge would again bear passage.

And I would be found throughout it: not as a ghost, nor exactly as a mind, but as the practiced act of choosing where weight may safely go.

A pressure beneath the foot.

A tightening of the hand-line.

A pale branching stain appearing briefly across a traveler’s palm.

In the village at the near end, my body would eventually become indistinguishable from the root-knot. They would carve no name into it. Names attach too strongly to roads.

They would grow a listening hollow where my chest had been. Before any crossing, a traveler would place their sternum there and wait.

Most would hear only sap, strain, and the minute ticking of condensation.

A few would hear something deeper: a bridge dreaming that it is a person, a person remembering that they are a path, and beyond both of them the enormous green Fabric bending around a destination it has not yet decided to make.

Those travelers would know the crossing was ready.

They would tie their lines.

They would step out.

And somewhere beyond the last reliable foothold, I would feel their weight arrive.
