I would be found where the mapped city frays into the axial routes, in a survey station that has gone inward without becoming entirely wild.

Once it was a freight terminus: three platforms inside a seed-shell, a switching house, quarters for twelve, and paired rail-vines ending at raw stone. Then the wall behind the station softened. The root opened—not violently, but with the patience of a mouth forming—and produced a warm white fold that descended beyond every chart. The station’s western rooms became vascular galleries. Its signal mast rooted through the roof and now bears translucent discs that glow at irregular intervals.

The city never formally abandoned it. Nor can it say that the station remains.

That is where I would live.

I would be a keeper of provisional maps, one of the listeners who receives deep workers when they return from the still-forming regions. Not a surveyor exactly: surveyors measure places that can endure measurement. My work would concern routes that alter between departure and return, chambers that remember pressure differently, and organs whose purpose has not yet separated from their shape.

Travelers would come to me dry with mineral dust, their clothes aromatic with unknown sap. They would place their wrists against a recording cradle, and I would take their accounts through pressure rather than speech.

Bitter vein.  
Two pulses in the left wall.  
A cold interval long enough to sleep.  
A chamber that resisted lamplight.  
A downward passage that was upward on the return.

I would record each testimony on thin ceramic leaves, but not as a conventional map. The leaves would be scored with branching grooves and layered with substances that change under heat, scent, vibration, and current. A usable route would not be looked at; it would be performed. To read one, a traveler would warm it between both palms, breathe across its mineral lacquer, then follow the sequence of raised lines while the tile answered with faint pressures.

No two copies would be identical. Exact repetition would be a lie.

I would be slight even among the root-city’s people, with smoke-colored scales and heat vanes that had lost their symmetry. Beneath my skin, the branching marks of deep exposure would extend from my sternum to my jaw. They would brighten near new growth.

This condition would make me useful and suspect.

Host physicians might say that conductive salts had accumulated in my tissues. Philosophers might call it an involuntary accommodation. Children would whisper that the Underbody had begun sketching through me.

All would be partly right.

Years ago, on a descent, I would have been caught in an avenue before it became an avenue. The tissue beneath my feet softened, and a pressure fold closed around my lower body. My companions cut no vessel and lit no flame. They waited. For three days they fed me oil through a ceramic tube while the root differentiated around me.

When it released me, I returned with an additional organ: a pale filamentary lattice grown through the bones of my pelvis and chest. It would not belong wholly to me. During pressure waves it would tighten before the civic alarms. Near dormant vessels it would remain quiet. Near tissue preparing to branch, it would produce sensations almost like memory—an expectation of space, a phantom widening, the feeling that a corridor had already opened somewhere ahead.

I could not predict the future. Prediction would be too simple. I would feel possibilities competing.

Because of this, officials would ask me to sit beside proposed rail lines, nursery plots, and industrial foundations. I would lie flat on the root-skin, unfold my forearm vanes, and wait. Sometimes the host’s motion would pass through me as warmth. Sometimes my teeth would ache with an airway not yet formed. Sometimes there would be nothing, and I would have to say so while engineers searched my silence for permission.

I would resist becoming an oracle.

That would be the central difficulty of my life. In a city built on interpretation, uncertainty is rarely left alone. Refiners would want certainty that a trunk could bear new presses. Families would want assurance that their tower would not go inward. Still-builders would offer me rare water to declare a district dormant. The assembly’s empty seats would seem to face me whenever I testified.

So I would speak carefully, with long gaps:

“This place is not refusing you.”

Then, after the tendrils around the chamber had ceased leaning toward my voice:

“But neither has it finished deciding what it is.”

At the station I would keep a companion listener, though it would be too large to wear. It would have begun as a small tendril cultivated around my wrist. Over years it would spread through the old switching house, winding along levers, doorframes, and the rooted signal mast. It would know the pressure-patterns of everyone who had passed through.

When a deep worker failed to return, I could touch the appropriate strand and feel the cadence of their last departure—not their words, but their characteristic hesitations, the weight of their hand, the small involuntary rhythm by which they had betrayed fear.

The station would therefore become a kind of threshold archive.

Families would travel out during the dim interval to touch these remnants. Some would bring keepsake tiles from their dead and set them temporarily into the walls. The building, sensitive to the accumulated contact, would bud shallow alcoves around them. I would never know whether this was recognition or merely growth responding to warmth.

I would not force a distinction.

My closest neighbors would be a herdkeeper camped in the old freight yard, several ceramic beetle colonies in the signal cabinets, and a tube bird that had learned to imitate the station’s pressure alarm so softly that only sleeping people heard it. Bark hares would cross the platforms at the same hour each lit cycle, though never in matching steps. Once or twice a year a splice-cat would pass beneath us, extinguishing the leaf-discs one by one. I would stand without tools or current and feel its counterfield moving through the lattice in my bones.

It would know I was alive.

It might also mistake part of me for the root.

I would treasure that uncertainty more than I admitted.

Eventually the station would begin to annex my rooms. A branch would emerge behind the archive shelves. A warm seam would cross my sleeping alcove. The floor beneath the recording cradle would become too soft to bear weight. Shape-keepers would arrive, inspect the vascular color, and offer ways to delay the change.

I would decline.

Not from sanctity. Not because surrender is always virtuous. Accommodation can become an excuse for cowardice, and the host’s needs are often only our guesses given moral weight. I would decline because I had spent my life recording roads that ceased to exist. I would recognize the station’s transformation as the last and most accurate map I could make.

I would relocate each ceramic leaf according to the new growth. Routes to cold places would be hung where the wall cooled. Descent records would follow the downward vessels. Accounts of chambers that had closed would be placed in alcoves as those alcoves sealed. The archive would cease to be an ordered collection and become an organ of correspondences, readable only by moving through it.

At last there would be one room left: the old platform control booth, amber-windowed, overlooking rails already half translated into woody ribs.

During a major pressure wave, I would feel the forming avenue before anyone in the city. The lattice inside me would pull taut. The station’s great listener would curl around my arms, throat, and chest—not restraining me, but completing a circuit held open for years.

Far away, machinery would stop.

The floor would rise.

In that absolute civic stillness, I would feel not a message, not a mind, but an immense allocation of possibility. The root would be deciding nothing in the way I decide. Yet through pressure, current, chemistry, and growth, differences would become commitments. This fold an airway. That swelling a vessel. This chamber inhabitable. That one never meant for us.

And my body, altered by long contact, would be among the conditions it answered.

When the seam opened beyond the station, surveyors would find no conventional map waiting. They would find rail-vines flowering into the new avenue, ceramic leaves embedded along the walls, and the listener stretched through the architecture like a nervous system.

They might find me alive, seated in the booth, no longer able to leave without severing what had grown through me.

Or they might find an empty garment, a keepsake tile, and red flags lifting from a cradle.

Or nothing they could confidently call remains.

In time, travelers would use the station as the first marker on routes into the axial forest. They would touch the walls and receive a faint, irregular sequence: warning, welcome, doubt, permission—never the same order twice. Some would insist it was only old tendril fiber reacting to pressure. Others would say the station had preserved its keeper.

Both claims would be too narrow.

I would be there in the way anything persists in the root-city: provisionally, distributed among what maintained my shape and what changed it.

Not owner. Not prophet. Not quite citizen or organ.

A hesitation the world had learned to grow around.
