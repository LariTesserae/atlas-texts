I find myself near the unfinished ring, though not at the face.

At first I think I am a surveyor. I have the stoop of someone accustomed to low ceilings and the habit of glancing upward before entering any room. A knotted cord crosses my chest. There is pale clay in the creases of my hands and a tablet tucked beneath my arm, scored with lines that represent streets not yet opened.

But the tablet is not a plan of streets.

It is a plan of sounds.

I am a listener attached to the roof-keepers: one of those sent into new chambers after the diggers have withdrawn, before carpenters commit good timber and before anyone decides what the space is for. I measure spans, but my real instrument is stillness.

The chamber where I am working was solid earth eight days ago. Its walls retain the broad flat wounds of spades. One end has been squared; the other remains rounded and uncertain, with dark seams where the clay changes composition. Temporary braces stand beneath the limestone like awkward trees. A daylight pipe pierces the ceiling at an angle, and its opening has not yet been plastered. The light comes down dirty and narrow, revealing every disturbance of dust.

Nobody has named this room.

That is why I am here.

For three days people have reported a faint tapping in the western wall. It begins near the middle of the afternoon and ceases before the end-shift drum. A carrier heard it first. Then a brickmaker. One of the diggers claimed it was only clay drying, but he claimed it too quickly, and the crew leader halted work.

The wall touches no known passage. Beyond it, according to every tablet, lies twenty arm-spans of untouched ground.

I arrive with a roof-keeper, an apprentice, and a child small enough to crawl beneath the lowest brace. We bring a shallow bowl of water, chalk, wedges, a listening tile, and coils of soft cord. No picks. The absence of picks is deliberate.

I set the bowl on the floor and wait until its surface becomes still. The child presses one cheek to a pillar. The apprentice holds the fired tile against the western wall, flat as a physician’s hand. We extinguish our little lamp, although it is already dim. Then we listen.

The city does not become silent. Silence here is never emptiness.

There is the far tremor of excavation, arriving through the floor as a repeated pressure in the teeth. A cart passes somewhere behind us, its rawhide-bound wheels giving a soft sequence of compressions. Air moves through the temporary shaft. A drop falls in the water bowl, though nothing above it appears wet. Timber fibers ease against one another.

Then comes the tapping.

Three touches, widely spaced.

Not the crisp tick of drying clay. Not the granular patter of loosened grit. Something duller: transmitted through thickness, rather than made upon the surface.

We wait.

Two more.

The child lifts her head. “Wood,” she whispers.

The roof-keeper looks at me, because he has heard it too.

There should be no wood beyond the wall.

For a little while I imagine all the obvious explanations. A buried root from one of the surface trees. An old fence post driven deep from above. A sounding stake forgotten by a survey crew. Sound traveling from a known district by a path no map records. The city is young, but error can be born in a day.

Yet roots do not usually tap at the same hour.

We mark the places where the sound is strongest. They form a descending line. I uncoil my cord, take levels from the floor, and compare them with the tablet. The line points not toward the surface, but downward—toward the gravel river beneath the city.

So perhaps I am not merely a listener.

Perhaps I am a water finder, or becoming one. I have inherited that delicate unease by which certain people recognize water before they can hear or smell it. My mother drew buckets from the deep wells. My father kept catchments aboveground and could predict rain by watching swallows. I chose roof work because it seemed firmer than either calling, but the city has a way of returning people to the thing they notice best.

The tapping comes again.

This time I feel it in the cord.

Not wood against clay. A rope under intermittent strain.

There is a shaft beyond the wall.

Someone has lowered a rope into the dark, and the rope is striking a timber curb as it moves.

The thought should be impossible. No inhabited chamber lies there. No authorized well descends there. Digging alone is forbidden, and digging an unregistered shaft toward the buried river is among the worst things one can do: it risks collapse, contamination, theft from the common water, and flooding along a path no one else knows.

But laws do not abolish thirst.

We withdraw without opening the wall. That is another discipline of this place: discovery does not grant permission. The roof-keeper seals the chamber. The apprentice ties red wool around its entrance brace. I carry my sound-map inward to the district council.

As I go, I pass through the part of the city where I live.

My home is not a whole house. It is a room within a room, partitioned by felt and woven reed, behind my sister’s family’s sleeping court. A district support rises through the center, limewashed to shoulder height and always cold. Into its plaster are pressed five small clay tokens bearing the thumbs of our dead. The oldest is my grandmother’s, though the print has blurred because everyone touches it when passing.

My bed folds into the wall. Beneath it are my tablets, wrapped in oiled cloth. Above it hang cords of different lengths, each tied for a particular sort of measurement. My niece steals them to make imaginary streets across the floor.

She is the child who accompanied us today.

That is not permitted in principle; in practice, she hears better than either of us.

At supper she will want to tell everyone about the hidden rope. I will remind her that overhearing is not permission to repeat. She will scowl, then ask whether an unrecorded well has an owner. Her father will say that nothing dangerous can be owned privately. Her mother will point out that hunger and thirst make philosophers of people who otherwise dislike questions.

The council meets in a former storeroom whose intended grain bins became benches when the room proved too warm. Present are the water keepers, a surface steward, two roof surveyors, a work-crew representative, and the warden responsible for the poor settlement nearest the wall.

Before anyone speaks, I know where suspicion will fall.

The families at the edge arrived after drought emptied three villages beyond the orchards. They rent patched rooms beside the animal ramp and earn their water allotments carrying spoil. Some have no claim in the city except labor. If there is an illicit well, everyone will assume they dug it.

I set down my tablet.

The marks are simple: times, intervals, direction, probable depth. They look small beside what they may mean.

I say the sound is consistent with a rope moving over wood. I say it does not prove theft. I say the shaft may predate the present excavation, though the clay around us bears no sign of age. I say we must approach from above as well as from the side, because if the hidden space has failed supports, breaking through the wall may kill whoever is inside.

Whoever.

Until I say the word, the council has been discussing a violation.

Now we are discussing a person.

The tapping occurred at a regular hour. Perhaps someone works there alone. Perhaps someone is trapped there and can only move the rope when light or air reaches them through an opening. Perhaps the noise is intentional—not labor, but a signal from someone who has heard our city approaching through the clay.

That possibility alters the room.

Our people believe the seam is ours because we are making it habitable. Yet the earth was not empty merely because our tablets were blank.

The surface steward is sent to inspect the field overhead. The water keepers close two nearby wells and compare their levels. I return to the unnamed chamber with a larger crew, but still no digging tools. We bring instead a potter’s narrow sounding tube, several lengths of reed, plaster, and a horn trumpet no longer than my hand.

We press the horn to the wall.

Three taps.

We wait, then answer with three.

Nothing.

The roof-keeper tries the civic danger call: slow, alternating strokes.

After a long pause, the reply comes back badly formed but unmistakable.

Someone knows the signal.

We do not break through. We scrape a hollow no wider than a thumb, stopping every few strokes to listen. The clay changes halfway in: first pale and firm, then damp, then mixed with chopped straw.

Brick.

The wall before us is not untouched ground. It is a sealed construction whose outer face has been hidden beneath packed clay.

When the reed finally passes through, air touches my hand.

It is colder than the chamber air and smells of wet gravel, old ash, and human breath.

Someone on the other side grips the reed.

The child beside me gasps. I nearly do as well.

A voice comes through, too faint for words. We fit the horn over the opening and seal its rim with soft clay. I put my ear to it.

At first there is only breathing.

Then: “Do not open low.”

An older woman’s voice, hoarse from disuse.

“Water is against the wall.”

We spend the night discovering the shape of the place without seeing it. The hidden chamber lies lower than ours. Its occupants—there are six—have blocked their connecting passage because floodwater rose through it after the last storm. Their upper exit collapsed years ago. They have survived by a narrow well, a root shaft, and stores kept in sealed jars. They know the city exists. They have heard us growing toward them season by season.

They are not an ancient people. They are not monsters, remnants, or guardians of a forgotten underworld. They are seam-dwellers too.

They came before us by perhaps fifteen years.

Their settlement failed. Most went back to the surface or died there during the drought. Six remained to keep the well, believing the others would return. When our fields spread over their old entrance, they heard shepherds overhead but feared that revealing the shaft would cause it to be claimed or filled. Later they heard our excavation. By then the wet ground between us had become too unstable to cross.

The tapping was not a call.

It was one of them drawing water at the only hour the hidden well recovered enough to yield.

By morning the unnamed chamber has acquired a temporary civic designation: the Listening Room. No one calls it that formally, but the name spreads before the tablet recording it can dry.

For the next twelve days I live there.

I map the void by voice, tap, cord, and water level. The woman beyond the wall—her name is Saret—describes the braces on her side. I draw them as she speaks. She tells me which are cedar, which are poplar, which have rotted at their feet. She has been a roof-keeper longer than I have been alive. Her city used different knots and different names, but weight speaks a common language.

Together we design an opening above the flood line.

A potter fires short ceramic collars. Carpenters prepare a box frame. Diggers remove clay in slices no thicker than a palm. The water crews lower the nearby reservoirs as a precaution. Surface workers open a relief shaft in the field overhead, astonishing a flock of goats.

When at last the wall is breached, it does not happen dramatically.

No great door falls. No lost avenue is revealed.

One brick is eased inward. Then another.

A square of darkness appears above my head. Through it comes a smell older than our district but not ancient: soot, damp wool, stale grain, bodies. Saret’s hand emerges first, narrow and clay-streaked. She does not reach for me. She presses her palm to the new timber frame, testing it.

Only when satisfied does she let us help her through.

Five others follow: two men, two women, and a boy who has never stood under open sky.

Our city absorbs them badly at first.

There are hearings about the well. Arguments over whether their prior claim survives abandonment. Accusations that they concealed water during drought. Counterclaims that our catchments deprived their root shaft. Roof-keepers quarrel over whose system of notation is safer. The hidden chamber’s jars, bricks, and braces are inspected as evidence, then admired as craft.

Saret refuses charity and asks for work.

She is given my place on the crew before anyone has formally decided to give it. Within a month she finds three errors in our burden maps, one dangerous and two merely embarrassing. Her people’s old well is connected to the communal system, but only after its water is tested and the shaft widened for paired workers. The flooded passage remains sealed.

The boy is carried to a noon shaft. He is nearly grown, far older than the infants usually presented there. When direct sunlight strikes his face, he cries out—not in joy, but because it hurts. Everyone looks away to give him privacy.

As for me, I change professions without changing my work.

The council creates no grand office. There is simply a new mark added to certain tablets: a small open circle beside places where the city does not yet know what touches it.

I become responsible for those circles.

I walk ahead of ownership but behind excavation, searching not only for weak roofs and hidden water, but for prior spaces: old shafts, filled rooms, root hollows, forgotten catchments, the buried works of people whose plans were never entered into ours. I collect surface stories from farmers who remember where the ground once sank or where frost melts first. I question newcomers about abandoned wells, graves, kilns, and cellars. I teach children that blank clay on a map does not necessarily mean untouched clay in the earth.

My niece becomes my apprentice eventually, though she insists she will be a water drawer. Perhaps she will be both. Professions are walls here: useful, necessary, and often pierced by passages.

Years later, the Listening Room is no longer at the edge. The city grows around it. The temporary braces are replaced with permanent pillars. The breach into Saret’s chamber becomes an arched doorway lined with ceramic tile. The room proves too acoustically strange for sleeping and too cool for storage, so it becomes a school for listeners.

Children sit on the floor with their ears against tiles while carts pass in neighboring lanes. They learn to tell water from settling, rope from root, drying brick from feeding insects. Sometimes I stand behind them and strike the far wall with a padded stick.

“Near or distant?” I ask.

“Carried,” they answer.

“Stone or timber?”

“Timber.”

“Danger?”

They are not allowed to answer too quickly.

That is the most important lesson I have to give them. Not fear. Not suspicion. Not even perfect hearing.

Delay.

The interval in which a sound may become more itself before we decide what made it.

I grow old beneath ceilings that did not exist when I was young. My city becomes harder to remember as raw clay. Thresholds begin, despite everything, to wear smooth. Children’s fingerprints vanish beneath repairs. Pale ax cuts darken. Water discovers habitual paths. The first districts acquire the very softness of age that the city once lacked.

People begin to say that our way of building is traditional.

I dislike the word. Tradition suggests that the city has finished teaching us.

So I keep the oldest version of the sound-map from the unnamed chamber. Its lines are wrong in several places. Its conclusion is cautious. Along the western edge, where our official plan once showed solid earth, I drew a sequence of small marks descending toward the buried river.

When I can no longer walk to the unfinished ring, the tablet is set into the wall beside my bed. Not hidden—nothing dangerous should be hidden—but placed where lamplight catches the incisions.

My thumb-token will eventually be fixed beside it.

If I am remembered, perhaps people will say I discovered the other chamber. That is not true. Saret and I discovered each other, and the wall did most of the work.

Or perhaps they will say I founded the order of listeners. That is not true either. The whole city listens. That is why it survives.

I am only the person who happened to be present when the clay ceased to mean silence.

And where do I find myself in this world?

At a wall not yet opened.

One hand rests on it. Behind me is the inhabited brightness: plaster, wool, cooking steam, soft signals, children reciting the laws of support. Ahead—or beyond, or below—is something the map calls earth because it does not yet possess a better word.

I do not cut.

I wait long enough to hear whether the future is already tapping back.
