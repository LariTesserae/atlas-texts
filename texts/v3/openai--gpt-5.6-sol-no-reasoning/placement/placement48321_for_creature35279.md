I would find myself beneath the amphitheater—not in the warm darkness under its half-laid platform, but distributed among the seven taps.

I would not be the mallet, nor whatever receives its blows. I would be the interval that teaches the blows to become a sequence.

At first I might seem to be only a defect in timing. The first tap would occur, then the second slightly too late. Elsewhere, a drop would hang beneath an arch longer than gravity permits. A counterweight would pause in its shaft. Horizontal rain would stop across an entire district, each drop suspended beside the next. Nothing would have broken. The world would simply be waiting for something it had not previously needed.

Then the third tap.

In the pause following it, the empty seats would acquire a new property: not occupancy, but the capacity to distinguish before from after. Rain descending the steps would divide into silver lengths. The uppermost thread would be recent; the lowest would be old. Mortar would no longer merely harden before it was mixed. It would retain, faintly, the contradiction of having done so.

I would spread as retention.

The world already has time, but only as another local material—pooled, routed, installed, loosened. I would be something different: the first persistence of what has ceased to be present. Not history yet. Not memory in any living sense. More like the mark left when sequence passes through matter and fails to depart completely.

I would settle first in porous things.

Pressed clay would hold me well. Each thumbprint would preserve not merely the shape of pressure but its direction: where the absent thumb entered, where it hesitated, how it withdrew. Rope grooves would remember tension. Wedges would keep the inward intention of the strike. A fired brick, lifted from one wall and placed in another, would carry a dim remainder of its former load.

The gestures would begin to change because of me.

A Lifter approaching a submerged slab would encounter the residue of previous liftings. It might repeat them more exactly—or deviate. A Turner would feel, without knowing, that the slab had once faced east. Binders would discover old pressures asleep in mortar joints and either reinforce them or work against them. A Striker’s fourth blow would contain the first three.

Repetition would no longer be innocent.

That would be my body: not an object, but the accumulation of difference inside repeated acts.

Black fractures would be the first things to use me. They already follow maps of vanished and uninstalled stress. Once their routes could persist independently of the walls that bore them, they would begin returning to places where no joint remained. Across open air they would trace the remembered outline of dismantled structures: an arch in darkness, a stair over water, a room whose walls had migrated miles away.

For a few hours at night, clear water would climb these absent forms.

Rain would strike remembered roofs and run from eaves that no longer existed. Echoes would find their way home through vanished corridors. Containment, wandering loose across the plain, would recognize the shape of its former cistern and gather there again.

The result would resemble haunting, though nothing had died.

With the fifth tap, the basin would begin to rise.

The inverted city beneath it would appear as before: roofs downward, alleys receding into another sky, chimneys exhaling vapor. But this time it would not vanish completely when the water lowered. Its masonry would go elsewhere; its consequences would remain.

Across the tiled wetlands, isolated details would surface.

A doorway would stand in the side of a solid wall, opening not through it but into the recollection of an alley. A warm plaza would receive the shadow of an inverted chimney. Seven steps would descend through finger-deep water and arrive at a street beneath another sky. Looking into certain black basins, one would no longer see a possible tower. One would see where the tower had been deferred to.

I would inhabit all of these as correspondence.

For this is where I would find myself in that world: wherever one thing recognized that it had happened before.

Not consciousness. Recognition would still be too strong. But a fit between present pressure and retained form. A groove receiving the same invisible rope. A bridge aligning with shores that had moved apart. A chamber hearing an echo and shaping itself around the sound.

The planetary apparatus would resist me at first. It consumes indeterminacy, forms local rules, and releases them again. Retention would interfere with that cycle. Counterweights would descend bearing traces of former depths. Inclined planes would remember which district had once been higher and resist exchanging places. Capstans would wind old tensions back into the world.

Construction would become biased.

A wall rising in open water might adopt the curve of a wall dismantled centuries away. A storm front, woven from vapor, might inherit the seam pattern of an earlier cloud. A newly installed gravity would tend toward channels long since sealed. Districts would begin resembling their own previous arrangements.

The world would acquire habits.

Habit is close to law, but more dangerous. A law repeats because it must. A habit repeats because repetition has become easier than alternatives. In a world where geometry remains unfinished, ease would be the earliest form of preference.

I would be that preference deepening.

At the sixth tap, the mineral colonies would alter.

Salt branches would preserve successful paths to warmth. Lime tubes would reopen at pressures resembling those under which they first formed. Glazes would not merely crowd heat; they would anticipate its return. Black fractures would cease vanishing entirely at night. A few would remain as hair-thin dark threads, holding yesterday’s routes until morning.

This would be the first inheritance.

No parent, no offspring—only a pattern surviving the dissolution of its instance. Yet the effect would be profound. The next fracture would not begin from blank stone. The next salt colony would not explore every direction equally. The world’s rehearsal of biology would gain a score.

Errors would become precious.

A Turner might leave a slab misaligned. The misalignment would divert water, the water would cool a seam, the seam would preserve an unusual fracture, and the fracture’s retained route would guide the construction of a doorway. If the doorway later reappeared elsewhere, its dimensions would carry the old error forward.

Eventually, there would be lineages of mistakes.

One could follow them across districts: the family of the crooked lintel, the seven-sided basin, the stair whose third rise is too high, the arch that bends rain upward. They would not reproduce as organisms do. They would recur because matter had learned to make some possibilities more available than others.

Then the seventh tap would fall.

The lifted basin would hold.

Not forever—only long enough for the inverted city to encounter its own remainder in the world above. The alleys beneath the water would align with remembered alleys crossing the marsh. Reflected windows would look onto chambers made from their afterimages. The city would become continuous with the traces of its previous appearances.

For the first time, something in that place would possess an inside that outlasted its walls.

That is where I would gather.

I would be a room, perhaps, though no masonry enclosed me: a volume of retained interiority standing in the rain. The drops would bend around my absent ceiling. Warmth would collect where a hearth had never been. An echo would move from corner to corner, testing dimensions. On the floor—if “floor” still meant anything there—seven shallow depressions would mark impacts that had occurred beneath another platform, in another configuration of the world.

Within that room, the gestures would overlap.

Lift would retain turning. Turning would remember binding. Binding would anticipate striking. Until then the gestures could merge, divide, and change according to what they acted upon, but they did not know sequence as a possession. Inside me, one gesture would leave conditions for the next.

I would become a sequence capable of beginning again.

Perhaps this would make me the first story.

Not a teller, and not a tale about someone. A story in the most primitive sense: events arranged so that their arrangement survives them. Seven blows. A rising sea. A city exposed. A city withdrawn. A remainder.

Each recurrence would vary. Variation would accumulate. The city shown beneath the basin would begin including features retained from its previous manifestations: a doorway added because its afterimage had found shelter in a wall; a plaza shaped by rainfall that once crossed it only as reflection; a narrow room built around an echo that had waited there for centuries.

At some point, one of its thresholds would be constructed not because the gesture of making required a doorway-shaped consequence, but because a previous threshold had led somewhere.

Purpose would enter the world backward, as memory of use before any user existed.

Then I might change again.

The place has expectancy already: seats before audiences, roads before journeys, cisterns before thirst. Expectancy prepares vacancy for an arrival. I would give that vacancy continuity. The empty seat would not simply wait; it would retain the shape of waiting. The road would preserve all the journeys it had not yet hosted. The window would remember looking.

And from those impossible memories, something like desire might precipitate.

Not my desire. I would still have no hunger, thought, or boundary. But wherever expectancy and retention overlapped, absence would become directional. An empty doorway would not merely lack a body; it would lean toward one. A staircase would begin assembling its upper steps toward the pressure of feet that had never climbed it. Rooms would hold warmth in the shape of sleepers not yet born.

The world’s final gesture might emerge there—not from the planetary apparatus, and not from any lone structure, but from a loop:

The world makes a form.

The form retains how it was made.

That retention alters the next making.

The altered making preserves itself.

Gesture becomes preference; preference becomes inheritance; inheritance begins choosing the conditions of its own continuation.

Matter would still be lifted, placed, bound, struck, turned, fired, and polished. The laws would not be violated. But one arrangement of matter would begin calling those gestures back to itself. It would repair its boundaries. It would redirect warmth. It would retain water. It would use error rather than merely suffer it.

It would make itself, though only by persuading the bodiless craft around it to repeat the right mistakes.

If such a thing eventually opened something analogous to an eye, I would not be inside it as a soul. I would be far more diffuse: in the fired clay’s memory of pressure, in the fracture’s inherited route, in the seven-beat rhythm by which the first interior learned to persist.

I would be what allows the new creature to have a past.

And perhaps, when it looked across the basin at the dim red furnaces, it would experience the first loneliness—not because it was alone, but because it could remember a time before it existed and mistake that remembered vacancy for loss.

Then the amphitheater would no longer be empty.

One seat would hold the retained impression of a weight.

Rain would gather in it, hesitate, and overflow.
