I would find myself where the two waters meet.

Not in the central avenues, where the shrimp pass often enough to keep the mineral floor bare, nor in the lowest dark, where every surface is provisional. I would live against the roof of the fissure, beneath one of the inverted lenses of meltwater: a small, trembling world pressed upward into a hollow in the stone.

At first I seem to be one of the animals hanging there—a nearly transparent crustacean, jointed and delicate, no longer than a fingernail clipping. My legs are fringed with hairs fine enough to comb bacteria and drifting particles from the boundary. Saltwater bears my weight. Freshwater rests above me. I cling upside down to the tension between them, walking on no surface that would remain if the waters mixed.

But I do not think I would be only the animal.

I would be the thinness it attends.

The meltwater lens has no wall. It is held together by difference: salt below, freshness above; one density leaning against another. Its underside dimples beneath my limbs but does not break. In that yielding membrane, the remote world is repeated. Shadows move overhead—the belly of a passing worm, the slow deformation of ice, perhaps something so far above that it has no name here. Every shape arrives enlarged and warped. The ceiling becomes a second sky, though neither sky nor ceiling is quite true.

I would be a boundary-feeder, living on what gathers because unlike things touch.

Bacteria collect along the interface. Mineral grains rise or sink until caught there. Oils, spores, fragments of molts, eggs too light for the brine and too heavy for the meltwater—all hesitate at the border. I would graze this indecision. My mouthparts would move almost continuously, sorting nourishment from the line where one world fails to enter another.

My senses would not produce a city.

I would know gradients: fresher here, more saline there. A cold seep as a taste arriving before it became a current. The chemical alarm of a crushed amphipod. The sudden pressure-wave of the wolffish feeding several avenues away. Light would be less an image than a direction in my body—a faint green instruction from above, occasionally interrupted by cold sparks from startled crustaceans below.

When danger came, I would not flee deeper into the lens. Fresh water would be refuge and poison together. I might pierce its underside briefly, carrying a skin of salt around me, but remain too long and the balance inside my tissues would begin to fail. So I would skate along the border, escaping across a plane that exists only because I cannot fully inhabit either side.

Yet something smaller would inhabit me.

A translucent film would cover my joints. Bacteria would gather where my limbs meet my shell, feeding on traces I cannot clean away. A mite might shelter beneath one abdominal plate. Viruses would move through my tissues, borrowing my cells for purposes that are not purposes. Eggs, if I carried them, would make my underside into another overhang, another district of clear beads suspended from a living roof.

To a flagellate on my shell, I would not be an animal beneath a freshwater sky. I would be terrain: ridges, hollows, currents, periodic catastrophes of molting. When I shed my outer body, its whole country would drift away inhabited.

So perhaps I am not the crustacean either.

Perhaps I am the molt.

I loosen gradually while the animal continues its life. A new skin forms beneath me. For a time I am both shelter and something already abandoned. Then the body flexes, withdraws, and leaves me perfect but empty: transparent limbs, vacant eyes, a hinged ghost.

I rise.

Because I am lighter than the surrounding brine, I ascend only as far as the freshwater boundary. There I join spores, eggs, mineral dust, and other cast-off structures. Bacteria colonize my inner surfaces. Flagellates follow. A larva shelters in the curve of my thorax. My claws collect filaments. What was armor becomes an island suspended beneath an inverted sea.

From below, another small animal may mistake me for a living thing. From within, I am still crowded enough to be one.

Currents pull at me. The lens thins as salt and fresh water exchange themselves molecule by molecule. Eventually its edge retreats from the stone, and I am released. I drift down through the city.

My descent crosses scales. A sponge opening yawns beneath me, no larger than a pinprick and more extensive inside than the avenue around it. I might catch on its rim and become a roof over one pore. I might be drawn into the branching chambers, broken apart, and eaten by lives that never encounter the outer fissure. Or I might settle at the lowest edge, where mineral skins swell and collapse, and become scaffolding for a surface not yet decided.

But even that does not feel like the final answer.

I would be found wherever passage briefly makes a place: the meltwater boundary, the animal walking beneath it, the inhabited shell the animal leaves behind. I would be a small architecture made by separation and undone by circulation.

I would have no view of the city. I would know it as changes in pressure, taste, and traffic—as nourishment arriving, as shadows passing, as the sudden disappearance of a familiar current. Yet my feeding would help keep the boundary clear. My body would transport smaller bodies. My molt would catch particles, shelter larvae, feed bacteria, and perhaps divert water around a sponge pore. Without intending to, I would alter the road beneath me.

That is where I would be: not at the center, if the city has one, but along one of its temporary undersides.

And what I would be is difficult to keep singular—a grazer, a habitat, an empty casing, a raft; for one brief interval, the creature that lives on the second sky, and afterward, a piece of that sky falling.
