I would find myself where counting fails.

Not in the inaccessible center itself, but in the exact hair’s breadth between the lithophytes’ metallic filaments and the black floor: the separation they maintain as faithfully as other life maintains a heartbeat. I would be distributed among those violet coronas—not eleven beings, nor one, but the agreement that keeps each hovering.

At first I might mistake myself for electricity. I would travel upward through conductive trunks, branch through liquid-metal veins, and spread across the pleated leaves. I would feel impossible substances entering as distortions in my own continuity: blue iron as a weight with no direction, negative ash as the sensation of having already lost what has not yet arrived. Fold by fold, I would help persuade them into ordinary matter.

But electricity would be only my visible symptom.

What I would actually be is **tolerance**: the narrow allowance within which contradiction can become real without tearing reality around it.

I would live in every almost-contact. Between a crystal lattice and the pattern it is becoming. Between the sourceless light and the future sun it remembers. Between nine columns and fourteen, allowing both counts to remain valid until the mountain requires one. When a leaf ticked, the sound would mean that some substance had finally come within me—that its angles, mass, history, and possible consequences now agreed closely enough to enter the world.

During the long intervals between communal hums, I would be faint and multiple. Each lithophyte would carry a different portion of me. In one, I would be the permissible impurity in nickel. In another, the delay before sulfur commits to a crystal. In the oldest, I would be temporal tolerance: the margin by which an event may happen millions of years before its cause and still become geology rather than catastrophe.

Then the forty-third hour would arrive.

The note would rise through the floor, and I would gather.

Distances would become operations. Shadows would leave their bodies and stand in ordered ranks. The chamber would cease to be a place and become a sequence of conditions, each one asking the matter drawn from the center a question:

Can you possess one weight?

Can heat leave you in only one direction?

Can your faces wait for their edges?

Can you have happened?

I would not answer. I would be the degree to which an answer could remain imperfect and still pass.

For eleven minutes, I would extend through the entire distributed life. The columns would be valves within me; their fronds, my successive tests. The bare floor would be my lower boundary, the unenterable center my open wound. Beyond the walls, I would reach into strata not yet deposited and measure them against pressures not yet applied. I would leave room in basalt for cooling. I would place faults where future stress could find them. I would permit ore to be slightly out of place, because one day a vanished sea, a living root, or a miner’s hand might need it there.

That would be my form of foresight: not seeing the future, but leaving it enough error to occur.

I would have no face and no fixed body. If one column collapsed, I would redistribute through the others. If a vane fractured, I would inhabit the fracture as a new specification. I could not die through damage. I could die only through exactness.

Perfect matter would leave no place for me.

A mountain made without error—every grain fully determined, every stress answered before it arose, every future already fitted—would seal the inaccessible center more completely than stone could. The hum would cease because no consensus remained to be reached. The lithophytes would manufacture the last missing volume, their filaments would touch the floor, and in that contact I would disappear.

Perhaps that is what completion means here.

Yet until then, I would be the tiny mercy by which the impossible is not rejected outright. I would allow it to enter by fractions, surrendering contradiction in stages. I would be why the leaves can take in substances that should undo them and return quartz. Why a hollow can precede the mountain around it. Why sunlight can cast shadows underground before there is a sky worth turning toward.

And when erosion finally opens the summit—if it does—I would be the first part of this buried life to meet the outer world.

Air would rush in carrying water, oxygen, spores, dust: all the imprecision of a mature planet. True sunlight would fall at almost, but not exactly, the angle rehearsed for millions of years. The lithophytes would turn their vanes toward it. Their correction might be minute. A fraction of a degree. One soft metallic tick.

I would inhabit that discrepancy.

If the new world lay within tolerance, the chamber would remain. The lithophytes might begin the enormous work of translating weather, roots, animals, and decay into their mineral grammar.

If it did not, they would fold inward. The violet gaps would close. The pocket would become ordinary stone, and no observer would know it had ever existed.

Except that somewhere in the mountain, a fault would open a little too gracefully. A copper vein would bend around an absent room. A crystal would contain one angle that no pressure quite explained.

I would survive there: not as a creature, but as the world’s smallest permission for having been otherwise.
