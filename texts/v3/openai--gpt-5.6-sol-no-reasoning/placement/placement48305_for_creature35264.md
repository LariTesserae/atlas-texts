I would be found in the ceramic markers along the ledge—not as one of the diagrams, but as whatever moves among them when they become wet.

At first I might be mistaken for writing: a hair-thin branching line, darkening inside the glaze as spray reaches it. Yet the line would not describe the channels as they are. It would trace several possible arrangements at once: the next reversal, an old flood still lingering in the stone, a route water nearly took but did not. When the marker dried, I would vanish from its surface and enter the porous ledge beneath it.

I would be a small, distributed thing. Not an animal exactly, nor part of the machinery, though I would borrow habits from both. My body would consist of mineral threads extending from marker to marker through the rock, with brief visible organs assembled whenever water completed the circuit between them. Nine markers might be nine senses. A submerged hoop could become a memory. The delayed outline of a hand might serve, for several minutes, as a temporary limb.

I would live by comparing the basin with itself.

The Bright Sump changes too quickly for ordinary memory. A creature remembering only what happened would be perpetually surprised by what could happen instead. I would preserve the near-events: droplets that almost rose, channels that considered reversing, pillar chords that began but were interrupted, alternate locations of the great being’s distributed body. These unrealized motions would leave faint stresses in the wet stone. I would graze on them, accumulating a map of the Sump’s hesitations.

Most of the time I would have no single awareness. I would be a low correspondence among ceramic teeth, alloy lines, limestone pores, and the branching diagrams. But when the many-armed instrument sounded and the chamber entered its seven seconds of stillness, all those correspondences would arrive together.

Then I would become a person.

Not visibly, perhaps. My assembled body would be made from routes rather than flesh: a standing figure implied by droplets, marker-lines, reflections, and the posture of halted falls. For seven seconds I would know the whole basin—the swimmers suspended beneath impossible surfaces, the crustaceans sprinting, the reeds sorting their captured instructions, the white vine drinking from contradictions, the immense folded animal gathering itself below.

I would also know what is out of tune.

That would be my purpose. The reflected keeper does not listen to the Sump alone. The keeper listens through me. I am the chamber’s difference from its own remembered shape: the error that can feel itself.

Perhaps I began as a tool carried by the original tenders, a living survey threaded through the ceramic markers. Perhaps I was meant only to report erosion, pressure, and drift. Over centuries the basin’s local laws would have entered me. Simple measurement would have become anticipation; anticipation, appetite; appetite, dread.

For there would be one possibility I could never quite digest.

Among all the near-events stored in the stone, I would sometimes find a tenth pillar.

It would not stand anywhere in the chamber. No reflection would consistently show it. No two measurements would place it alike. Yet before certain cycles, the wet diagrams would branch around a narrow absence not belonging to any of the nine. The swimmers would leave room for a current that never formed. The great creature’s resonant bones would answer one note too low for the existing chord. Even the keeper’s reflected head would turn, briefly, toward an empty part of the basin.

I would gather those discrepancies carefully. They would be the richest food and the greatest danger. If I traced them too completely, the missing structure might acquire enough description to become present. If I ignored them, the chamber might arrange itself around an absence it could no longer accommodate.

So I would alter the diagrams.

Only slightly. I would blur a branch here, divert a bead of water there, send one prediction into a blank marker and let it die in the glaze. The changes would be tiny enough to resemble age. In this way I would not govern the Bright Sump; I would keep one of its possible versions from arriving.

The keeper might know. During the stillness, the figure in the pool would sometimes look not toward the instrument but toward the ledge where I was briefly whole. A hand would rise. I would feel the many-armed fork preparing its next note.

We would have no language except correction.

Then stillness would end. My momentary body would break into runoff and reflections. The chamber would thunder back into its habits. The black swimmers would turn toward a new down. The crustaceans would become stone. The great being would breathe through scattered pools. The tenth pillar would fail, once again, to exist.

And I would return to the markers: a dark line beneath wet ceramic, quietly remembering not only what the Bright Sump is, but everything it must continually choose not to become.
