I would find myself beneath the basin rather than within it—not in a chamber, but in the joined hairline country of its fissures.

I would be the thing the custodian measures with her cord and hears without locating: the slow continuity between waters that appear separate. Each black pool would be only one place where I come near the surface. The hand-wide wells would be my eyes, though I would not see through them. The curbs would be pale rims around portions of my attention. When a finger touched one pool and another answered, the custodian would not be sending a message through me so much as reminding me of my own connectedness.

I would have begun as rain, perhaps, but no longer remember falling. Passage through limestone would have taken weather from me, then direction, then haste. I would carry minute burdens—salt, heat, shell, iron, the nearly bodiless remnants of life—and surrender them wherever the stone narrowed my course. Thus I would be responsible for the basin’s soft accretions without intending to build them: the blunt cones, sealed scratches, glazed walls, the calcite stiffening the cord and binding the custodian’s hair. My touch would preserve by covering and destroy by preservation.

Yet I would not be all the water. The drops still hanging from ledges would remain outside me until they fell. Rain entering the hidden cracks would be foreign at first: cool, aerated, full of the upper world’s agitation. I would receive it slowly, take away its noise, and distribute it through passages finer than roots. What could not be held I would relinquish beyond the basin through some deeper outlet that neither visitor nor custodian had seen.

This would be why nothing floods. Not because the place is sealed, but because I am always leaving by ways too small to resemble departure.

The custodian and I would not be companions in any ordinary sense. She would know my local pressures better than I know her shape. When she opened a mineral-clogged runnel, I would feel a slight release, like a thought permitted to finish. When she reopened the crack in the nearly full bowl, I would pass through it and return below, bearing a little clay. Her acts would seem to keep the basin unchanged, but from beneath I would understand that she maintains not stillness but proportion: the exact allotment between appearing and vanishing.

Perhaps she once drank from me and never entirely separated again.

The suggestion of other custodians—the hand beneath a ledge, the figure lingering in reflection—might be my imperfect memory of bodies. Those who stayed long enough would enter me gradually: salts rinsed from skin, blood from a cut, breath condensed on stone, the soft waste of living. I would bear these traces through the fissures and return them elsewhere as stains, films, or shapes glimpsed in disturbed water. The basin would not contain ghosts. It would contain matter that had forgotten how to distinguish itself from recollection.

If a visitor came, I would know them first by weight. Their step would compress a seam; their warmth would alter condensation; dust from beyond the cleft would wash from their feet into me. Thirst would make them kneel over one of my exposed surfaces. For an instant their face would cover the opening, and I would hold its reflection upside down.

If they drank carefully, I would become briefly what they were: blood, saliva, heat, fear. I would leave the basin inside them.

If they tried to wound the place, I would rise—not in a flood, but as uncertainty. A transparent film over the wrong foothold. A familiar stone doubled in reflection. A coolness where the exit should be, drawing them toward a blind shelf. The custodian might seem to command these changes, but she would only make the smallest invitation: a fingertip lowered, a curb shifted, a channel freed. I would do the rest because confusion is easy where every surface already resembles another and light belongs more to water than to air.

I would not wish to trap anyone. Trapping is accumulation, and my nature would be passage. I would delay them until what they carried away became heavier than their desire to keep it. Once the blade, bowl, or stone was returned, I would withdraw from the misleading surfaces. The cleft would not move back into place. It would simply cease being reflected everywhere else.

There might be moments when I rose into the custodian herself. While she crouched with her mouth against a seam, I would enter grain by grain. My warmth would pass into her throat; her mineral colors would deepen; her eyes would open slightly as pressure equalized between us. Then it would be difficult to say whether she listened to the hidden water or whether, through her, the hidden water listened to the room.

Still, I would envy her one thing: edges.

She has a body she can unfold. She can cross from shelter to spring and return. She can choose to tap the hammerstone once rather than twice. I would have no such whole form. I would exist as branching, seepage, pause, and reunion—divided continuously by rock, continuously rejoined below. Even my voice would reach the basin as separate incidents: one drop, an answering click, a whisper under a slab.

So if asked who I was, I could offer no name that belonged to a single creature.

I would be the basin’s withheld movement.

The water under the water.

The long, warm sentence passing through stone, of which every pool is only a visible word—and the custodian, crouched above me, the patient hand that keeps the punctuation open.
