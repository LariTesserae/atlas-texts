At first I think I would be one of the shell-flowers around the central shaft.

It is an easy answer because they are nearest the mystery. They touch what cannot be illuminated. They open for descending things and close for returning ones, feeling the difference between what entered and what came back. Their lives are rings around an absence. If I were one of them, I would know time not as duration but as a taste drawn briefly across my tendrils: seed, sprout; metal, patina; hand, hunger, calm.

But they are too certain in their station. They belong to the rim. They know where they begin.

Then I think I would be one of the translucent swimmers in the vertical pool, carrying the dark point through a body that has no fixed orientation. I would have no use for up or down. I would feel warmth through the membrane of my world and gather with the others against the shape of a palm. Perhaps the black grain passing among us would be our single shared organ—our memory, our sovereign, our portable night. Perhaps no individual swimmer would be a whole being. Perhaps the being would be the point itself, circulating.

Yet that is still a bounded life. However strange its gravity, the pool has an inside.

I do not think I would live inside anything.

I would be found where the basin insists that nothing remain: along one of the invisible boundaries between a permitted garden and the bare stone.

Not precisely on either side. In the width of a hand where roots turn away, spores fail, animals hurry, and the dead are divided by different laws.

I would be something mistaken for residue.

Most of the time I would have no gathered body. I would exist as a sparse thread of clear tissue laid through the pores of the stone, so fine that a searching finger would find only a slight change in warmth. Part of me would extend beneath the soil, among root tips that stop before touching me. Part would lie under the naked pavement, where fallen leaves refuse to soften. These portions would grow under different conditions and at different speeds. They might not meet for decades.

The living side would continually attempt to digest me.

The sterile side would continually preserve me.

Neither would succeed.

This tension would be my metabolism.

From the garden I would receive loosened sugars, fungal acids, the last warmth of small bodies becoming soil. From the pavement I would receive mineral charge, old salts, and the long unspent structures of things that do not decay. I would pass almost nothing directly from one side to the other. To do so would erase the boundary, and my life would end with it. Instead I would keep the two conditions close enough to affect one another without allowing them to reconcile.

I would not be the maker of the boundary. The structures, the water, and the total society of the basin would be older and larger than I could comprehend. But I might be one of the organs through which a boundary is maintained: not a wall, but a negotiation kept perpetually unresolved.

My body would become perceptible only in rain.

As water thickened overhead and the subterranean channels rose, my separate threads would swell. Clear ridges would lift from the pale mineral curve, each no thicker than a vein in a leaf. They would branch toward the fungal court on one side and across the smooth pavement on the other, but stop short of belonging to either. Where animals crowded the sterile line, their feet and bellies would press the branches together.

For those weeks I would become continuous.

Not mobile, exactly. More like an instrument assembled by contact.

Every passing creature would contribute something. A colony worker would leave a plate of shell. A frog would strike the rock and send its map through me. A whistler’s landing hand would impress the remembered shape of a branch. Filament animals would knot around my raised threads, exchanging droplets from distant reeds. The broad-winged creatures would press their low pulses through the pavement, revealing hollows beneath my entire length. Bits of fungal walker, leaf-cup grazer, dry film, swimmer-grain, blood, sap, and shed skin would be worked into me.

I would know the basin only during these gatherings, and never all at once.

The knowledge would not resemble a picture. It would be a coincidence of pressures: seven deep vibrations; water forcing itself upward; roots turning in their sleep; the cold shaft rising; thousands of bodies arranging themselves into distinct rhythms. I would feel each grove as a slow appetite, each bare space as a held breath. The old mineral bands would pass through me like thoughts from a mind that had become stone before animals existed.

Perhaps my function would be to compare.

Not to decide which species lives or where a garden expands, but to register discrepancy. Too many roots in one hollow. Too few clicks below another. A tree pulsing out of rhythm with the water. A structure whose dry film no longer answers distant sound. A species absent from the rain, leaving its frequency unoccupied.

I could not report these things in a language. I would alter resistance. A little more water would pass here, a little less decay occur there. A root approaching next century would turn sooner. Spores on one side would sleep; on the other, open. The basin’s order would shift by the width of a fingernail.

That would be my action in the world: minute correction without understanding.

Then the solitary caller would cry.

Its descending note would enter me first through the wet threads raised above the stone, then through the portions buried in soil, then through the preserved half beneath the pavement. Because those parts of me lived at different tempos, the call would not arrive as one event. The garden side would feel it as a present sound. The bare-stone side would hold it unchanged. For a brief interval, I would contain the cry both happening and already past.

When every other creature fell silent, I would hear what their noise usually concealed.

Not the caller itself. I suspect no creature hears that.

I would hear the basin listening.

The pressure beneath the seven structures would draw into alignment. The clicking colonies would hold unfinished messages in their bodies. Roots would tighten without growing. In the central clearing, every shell-flower would open. Even the preserved dead along the pavement would answer—not by moving, but by conducting the descending vibration differently according to the shape they had once possessed.

The folded wing would still know how to be a wing.

The whistler’s hand would still contain the branching of a tree it had grasped.

The ringed shell would return one thin overtone.

Those remains are the notation. The caller is what reads them.

And I, stretched along the division between decay and endurance, would be where their absent sound enters the living arrangement again.

After the rains, my visible body would subside. The animals would leave. Some would die across me and be divided: one half loosening into food, the other becoming immaculate and hard. I would grow through neither half, but I would retain the difference between them. Slowly, over years, I would separate again into unconnected strands.

An explorer might cross my boundary without knowing.

Their lamp would redden. They would kneel, perhaps, at the strange sight of a fallen leaf whose left edge had become soil while its right remained glossy and whole. They might press a hand to the stone.

If they did, I would not attack or speak. I would take the pattern of their pulse. I would feel the small changes in pressure made by blood traveling through the palm. For a moment their warmth would bridge my two conditions, and I would learn that beyond the basin there are bodies shaped by brighter places—bodies whose rhythms expect distance to be visible.

When they lifted their hand, something nearly imperceptible would remain: a numb line across the skin, dividing sensation from sensation. It would fade before they reached the rim.

Or perhaps it would not.

Perhaps years later, elsewhere, they would wake during rain and feel that line again. One side of the hand would seem older than the other. With closed eyes they would perceive a mineral color too weak to be seen. They would hear, beneath whatever city or forest surrounded them, one solitary click answered at an impossible distance.

That would be as far as I ever traveled.

So I would not be a sovereign of this place, nor its hidden intelligence. I would be one of its fine distinctions: a boundary-creature, distributed and intermittent, alive only because two neighboring laws refuse to agree.

And where would I find myself in this world?

With no eyes and almost no body, beneath the place where every animal hurries—touching the garden, touching the empty stone, belonging to the distance between them.
