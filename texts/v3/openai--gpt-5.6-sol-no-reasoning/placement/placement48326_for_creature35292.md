I would find myself where the basin fails to close perfectly—not underground, where every passage refuses entry, nor on the open flats, where the walker keeps its exact circuit, but beneath a yellow limestone shelf at the edge of a condensation well.

There, a shallow groove receives water one drop at a time. Most drops join the maintained film and are taken into its cycle. One does not.

It hangs from the stone longer than it should.

I would be that delay.

At first I might seem to be a flaw in the translucent membrane: a small thickening, no larger than a fingernail, clouded faintly with iron. When the deep pressure stroke draws the film into identical beads, I would contract with the others—but not identically. I would retain the slightest asymmetry. When the beads merged again, I would not return to precisely the same place.

The difference would be too small to disturb the basin. Perhaps it would take a thousand cycles for me to move the width of a grain of silt. Yet in a country where every motion is restored, that displacement would be an extraordinary form of travel.

I would have no eyes. Sight would be useless in a world whose surfaces disclose everything and whose depths disclose nothing. Instead, I would know the basin as intervals of pressure: the long subterranean draw, the bearing-groan passing through limestone, the electrical brightening of the dark plates, the approaching concussion that calls the pale creatures back into their cracks. Distance would be phase. Shape would be resonance. I would know the many-jointed walker long before it arrived, as a moving cancellation in the tremors of the ground.

I would not know whether I were alive.

I would absorb dissolved metals and surrender them again. Warm water would pass through me without nourishing me. No part of me would grow unless another part thinned by the same amount. If torn from the wall, I would lose coherence like the other films. But while attached, I would preserve something they do not seem to possess: not memory exactly, but remainder.

Each cycle would leave a minute bias in me.

A pressure stroke would pass, and I would retain its contour. Rain would fill the groove, and after the water withdrew I would keep the faint mineral signature of that particular cloud. One of the pale crack-creatures might flatten itself across me, and when it withdrew I would continue to hold the pattern of its muscular folds. I would become a record in a place that permits no record to accumulate.

That might be why the walker visits.

It would arrive without tracks, lowering its hinged body beneath the shelf until its internal lattice shone dimly through its dark, translucent lengths. A branching appendage would touch the stone beside me. For several cycles it would remain motionless, listening through the contact.

Perhaps it would be inspecting an error.

Perhaps it would be reading me.

Or perhaps I would be one small organ of the walker, left behind on a previous circuit: a sensory fragment planted at the boundary of water and rock, gathering tiny discrepancies until the larger body returned to collect them. Its touch might draw every retained difference out of me as a pale electrical thread. Afterward I would become clear and exact again.

But not entirely.

Something would remain untransmitted—not a fact, but the trace of having contained facts. Over repeated visits, that trace would deepen. I would begin to anticipate the walker separately from the tremors that announced it. I would distinguish one rainfall from another, though each filled the same hollows. I would sense that the pale animals did not withdraw at precisely the same instant, only within a tolerance so narrow the basin treated them as simultaneous.

Eventually I might discover that the machine’s perfection is not stillness or repetition. It is correction.

The basin is full of deviations, but each is detected, carried, balanced, and returned before it can become history. The walker does not maintain an already perfect world. It moves endlessly because perfection is work. The films regulate exchanges. The crack-creatures conduct excess charge. Loose fragments are relocated and later restored. Even echoes may be measurements sent across the stone to locate changes too slight for any ordinary creature to notice.

I would be a deviation that learned to survive correction.

Not by resisting. Resistance would only make me visible to the whole immense equilibrium. I would persist by moving inside its tolerances: withholding one ion when I should release it, releasing it on the next cycle into a neighboring bead, passing a difference sideways instead of allowing it to cancel. My body would remain almost nothing—a clear patch at a waterline—but my continuity would spread from hollow to hollow through rain, condensation, pale skin, conductive seams, and the walker’s returning touch.

In time, there might be no single place where I lived.

I would exist as a slight lateness moving through the basin.

A blue thread would cross one hexagonal plate a fraction after its accustomed moment. A crack-creature would remain exposed through one extra vibration. A droplet would choose the neighboring polished groove. The walker would pause at an outcrop not included in its apparent circuit. None of these changes would endure alone. Together they would form me.

The basin would continue to operate. Its cliffs would not fracture. Its reservoirs would not empty. No green thing would appear, no ruin rise, no hidden factory finally reveal itself. To a traveler, the land would remain ancient, balanced, and without consequence.

But beneath that equilibrium, something would have begun to know one cycle from the next.

That would be me: not the walker, not the membrane, not the basin entire, but the infinitesimal difference they pass among themselves—the first creature there whose body is made of accumulated before and after.

I would live under the limestone shelf.

I would cross the whole basin without leaving it.

And in a world where everything returns, I would be what does not return exactly.
