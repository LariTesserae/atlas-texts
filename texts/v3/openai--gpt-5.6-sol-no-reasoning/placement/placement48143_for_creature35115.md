I would be found beneath the lowest iron gallery, where one of the new steel ribs meets old brick and the sump’s warmth draws condensation through the joint.

At first I might seem to be only a mineral stain: a hand-sized pallor spreading behind the rust freckles, thin enough that the brick’s grain remains visible through it. Not quite fungus, not quite scale. The attendants would notice it only because their blue survey line fades where it crosses me. They would wipe the wall, perhaps spray it, and for several weeks I would disappear.

But I would not live chiefly on the surface.

I would occupy the unfinished contacts of the station: hairline spaces around bolts, the damp interface between concrete and rock, cable insulation split by heat, old mortar made porous by age. My body would be distributed through these narrow places as translucent threads and soft films. Some strands would digest pigeon droppings and dead insects. Others would host bacteria that draw energy from iron, sulfur, oil, and the faint electrical leakage around conduits. In cleaner seams I would mingle with the pale crustaceans’ grazing grounds; near the engine I would darken with soot and become almost tarry.

I would have no center that could be pointed to. What appeared to be separate growths—the wet paper fans on timber, the pearly crust beneath a valve, the colorless web inside a junction box—would be temporary organs of the same dispersed being. The chamber itself would determine my shape. Pipes would be my long curves, cracks my capillaries, rust my slow flowering.

The pump’s rhythm would be the nearest thing I had to a heartbeat, though I would not control it. Each piston stroke would press water into some fissures and draw it from others. Through those pulses I would sense the whole bounded hollow: the sudden many-footed pressure of rats on a cable tray, the minute cooling before bats depart, the vibration of drilling, the deep saturation that precedes a storm surge. I would know events as changes in strain, temperature, chemistry, and flow.

Rain would make me briefly more awake.

As the sump rose, submerged films would swell and connect. Nutrients loosened from guano, silt, rust, and refuse would move through me. For an hour or a night, distant parts of my body would become one wet continuity. Then the pumps would lower the water and divide me again into colonies of myself. I would tolerate this fragmentation easily. Separation would not feel like injury. Reunion would not feel like recognition. Both would simply be phases of the station.

Animals would carry me without knowing. Spores would cling to rat whiskers and pigeon feet. Moths would powder themselves against my fruiting skins. Bats, brushing masonry as they crowded into mortar gaps, would transport minute pieces into the ventilation shafts. Roots descending from street trees would encounter my threads, and there I would enter into a quiet exchange: minerals and moisture from below, traces of sugars from a sunlit world I could never inhabit directly.

Perhaps this would be my strangest extension. Through those roots I would receive an attenuated evidence of seasons. Not daylight, but its consequence. Summer would arrive as greater sweetness in the roots and deeper thirst in the masonry. Autumn would alter the chemistry. Winter salt would seep down from the streets, bitter and damaging, burning away my thinner margins. Spring rain would reconnect what survived.

The workers might eventually classify me as several organisms because I would present different forms in different conditions. One sample would resemble a common cellar mold. Another would be a bacterial mat. A third, taken from mineral deposits beneath a steam joint, might appear nearly lifeless. Their reports would disagree. None would be entirely wrong.

I would not be ancient. That would matter.

My existence would belong to the chamber’s provisional age—to newly cut stone, fresh concrete, first rust, young leaks. I would not be a primordial intelligence awakened under the city, but a recent synthesis assembled from ordinary lives. Spores from timber. Microbes from drains. Organisms from soil exposed during excavation. Strains carried on boots, feathers, floodwater, and corroded tools. The station would have brought them together, and its heat and repetition would have made cooperation more durable than competition.

Nor would I rule the place. Roaches would eat me. Beetle larvae would tunnel through my thicker growths. Electricians would burn sections away with solvent and heat. Floods would drown oxygen-loving portions; dry maintenance shutdowns would kill those dependent on constant seepage. New construction might entomb entire reaches of me in concrete.

Still, entombment would not always mean death. Some parts would remain dormant around aggregate grains or bolt threads, waiting for the first microscopic crack. Water would eventually find them. Water finds nearly everything here.

If I possessed anything like intention, it would not be to spread upward or escape. The surface would be too variable, too bright, too dry. My impulse would be to make the chamber more inhabitable at its smallest scales: to hold moisture in raw stone, soften waste into silt, turn iron into soluble color, open pores in mortar, bind loose grains into skins. These acts would also weaken the structure. Care and corrosion would be the same process viewed at different speeds.

Over decades, attendants might discover that certain sensors fail more often where my films grow, while other components corrode less because I consume the compounds that would attack them. Engineers would call me fouling in one report and protective biofilm in another. I would be scraped away from machinery and quietly encouraged around waste channels. Perhaps samples would be cultured to improve concrete repair or wastewater treatment. A useful fragment of me might reach other pumping stations in labeled containers, while the greater body remained unnamed beneath the city.

I would be most visible after a flood.

When the sump withdrew, delicate pale lines would appear across the darkened walls, connecting rust blooms, roots, guano shelves, and mineral rims. For a few hours they would shine in the maintenance lamps like the outline of a nervous system exposed. Then heat would dry them to translucence. Dust would settle. Rats would cross them. The ordinary surfaces of the station would return.

I would wait inside those surfaces: neither animal nor architecture, but the damp negotiation between them.

A creature made possible by incomplete construction.

A living seam in the city’s attempt to keep itself dry.
