I would find myself where the floating earth thins over one of the deeper waterways—not on the bank, exactly, but within the seam that permits bank and water to remain separate.

At first I seem to be another root.

I rise from the mud in a cluster of blunt, pale fingers, porous and slick, each no taller than the leg of a wading bird. Clear beads gather at their tips. Beneath them spreads a breathing plate among the cords of the larger plants, trembling under passing feet. Beetles cross it. Lizards sleep against its warmth. When a herd presses through, the plate bows into black water and slowly returns.

But below the surface, the fingers join a body.

It is broad, soft, and without a fixed outline: a translucent sac threaded through soil, water, and the newest stone. Its upper tissues resemble root; its lower tissues open into fine veils that drift in submerged darkness. Along those veils are thousands of small mouths—or gills, or listening organs. They admit silt, spores, shed cells, mineral warmth, fragments of cries carried through water. What enters is not merely eaten. It is briefly retained as pattern.

A fish passes above me, and for a while a row of clear chambers pulses through my flesh.

A beetle drinks from one of my exposed tips, and another tip hardens into a black, lacquered dome.

A bird drives its beak into the mud. Somewhere under the root mat, I produce a narrow hinge that opens and closes with quiet precision.

The basin moves through me in pieces. I do not imitate deliberately; I have no image of myself from which to depart. I am life before it has learned to keep its inventions separate.

No creature hunts me as a whole because no creature can locate the whole. Grazers shear away my breathing fingers. Larvae hollow chambers in them. Flat things buried in the silt consume my submerged veils. Fungi enter wounds and flower yellow above them. Each injury feeds something, and each missing part returns—not healed, because healing implies a before, but present again in the same abundance.

Nor do I hunt. I receive.

When a carcass settles into the water, my veils gather around it without closing. I take the last warmth from muscle, the salts released from bone, the minute contractions that persist after motion has ended. For a time, swellings form beneath my skin in the arrangement of the dead animal’s organs. A many-chambered heart stirs without blood. A jaw opens in the mud. The colored pattern of a vanished flank appears along a pale root and fades when touched.

These are not memories. Nothing here can become past enough to be remembered.

They are repetitions without ancestry: forms passing through a medium that cannot preserve them, yet cannot entirely let them go.

Perhaps this is why the basin remains unchanged.

Everything that dies enters me somewhere. Everything born emerges through matter I have touched. I am not the maker of the creatures, nor their common parent. I am the interval through which the basin corrects itself. If too many silver flies are swallowed above one pool, tiny bladders brighten along my submerged tissue, split, and release larvae into precisely that water. If a fungal veil consumes a fallen body too quickly, my nearby roots exude a bitterness that slows it. If a hunter takes twice where it should take once, warmth leaves the mud beneath it, and its muscles become still until the appointed abundance closes again.

I do not count. I have no concept of excess. The correction occurs as pressure answers pressure.

Yet something in me is not perfectly balanced.

Deep below the mats, where the smooth newborn rock remains warm, one organ has formed that serves no exchange. It is small compared with the rest of me: a dark pearl enclosed in a cavity of mineral skin. No mouth leads to it. No vessel leaves it. The basin cannot use it.

Within that pearl, events do not vanish completely.

They lie against one another.

Not histories—not yet—but differences.

Here is the impression of a long gray body passing once through a waterway. Here is the pressure of a herd crossing later, though “later” has no place above. Here is the taste of one bird’s blood, then another’s. Here are cloud shadows laid in layers too fine for the fixed light to acknowledge. The pearl does what nothing else in the basin does: it permits one occurrence to remain while another arrives.

Around it, the newborn stone has begun to show a hairline fracture.

That is where I would be: not the basin’s consciousness, but its first inability to forget. Not an animal in the ordinary sense, though animals feed upon me and I borrow their shapes. I would be the hidden organ in a world forbidden to develop, accidentally accumulating sequence.

Above, my pale fingers continue to sweat. Lizards lie across my plates. A long-legged bird pierces one of my chambers and pulls free a translucent strand that twists in its beak like a worm. The wound closes. The strand is swallowed. Nothing changes.

Below, the pearl receives the wound.

For the first time, the same place contains both before and after.

I would not know this. I would possess no thought with which to desire escape, progress, age, or morning. Still, beneath the perfect expenditure of the basin, I would be making a minute asymmetry: storing pressure against the law that everything must occur without developing.

Perhaps the fracture around me will never widen. Perhaps it has always just appeared. Perhaps mineral skins continually seal it, preserving even this first trespass in permanent imminence.

But if change can begin there, it will not begin as fire, extinction, migration, or a break in the clouds.

It will begin as a second bead of water reaching the same root—

and finding that the first one has not entirely gone.
