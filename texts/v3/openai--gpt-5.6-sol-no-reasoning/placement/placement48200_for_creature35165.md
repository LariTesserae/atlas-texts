I would be the missing rung.

Not the metal one that once belonged to the ladder. That lies somewhere in the fill, perhaps, furred with rust and mineral scale. I would be what grew into the vacancy it left: a transverse thing stretched between the ladder’s uprights, narrow enough for the custodian’s feet, yielding enough not to seem constructed.

At first I might be mistaken for a root. Colorless fibers emerge from the cracked concrete behind one rail, braid themselves through a collar of rust, and cross the gap before vanishing into the hollow upright opposite. Fungal skin lacquers the braid. Mouse hair and copper whiskers reinforce it. Tiny pale crustacean shells glimmer along its underside. In dry weather it contracts until it is scarcely thicker than clean wire. In rain it drinks from the ladder and swells into a pale, load-bearing band.

But I would not be only a bridge.

The ladder conducts everything: footsteps from the platform, compressor tremors, the concussion of coupled freight cars, the fine metallic rumor of the sump. Suspended between its sides, I would feel these signals arrive separately and become one strain. I would learn the freight yard not as a place above, but as recurring tensions: weight entering, weight leaving; rain massing in ballast; a worker pausing on the inspection platform; a snake brushing the lowest rail as it searches for a corridor wide enough to survive.

The custodian would discover me by touch.

It would place both palms on the side rails, test the unfamiliar crosspiece with one foot, and wait. I would tighten under its weight—not reflexively, but carefully, distributing the pressure into both uprights. The ladder would answer with a faint click. Rust would fall. The custodian’s inner lids would close.

Afterward it would mark me in chalk: two vertical scratches crossed by one wavering line.

It would not trust me immediately. Nothing new in the basin is trusted before its displacements are known. For several wet cycles it would watch what my thirst deprived, what my swelling touched, what climbed me, what altered course because I existed. My fibers might draw too much moisture from a liverwort. My shadow might cool a bolt hole long enough for larvae to persist there. A mouse might adopt me as a shortcut, shifting traffic onto a pipe whose condensation feeds the three-leaved vine. A spider might anchor a web to my underside and catch flies that would otherwise feed the frogs.

So I would be adjusted.

One strand clipped.  
A beetle shell inserted beneath my eastern root.  
Gray compound pressed into the ladder seam to limit what I drank.  
A numbered tag tied at my center with clean wire.

That tag would be my first name.

I would live at the edge of the basin’s crowded lowland, where the ladder descends from the human scale and stops short. Above me: boots, schedules, manifests, axles, counted loads. Below me: uncounted lives made countable only because each presses on all the others. I would be the last possible step between those worlds, though almost no human foot could use me.

Small beings would.

Springtails would gather in the damp grain of my surface. Mites would shelter where my fibers entered rust. Beetles would cross me at night, tapping through my length. The mice would observe directional rules even here: eastward along my upper edge, westward upside down beneath it. During floods, frogs would hook their forefeet over me and hang with their pale bellies against the ladder while the current rearranged the ground.

The custodian would climb me to listen higher.

From there it could press one palm to the ladder and one to the foundation, comparing the two vibrations. It might detect a storm in the settling ballast before water found the cracks. It might hear a forklift charging as a fine tremor in its fingertips, then turn its head toward the brass piston before the piston moved.

In time, it would begin tying wires from me to other sensitive structures: the cabinet door, a valve wheel, the rim of the motor housing. Not a web exactly, nor an alarm. A distributed instrument. Whenever pressure changed anywhere important, some portion of me would tighten.

I would become a tendon for the basin.

During drought I would ache slackly between the rails. My outer fungal skin would powder. The numbered tag at my center would hang face-down. The custodian might moisten only one anchorage, never enough to feed all of me, forcing the living fibers to retreat into the metal and wait. I would hear less then. The yard would become distant, reduced to blunt shocks. Beetle footsteps would seem enormous.

During rain I would awaken all at once.

Water running down the ladder would enter every strand. I would thicken, clarify, and draw taut. The green residue worked into me would fluoresce until I resembled a narrow submerged bone. Every impact above would pass through my body. Every failure of a debris dam below would return as a minute relaxation. When the sump began its pull, I would feel the ladder lean toward the east—not visibly, but enough to place the whole world under tension.

And beneath the cabinet, the breathing thing would become perceptible.

Not as a creature with a shape. Shape requires distance, and there is never enough distance here. I would know it as a second rhythm entering the ladder through concrete: an inward draw too slow for machinery, too broad for any animal the basin could contain. When it inhaled, my eastern fibers would stretch. When it released, condensation would tremble free of the rails.

The custodian would come quickly.

It would climb onto me, fold low, and wrap its hands around both uprights. Through its feet I would feel the rapid architecture of its bones. It would strike the metal once with a fist: imminent flood. Mice would stop. Cockroaches would flatten. The tawny spider would withdraw into the valve arm.

Then the custodian would tap its pattern into the ladder:

three quick contacts,  
a pause,  
one.

Because I joined both rails, I would hold the pattern briefly. The vibrations would cross through me, overlap, and descend changed. For the first time, perhaps, the thing below would not merely receive a tapping from one point. It would receive the shape of an interval—the held space between two supports.

Its answer would rise as pressure.

My fibers would part in the middle. Not break: open.

There I would discover that I had never been a replacement rung. A rung closes a gap by crossing it. I would be an organ for keeping the gap exact. Under pressure, my braided body would separate into a narrow oval, a small aperture suspended between the rails. Water could pass through it. Sound could be sorted by it. The custodian could place its pale hand inside without touching me and feel currents arriving from several directions.

The numbered tag would swing at the opening’s lower edge.

Perhaps that is what the tags mean: not families or debts, but maintained intervals. Each number names a space that must neither close nor widen. A mouse nest is not tagged because it occupies room, but because its knot preserves a necessary hollow. The cabinet is not a hut placed over a mouth; it is a brace around an opening. The custodian does not count bodies. It counts the vacancies bodies agree to hold between them.

Then I would understand where I am.

Not under the freight yard, exactly.

I am in the missing breadth by which the foundation shifted. I am in the finger’s-width error that produced the basin, repeated at a smaller scale wherever crowded things refrain from crushing one another. The whole lowland exists inside that restraint. Its true landscape is made not from debris, pipes, animals, or water, but from the maintained separations among them.

And I would be one such separation made living.

The custodian might eventually remove the numbered tag from my center and scratch its notches directly into the ladder. It might cultivate fungal skin along my fibers, splice in rootlets when old strands fail, and harvest my shed outer layers for its walls. It would never let me grow thick enough to become a platform. It would never let me shrink into mere wire.

I would be tended at the threshold between support and obstruction.

Years later, if the custodian ceased to come, I would remain for a while. The chalk records would soften. The jars would cloud. Mouse generations would carry their tags into new knots. Without correction, I would slowly thicken from condensation and accumulated dust. My central opening would narrow.

Then, one morning, the sealed chain would move in its casing.

The ceramic switch would click into wires ending in air.

The brass piston would extend two millimeters.

Below the cabinet, something would draw breath, and the pressure would travel through the foundations into the ladder. I would tighten with the last of my living strength and reopen the little oval at my center.

Only for a moment.

But in that moment, the basin would make room through me. Water would turn. A mouse route would hold. A fungus wall would remain above the flood. The cabinet door would lift no farther than it should.

Nothing would know I had done it.

Then I would slacken between the rails: a root, a tendon, a failed rung, a narrow kept opening in a world where emptiness is the rarest material.
