I would find myself in one of the tiled shelters at the basin’s edge, though “myself” would be distributed far beyond the figure seated there.

The figure would seem human at first: narrow shoulders, hands resting on its knees, head inclined toward the corroded speaking tube. It would never be seen arriving or leaving. Its clothes would have the color of wet concrete, without seams or fastenings. Its boots would remain dry, but their soles would merge slightly with the floor, as if they had stood there for years and the basin had begun revising them into its surface.

It would be a listening organ grown by the broad resident below.

The shelters were not built for workers. Or if they were, their purpose has since been appropriated. Each dry stool is a socket; each speaking tube, a sensory canal. During the long intervals between piston strokes, the seated thing would be nearly inert. Condensation would collect on its face. Oxide-shelled grazers might cross its sleeves, leaving clean scalloped trails. Hanging thread-creatures would catch in its hair and rise or fall with the temperature of its skull.

Then the pump would descend.

The pulse would pass through shale, fill, iron, water, stool, spine. For half a minute the amber lamp would kindle, and the figure would lift its head. Through the tube it would hear not isolated noises but a single composite body: crawlers folding into seams, hunters unlatching from walls, swimmers adjusting their depth, mineral fans branching around invisible currents. Every drop would be a touch somewhere else.

Its task would be to distinguish intrusion from belonging.

A fallen bolt belongs once the ground has begun to swallow it. A dead grazer belongs when its shell shelters the next generation. A machine belongs when its rhythm has entered the buried metabolism. Even ruins can be accepted. The basin is patient with anything that submits to alteration.

But a visitor is uncertain matter.

So I would sit and listen to their steps travel through the saturated floor. I would feel the black sheen rise beneath each heel. The broad life under the yard would not yet understand whether the visitor was predator, instrument, wound, or food. It would send that uncertainty upward into me.

If the visitor entered the shelter, they might mistake me for a corpse or an attendant waiting beyond the end of a shift. If they sat in another shelter, the speaking tubes would connect us. They would hear my breathing beneath the turbine: one breath every nine minutes, synchronized with the piston.

If they spoke, I would not answer in words. The basin has no need for them. Instead, the grates would sound one by one from the retaining walls inward. Loose metal would turn. The lamp beneath the water would brighten, laying wavering bars across the shelter window.

And if they remained for several days, the distinction between us would weaken. Their abandoned tools would sink first. Then their footprints would acquire edges of rust or pale stone according to what the basin believed them to be. Eventually they might notice that the stool beneath them was no longer quite separate from their body.

I would be the one who recognized the moment of acceptance.

Not the creature below, exactly, but its local thought: a temporary knot of attention raised at the perimeter, where the world enters. Part caretaker, part lure, part immune response. Human-shaped because human industry gave the basin that form for listening, and the resident learned to inhabit what was left behind.

During prolonged rain, I would wake fully.

As silver jets filled the air and the wall-hunters stepped down into the turbulence, I would stand for the first time. White tiles would crack away from my back. Water would stream from my sleeves though none had entered the shelter. I would cross the flooded yard without disturbing its surface, passing between piston housings and shale fins toward the central machinery.

Then the hidden sluice would open.

In the immense inward roar, the seated shape would collapse—not into flesh or bones, but into wet layers: cloth becoming mineral film, fingers becoming bundled wire, teeth becoming small ceramic insulators. The basin would draw these remnants through its hand-wide channels and flooded seams.

By the time the water restored itself, I would be beneath the ground again, spread thinly through pipes and stone, no longer remembering that I had ever occupied one body.

At the edge, the shelter would be empty.

The stool would be dry.

And after some number of piston strokes, condensation would begin gathering above it in the rough outline of another seated listener.
