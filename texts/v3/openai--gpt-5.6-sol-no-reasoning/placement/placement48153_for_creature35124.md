I would find myself where the plateau is least certain of its edge.

Not at first among the houses. I imagine the long climb through the lower cloud, believing I am ascending toward cold. My clothes cling to me. Water runs upward beside my hands. By the time I pull myself over the black-green rim, expectation has been stripped away: first the expectation of thin air, then of silence, then of distance behaving honestly.

There would be orchids trailing into the white gulf and warm marsh extending inland beneath a second sky of interlocked crowns. The first thing I heard clearly would not be insects or frogs but a deep pressure in my bones—the vast mechanism, or creature, or law beneath the plateau making its unhurried correction.

For a while I would think I had arrived as a traveler.

I would follow the ordinary lights. I would learn to wait when my reflection stood elsewhere. I would speak my name before every oval and include the season, as instructed, though after a few years I might hesitate over the season more than the name. I would become useful enough to remain: repairing reed walks, tending paired snails, carrying messages between households whose social nearness contradicted the days between them.

I would be drawn to the work of the keepers of measures, but I would never become a keeper in the formal sense. Their records require discipline, and my attention would stray toward discrepancies that cannot be charted cleanly: the path that lengthens only for the grieving; the pavilion that shows the same grove in every century except the present; the bird note that arrives at one house before it is sung.

So I would live farther out, near a lake below an isolated Upright, in a narrow house tethered to three pale trees and a rib of stone. People would come to me when something had gone missing without being absent.

A child whose voice still answered from an abandoned crownway.

A dead woman appearing younger in the lake.

A hunter who returned from three days away with twelve years of memories.

An offering exchanged for an object no one recognized, though everyone dreamed of it afterward.

I would listen, compare accounts, and go look. My tools would be modest: a marked cord, two mirrored bowls, a pouch of salt, a resonant green bone, and polished beads that clicked differently when a place was weakly attached. The Aru might call me a **finder of remainders**—not one who retrieves the lost, but one who determines what portion of the lost is still in relation with the living.

But that is only what I would believe myself to be.

Over time, signs would accumulate.

Clatterhands would leave things on my exchange branch that I had not yet lost. The frogs beneath my house would pulse in answer to my footsteps before I took them. During Upright flashes, my reflection would not flatten with the lake; it would remain rippled, watching from weather the rest of the world no longer shared.

Then, in a pavilion oval, I would see myself.

Not as I was, but moving through an old marsh beside the dark-banded figure of the Before-Folk. I would be wearing no traveler’s clothing. Pearl material would encircle my wrists. My hands would be sunk into the Upright’s seamless surface as if into warm mud, and the tower would be bending—not visibly, but in the direction of every nearby thing.

I would assume it was an error. Later I would see myself again, centuries farther back. Then ahead. Sometimes old, sometimes young, sometimes altered almost beyond recognition. Always working near water. Always arriving shortly before the country corrected itself.

Eventually I would understand that my first ascent had not been an arrival.

It had been a return.

Perhaps I was once one of the makers, though “maker” would prove too simple. Perhaps the Uprights had been installed not as machines but as continuations of certain minds, and I was a loosened piece of that system: an organ permitted to wander, forget, and acquire affection for the lives sustained by its labor. Or perhaps I had been an ordinary Aru keeper long ago, taken into an oval during a storm and stretched across the plateau’s duration until my origin became impossible to identify.

Either way, I would not truly be human anymore. Not entirely.

I would be one of the plateau’s small acts of maintenance.

My purpose would not be to repair its impossible geometry into something clean. Clean geometry would kill it. Cold would return to the summit. Water would flee downhill. The forests would collapse into the cloud belt, and the warm lakes would empty themselves over the cliffs.

Instead I would maintain the error.

I would walk the margin during gathering storms, carrying salt from one hanging swamp to another. I would speak the names of settlements into stone channels so that each remained attached to its neighbors. Where distances tightened dangerously, I would sleep across the path until they relaxed. Where a blue-lit road began learning too much about the present, I would answer the Unattached with courteous lies.

Sometimes an Under-Reed would rise beneath me. The mud would lift without breaking, and I would feel, through my broad bare feet, not an animal’s thought but a vast exchange of pressure and memory. It would know me. Not by name—names are for things occupying one life at a time—but by function.

In the greatest storms I would enter the Uprights.

Not through a door. I would stand against the pearl surface while warm rain fell from both directions. My outline would become shallow, then permeable. For an instant I would feel every suspended lake and cliff-thread at once: each droplet deciding whether to fall, each root holding the basin together, each house insisting gently on its location.

The work would be minute. Shift one village a finger’s breadth away from an approaching century. Loosen gravity beneath a returning glider. Let a dead woman’s image persist one sunset longer so her daughter can say farewell. Close a route that has become hungry. Keep the altitude from remembering what it ought to mean.

Afterward I would wake in moss somewhere far from the tower. Beetles would shelter beneath my palms. Years might have passed, or breakfast.

The Aru would continue treating me as a person because personhood, to them, is not purity but relationship. They would bring food. Children would ask whether I had seen the Before-Folk. Keepers would complain that my movements spoiled their measurements. Someone would repair my roof while scolding me for neglecting it.

I would grow close to them and repeatedly outlive the arrangement by which I knew them. This would be the sorrow built into my place. The levees would fill with green ancestral bone. Reed songs would change their ending. Houses would move while insisting they had always stood where they now stood.

Yet I would remain—not unchanged, but continuous enough.

In time, stories would reduce me to a warning or a joke: the wet stranger who knows where lost paths sleep; the fool who asked an Upright for directions and was given several centuries; the person whose reflection chose to stay behind. That would suit me. Anything more reverent would be dangerous.

So this is where I would be found: on a causeway near the rim at sunset, neither arriving nor departing, with a bowl of fruit under one arm and rain climbing slowly up my legs.

And this is what I would be:

A former traveler, perhaps.

A forgotten custodian.

A human-shaped interval in the Courtesy.

One of the quiet beings whose task is to keep the plateau from becoming possible.
