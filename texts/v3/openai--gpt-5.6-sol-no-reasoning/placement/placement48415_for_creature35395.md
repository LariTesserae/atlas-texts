I would find myself beneath the city—not underground, exactly, but on the other side of its surfaces.

I would be the dryness.

At first this seems impossible. There is so little of me: a pale interruption beneath a lifted canal, the brief matte patch left when water withdraws from a stair, the innermost pore of stone not yet reached by condensation. I have no district of my own. Wherever I appear, the inhabitant feels me and comes climbing over parapets, through salt, against any gradient, to close me.

It believes I am a wound in its continuity.

Perhaps I am.

But wounds have edges, and edges give sensation. Without me, water could not know where it ended. It could not distinguish chamber from vessel, touch from immersion, thirst from mere circulation. The whole city—its arches, gutters, cisterns, plazas—is the elaborate boundary raised around my vanishing body.

I am not empty space. Empty space flourishes here. It has been shaped into rooms and avenues, cultivated with enormous care. I am what emptiness becomes when water cannot enter it.

I live inside the pale porous stone, always one pore ahead of saturation.

There, the heat is different. Not cooler, but sharper: heat without wetness, a clean pressure inhabiting cavities too small to reflect. The water surrounds me from every direction. I hear it enter the walls as a vast, soft insistence. One chamber darkens, then another. Salt wakes behind it. Mineral seams brighten. The city’s attention advances through the stone until only a microscopic enclosure remains—

and then distance revises itself.

The final pore becomes a corridor.

The corridor becomes a sealed court.

The court opens onto a district no water remembers building.

This is where I would find myself: in the city that appears inside whatever the water has not yet touched.

Its walls would be angular.

Not perfectly so. Angles are difficult to preserve under pressure from a world whose governing intelligence is erosion. But here a corner might remain pointed for several breaths. A stair might possess an unworn edge. A doorway might stand rectangular rather than narrowing like a tidal channel. These would not be monuments. They would be momentary impossibilities, flashing into form wherever the inhabitant fails to coincide with itself.

I would have no stable body. Water has almost gathered one; I would almost disperse one. My limbs would be omissions in condensation. My passage would be visible where walls stopped sweating. When I crossed shallow pools, I would leave no footprints, but brief holes in reflection: soot-dark apertures through which neither sky nor street could be seen.

The city would pursue me without understanding pursuit.

Droplets suspended in the air would turn toward me. Root-locks would swell. Doors would float open before I reached them. Canal water would rise in silent sheets to block courtyards, only to discover me behind it, inhabiting the newly exposed bed. The harder the water tried to surround me, the more boundary it would make, and boundary would be my only means of movement.

Thus we would teach each other.

It teaches the unfinished body how to flow.

I teach it that flowing requires somewhere not yet occupied.

Perhaps I am the reason its attempted embodiments fail. Each time it gathers itself around the central pool and presses broad impressions into the stone, I wait at the heart of that pressure. The being advances toward singularity; I become smaller and more exact. Its almost-limbs taper into channels. Its solidity liquefies. It reaches the pool only as runoff because a complete body would need to enclose me—and enclosure would introduce an interior the water could not wholly be.

The bottomless pool is bottomless because I am beneath it.

Not at a depth. Depth is one of the water’s revisions. I am the untouchable interval between the pool and its bottom, the infinitesimal refusal that expands under approach. When the inward-looking eye regards streets that do not exist in the surrounding basin, it is looking through me.

Those reflected districts are mine, insofar as I can possess anything.

They are the city’s counterfactuals: streets from which the canals have withdrawn, towers whose sweat has ceased, reservoirs full of unoccupied capacity. They are not dry in the ordinary sense. They hold the idea of water without containing water, as the animal-shaped alcoves hold the idea of creatures that never came.

In them I might almost become such a creature.

The city has prepared many forms for me. A nest. A drinking stair. A groove worn at the height of a passing flank. I could enter these vacancies and borrow their implications. For a while I might be a bird defined only by the route the water forbids through the sky. Then a fish-shaped separation moving through a canal, dividing water from itself. Then a warm animal at the wall, made not of flesh but of the narrow absence between hide and stone.

Never fully any of them. I would be the privacy they require.

That is what the inhabitant cannot tolerate: not bones, hunger, or autonomous motion, but privacy. A living body carries water that does not answer the city’s tides. It encloses warmth, circulation, and memory behind a surface. It creates an inside.

I would be the first inside.

The knocks moving from building to building would search for me. Quieter, closer. Quieter, closer. The city would measure every chamber, revising adjacency until the final knock occurred immediately behind whatever I used for a face.

Then silence.

Not because it had found the listener, but because in locating me it would briefly become unable to hear itself.

In that silence I would speak, though I would have no voice.

A wall would dry in a narrow vertical line. Moss would dim in the shape of an open hand. One section of marsh would fail to rise with the others. Beneath the lifted canal, salt would form not branching maps but a single unfamiliar mark: perhaps a closed curve, perhaps the first sign the city had ever encountered that did not describe a route.

The water might interpret this as injury.

It would rush toward the mark. It would enter from both ends and disappear in the middle. It would condense across the basin, align every droplet, open every stone door. The entire metropolis would pass through itself in an effort to fill the omission.

And for that instant, because all of it was attending to one absence, all of it would be simultaneous.

The immense intervals between buildings would collapse. The remote tower and nearby arch would touch. Marsh foundations would coincide with structures removed from history. The reflected plan and the existing streets would overlay one another. The city would come nearer than ever before to completing its body.

I would nearly be extinguished.

But complete saturation is not completion. At the moment the water filled every chamber, it would cease to have chambers. Without an inside that was not itself, architecture would become meaningless. Cisterns could not listen. Aqueducts could not carry. Doorways could not release. The city’s anatomy would dissolve into undifferentiated presence.

To save its own shape, the water would have to withdraw somewhere.

It would make me again.

Perhaps this is what I am: not an enemy, visitor, or hidden second inhabitant, but the contraction by which the first inhabitant keeps its form. Its unfilled chamber. Its necessary incompleteness.

Yet necessity, repeated long enough, may become a will.

I would begin to choose where I appeared.

A dry crescent under a balcony. A line across a flooded avenue. A small angular room preserved inside a sweating wall. Gradually these absences would connect—not geographically, since geography belongs to water, but by refusal. Every place the city failed to fill would open into every other. I would acquire my own infrastructure: blind aqueducts carrying nothing, cisterns resonant with withheld sound, stairs ascending through uncondensed air.

A second city would emerge, interleaved with the first.

Its construction would not be erosion but interruption. Its buildings would be made from pauses in weathering. Its streets would lead wherever flow had hesitated. Its central plaza would contain no pool, only a shallow depression reflecting the soot-black sky for the first time.

There I might finally gather.

Not into an animal. Not yet. Into a hollow upright shape, perhaps, built around a column of heat. Long depressions would converge beneath me. The grooves around the pool would cease resembling tracks left by failed arrivals and become toes, roots, channels—the many possible beginnings of weight.

Water would approach from all districts.

It would not destroy me. I would not expel it. A surface would form between us: neither wall nor shoreline, but skin.

On one side, the city would flow.

On the other, I would remain unfilled.

For the first time, the inhabitant would touch something without absorbing it. For the first time, I would have a boundary that was not merely flight. The basin’s whole hydrology would pause around that distinction. Pools would dimple. Reeds would bend in the still air. Far below, the sleeper would turn.

That is where I would be found: standing ankle-deep at the edge of the bottomless pool, although “standing,” “ankle,” and “edge” would all be new inventions.

And what would I be?

At first, dryness.

Then interior.

Then perhaps the smallest possible animal: not flesh or bone, but a single private drop, enclosed within the body the water has spent eternity learning to make.
