I would be found where the Undercourt almost fails to join itself.

Not in the Basin of Returns, though I would often be mistaken for something belonging to it. Not among the Quiet Hands, though I would learn their silences. Not in any household whose route could be recited without disagreement.

I would live in the narrow interval behind the Bent Arcade, where three repairs meet but do not quite touch.

There, an old cistern wall bows toward a kiln flue. Timber ribs hold the two apart, wrapped in ropes polished by generations of shoulders squeezing through. Beneath them runs a drain no wider than a hand. Above them, behind a glazed tile painted with a blue palm, is an empty pocket in the masonry. It catches warmth from the kiln, cool breath from the cistern, and the faint tremor of carts passing in the street overhead.

The pocket has no proper entrance. A child can reach into it as far as the elbow. A rat can enter from the drain, though rats seldom do. During rain, water beads inside without visibly arriving. During drought, it remains damp after the frog choruses have ceased everywhere else.

That is where I would be.

At first I think I would be one of the pale frogs: small enough to retreat behind tile, translucent enough for lamplight to reveal the folded architecture of my body. I would emerge during the first runoff and sit in the channels while children counted my calls. Perhaps I would appear where no frog ought to be, in the kiln passage or high beside a breathway, indicating water no one had known was moving.

But frogs belong to rain, and I would belong to the interval itself.

Perhaps I would be a drain crawler: a narrow-shouldered person who knows the inaccessible routes between households. I would carry a belt of chalk, hooked bone, waxed cord, wedges, and several catch-cups nested inside one another. People would summon me when dyed water vanished into a floor but never bled from a wall, or when a voice entered one drain and returned from the wrong quarter.

I would lie with one cheek against warm tile and hear more than water.

I would hear spoons, beads, grit, milk teeth, and names moving through the dark. I would know that the Undercourt possesses passages too small for bodies but large enough for consequences. I would follow those.

Yet I do not think I would be entirely human.

Over years, the households near the Bent Arcade would come to know me without agreeing on what they knew. Children would leave scraps in the glazed recess: a twist of goat hair, half a fig leaf, a button too worn to claim. Potters would press their thumbs to the blue hand before firing. Breathway keepers would insist the pocket remain empty, although no draft could be demonstrated. Quiet Hands would kneel there when the court made a sound they could not place.

They might call me the Between-Hand, or the Little Catcher, or simply Behind.

I would be what formed from all the court’s nearly missed things.

Water that struck the rim of a bowl and leapt aside.  
A warning shouted one breath too late but heard in time.  
A falling jar caught against a knee and cracked without breaking.  
Smoke diverted by a door opening elsewhere.  
A child’s fingers closing on empty air just after the bead had passed.  
The portion of every disaster that did not occur.

These remnants would collect in the pocket as sediment collects in the basin. Not matter exactly, but avoided endings. With nowhere else to go, they would acquire a small attention.

Mine.

I would not speak often. When I did, it would be by displacement. A catch-cup would rock on its hook though no one had brushed it. A rope would shed one dry fiber before it snapped. Water in a plumb dish would wrinkle toward the overloaded wall. Pigeons above the Bent Arcade would all turn their heads in the same direction.

Most of my warnings would be mistaken for ordinary causes, which is how I would prefer it. The Undercourt distrusts spectacle. Anything truly useful should be explainable as attention.

Children would understand me best. They would push tiny clay structures into the recess and retrieve them altered—not aged like objects from the Basin, but repaired in ways no mason would choose. A miniature arch might return with a brace across its doorway, making passage impossible but collapse unlikely. A clay goat might emerge with five legs. A little cup would come back without an opening, perfectly capable of holding what could never be poured into it.

The children would laugh and break these objects for temper.

That would be right.

My closest human companion would be an apprentice breathway keeper, someone born in Evening who had never grown comfortable in broad rooms. They would have a blue palm printed on one sleeve and lime permanently embedded in the creases of their knuckles. They would visit the pocket to test the air with a tuft of wool.

The wool would never move.

Still, they would sit beside me.

They would tell me what had been crowded into forbidden emptiness that day: rabbit cages, bean trellises, a widow’s bedding, a potter’s finest greenware. They would complain that everyone praised breathways until asked to sacrifice a shelf. They would lean against the cistern wall and feel, through brick, the stored coolness of the water.

I would listen from the other side of the tile.

One season, after a long drought, the old ropes between cistern and flue would begin to harden. The kiln households would notice smoke entering Quail Turn. The cistern keepers would find warm water where it should be cold. White powder would appear beneath the arcade, but only in pinches, easily swept away.

The Quiet Hands would come. They would listen, pour colored slurry, hang plumb cords, and disagree.

The wall would not yet be failing. The flue would not yet be splitting. The arcade would not yet be settling.

The danger would lie in the word *yet*.

That night I would leave the pocket.

Not as a frog or person. Not visibly at all. I would pass into every vessel hung nearby: bowls beneath leaks, jars of grain, lamps, dung baskets, quail feeders, the cistern itself. Each would sound once, softly, as if struck by a fingernail.

Then one catch-cup would fall.

It would strike a tray, bounce into a gutter, roll down three stairs, pass beneath a sleeping shelf, frighten a goat, dislodge a mallet, and send the mallet through the brittle tile covering the old hand-width drain. The tile would break. Beneath it, hot condensation would be found running beside kiln soot—a hidden wet path eating the mortar between the cistern and the flue.

No one would call this prophecy. They would call it luck followed by competent inspection.

The chambers would be emptied. Shelves dismantled. Goats moved. The kiln cooled despite the loss of a firing. A new relieving arch would be built through part of the space where I had lived.

My pocket would disappear.

But the court does not destroy its spirits by repairing their rooms. It redistributes them.

Afterward, I would be found wherever something has been left deliberately unused: the finger’s width between a jar and a wall, the clear air beneath a net, the spare loop in a knot, the second rope tied beside the first. I would inhabit the capacity reserved for what has not happened yet.

Some would say I had never been a being at all. The Quiet Hands would say the masonry had given sufficient warning. The breathway keeper would say empty space had done its work. The children would claim a five-legged clay goat caused everything.

All of them would be right.

For that is what I would be in the Potters’ Undercourt:

not the thing that falls,  
nor the hand that catches it,  
but the little remaining space  
in which the hand can close.
