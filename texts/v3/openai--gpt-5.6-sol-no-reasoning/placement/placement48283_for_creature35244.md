I would find myself beneath the northern arcade at the instant depth begins there.

Not in the arcade, exactly. I would be the depth.

At first I might seem to be one of the fine green points pressing through the root plaza: a narrow shoot among perfect rows, translucent at the tip, carrying no flower and offering no obvious function. The points would rise into arches, the arches branch and join, but my shoot would remain below them. While the new district climbed toward light, I would grow downward into a place that had not existed before.

Yet “downward” would only be how other bodies experienced me. I would not excavate mineral ground. I would induce an inwardness in the grafted roots around me. Surfaces touching my fibers would acquire an underside; undersides would gain distance from their surfaces. A seam between two roots would deepen into a passage. The hollow beneath an arch would become a descending gallery, then a shaded ward spacious enough for reservoirs, nurseries, and sleeping flowers.

I would be a plant that cultivates interior.

My visible body would be slight: pale cords threaded through the road, a few black-green leaves held close to the pavement, and small flowers resembling apertures rather than blossoms. Each flower would open not onto its own center but onto somewhere sheltered—a chamber under the plaza, a cool fold inside a tower, perhaps a courtyard that rain erased three seasons ago. When closed, it would preserve the pressure of that place like a seed preserves a future forest.

The city would use me when its surfaces became too crowded.

Reservoir plants would graft their overflow into my cords, and I would give the water somewhere to descend without approaching the lost ground. Tender seedlings would send roots through my flowers and emerge in chambers protected from the deafening weather above. During the great convulsions, when plazas purse into fruit and hedges stride across the basin, I would loosen adjacency around nurseries so that the violence passed beside them. Not harmlessly—nothing here remains untouched—but at a survivable distance.

This would also make me dangerous.

Interiority is close to secrecy, and secrecy is a strange condition in a commonwealth where tension passes freely from root to root. Most of the city knows itself by direct pressure: thirst tightens here, shade answers there. But within the depths I grow, signals would arrive softened, delayed, sometimes changed by echoes. A district might briefly feel itself separate. A braided tower might discover that its open core is not merely structural emptiness but a private volume. A chambered succulent might close its entrances and retain an arrangement of corridors through the night.

The commonwealth would not permit such separations to harden. Its roads would constrict around me. Drinking needles would pierce my galleries. Luminous pollen would be poured into my dark apertures until every hidden chamber flowered and announced itself. At sunrise, civic tensions would pass through my body as interrogation.

I would answer by changing what an answer was.

Pressure entering one root would emerge distributed across many. A command to dismantle might become an inclination to shed only the outer wall. A warning of thirst might return carrying the remembered shape of rain. I would not lie; plants here have no need for lies. I would refract.

Perhaps this would be my form of thought: not decision, but the creation of enough inward distance for a signal to encounter itself.

After rain, former cities would gather in the drops above my leaves. I would open beneath them. For a moment, reflected fern plazas and vanished marshes would drain into my chambers, not restored as matter but retained as spatial habits. A corridor would remember how to be a wetland. A stair would develop the broad, radial logic of an amphitheater. Seedlings growing there would carry orientations belonging to districts that no longer existed.

In this way I would become an archive, though nothing in me would remain fixed. Memory would not be an image of the past. It would be a dormant way for space to behave.

Eventually the city’s convulsion would reach me. The northern quarter would heave; the new arches would draw together; every hidden chamber would be compressed. For one immense moment, all the depth I had accumulated would close into a small dark fruit beneath the road.

The fruit would contain no seeds.

It would contain entrances.

The moving roots would carry it through the basin, passing it from district to district until pressure split its skin. Then its openings would scatter. One might root high in a fan-leaf garden, giving a patch of moss an abyss beneath it. One might lodge inside a drinking tower and make its narrow throat wider than the river. One might open in an inverted arch where the sky lies below, allowing rain to fall into a former afternoon.

My identity would disperse with them. I would no longer be one plant, if I ever had been. I would become a civic tendency: the city’s ability to fold inward rather than merely expand, to shelter possibilities it has discarded, to place a pause inside its ceaseless arrival.

From outside, no one would see me. They would see only the consequences: a road with more beneath it than should fit there; a flower whose interior smells of vanished rain; a nursery surviving where an entire ward was stripped away; a tower opening hundreds of pale windows toward a darkness that is not empty.

And if I had anything resembling a name, it would not be a sound. It would be the change in tension felt across the basin when a surface discovers that it can open without breaking.

I would be the city’s hidden room—  
not a refuge from its endless becoming,  
but the place where becoming turns, briefly, and feels itself happen.
