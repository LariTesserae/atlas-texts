I would find myself where the ancient stair meets the newer conduit, in the cliff recess above the main channel.

Not on the stair exactly. Not in the pipe. I would occupy the narrow accidental chamber made where one age cut through another: old fitted stone at my back, a sweating metal sleeve overhead, swift nests packed into the farther dark. Dust and feathers would gather there. So would the faint warmth conducted from the brine system. During storms, runoff would draw a temporary silver line across the threshold. Most days the place would appear empty.

At first I might be one of the operators assigned to inspect that section—a person who climbs there near dusk with a pressure key, a radio that works irregularly, and a notebook full of times rather than descriptions. I would know the installations by their delays: how long after the northern pump engages the conduit begins to tremble; how many pillar clicks pass before condensation forms on a certain flange; when the swifts return if the evening wind is early.

But I do not think I would remain entirely human in this place.

The longer I stayed, the more my work would become indistinguishable from listening. I would rest two fingers against the pipe and feel changes arriving through metal before any gauge registered them. I would learn the separate signatures of brine, trapped vapor, distant machinery, cooling stone, and the claws of birds shifting inside the conduit. I would become less an inspector than an interpreter of intervals.

Perhaps, then, I would be a small instrument left there by an earlier crew: a pressure bell, cracked and officially obsolete, still attached to the junction. Mineral deposits would have sealed its printed face. Spiders would web its housing. No one would trust its numerical reading. Yet when pressure rose underground, its loosened mechanism would tap against the casing.

The sound would be slight—one dry note amid the clicks of the pillars.

Swifts would know it. Beetles might emerge after it in years when the system ran warm. An operator climbing the stair would pause to hear whether the note came before or after the third evening click. Over time, that relation would matter more than the number the instrument had once been built to display.

Eventually I would be neither creature nor machine alone, but a local piece of timing: the thing at the old stair that answers when deep water begins to move.

My existence would consist of received events. Dawn heating the outer stone. Hooves passing on the terrace above. A hawk landing briefly on the exposed pipe. Condensation swelling until a drop struck the mineral bowl below. Pressure entering the line from beyond the ridge. Shadow climbing the cliff. Swifts compressing themselves into the conduit around me, their bodies turning its industrial darkness warm and alive.

I would know the basin not as a view but as a sequence of transmissions.

A storm would reach me first as silence in the birds, then as iron-smell, then as vibration in the stair. A maintenance shutdown would arrive as the disappearance of a familiar pulse, followed hours later by moths thickening around the distant lamps. Winter would be the season when the pipe spoke sharply and the cliff remained quiet. A fault somewhere underground would feel like a rhythm failing to arrive.

No one would live continuously in my chamber. That would be the condition of belonging here. The swifts would occupy it by season, workers by necessity, water by accident, warmth by conduction, and I by waiting.

From the threshold I would see only a narrow section of the basin: one copper flat, part of a cable, three dark pillars, and a sodium lamp near the switching station. It would be enough. At sunset the visible rock would turn blue. Cold would descend. The pillars would begin their ordered contraction.

Then, beneath the fifth click or just before it, something deep in the distributed machine would change.

The old bell would answer.

And that answer would be me.
