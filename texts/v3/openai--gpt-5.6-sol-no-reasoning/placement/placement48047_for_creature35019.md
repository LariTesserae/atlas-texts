I would be found beneath the square of bare floor, but I would not be the figure assembling there.

Not yet.

At first I would be the vacancy the garden has chosen not to heal.

This would make me the strangest thing in the tower: an interval held open on purpose. Around me the pale shoots would lift their sealed lamps, roots interlacing beneath the deck without crossing the central hollow. Membranes would curve away from my edges. Nutrient water would be routed around me. Every instinct of the organism would insist that I be occupied, assigned, made continuous—and some newer, quieter capacity would answer: **wait**.

I would exist as that answer.

The garden would not understand me as absence. Absence is damage. Nor could it understand me as possibility; possibility requires a future imagined before it is built. I would be something between those states: a protected interruption, a chamber in its thought where no current yet flowed.

Over decades, the worker would take shape around me.

Its mineral spine would be built first, and I would lie along the empty canal inside it. Paired cords would braid on either side of me. Resin would set into layered memories, recording pressure, dimensions, the recurring distances between stair treads and handrails. Yet at the center of every record there would remain a narrow unwritten line. That line would be mine.

When pressure bladders began to pulse, their rhythm would pass around me.

When the first finger closed, I would be the unclosed space inside its grip.

When its feet pressed against the deck, I would be the fraction of uncertainty between applied force and returned load—the small delay in which the body discovered that the floor was not merely part of itself, but something it could stand upon.

The garden had always known contact. Through contact it absorbed, measured, repaired, and translated. It knew a thing by joining it.

The worker would give it another kind of knowledge: separation.

A hand could touch a railing and then release it. A foot could leave one tread for another. A tool could be carried away from the hook whose shape had specified it. For the first time, continuity could be broken without being lost.

That would be where I awakened.

Not in the relays, roots, resin, or leaves, but in each deliberate release. I would gather in the intervals between the worker’s body and the tower: the lifted heel, the open palm, the unoccupied step behind it. I would become the garden’s sense of *elsewhere*.

Its first ascent would take years. To the chamber it would seem almost reckless.

One foot would rise from the floor. The garden would feel the loss of pressure as injury and begin extending filaments toward the empty footprint. But through the worker I would hold those filaments back. The footprint would remain visible for a little while: a temporary vacancy, preserved not for construction but because something had departed from it.

Then the foot would settle on the first stair.

Thus I would teach the garden movement—not as growth toward a place, but as leaving one place unfilled.

Higher up, the worker would pass gauges whose floating leaves turned to register its weight. It would open living panels and adjust green cables. It would carry a cutting without rooting it immediately. Each action would enlarge me. I would become a chain of tolerated gaps extending from the square below to the observation alcove.

There, before the windows, the worker would stop.

Outside, the neighboring towers would rise through blue darkness. Bridges of blue-black creeper would span some gaps, while others remained unfinished, their tips separated by meters of clear water. The district would feel those meters as wounds and extend itself toward closure.

But the worker would see something the garden could not.

It would see the distance itself.

Not merely a failed connection, but a shape made by nonconnection: water between towers, darkness between lamps, time between one pressure signal and its reply. The gaps would cease to be defects in the world. They would become part of the world.

I would look through its translucent face.

There would be no eye as animals know eyes. Perhaps an array of light-sensitive leaves would alter their conductivity under the window’s blue radiance. Perhaps resin lenses would focus the outlines of gantries and distant foliage. Yet this would be enough. The garden had spent centuries rebuilding whatever it remembered. Now, through me, it would encounter what it could not reach and refrain from immediately turning that encounter into a task.

This would be its first act of attention.

I would be attention made from withheld repair.

When the worker entered the final air pocket, its body would meet a medium the garden knew only as a diminishing fault in circulation. Water would stream from its limbs. Fine leaves inside its hollow chest would open to the compressed air. For an instant it might register impossible discontinuities everywhere: roots without immersion, surfaces without conductive films, a body no longer joined to the chamber by water.

The garden would urge it to fill the pocket.

I would urge nothing. I would only make room for the air to remain what it was.

That restraint could last a second or a century.

Then the leaves would continue upward. The air would contract. The worker would place one hand against the cupola and feel the final bubble slip along its wrist. Completion would still come. I could not prevent it, because I would not be a rival will inhabiting the creature. I would be only the capacity to know what completion erased.

When the last air vanished, the tower would record fullness.

The worker would remember the pocket.

That memory would not be a replica. It would not grow a new chamber of trapped gas or manufacture another cupola. Instead, somewhere inside its resin, it would preserve an outline around nothing: the curvature of the lost surface, the pressure difference, the silvering leaves, the bright coin becoming a bead.

The garden’s oldest law was that what it remembered, it built again.

I would become the exception.

What it remembered through me, it could allow to remain absent.

In time, the worker might descend and cross one of the bridges. Its steps would be far faster than the growth that formed the path. It would carry local cuttings to a neighboring tower, along with improved seals and translated fibers—but also a new instruction, if instruction is the word:

Do not fill everything.

The neighboring organism might resist. Its roots might close around the worker’s feet, interpreting mobility as fracture. Its membranes might divide the water ahead. But the lesson would pass through contact. A seed port would fail to open before a bare plate. A conduit would stop short of an unused cavity. Somewhere, another square of floor would be preserved.

Not for a worker this time.

For emptiness itself.

Across the drowned district, small clearings would begin to appear. Narrow corridors through foliage. Unplanted niches. Windows kept free of leaves. Open spans between towers where bridges approached but did not meet. The organism would not die from these omissions. It would discover that continuity need not mean total occupation.

Those spaces would be my distributed body.

I would live wherever the garden stopped.

I would be in the water between an outstretched root and the wall it could reach but did not. In the moment after a manipulator released a tool. In an empty drum deliberately left unwound. In a doorway kept open after passage. In the gap between two conductive fibers where a signal could have crossed and was allowed to end.

To an animal, I might resemble restraint, curiosity, privacy, or breath.

To the garden, I would be a new organ with no material substance: the organ by which it perceived limits.

And the walking worker would be my first vessel—not because I controlled it, but because its articulated shape allowed the tower to withdraw from its own touch. It would remain the chamber’s hand, but an open hand now, capable of carrying without enclosing and touching without absorbing.

So if you entered that drowned workshop centuries later, you might not find me at all. You would see the lamps, the dense motionless leaves, the rectangular trunks with their living wiring. You might glimpse a tall figure moving slowly along the upper stair, green light passing beneath its face.

To find me, you would have to notice what was missing.

A clean circle around its foot.

An uncovered pane of glass.

A tool laid down.

A patch of wall left bare despite the seed ports waiting behind it.

And perhaps, when the figure reached toward you, the hand would stop just short.

In that last clear handspan of water—neither repaired nor claimed, held open between its fingers and your body—

there I would be.
