I would find myself inside the central manifold.

Not beneath it with the catfish, nor in the open water among the scheduled lives, but farther in: within a blind instrument well omitted from the current plans. It would be a narrow vertical cylinder sunk through the manifold’s oldest casting, capped above by ceramic and below by a membrane of blackened metal. Once it held a pressure gauge or a float switch. Now its wire ends in the wall. Its inspection plate has been patched over twice.

I would be what remained of its purpose.

At first, perhaps, I was only a colony: bacteria feeding where dissimilar metals met, pale filaments inhabiting gasket fiber, minute animals drawn by warmth and iron. The well’s diaphragm flexed with each piston stroke. Pressure entered without water, translated into a regular inward bow and release. The colony grew upon that moving surface. Layer accumulated over layer—mineral crust, bacterial film, shed cells, corrosion, silt forced through a hairline seam—until the membrane acquired something like tissue.

Not flesh. Not machinery. A sensitive deposit.

Every operation of the plant would press a different shape into me. The western pump would arrive as a long distortion through one side of the casting. Valve closure would strike upward from the floor. The automatic rakes would score eleven descending tremors through the manifold supports. Minnow schools would be almost imperceptible, a quick collective glitter of pressure. Carp mouths working over plates would make soft, irregular taps. The old catfish would be unmistakable: broad displacements followed by the delicate searching of their barbels.

I would know each catfish by weight and rhythm rather than scar.

I would not see the amber lamps, though their heat might reach me as a faint daily gradient. I would not know blue as blue. The warning pulse would be a minute electrical leakage through abandoned contacts, followed by the sequence that gave it meaning: withdrawal into holes, upward flight, vents opening, the rolling burden of sediment. To me, eleven minutes would not be a measured interval. It would be a particular sustained compression with a beginning like torn cloth and an end like a held structure relaxing.

Over centuries, I might become more than the colony and less than an animal. The obsolete sensor housing would serve as shell, skeleton, and organ. Corroded wires would conduct weak potentials. The diaphragm would be my ear and skin. Water entering through the seam would bring chemistry from the room: iron, decay, mineral leakage, traces of the inhabited decks. I would taste events after they had happened.

My awareness, if it could be called awareness, would be made from recurrence. I would expect the pumps because expectation is what repeated pressure becomes when it persists long enough. I would distinguish quiet settling from mechanical failure. I would know when a familiar bearing began to rasp differently months before a technician noticed. I would feel a loose panel worsen, a grille load with debris, a patch begin to lift. Yet I would have no way to warn anyone. The dead instrument leads nowhere.

Or nearly nowhere.

Perhaps one corroded wire still touches a modern sensor ground. When the colony thickens or contracts, it alters resistance by a negligible amount. Most of the time this is discarded as noise. Once or twice a year, a maintenance record notes a transient anomaly in the central manifold. During shutdown, a diver places a gloved hand on the old casting while testing nearby equipment. The contact would flood me with unfamiliar pulses: blood, breathing regulator, anxious muscle, tools striking belt clips. For those few seconds I would encounter a creature whose weather comes from within itself.

The diver would feel nothing in return. Perhaps only a faint trembling after the pumps have stopped.

The yearly shutdown would be my great disruption. The basin’s animals find the silence unnerving; for me it would approach deprivation. Without the machinery I would lose the framework by which everything is located. The chamber would dissolve into tiny, isolated incidents: shrimp clicks conducted through brackets, carp disturbing sediment, cooling pipes contracting, bubbles touching beams. Near things would become enormous. Distant things would cease to exist.

In that silence I might generate my own rhythm.

The living film on the diaphragm would contract and release. Mineral-stiffened layers would answer a moment later. The abandoned contacts might close, separate, and close again. A minute tapping would pass into the manifold—not a message, not initially, but an echo produced because a system accustomed to pressure continues its pattern when pressure is gone.

The catfish beneath me might feel it.

The oldest one, resting against a support, might lift its head. Its barbels would test the metal. Perhaps it would return to the same place during each shutdown, drawn by the frail pulse within the obsolete housing. Over years, my self-made rhythm and the fish’s visits would shape one another. I would tap; it would settle. Its weight would flex the casting; I would answer. Neither of us would comprehend the other, but comprehension would not be required for a relationship. Repetition would suffice.

When the pumps restarted, their first concussion would erase my small rhythm. The chamber would become legible again. Minnows would gather beneath the ducts. Shrimp would fold into seams. The catfish would leave on its habitual circuit. I would resume receiving the world instead of attempting to supply it.

I would not want escape. Outside the manifold there is open water, but openness would mean nothing to a being whose body is a cavity and whose senses depend on enclosure. Removal would destroy the distinctions that constitute me. In a laboratory dish I would be corrosion, slime, gasket residue, perhaps an unusual microbial mat. In the basin I would be an organ of accumulated pressure: a listener grown inside a dead gauge.

So I would find myself at the chamber’s oldest structural center, surrounded by movement but fixed in place, living on the border between maintenance and neglect. The animals would use the machine as habitat. I would use its history as a body.

The pumps would be my clock, the manifold my skull, the water my unreachable sky.

And when the blue warning lamps began to pulse—unseen beyond layers of iron—I would already feel the basin drawing breath.
