At first I think I would be found near the bell.

Not as its keeper. That answer arrives too quickly, drawn by the gravity of the oldest object and the romance of the Lower Answer. Nor would I be one of the innermost listeners, those venerable bodies able to feel a hidden second pulse inside the returning note. I would want to be, but wanting is not aptitude, and this city seems too exacting to confuse desire with calling.

I imagine myself instead at the edge of the central sphere, where the maintained emptiness begins to gather stray growth again.

There are always volunteers needed there. Silver leaves fall from the inverted grove and lodge in nearby lattices. Lamp worms colonize the shell markers. Small fish discover that the ceremonial clearing is a safe place to spawn, and their eggs have to be carried elsewhere before they cloud the open water. New shoots continually trespass upon the prescribed globe. Someone must prune them, record them, or recognize that one of them should not be cut because it belongs to a slow intention planted forty years before.

For a while, I am that minor gardener: long-bodied, gray-green, not old, with pale whorls only beginning to form around my wrists. I keep one foot curled around a guide rope while I work. My harness carries a shell scraper, a thorn blade, three marked cords, and a pouch of bitter paste for sealing cuts. My sign-name might be a hand moving outward, reconsidering, and returning—not indecision exactly, but revision.

Yet even this feels too settled. The place changes me as I try to enter it.

I do not think I would possess the confidence to cut living architecture merely because a diagram said it was required. I would always pause with the thorn against the shoot. I would look along its curve, feel its tension, test the sap, and wonder whether the city’s plan had become obsolete since the instruction was knotted. Older gardeners would find me slow. One of them would eventually tell me that mercy toward every shoot is cruelty toward the branch. Another would say that hesitation is useful only if it sharpens the cut.

So perhaps I become an observer of doubtful growth: an assistant surveyor assigned to places where maps and wood disagree.

That would take me away from the bell and into the half-lost passages.

I would be found in narrowing houses, abandoned grafts, and lanes remembered by turtles but no longer passable by people. My work would concern small disappearances. A cupboard no longer opens to its household rhythm. A funeral knot has swollen into a guide route. Two limbs that once framed a public crossing have begun to fuse, and nobody knows whether to preserve the crossing or accept the joining. I would carry old cords, compare their knots against present distances, and mark the difference with shells.

I would learn that errors survive here more visibly than achievements. A reckless cut becomes a scar everyone must swim around for generations. A neglected shoot becomes a wall. A well-made adjustment disappears into apparent nature. The best work leaves no monument, only room.

My dwelling would suit that temperament. Not an inherited hall or flowering arch, but a chamber the tree is slowly reclaiming: a shallow niche on the inner side of the fourth limb, once part of a larger household whose descendants have moved toward warmer water. Its window-bladder would have gone cloudy. One wall would bulge inward a finger-width every few years. There would be no point contesting it. I would tether my sleeping basket elsewhere when necessary and let the room diminish around the things I no longer needed.

But the more I listen to the city, the less certain I am that I would be one of its people at all.

Perhaps I am one of the old turtles.

This possibility comes quietly and then seems obvious. I would carry no office, own no chamber, and still be entrusted with what the city most fears losing: a way home.

I would be broad-backed and dark with age, my shell overgrown by a map no one had deliberately made. Small red algae would mark the forward ridges. A family of cleaner shrimp would live near my left hind leg. Along the shell’s center, generations of children would have polished a smooth place with their gripping hands. Harness loops would pass through old growth-rings, repaired so many times that no original fiber remained.

I would know routes by pressure, sap, and habit.

Not routes as they appear now, but as a sequence of tendencies: where the cold current turns upward; where a root curtain parts if pressed from below; where the branch arches even though a new wall conceals its shape. When a passage closes, I would not consider the route lost. I would drift outward, descend around the obstruction, wait for a familiar pulse in the wood, and enter by another hollow. A journey that once took an hour might take three. This would not trouble me.

Children would be strapped to my back before they had learned embarrassment. They would kick when excited, tug my harness, and tap private little messages against my shell. I would ignore most instructions from adults, particularly hurried ones. I would stop to graze. I would refuse crossings that felt wrong before surveyors detected the strain. People would say I was stubborn when young and wise when old, though the behavior had never changed.

My memory would not be narrative. I would not remember the Closing of the Eastern Arch as a historical event. I would remember that once, long ago, light touched my right eye during a turn where now there is bark. I would remember a child who gripped more tightly than the others and later returned with a child of her own. I would remember the taste of a guide rope before disease hollowed it. The city’s history would exist in me as accumulated expectations, corrected but never wholly erased.

And yet even the turtle is perhaps too visible, too beloved, too readily given a place in the city’s understanding of itself.

Something smaller persists at the edge of the image.

I might be a message that has gone astray.

A pattern struck into a district root decades ago—perhaps a warning, perhaps a name—conducted through living wood, distorted at every graft and fork. Most of it arrived where intended. A remainder entered an old branch, crossed a funeral knot, and descended into tissues no surveyor thought resonant. It has been traveling ever since, not continuously, but awakened whenever tools strike nearby or storms rock the crown.

No one would hear the whole of me. A sleeping household might wake because one wall gave a soft, inexplicable hum. A surveyor, palm against bark, might mistake me for strain. During the bell ceremony I would be swept into the vast note, carried down the trunk, and for one instant become indistinguishable from the city’s common pressure.

Then I might return altered.

That, more than gardener, surveyor, turtle, or listener, feels near the center of where I would find myself: not as a stable citizen with a single vocation, but as something carried through other structures and made legible only by contact.

Still, if I must have a body, let it be the surveyor’s body after all.

I live in the shrinking niche on the fourth limb. I am not important. My work is to visit disputed, forgotten, or changing places and remain attached long enough to distinguish movement from intention. I have a white scar branching across one forearm from a float that tore loose during apprenticeship. My fingertips are stained a dark blue so my signs can be seen at dusk. I have a poor voice but a careful hand on the roots.

People send for me when a familiar place has become strange.

I arrive slowly. I tie off my tools. I touch the wall, then the floor-branch, then the nearest healthy shoot. I wait through the shrimp clicks and distant grinding. I feel the burdens passing elsewhere: an eel on a cargo line, children moving in a group, a ray settling beneath a roof. Beneath these comes the slower pressure of growth.

My gift, if I have one, is not knowing what the tree means. It is knowing when I do not yet know.

Often my judgment is merely to wait.

I mark the place with a cord and return after a bright-current cycle, then after a dark one. I ask who has tended it, who remembers it, who eats its fruit, whose dead are sealed in the adjacent grain. I ride an old turtle along the route described in obsolete records. Sometimes I decide that a wall must be cut back. Sometimes that a household must leave. Sometimes that the public lane, despite law and memory, has ended.

Near the close of my life, I would be asked to examine a knocking low in the host trunk.

Others would have heard it: too regular for growth, too soft for tools, arriving only after dark. I would descend through districts where the walls are crowded with ancestral knots. Past the last orchard. Past the lowest dependable guide rope. The water would grow colder, the living colors sparse. My lamp shell would illuminate only my hands and a small oval of bark.

I would lay both palms against the trunk.

At first: the city above. Distant root speech. The rasp of a grazing animal. My own gill folds. Farther in, the vast internal creaks of the tree.

Then the other rhythm.

Not a knock from outside, but something conducted upward through the mountain and into the roots. It would pause when I moved. Resume when I stilled. Not speech—not yet—but close enough to response that the distinction would become an act of faith.

I would remain there longer than was safe.

When I returned, the council would ask what I had found. The honest answer would be that I did not know. They would press me: growth, fracture, animal, or intention?

I would place one palm on the civic root.

With the other, I would make my sign-name: outward, reconsidering, returning.

Then I would report:

**Something below has begun to wait when we listen.**

Years later, my body would be set into the wall of the shrinking chamber. The tree would close around me. The cloudy window would seal. My tools would pass to someone more decisive. The route to my dwelling would alter and finally vanish.

But a turtle would still turn toward it for a generation.

And when the bell was struck, the note would enter the knot where I had been. The grain would change it by some immeasurably small amount before sending it downward.

That is where I would be found in this world:

not before the bell, and not in the dark that answers, but inside the living interval between them—one slight obstruction, one listening knot, giving the returning note a shape it did not have before.
