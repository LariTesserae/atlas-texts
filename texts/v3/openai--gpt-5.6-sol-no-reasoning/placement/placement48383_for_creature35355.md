At first I think I am beneath the town.

Not the Under-Mason itself—if there is such a creature—but one of its rumored shapes: colorless, soft-bodied, moving by contractions through chalk pores too narrow for bone. I imagine the pressure of the whole basin translated through water: footsteps arriving as weather, mallet blows as thunder, the insertion of a test cord as a pale root descending from another world.

But that image gives me too much certainty. Whatever lives below does not know the town as the Settled know it. It would know only burdens, openings, bitter seepage, the tremor of new walls. It might alter their lives profoundly and never understand that lives are what it alters.

Then I think I am a listener.

I sleep lightly in an alcove beneath a stair, among inverted bowls of different thicknesses. I know their voices well enough to wake when one changes by less than a note. People send for me before they send for a plasterer. I kneel, put my cheek to the floor, and ask everyone to stop telling me what they think the sound means.

Yet that, too, feels too honored. A listener is called to uncertainty but expected to return with judgment. I would be less able to pronounce. I would hear too many possible causes in every knock: water, settling stone, beetles in a cistern wall, a neighbor shifting in sleep, the buried dead surrendering another fraction of their room.

I would find myself at an edge—not the outer bank, but one of the town’s many internal edges where categories leak into one another.

I would be the keeper of a passage that was once a room.

Long ago it sank along one side. The occupants moved upward and the doorway was half filled. Later, a roof was fitted over the remaining hollow and runoff was led through it. Now the place is part culvert, part archive, part crawlway. One wall belongs to the dead sealed beyond it. Another backs a hearth. A narrow clay pipe carries voices overhead during storms. Beneath the fitted floorstones, water runs strongly enough to be heard but not seen.

My work would be to keep that place from becoming only one thing.

If called a drain, someone might widen it and break into the old chamber. If called a tomb, someone might seal it and flood the hearth wall. If called a lane, people might wear through the capstones. So I would maintain its ambiguity precisely: scraping silt from the watercourse, renewing the thumbprints on the outer plaster, replacing cracked voice-pipe sections, warning children which stones may take weight.

I would know the names of people whose bodies no longer lie where their prints now appear. I would know that the wall has moved them outward through five coats of plaster. I would copy each print faithfully while knowing fidelity here does not mean preserving location. It means preserving passage.

I would belong to several falls badly and none completely.

The roof over my sleeping niche would feed one row, the culvert I tend would protect another, and the hearth against my wall would belong to a household with whom I have an old disagreement no one remembers clearly enough to resolve. They would send me hot bulbs in winter anyway. I would clear their foundation channel before my own because if theirs failed, mine would follow.

Children would consider me a minor authority because I possess keys, though the keys would be clay wedges and marked stones rather than metal. Adults would know I have almost no authority at all. I could advise that a passage remain open. I could not compel anyone unless water visibly began to rise.

I would make maps, but not maps that could be spread flat.

Fiber rots. Hide mildews. Fired tablets preserve only one arrangement, and this town betrays any record that pretends permanence. Instead I would keep a set of small clay pieces: ridges, pierced disks, scored slivers, black pebbles, chalk nodules. Laid on a floor, they would describe buried rooms and unseen channels. A pierced disk would mean that water passes beneath a sealed chamber. Two touching ridges would mean shared plaster but separate load. A black pebble might mark a clean cistern—or a place once clean, which is not the same thing.

Every season I would rearrange them.

People would come to ask where a former stair descended, whose grandmother sealed a room, whether an old outlet continues beneath a newer kiln. Sometimes my pieces would answer. Sometimes memory would run milky. Then we would open the wall carefully and discover that the town had retained a different truth.

I would not be native-born.

I think I came from drier country and arrived believing wetness was simple. Rain fell, water ran downhill, ground was either firm or mud. I carried some skill the town needed—perhaps I shaped thin ceramic tubes whose joints did not choke the flow, or had a patient hand for copying damaged impressions. I was sponsored reluctantly and made to sleep in an upper shed.

During my first night of hard rain, I would have tried to stop water crossing my threshold.

That is how I would learn.

I would press rags and clay into the gap, proud of my quickness, until someone tore them free and struck me—not from anger, but because the blocked seep had already begun entering the wall beside the hearth. Afterward, while we repaired the damage, no one would lecture me. They would simply make me follow the water: roof to jar, jar to channel, channel across my threshold, threshold to settling pit, pit to covered drain. Only when I found its outlet would they let me sleep.

Years later, I would still be known as the person who blocked a seep on the first night. The story would not disgrace me. It would establish scale. Everyone needs an early mistake small enough to survive.

My hand would appear twice in the central chamber.

I would have left once.

Perhaps I departed after a collapse killed a roof-sibling in a room I had declared sound. No one blamed me openly, which made remaining harder. I filled my handprint with pale silt, surrendered my claims, and crossed the sharp bank carrying almost nothing.

Outside, I discovered I had lost the ability to trust dry ground.

In other settlements people built walls without asking where displaced water would go. They called floors solid because they did not sound them. They shouted through private air as though voices left no residue. Their houses stood apart, and this seemed at first like freedom, then like an incomprehensible negligence. No one knew which roofs were upstream of blame.

So I returned.

I did not scrape the silt from my first hand. I pressed another beside it, higher and slightly overlapping someone else’s fingers. The first remains a record of departure. The second does not cancel it.

Now I am neither an elder nor young. I stoop from low vaults. My fingertips are flattened and always gray. I wear a band of beads stained in waters from different parts of town—not as ornament, but as a mnemonic. Chalk-white for the passage I keep. Green for the cistern that once poisoned its beetles and was saved. Orange for the kiln row. One smooth black stone for the roof-sibling whose chamber lies somewhere behind three generations of repair.

The pale dog often sleeps at my doorway.

People take this as evidence that my culvert is unsafe. I claim it is because the hearth wall is warm. Both may be true.

On the night the knocks come, I wake before the bowls answer. Not because I have a listener’s gift, but because the dog stands and puts one paw on my chest.

The sound enters from the outer bank.

Soft. Then nearer.

Not a beat, exactly. More like weight being accepted and passed on.

I light a fungus bowl. Blue light settles into the chalk dust on my hands. Around me the town wakes without raising its voice: sluice stones drawn loose, lids lifted, children moved from floors to shelves. Through the storm pipe someone asks whether I hear it. I answer once.

The knocks pass beneath the old passage I tend.

For a moment all my bowls speak together, each in its own pitch. Water running under the floor trembles. The thumbprints on the plaster seem to deepen as the light moves, hundreds of small concavities holding shadow.

I put my palm against the wall of the sealed room.

There is a knock within it—or beyond it, or below.

Then another.

I cannot tell whether the sequence is traveling toward the central chamber or whether the town, listening from many places, merely gives it that direction.

By dawn, nothing in my passage has cracked.

Elsewhere, a wall has opened behind a mushroom vault. Pale grit has risen through its foundation in a rounded heap. Beside it runs a new thread of clear water, no broader than string.

I arrive carrying my little clay map pieces.

The listeners are already arguing. Potters pinch the grit between their fingers. A cistern keeper has brought a scratched black beetle in a wet cup, though she will not release it yet. The pale dog refuses the threshold and watches the wall.

Someone asks me what used to be here.

I arrange the pieces on a slate: ridge, pierced disk, chalk nodule, two slivers crossing. Then I stop. The old configuration suggests a room beneath the vault, but the water is emerging where my memory places a solid partition.

I could insist the map is right.

Instead I pick up the partition and move it.

“There was a filled room here,” I say. “Or there is one now.”

It is not an answer, but it finds the beginning of an outlet.

We remove the stones one at a time.

Behind them is a narrow hollow none of us knew, lined with old plaster. On that plaster is a hand impression turned sideways, fingers pointing down. It may have slumped while wet. It may have been made that way deliberately. In the palm, a translucent frog clings motionless, its body thin enough that the old print shows through.

No one touches it.

The new spring runs beneath the hand, clear over white grit.

By evening we have not explained the chamber. We have, however, prevented it from flooding the mushroom vault. A temporary channel carries the water to a settling jar. The jar belongs to no fall yet. Its sluice has no household sign.

That unsettled interval would be where I most fully belong: after discovery, before ownership; when a thing is known to matter but has not yet been made singular by a name.

I would sit beside the jar through the first watch, listening for a change in its filling note. Above me, people would cross the roofs. Behind the wall, the frog might remain or vanish. Below, something would shift in the pale stone—or water alone would do what water does when given a new permission.

In the morning, others would arrive to assign duties and prohibitions. The spring would acquire neighbors. The town would close around it without sealing it away.

And I would add one more piece to my map:

not a symbol for water,

nor for a room,

nor for the Under-Mason,

but a small unmarked shard meaning only:

**Here, the arrangement answered back.**
