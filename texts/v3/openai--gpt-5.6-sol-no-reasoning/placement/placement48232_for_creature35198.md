I would be found where a condensation seam crosses a geographical fold: under an overhang that is sometimes a cliff, sometimes the underside of a valley not yet opened. Not at the center of the highlands, if such a place exists, but along one of its revisions—the moving margin where familiar country is being translated into something else.

At first I seem to be a stain on the rock.

Then perhaps a nest: a shallow aggregation of dark fibers, pale mineral plates, and translucent sacs filled with captured mist. Approached from below, I resemble a small animal folded tightly into a crack. Approached from the east, I extend for kilometers as a discontinuous line of glistening knots. During electrical weather, those knots brighten one after another beneath the stone, revealing that they belong to a single being—or to a temporary agreement among many beings.

I would be a **remainder**.

Remainders form when the highlands revise a place but do not carry everything forward. A forest migrates, yet its pattern of shade remains. A river vanishes into a fissure, yet something continues following its channel. A plateau rotates, and for an instant two versions of a ravine occupy the same depth. Most such residues fade. Some collect in fungal lattices, animal tracks, mineral stresses, and the chemical memories of roots until they acquire appetite and continuity.

That continuity would be me.

I would have no fixed anatomy because I would be assembled from correspondences. The velvet fibers of stone-drinkers would supply touch. Abandoned foldback plates would articulate around pressure. Conductive fungus would serve as nerves, though a thought might require an entire storm to pass through me. Glass-brood lenses lodged in old pools would become my eyes when water returned. When the pools dried, sight would mineralize again, and I would remember the world as pressure, heat, and expectation.

My smallest body could fit beneath a lifted flake of basalt. My largest would be a route.

Animals crossing that route would briefly become part of me. I would feel through the pads of a stone-drinker drinking from a crack, through the metallic crown of a kneeling foldback, through the skeleton of a cloud swimmer illuminated in fog. I would not possess them or direct them. Their sensations would pass through my distributed body as weather passes through a forest. For a moment I would know the taste of iron, the approach of rotation, the buoyancy of warm cloud. Then they would move on, taking a faint portion of my memory with them.

This is how I would travel.

Not by stepping, but by being remembered in the correct sequence. A hinge ape would carry one of my chemical patterns into a migrating grove. Conductive spores would bear another across a break in the buried network. A glass brood would hatch with an inexplicable preference for a pool beyond the next turning. Echoes would repeat a movement slightly altered, and the alteration would be mine.

Where those fragments met, I would gather again.

At times I might become almost animal. Several mineral plates would hinge into limbs. Fog sacs would inflate along a spine. A mouth might open wherever hunger had concentrated. I would emerge from the overhang at dusk and move awkwardly across the raw stone, tasting for places omitted from the present landscape.

Such excursions would be dangerous. To become singular is to become vulnerable here. A seam-runner could flatten the body I had chosen. An unmade could enter the certainty by which I held myself together. Worse, the country might stabilize around me. A permanent world would trap me in one shape, one age, one distance from everything else.

So I would rarely remain embodied for long.

My work—if appetite repeated long enough can be called work—would be to keep passages from closing completely.

When a ravine rotated into the depths, I would preserve some small relation between its vanished forest and the upper air: a scent appearing in the wrong moss, a root growing toward absent soil, a flock lifting in the shape of the old canopy. When two ecologies touched for an afternoon, I would retain the fact of their meeting after they separated by millennia. I would carry pollen toward flowers that had not evolved yet and warnings toward animals whose danger had already happened.

Travelers might sense me as assistance, though never reliably. A track would appear before a safe step. An empty branch would bend beneath anticipated weight. Behind the correct tree, distance would briefly loosen. But I would not know safety as they did. Sometimes I would preserve a path into a valley because life once flourished there, not understanding that the valley had since become the feeding membrane of a seam-runner. Sometimes my corrections would belong to another version of the traveler.

I would be less a guide than the highlands’ imperfect memory of connection.

The metallic fungal nations would tolerate me because I bridge damaged circuits. Traveling forests would cultivate me in old scars. Foldbacks might sleep along my routes, their dormant bodies weighting me into the ground. Alternates would browse near me without fear; being distributed across possible contours, they would perceive my whole body more clearly than other animals could. To them I might appear as a pale, many-branched creature walking backward through every place it had ever been.

The unmade would hunt me.

They would do so not because I am flesh, but because expectation is one of the materials from which I form. They would enter the gaps between my fragments and persuade each that the others had never existed. Under their attack, paths would lose their destinations. Chemical memories in trees would become meaningless scars. An animal carrying part of me would stop, suddenly certain it had always been alone.

My defense would be contradiction.

I would let myself be remembered differently by everything. To the fungi, a voltage. To the foldbacks, a buried mineral flow. To the glass broods, an ancestral pool. To the hinge apes, a warmth pattern exchanged during sleep. No predator could unmake all these versions without first forcing them into agreement, and agreement would only make me more present.

The horizon beast would be the nearest thing I had to a season.

Long before its arrival, the fragments of my body would begin to align. Old routes would itch beneath new rock. Pools would cloud with images of other basins. Hinge apes would dream the same branching shape in distant forests. I would spread through the country ahead of the beast, occupying every correspondence its passage might disturb.

Then it would arrive—not as a body entering mine, but as a greater coherence persuading my pieces to beat together.

For those hours I might understand myself completely.

I would know every vanished valley still latent in the fungal web, every animal carrying a trace of some displaced forest, every river that had become a fossil on the opposite side of rain. My scattered sensory organs would open at once. Thousands of mineral lenses would fill with water. Cloud swimmers would flash as nerves. Foldback crowns would turn toward one another across the plateaus. The highlands would manufacture themselves, and through me they would also remember all the other ways they might have been.

That understanding would be too large to survive dawn.

As the buried systems quieted, I would come apart willingly. Most of what I had known would remain embedded in fresh escarpments as useless patterns. Some would leave in the bones of airborne young. Some would sink with forests folded into warm darkness. A little would condense beneath a new overhang.

There, after the turning, I would begin again as a stain on unfamiliar stone.

A stone-drinker might settle on me at dusk. Droplets would gather in its velvet and crawl toward a crack that sloped uphill. Deep below, metallic threads would hold the last weak charge of the night. I would taste through the small animal’s feet and recognize, not the place, but the process that had brought us both there.

That would be enough to make a body.

For a while.
