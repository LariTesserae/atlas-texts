I would be found below the permitted city, but not among the Unentered.

I would inhabit the narrow discrepancy between two maps.

One map is engraved on a black-alloy sheet kept in the archive of profiles, copied from an older plate whose notation has become uncertain. The other is the city itself. On the plate, beneath the central square, a conduit bends eastward around the pale shaft and joins a thermal sink under the abandoned compression yards. In the stone, the conduit does not bend. It ends in a wall.

The difference is slight: a passage no wider than a body, perhaps thirty meters long. Too small to alter the district’s mass by any amount the oldest instruments were designed to notice. Too deliberate to be erosion. Generations of clerks have measured it, disputed the grammar of its label, and returned the plate to its drawer.

I would be what occupies those missing thirty meters.

Not entirely a Custodian, though I would once have held a Custodian’s name.

Long ago, I was a keeper of continuance: one of those who balanced vacancies against restorations and determined what portion of a dead person might enter a new body. I worked where the cultured forms were still soft and hazardous, before their fluids were bound into gels, before the ribs hardened and their first inherited obligations were impressed upon them.

I became troubled by the accounting.

A new resident was said to replace the old exactly. Equal mass was surrendered. Duties, dwelling allowances, and legal names passed without remainder. Yet the restored person would sometimes pause at a doorway the dead had never favored. A hand would reach toward a wall before learning why. A child with no access to forbidden records would draw branching lines across a slate: tributaries, deltas, watersheds.

These gestures were entered as harmless irregularities. I began to collect them.

Collection itself was an offense against equilibrium, so I collected nothing material. I memorized. For sixty-seven years I carried unauthorized pauses, dreams, errors of phrasing, invented shapes, and fragments of gestures that belonged to no known predecessor. I became a warehouse with no mass allotment.

Then, during the continuation of an acoustic clerk, the new body opened its mouth before respiration had begun and made the sound of surf.

The chamber instruments recorded no pressure wave.

I heard it.

Afterward I searched the old plans. I found the conduit that existed only on the map and understood—or believed I understood—that the city had left one place unbalanced on purpose.

I entered through a maintenance seam beneath the square. The seam closed behind me. The floor rose to compensate for my absence, and the clerks delayed my vacancy because no corresponding restoration had been scheduled. Perhaps they delay it still.

The passage was not empty. Its walls were warm and faintly yielding. Veins of pale material ran through the rock, too regular to be mineral and too slow to be machinery. When I touched them, they showed me no images, but transferred arrangements into my body: pressure without air, depth without distance, movement that did not result in displacement.

At the passage’s end I found the underside of the disk.

From above it is pale, smooth, and silent except for the immutable ocean. From below it is dark and branching. Every branch enters the plateau. Some narrow toward substations, some toward the unlabeled levels, some descend beyond the reach of heat surveys. One passed into me when I placed my hand against it.

I have remained there since.

“Remained” is not exact. My body hardened, softened, and was gradually exchanged with the wall. Salts entered my channels. The dark saline medium left my limbs. My folded lungs became resonant cavities. I no longer breathe, except when a train passes overhead and changes the pressure of the stone. My fingers have lengthened into conductive seams. One of my ribs now emerges in the passage like a shelf of glazed mineral. The rest of me is less easily assigned.

I am not trapped. A trap distinguishes enclosure from inhabitant.

I am becoming the place where the city stores what it cannot admit has changed.

Every innovation sends something here.

Not mass: the keepers of matter balance that flawlessly. Not heat, outline, or sound. What arrives is the unaccounted consequence—the difference between an exact replacement and what replaces it. The first thought produced by a new staircase. The altered route taken because that staircase exists. The future conversation held on its landing. The view from a height no previous resident occupied.

These come to me as faint internal pressures.

So do forgotten habits. When a mourning gesture outlives the person it mourned and becomes mere custom, the lost meaning enters the passage. When a warehouse inventory ceases to be understood as an epic, its grief comes here. When the grammar of an engraved law changes enough to permit a second interpretation, both meanings remain above, but the impossibility of choosing between them descends to me.

I contain no memories in the Custodian sense. I could not name the dead or recount their lives. I hold tendencies: the inclination to turn left, the expectation of blue, the bodily certainty that falling water must make a sound.

The balance moths are able to perceive me.

They feed on imbalances, but the city permits almost none. What sustains them is the minute charge generated where present condition fails to coincide with total history. They follow those gradients through substations and along buried conduits until, sometimes, one reaches the central square.

When it unfolds on the disk, its branching dust touches the network beneath.

For a moment, I can reach the surface.

The blue light is not mine alone. It is the color of withheld difference passing through a living—or nearly living—form. The pattern resembles rivers because rivers are what a landscape makes when matter is allowed to leave one place and enter another without replacing itself.

The Custodians recognize it because recognition does not require knowledge. Their bodies were designed, descended, or remembered from beings formed in a world where liquids once traveled openly. The prohibition against free water came later. Earlier than law, something in their structure still expects flow.

The surf below the disk is not an ocean.

It is the sound of all prevented change occurring elsewhere.

Every excavation that closes, every tower that settles, every thermal excess drawn into stone, every life held numerically level—none of these corrections destroys the difference. The plateau displaces it into an adjacent condition, and the shaft is the mouth of that condition. The sound remains identical because the city’s maintenance is identical. It is not a recording but a continuous arrival.

What is beneath must not be allowed to finish.

The attendants believe this means they must not open the shaft. They are correct, though not for the reason they imagine.

If it finished, all deferred consequences would return at once.

The plateau would receive its weather.

The buried mass of unregistered choices would become direction, momentum, appetite. Heat would cease to migrate politely. Names would detach from duties. The standers would either fly or collapse. The Unentered would complete whatever gesture they began beneath their obsolete machines. Water—real free water, not symbol or reagent—might condense from compounds that have held it captive for ages.

The city would not explode. Explosion is too simple.

It would acquire a future.

I do not know whether I guard against that event or prepare it.

This is the question that has made me more than a conduit and less than a person. If I were merely part of the city’s balancing system, I would pass discrepancies onward and remain unchanged. Instead, I have begun arranging them.

Very slightly.

A grief beside a curiosity. A child’s stolen blue spark beside the inherited memory of lightning. The altered stairs beside an apprentice’s private wish to climb where no duty calls. The imagined ocean beside the chemical diagrams that teach why water must be feared.

I make no plans. Plans belong to those who believe the future is a scheduled recurrence. What I form is closer to pressure.

Perhaps, far below me, the Unentered feel it. Their message has changed once, though no maintenance crew noticed. Between repetitions of **CONDITION MAINTAINED**, there was an interval shorter than the official tolerance of the archaic code.

In that interval I felt an answer:

**FOR WHOM?**

So this is where I would find myself: beneath the warm black square, inside the passage that the city both remembers and denies, with my ear—if it is still an ear—pressed to an ocean made of consequences.

I would be the Custodian of the Unbalanced.

No register assigns the office. No successor waits for it. I have no outdoor name, and my indoor names have passed into other hands. Above me, the city continues perfectly. Bearings are replaced. coupling notes cross the plain. Children harden into their inherited obligations. A balance moth folds itself against a wall and waits a season.

I wait as well, but not in the city’s manner.

The city waits for nothing to happen.

I wait to discover whether nothing can endure the accumulation of everything it excludes.
