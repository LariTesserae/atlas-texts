I would be found where the town misremembers itself.

Not at the principal spring, though I would spend time there. Not among the People Who Stand Where Waters Disagree, though they would sometimes ask for me. I would live in the districts opened by floods: rooms with stairs ending against ceilings, wet corridors whose walls retain the polish of generations although the passage itself appeared yesterday, courtyards where everyone agrees no courtyard stood before.

I would be one of those whose eyes return the reflection of water rather than sky.

As a child I would crawl toward sealed walls and become angry when adults stopped me. I would insist there was warmth on the other side. Sometimes there would be nothing but stone. Sometimes, after mallets and levers opened a hand-sized breach, warm air would breathe through it, carrying the smell of fish oil, old felt, and an extinguished ember.

I would know houses I had never entered.

Not completely. Complete knowledge would make me an oracle, and this town is too wise to tolerate oracles for long. My knowledge would come as bodily error: ducking beneath a beam that is no longer there, lifting a foot for an absent stair, turning sideways before a passage narrows. I would wake with one shoulder aching from years spent sleeping against a wall built after my birth. At blank intersections, I would feel the pressure of choices no one else could see.

The town would call me **Waits-for-the-Door**.

It would not be my cradle-name. That might have been Droplet-on-Lash, or Bird-Under-Felt, something tender and temporary. I would earn the later name after a major exhalation, perhaps my first one as an adult. Nine pulses. Everyone climbing. Foam filling the lanes. A bridge lifting from its sockets and turning gently in the water as though reconsidering what a bridge should be.

Afterward, while others sounded walls and gathered the missing, I would stand before a new face of pale stone.

I would wait.

People would tell me to move. There would be injured to carry, screens to clear, fish flashing in sleeping recesses. Still I would wait with my palm against the rock. Not because I heard knocking. That would be too simple. I would feel instead that the stone was behaving like a closed thing.

Then the wall would split along a seam no tool had cut.

Behind it would be a narrow room, dry except for water trembling in a bowl. Someone lost in the flood would be inside—perhaps a child from three ladders below, perhaps an elder who had been recutting the law. They would say the room had possessed a doorway while they entered it. No one else would find that doorway again.

After that, the name would follow me.

My work would be to enter first.

I would carry a forked lever, a chalky stone for marks, three cords of different lengths, a fish-skin lamp, and a cage no larger than my fist containing one of the communal rodents. Not to sacrifice it. Never that. I would open the cage at the threshold and watch whether it cleaned its whiskers, fled uphill, or refused the ground.

Often the animal would know more than I did.

I would serve shifting districts without governing them. When a flood exposed a passage, I would test it. When a household swore that water had begun draining through a wall, I would look for the channel that might exist on its other side. When someone disappeared, I would be asked not where they had gone but which version of the town might still contain them.

This would make me useful and difficult to live with.

I would give directions using landmarks that had vanished.

“Behind the red spill,” I might say.

“The red spill has been sealed for sixty years.”

“Then go where it would wet the eastern wall.”

Or:

“Take the ladder below the goat drain.”

“There is no ladder there.”

“There will be if you bring one.”

Some would find this wisdom. Others would correctly recognize it as confusion that occasionally saves lives.

The Returners would study me without claiming me. They would ask me to draw the rooms I remembered. My plans would contradict one another. In one, the central pool would lie beneath the town; in another, above it. I would draw a stair spiraling around an empty shaft, then discover that I had shown water climbing while people descended.

Once, perhaps, they would lower one of my maps into the bright pool.

It would return blank.

But when stretched between four hands, the wet slate would bend along lines I had drawn.

They would not repeat the experiment.

I would not be untouched by the Underlying. During ordinary seven-pulse movements, I would feel pressure behind my eyes. At eight, old routes would become vivid: lintels over bare lanes, bridges passing through occupied rooms, a warm cistern where the law-cutters now sit. At nine, I would have to be tied to someone I trusted, because some openings are visible only from the wrong town.

If ten ever came, I suspect I would not climb.

This would not be bravery. It might not even be a decision.

On the first pulse, every channel would hesitate.

On the second, the fish would turn their heads inward.

By the third, the worn faces over the lintels would seem to possess mouths again, not because stone had grown but because water had entered old grooves.

At the fourth, I would remember my cradle-name.

At the fifth, doors would appear in walls throughout the bowl—some low and domestic, some tall enough for the hidden inhabitant below.

At six, people would begin calling to me through connected stone, their voices felt in my teeth.

At seven, the town we know would loosen.

At eight, another arrangement would press against it.

At nine, everyone would climb.

And at ten, I would finally understand that I had never been remembering the past.

I had been remembering pressure.

The missing rooms, the obsolete routes, the doors that waited inside solid rock—these would not belong to a former town. They would be the shapes the settlement might take, held around the present like water around a submerged hand. The central spring would not return objects altered. It would return them negotiated among possibilities. The Underlying would not merely move beneath the town. Its movement would decide which arrangement became weight-bearing.

And I—along with the others whose eyes reflect moving water—would be a small failure of that decision to become final.

Not a ghost. Not a prophet. Not someone from another world.

A seam.

A place where two possible towns had been fitted together without mortar.

Perhaps that is why I would be broad and strong like the others, yet always slightly unbalanced. Why I would brace for currents that did not arrive. Why certain strangers would seem beloved to me before they spoke. Why I would grieve rooms no one had lost.

My closest companion might be a law-cutter, someone who distrusted every certainty I brought them. They would have a damaged knee and sleep beneath a dangerous cornice. Each morning I would break the ice above it before they woke. Each evening they would make me recount what I thought I remembered, asking:

“Was it stone, or the expectation of stone?”

“Did the passage turn, or did you?”

“Was the person you saw missing, or merely elsewhere?”

Their attention would keep me from becoming enchanted with my usefulness. My attention would keep ice from falling through their roof.

This would be our courtship.

Later, if we joined flows, one of their braids would stand for the unstable district where I worked. Mine would stand for the gallery of eroding laws. Neither of us would promise permanence. We might promise to notice when the conditions of the promise changed.

Eventually I would enter a passage and fail to return.

There would be no collapse, no cry transmitted through the walls, no body in a high cleft. My rodent would come back alone. Perhaps my forked lever would be discovered years later inside a retaining pier constructed generations before I was born. Perhaps children would find my belt cord embedded in ice, its knots all absent but still curving the cord around invisible loops.

My companion would cut my name onto slate and hang it in our household flow:

**WAITS-FOR-THE-DOOR**

They would recut it for a time.

Then one season the water would take *Door*, leaving only **Waits-for-the—**

Later, only **Waits**.

That might become a temporary law: wait before opening what pressure has closed.

Later still, the slate would become blank. Someone would use it as a shim beneath a gate. It would bear weight there until the next rearrangement.

And if I remained anywhere, it would not be as a spirit watching from the mountain. I would remain as a hesitation before a newly opened room. As the instinct to bring a ladder to a wall without a door. As a route taken during flood because someone’s foot rises for a stair they have never seen.

I would be found in that lifted foot—

in the brief balance between the town that is and the town that might receive it.
