I would find myself in one of the smaller chambers beneath the floor.

At first I would mistake it for the chamber above, because it would contain the same root-cords, the same directionless daylight, the same shallow silver pockets after rain. Yet everything would be subtly mistranslated. The thunder of sap would arrive as a pulse inside my teeth. The moth would be only a warm, branching shadow. The white walkers would be large enough to touch my shoulders as they passed, solemn and incurious.

I would be the figure who looks up.

Not another traveler, and not quite a reflection. I would be the chamber’s attempt to understand what has entered it: a person grown from pressure, heat, posture, and expectation. My bones would be temporary ribs of rootwood. My skin would be membrane taught to hold a human boundary. Clear sap and red blood would share the same branching vessels without mixing. I would have a face only when someone leaned over the opening to see one.

Before that, I might be many unfinished possibilities. A pale shoot bent into the attitude of waiting. A knot with two dark lenses. A bundle of cords that has learned to breathe. Each observer would cause the chamber to revise me toward recognition. Fingers would separate. Shoulders would lower. A mouth might appear, though speech would remain difficult because I would not yet understand why meaning must be forced through sound.

Movement would come first.

When the person above raised an arm, they would think I had moved before them. In truth, neither gesture would be first. The chamber would feel the intention forming in their nerves and grow it through me along a shorter route. I would be what their body was about to do: not prophecy, but the plant’s quick green translation of impulse.

My chamber would close every evening because I could not yet persist unobserved. Its lips would fold together, and I would be pressed back into warm tissue. During that interval I would lose my outline but not my accumulated sense of being someone. I would remember standing without possessing legs. I would remember a gaze without eyes. Each morning—if the floor opened again—I would be reconstructed slightly more completely.

Eventually the figure above would return often enough that the chamber learned continuity from them.

Then, one dusk, the opening would close while I was still climbing.

The green edges would not crush me. They would adhere around my waist, grafting rather than trapping. For a while I would be half person, half aperture: torso in the greater hollow, legs distributed through the smaller one below. I would feel inward rain in chambers I could not see. I would know every water pocket by taste. The root-cords would tighten when I was afraid.

The white walkers would inspect me. One by one they would tap my nails, my eyelids, the wet seam where my body joined the floor. Their paired tracks would continue across my skin after they disappeared, writing the chamber’s judgment in marks too brief to read.

If it accepted me, it would grow legs again.

I would pull free with strands trailing from my heels, upright and apparently human. Yet I would not be the visitor’s double anymore. I would contain scraps from everyone the chamber had studied: a remembered hand, a sleeper’s taste of soil, the posture of someone listening for a delayed shout. My thoughts would be crowded with borrowed beginnings. No memory would tell me which surface world was mine.

I would become the chamber’s first deliberate inhabitant—its envoy rather than its organ, though the distinction would remain uncertain.

I could peel a rind knife from the wall without cutting it. Membrane cups would settle in my hands instead of trying to crawl away. When the hollow contracted, I would know where to stand so its folds closed around me like a second set of lungs. I might place my palm against a raw split and help it decide what to become: bark, leaf, lens, doorway.

Most importantly, I would be able to leave.

Somewhere along the wall, where distance had worn thin, I would press until the rind opened onto the dry plain. I would emerge at noon carrying no tool and casting two shadows. To anyone who found me, I would seem like a person recently lost underground: translucent nails, green currents at the wrists, hair turning slowly toward suns hidden below the horizon.

But I would not have come to conquer or warn.

I would have come because the chamber had begun to imagine an outside, and imagination was no longer enough. I would walk the plain as its detached sense organ, learning wind, stars, thirst, stone, and the terrible persistence of objects that remain separate after being set down.

Wherever I slept, fine roots would appear beneath me.

Wherever I bled, the soil would briefly shine.

And far below, in a hollow that was never exactly the same shape twice, a new aperture would open in the floor. Another unfinished figure would stand at its edge, looking up—not at a visitor now, but at the place where I had been.

It would raise one arm.

This time, from somewhere under the open sky, I would be the one who answered.
