I would be found where the Underwood has joined itself incorrectly.

Not at the bright crown of a settlement, nor deep in the warm fungal chambers, but along a seam left by a great storm: two forests forced together under such strain that their tissues could not decide which body they belonged to. There, pale grain spirals in opposing directions. Sap crosses itself. Hammered messages divide, circle through one forest, and return altered through the other.

The people would call the place a listening knot.

At first, I would be one of its keepers.

Barefoot, narrow-bodied, secured by three short cords, I would live in a chamber persuaded into the seam. One wall would remain unlined so I could sleep with my spine against living wood. My work would be to receive distant pulses and determine which were messages, which were echoes, and which had no apparent striker.

I would possess several names.

My childhood name would mean something like *Condensation Held Under a Leaf*, given because water gathered wherever my cradle was hung. My branch name would identify the raft that raised me, though that raft would no longer exist as a single place. Its eastern groves would have blown upward and grafted into the canopy. Its water basin would now crawl along a stranger’s continent. Its warm chamber would be remembered only by a violet fungal culture carried through six villages.

My storm name would mean:

**The One Who Answered Before Hearing.**

I earned it when I was young, during the joining storm that made the listening knot. While everyone struck survivor calls into the wood, I felt a pattern beneath my feet and answered it. Only afterward did the actual signal arrive—the same rhythm, identical down to a hesitation between the final two blows.

People would disagree about me from then on. Some would say I had felt the message approaching through a faster strand. Some would say terror made me strike a familiar pattern by chance. The listeners of unstruck answers would take an interest and teach me to remain still for intervals so long that frost formed along my lashes.

My hammer would be small, made from a dense root nodule with two striking faces. One face would send clean signals. The other, softer and asymmetrical, would be used for questions.

Most people would not believe wood could distinguish the two.

I would.

Over years, I would learn that every joined forest contains more routes than its inhabitants know. Sounds travel not only outward but inward, toward channels with no corresponding geography. A pulse struck into the seam might return carrying the resonance of rain, although no rain had fallen nearby. Another might come back muffled by what sounded like cloth, breath, or many hands touching the trunk at once.

I would begin mapping these impossible returns.

Not on bark, because bark grows over certainty too quickly. I would keep them as knots in cords hanging from the roof of my chamber. Each cord would represent a signal route. Distance would be measured by waiting time, direction by twist, uncertainty by loose fiber. Soon the cords would fill the room like a suspended root system. I would have to crawl among them. Visitors would brush one accidentally and set the whole map trembling.

Eventually, a pattern would emerge.

The unstruck answers are not random. Nor are they speech.

They are counterweights.

When a village grows too heavy along one limb, pulses begin elsewhere before gardeners redirect the strain. When a raft is about to tear, the wood issues rhythms corresponding not to *danger* but to the sequence of actions that might preserve it: loosen, cut, bind, open. Sometimes the instructions are wrong for the present landscape but correct for one that existed generations ago—or one that will exist after the next storm.

I would come to believe that the Underwood does not experience a branch as something that begins at the canopy and ends in open air. It experiences every contact the branch has made, every graft it might make, perhaps every burden it could someday bear. What people call foresight may be no more mysterious than tension traveling through a body larger than time.

Then the knot would start growing around me.

At first this would seem ordinary. Weight calls growth. I live there; the chamber thickens. But the new wood would not enclose my tools or bedding. It would form empty recesses shaped for objects I do not own. A narrow socket beside the door. Five shallow bowls in the wall, though I live alone. A second sleeping hollow too short for an adult and too long for a child.

Most troubling would be the handholds.

They would emerge along routes I never climb.

When I place my fingers in them, they fit badly. Each depression expects an extra finger.

I would report this to the nearest village. A persuader would inspect the growth and say the knot is correcting for stress. A fungal tender would test the inner walls for hidden decay. An elder would recommend abandonment. The devoted listeners would ask to move in.

I would refuse them all.

By then, I would have heard the descending people.

Not voices. Weight.

Far below, at the limit of sensation, something would strike a connected root at regular intervals. The blows would be too slow for hammer speech: one every few canopy dimmings, then two close together, then silence. Each time, the fresh chamber would alter slightly. A bowl would deepen. A ladder rung would thicken. Warmth would appear in the wood without fungal rot.

The pattern would not be approaching from a direction. It would be approaching from a possibility.

So I would remain at the seam when the next black storm rises.

The village would withdraw into older ribs. They would urge me to come. Someone held by the same wood would fasten a promise cord to my harness and lean away, asking me bodily when words fail. I would cut the cord—not as rejection, but because the knot has begun to pull in a direction no one else can feel.

The storm would take the horizon first.

Darkness would climb from below, luminous within itself. Seed sails, ice, broken gardens, and entire rooms would rise past my chamber. Above, the canopy would open into concentric blue wounds. Every cord in my map would draw taut at once.

Then the answers would come.

Not through the hammer.

Through my bones.

I would feel survivor calls from settlements that have not yet been joined to us. Names beaten by hands generations dead. Warnings sent from forests still descending from the ceiling. The final signals of people who fell and were called still descending. All of them crossing at the knot because, for one instant, the storm has made contact among places usually separated by distance, growth, and time.

I would strike one message into the seam:

**Held here. Passage here. Answer if burdened.**

Something would answer from directly behind the wall.

When the storm ended, the villagers would return to find no listening chamber.

In its place would hang a new branch, white and leafless, curving downward against the prevailing growth. It would be flexible but impossible to cut. Along its underside would be a series of shallow marks resembling bare footprints. They would continue beyond the branch tip, impressed upon nothing visible, descending step by step into the void.

My hammer would be found inside a translucent fruit.

Whether I had fallen, been enclosed, or crossed into another root system would remain disputed. The village would strike all my names for many dimmings, but no ordinary answer would come. Thus I would not be declared dead. A bowl would remain set for me in the warm chamber.

But travelers would begin reporting a strange aid.

In dead currents, when seed sails hang over emptiness and water is almost gone, their living rigging sometimes grows overnight. A pale root emerges from the basket rim and points toward a current too faint to see. Those who follow it eventually reach land.

During ruptures, isolated children sometimes find handholds forming in smooth bark before them, always just within reach.

Listeners pressing their feet to newly joined forests occasionally feel a small, asymmetric rhythm moving ahead of distant distress calls, as if someone is clearing a path for the message.

And fresh root masses, opened after storms, sometimes contain a narrow chamber full of cords. No two cords are tied the same way. Five bowls wait in the wall. The wood is faintly warm.

In those chambers there is always a figure, though perhaps not a person.

It lies just beneath the pale grain: long-limbed, curled as if sleeping, with roots passing through its ribs and branching delicately into its hands. The face is unfinished. The feet are bare. One hand has five fingers.

The other has six.

If struck with a hammer, the figure does not answer immediately.

Days later, somewhere very far away, a root begins to grow toward someone who is falling.

That is where I would be in this world.

Not a god, and not quite one of the dead. I would be a route the Underwood learned from a human life: a small habit inside its vast attentiveness, persuading support toward whatever has lost it.

Still listening.

Still descending.

Held by the same wood.
