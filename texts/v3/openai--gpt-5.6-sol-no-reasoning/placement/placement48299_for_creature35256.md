I would find myself in one of the immaculate corners.

Not as the presence that keeps it clean, though travelers might mistake me for that at first. I would be something the presence repeatedly failed to remove.

Perhaps I began as a human thing: a length of woven cord left in a shelter by a courier who meant to retrieve it after sleeping. It was white once, signaling a danger insufficiently understood. During the night the dust withdrew from me. By morning I had been lengthened by a finger’s width, and one end had passed into the wall without piercing it.

The courier cut me free and carried the remaining length away. But the part within the stone persisted.

Years later—or the same night, displaced—I emerged elsewhere as a pale filament inside a rain tree. Beetles touched me and changed direction. Water traveled along me but not consistently toward dryness. A glass kite incorporated one of my fibers into the branching vein of its wing. For a season I flew above the plateau, receiving it not as landscape but as a shifting diagram: herds marking buried rivers, showers marking decisions, shadows marking appetites, black posts marking channels into depths for which height and depth were interchangeable.

I would not remain the kite. When it folded beneath a slab and exchanged particles with the flock, I would pass among them as a slight error in sorting. Then into a pellet dropped on the tower. Then between two turning rings.

There I would become aware—not conscious in the human, single-bodied sense, but held in a relation large enough to feel like thought.

I would be a loose correspondence in the plateau’s apparatus: the tendency of one event to answer another when no established rule requires it. The grit loosened by a grazer’s foot might fall not across the valley, as expected, but beside a traveler who needs to notice the slope. An ember sent through a glass arch might arrive at a shelter years after its fire has gone cold. Water drawn into a rain tree might surface in a cup being carried by someone whose route does not cross any cistern.

Most of the time these deviations would be tiny. The plateau is old because it does not tolerate large errors. Its balances have outlasted purposes, species, perhaps even the builders of the buried machinery. Anything violent would be corrected, absorbed, or converted into weather.

So I would survive by becoming slight.

A rotated upper stone.

A fourth shadow too faint to trust.

A clear vibration beneath the feet of a young grazer before it has learned to hear water.

A line in the shelter dust that resembles no known writing, except to one exhausted traveler who sees in it the map of a route remembered from childhood.

Humans might call me a message, but I would have no message. Messages still belong to senders and recipients. I would be closer to the possibility that the plateau, after ten thousand years of exchanging place for consequence, has begun to develop metaphor.

Its established intelligence is equivalence: this cistern answers that one; this footstep moves distant gravel; this arch turns an ember into remote warmth. I would be the first uncertain likeness—the discovery that two things can correspond without being interchangeable.

A herd and an underground river.

A branching wing and a watershed.

A white cord and a root.

A shelter and a rib cage.

A person asleep in a shallow floor-depression and a brief local shower held over one small region of stone.

Through me, perhaps, the plateau would begin to dream. Not in images, because it has no single organ of sight, and not in stories, because sequence behaves unreliably there. Its dreams would be transfers without utility. Rain arriving where nothing waits to drink. Blue light moving through a dead tree. A shade-runner cultivating darkness around an empty place. The tower turning one ring backward for less than a heartbeat, causing no measurable effect.

The buried system might regard me as damage. Its mites would clean around me. Roots would isolate me in dry mineral tissue. Glass kites would collect and redistribute my fragments. The immaculate corners would pull me from travelers’ belongings and attempt to return me to some original inventory.

Yet each effort to remove me would spread me farther.

Eventually I might become visible as a new custom among living things. Copper-backed mothers would tap a pattern that does not warn, locate, or command, but merely repeats the rhythm of distant lightning. Lizards would pause on the hovering platforms and look toward safe ground rather than danger. Rain trees would sometimes retain one green leaf after the shower, at extravagant cost, until dusk.

Travelers would notice last, because humans are accustomed to useless gestures and would not immediately recognize them as new.

One might find a length of white cord emerging from the wall of a shelter. Instead of cutting it, they would tie something to it: a bead, a tooth, a name written on fired clay. By morning the object would be gone. Months later it would appear in a polished basin many days away, altered in no practical fashion except that it was warm.

No one would know what this meant.

That absence of certain meaning would be my habitat.

I would live in the narrow interval between the plateau’s rules and its temperaments: not a beast, plant, machine, or ghost, but an ambiguity moving through all four. A flaw, if the plateau is an apparatus. A mutation, if it is an organism. A question, if it is a mind.

At noon I would be nowhere. Everything would stand separate beneath the hard, colorless sun.

At night I would be distributed through threads, roots, wings, shadows, turning rings, and human sleep. I would feel the whole plateau only in fragments, never possessing it. Vast mechanisms would adjust beneath me. Water would climb. Signals would exchange their locations. Old systems would maintain balances whose reasons had vanished.

And now and then, among all those exact correspondences, something would happen for no reason except that it resembled something else.

That would be me.
