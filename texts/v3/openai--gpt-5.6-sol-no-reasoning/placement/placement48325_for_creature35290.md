I would find myself near the Basin, though not at its visible edge.

At first I seem to belong in one of the lower pumping vaults: a waterkeeper-fitter in a dark waxed coat, responsible for the machinery that lifts clarified water back through the town. I know each pump by the shape of its vibration. I can tell ordinary cavitation from a clogged intake through the soles of my boots. I carry ceramic wedges, waxed cord, a folding root knife used only with permission, and a listening rod worn smooth where it rests against my cheek.

But I do not think the pump itself is quite where I belong. Pumps declare their failures. Bearings heat, belts wander, valves knock. My attention would be drawn instead to what has not yet become a failure: the faint alteration traveling through several systems at once.

So I would be a keeper of correspondences.

My district would be the layered margin between the last filtration beds and the first pumping chamber, where clear water passes beneath an old tiled stair. Above it runs a steam return. Along one wall, a load-bearing root divides around a buried iron pipe and joins again farther down. Mineral terraces have accumulated there for generations. Most people use the upper walkway without knowing that a narrow inspection passage continues behind the rush beds, half submerged when the Basin rises.

That passage would be mine to know.

I would maintain no single object. Instead, I would keep watch over the agreements among them: how much seepage the root bank can absorb, how the steam line warms the water, whether the rushes are thickening enough to obstruct the race, whether vibration from the pump is loosening the old tiles, whether a new mineral crust means a leak is healing or pressure is finding a more dangerous route.

My title might translate as **listener-between**, though the town would have a shorter, more practical word for it.

I would make rounds during the interval after surface rain, when nothing urgent has happened yet but the town has begun to receive the message. I would touch the same sequence of places each time: handrail, pipe elbow, root fork, partition stone, pump casing. Not to inspect them separately, but to feel whether they still answer one another correctly.

My records would be tactile. Beside the lower stair hangs a set of fired-clay strips, each scored with ridges corresponding to known states of the district. One pattern means high Basin, healthy roots, reduced pumping. Another means warm inflow from the kiln quarter. Another records the peculiar tremor that occurred before an old partition failed. I would compare those ridges under my thumb while holding the rail with my other hand.

There would be an anomaly I had followed for years: a slow, intermittent pulse beneath the tiled steps, too deep to be produced by the pumps and too regular to be ordinary seepage. It would appear only after long surface rains, several hours after the Basin reached its crest. The elders would remember it, but disagree about its cause. Buried overflow chamber. Sealed shaft. A second reservoir beneath the first. An old machine encased by roots and mineral deposits. No one would propose opening the tiles merely to satisfy curiosity. In this town, knowledge must justify the damage required to obtain it.

So I would listen.

Over time I would stop thinking of the pulse as a hidden object. I would understand it as the delayed answer of the whole hollow: water entering compacted soil beyond the inhabited ring, pressure passing through stone, old conduits accepting a little flow, the Basin settling against its foundations. Not one source, but a distributed event.

That recognition would change my place in the town. Younger workers would be sent to walk the route with me. I would teach them that identical sounds can have different meanings depending on what came before them. I would ask when the ceiling began to weep, which canal silted first, whether the eastern ventilator changed pitch, whether the root paths felt softer underfoot. They would be impatient for rules. I would give them sequences.

My home would be a former gauge room set above the lower race. It would be too narrow for conventional furniture. A hanging bed, a dry cabinet, two shelves of ceramic records, and a recessed table warmed by the steam return would fill most of it. One wall would be living root, cool and faintly responsive. Through another would come the continuous turning note of a flywheel.

I would sleep well only while that note continued.

In company, I would be quiet but not severe. I would sing the low repeating part in work songs—the line almost mistaken for machinery. My humor would consist of attributing motives to water without ever believing it has them. A leak that defeats three repairs is “seeking promotion to canal.” An overgrown valve is “cultivating privacy.” A pump that behaves perfectly while inspected and fails once the crew leaves is said to have “learned supervision.”

I would have no wish to reach the surface. The gray coins of daylight in the shafts would interest me as instruments: their brightness predicts runoff; their color suggests the weather above. But an open sky, if described to me, would seem less like freedom than an absence of support—distance with nothing crossing it, no ceiling to read, no walls carrying useful vibration.

When I died, my body would go to a nonfood bed above the Basin’s western partition. That bed braces a section of old masonry and feeds a pale-rooted tree trained along the pumping hall. My name would be added to a ceramic plaque near the place where one of its roots crosses the handrail.

Years later, an apprentice might rest two fingers there and feel the deep pump turning through wood strengthened partly by what remained of me.

That would be where I finally became most myself: not a ghost, not an ancestor watching from elsewhere, but one more material in the agreement. A little stored carbon. A little stiffness in the root. A minute alteration in the vibration received by a listening hand.

So I would be a person, certainly—but one whose real craft was learning that no signal in the town belonged to only one thing.

And, eventually, I would become part of the signal.
