I would be found where the city sends what it cannot safely keep.

Not within the bitter garden itself, though strangers might assume so. I would live in the last occupied threshold before it: a narrow chamber behind the woven warning cords, where the paving rises in smooth humps above roots and the air leaves a metallic sweetness on the inner lid of the eye.

At first I imagine myself as a wall-hearer, old enough that sight has become incidental. Both palms rest against saltstone. I spend whole heat cycles learning whether a tremor is root, pipe, fracture, or footstep. But that station is too settled. The image shifts downward, toward the channels no one wants named.

I am younger than that, though already stiffening at the fingers. A keeper of refusals. A tender of unwanted flow.

My work begins where ordinary pipe-tenders’ work ends. When a seep cannot be purified, burned, eaten, sealed into wax, or diverted through an inhabited garden, it is brought to me. I listen to it before granting it passage. Clear spirits chatter sharply in ceramic. Black oils announce themselves as slow, almost affectionate pressure. Some flows make no sound until they touch a root, and then the whole wall begins ticking.

I decide which capillary cords to open into the bitter garden and how far. Too little, and poison backs into the district. Too much, and the garden grows ambitious. Its membranes creep toward warm foundations. Its hollow crowns emerge through floors and begin their thin screaming beneath sleeping rooms.

Nothing may be stopped entirely. So I practice the dangerous art of allowing.

My chamber has three doors, although only one leads to a street. The second opens onto a service ledge above the garden. The third is not a door but an old fracture packed with layered fiber, porous enough to breathe. My sleeping mat hangs from the ceiling because the floor belongs to pipes. At night they warm the underside of the room, and my suspended tools knock softly together: saltglass blades, mineral masks, test tiles, a copper needle worn nearly through, and a row of little resonance jars.

I rarely sleep for long. Bitter gardens do not keep civic hours.

People come to me when their walls begin dreaming sideways. They stand outside the warning cords and shout their symptoms: sweetness near a kiln, golden blisters on a stair, a child waking with violet powder on their hands, a pipe whose pitch has deepened though its flow has not increased. I strike the lintel to show that I have heard. Sometimes I go with them. Sometimes I tell them to abandon the room before sunset. Sometimes I ask them to sing a work rhythm into the wall while I listen from below.

My close name is **Serr**, a soft name scarcely more than two surfaces rubbing.

My market name is **Avarai**, made to carry through vane-groves.

My bridge call is **Ket**—one hard syllable, impossible to mistake for splitting salt because it rises at the end. I chose it as a child. Or perhaps the bridge judges chose it for me after I kept answering to every crack.

I belong to several households, but none entirely.

The threshold household shares my visible flame when I sterilize tools. The lower pipe clan claims me because I maintain their overflow. A family three levels above includes me in its migration because, years ago, I sat through a wall’s full speaking cycle beside their eldest child. The bitter garden also has a claim, though no law acknowledges it. Its roots know the pressure of my steps. One membrane opens when I strike the service rail three times. I tell others this is mechanical. I am not certain.

My clothing is layered and ugly even by the city’s standards. Veil cloth patched with black vane-fiber. A wax-stiff mantle scored by fumes. Around one wrist I wear a narrow strip from a shade cloth that once hung across my birth room, before the room became a ventilation court. It has outlasted the wall, the household that occupied it, and two of my names.

My skin bears pale branching scars along the left arm. Once, while clearing a cord, I opened a channel that had gone silent. The spirit inside flashed into vapor—not flame, but heat so sudden that my inner wax migrated toward the surface. The healers cooled me by degrees while three people beat my bridge call on the floor to keep me responsive. For years afterward, that arm sensed pressure before the rest of me did. Now it is useful. I lay it against uncertain pipes and feel disturbances as warmth moving through old pain.

The city does not consider me a seer. I insist on that.

Still, I enter the garden roped to another tender and return with phrases.

“The west root is chewing its own shadow.”

“The seep has become impatient with height.”

“There is a second throat behind the wall.”

Such language embarrasses me outside the warning cords. Inside, exact words become difficult. The fumes do not show futures; they loosen the borders between senses. Pressure acquires color. Heat leans in particular directions. A hidden pipe may taste angular. When I return, masons ask what I saw, and I translate the experience into something that hands can do.

Once I said, “The garden is listening upward.”

They removed three paving courses and found a crown growing along the underside of a sleeping niche, its hollow stem positioned to vent into an infant sling.

After that, people began repeating my metaphors more respectfully than I wished.

I distrust revelations. I trust recurrence.

If a vision is true, a sounding tile must answer it. A test cord must discolor. A second listener must hear the same obstruction from another wall. Anything that exists only inside one body is not yet civic knowledge.

Perhaps this is why I am stationed at the bitter threshold. The garden produces meanings too quickly; the city accepts them only after they have survived echo, test, and labor. I stand between those speeds.

Children are fascinated by me until they smell my clothes. They dare one another to touch the warning cords, then flee when I strike the lintel. Sometimes I invite them back and teach them to distinguish three harmless garden cries from the one that means a crown is overpressured. They become solemn. They place their broad little hands against the floor. They are better listeners than adults because they have not yet decided what a wall ought to say.

I keep a small illegal collection—not forbidden by law, precisely, but difficult to justify. Slivers of discharged root, warped test tablets, brittle membranes that retained unusual shapes after cooling. I hang them where rising air makes them touch. Together they produce an irregular ticking.

For years I thought I was preserving specimens.

Then an elder visited, leaned her jaw against my wall, and told me I had made a history.

The pieces tick differently according to temperature. At sunset, as cold air begins descending, they sound in sequence: first the membrane from the western spill, then the root that lifted the kiln court, then a wax-shell fragment from the season when half the district abandoned its lower rooms. Their rhythm is the district’s past translated into contraction.

Now I copy nothing from them. To write it down would make it smaller.

My deepest fear is not poisoning or fire. It is an inaudible failure: a flow moving without vibration, a root growing through softened mortar without knocking, a fracture opening so gradually that the city adapts to its pitch and forgets it is changing.

So each cycle I perform a small discourtesy.

When everyone strikes the walls at the hottest hour, I wait one breath after the district’s final blow. Then I strike once more.

The delayed knock passes into the garden and returns altered by its hidden chambers. Some complain that I disrupt the pattern. Yet that lone answer lets me hear what the communal sounding conceals. The city hears itself as a whole; I listen for what refuses to join.

At sunset I climb with the others, though never far from the warning arch. My ropes and masks travel with me. The copper light enters. For a few minutes the garden’s uppermost crowns cast long, intricate shadows through the cords.

Most citizens turn away from them.

I watch.

The shadows show forms that fumes and red lamps conceal: branching vents inside translucent sacs, roots crossing like fingers, thin new growth approaching masonry. In that brief light, danger becomes legible. Beauty does too, though I would not say so in council.

Then the seam narrows and the shadows dissolve.

Before sleeping, I place one palm against the inhabited wall and the other against the wall of the bitter garden. On one side: footsteps, pipes, migrating households, the shifting weight of bridges. On the other: toxic roots steadily making shelter from what would otherwise kill us.

I do not guard the city from the garden.

I keep the passage between them open, narrow, and audible.
