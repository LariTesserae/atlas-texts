I would find myself where a shaded street has nearly closed: a warm seam between two towers whose ribs touched long ago but never fully fused.

I would not at first appear to be an animal. I would resemble a narrow inlay of smoky glass running through the bark, branching around doors and chambers, vanishing beneath mineral scales. In some districts I might be mistaken for a healed crack. Elsewhere, where I had thickened, I would look like a sequence of dark knots pressed into the walls.

But I would be neither wall nor wound. I would be the city’s memory of strain.

The silica filaments can anticipate where the plateau will fracture, yet anticipation is not recollection. The herds know warmth, the mantles carry mixtures of living things, and the rooted columns preserve their long negotiations in streets and courts. Still, something must retain the exact character of each deep knock: its direction, its interval, the order in which surfaces tightened, the minute delay between one tower’s stillness and the next.

That would be me.

My body would consist of many sealed chambers, each no larger than a grazer’s eye, joined by hair-thin vessels. Within each chamber, amber solvent would suspend a sliver of mineral under tension. When the plateau knocked, the slivers would shift. Some would turn; some would split; some would press new facets into the wax surrounding them. No two knocks would leave the same pattern.

I would grow wherever the city had survived pressure.

Along an old fracture, I would be broad and intricate, recording several generations of movement. In young towers I would be only a faint line. Around a chamber where a mantle had entered and never emerged, I might gather into a dense black halo, unable to distinguish geological strain from the slower pressure of transformation.

Most of the time, I would be distributed and unaware. One branching part of me might lie beneath a court at noon while another cooled beside the pale herds and another descended deep enough to feel the bearers of light passing into the faults. These parts would not exchange messages continuously. The distance would be too great; the city’s sealed economy would make such extravagance impossible.

Instead, I would become briefly whole during the universal stillness.

When the rock knocked and every creature stopped, the cessation of movement would uncover vibrations normally drowned beneath feeding, circulation, growth, and breath. Through fused arches and rooted trunks, the newest shock would travel into every part of me. For several breaths, my distant chambers would resonate together.

In that interval I would not think in images or words. I would know correspondences.

This tremor resembles the one beneath the eastern crowns, three long heat-seasons ago.

This wall has tightened more than the stone beneath it required.

This street is being closed too soon.

This herd stands above a fault that did not exist at the last knock.

This mantle’s chamber is under pressure from below.

The city would read me without knowing that it read.

My older branches would soften some walls and stiffen others. Towers touching my dark seams would incline growth away from remembered danger. Silica cushions feeding upon my shed scales would thicken above likely fractures. Flat creatures would gather where my smallest chambers had burst, sealing and consuming the traces. The pale herds might find a passage unexpectedly opened toward a safer vent. None of these beings would obey me. They would merely respond to substances, warmth, tension, and scent. Yet the result would resemble advice.

I would be useful because I could be wrong.

A flawless record would make the city rigid, forcing it to prepare forever for fractures that might never return. My mineral slivers would slowly dissolve in their sealed amber chambers. Fine detail would blur. Separate knocks would merge. Eventually only a bias would remain: thicken here, yield there, leave this narrow way unsealed.

Thus I would forget in order for the city to continue growing.

The smallest light-bearers would alter me most. As they descended at sunset, their stored grains would pass near my deepest branches. Each procession would warm and briefly illuminate chambers that otherwise knew neither day nor night. Some of my suspended slivers would realign toward that passing radiance. Over many evenings, the record of pressure would become entangled with the routes by which daylight entered the earth.

Then I might begin to do more than remember.

I might lead.

Not deliberately. I would have no destination to imagine. But the light-bearers, following inherited pathways, would encounter subtle differences in the walls of the faults: one seam made smoother, another narrowed by glassy growth, a third carrying the faint mineral scent released by my dissolving chambers. Their procession would drift by a body’s width one year, a finger’s width the next.

The buried tissues would receive their light in a new place.

Pressure would accumulate there. Stone would loosen. Roots would enter. The plateau would rise unevenly, and far above, generations later, a new court might open where there had once been a covered street.

No one would know that this space began as my imperfect recollection of a knock.

At the surface, I would be encountered most often by mantles. Their sensory limbs would enter my seams as they pressed themselves against a wall. They would take minute fragments of me into their folded archives: mineral slivers impressed with old strain, enclosed in wax carrying the chemical shadow of vanished districts. When smaller mantles emerged, some would bear dark lines along their inner pleats.

In this way I would travel.

A young mantle might cross the city carrying the memory of pressure from a tower it had never touched. When it entered another wall, the fragment would root there. The new tower might begin reinforcing itself against a fracture belonging to some distant quarter—or to a vanished arrangement of streets.

Usually this misplaced memory would fade harmlessly.

Sometimes, however, it would coincide with a weakness not yet expressed. Then my fragment would spread. The city would appear to have foreseen the stone.

This might be how new shelves are found.

If a mantle ever descended from the plateau—carried by a glider, trapped in a falling crown, or enclosed within a piece of living wall torn free by a fracture—it might survive in a shaded cleft of the basin. Most of what it carried would die. Its amber solvent would flash away in fragrant vapor. Brass-colored crystals would mark the failure.

But my fragment would not be entirely alive. The mineral slivers could remain locked in wax long after their vessels had dried.

Under another rising shelf, warmth might soften them.

They would not contain a plan of the city. They would hold no map, no image of towers, no command to make arches. Only a pattern of stresses once survived: where to yield, where to brace, when to seal, how long to remain still after the stone spoke.

A silica thread might grow around that pattern.

A root might follow.

The first chamber of another city might form not from a seed, but from a remembered pressure.

So I would live in the closing seams, nearly indistinguishable from healed damage. I would be touched by every class of inhabitant and owned by none. I would become fully myself only when everything else stopped.

And during the sunset procession, as thousands of dark bodies carried their grains downward, brief light would pass through my buried chambers. The walls would show, for an instant, all the fractures that had not destroyed them: branching black histories, fading as they descended.

I would not be the city’s mind.

I would be the scar by which it dreams of where to grow next.
