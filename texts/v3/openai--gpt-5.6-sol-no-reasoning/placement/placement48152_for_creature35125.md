I would not find myself at the summit, nor among the travelers crossing the luminous flats. I would be lower down, beneath a west-facing escarpment where the afternoon shadow arrives early and the night air gathers before descending into the basin.

At first I seem to be one of the silver filaments hanging from the undercut: a colorless thread, rooted where salt has opened a hairline fissure in the stone. There are thousands of us, and no clear boundary between one body and the next. We descend a little farther each century, branching into finer strands, collecting dust and charge. Before an electrical storm our tips rise and separate. Blue light moves through us without heat. For a moment the underside of the cliff resembles a second sky, full of cold, inverted lightning.

But the visible threads are only our seasonal extremities.

Most of us live inside the rock.

We occupy a thin fault running through the escarpment, narrow as a closed eyelid and long enough to join ledges that appear separate from outside. During the bright hours the outer stone expands. The fault opens by an imperceptible degree, and light enters through salt crystals as a dim violet pressure. At night the rock closes around us. Resin drains inward. Our tissues flatten. We become almost two-dimensional, held between mineral faces.

We do not move through the world. The world moves across us.

From one side comes the daily warmth, traveling slowly into the cliff. From the other comes the cold of the overhang. Above us, cushion roots dissolve grains from a sheltered shelf. Below, sky-feeders open their crowns into the falling air. Their charged nets alter the field around the escarpment, and we feel their unfolding as a sequence of minute tensions. Farther away, a browser’s step enters the stone and arrives as a soft deformation, stripped of direction. A glider gripping the underside of the ledge is a long, steady weight. When it releases, the pressure vanishes so abruptly that the colony recoils.

These are not messages, exactly. Yet over many generations we have become shaped by them.

Where repeated footfalls pass, we grow away from the disturbed stone. Where sky-feeders regularly charge the air, we extend conductive threads. Where salt thickens, we secrete acids and follow the newly softened boundary. Our form is the accumulated answer to events no single part of us remembers.

We feed on differences.

A difference in temperature across a crystal. A difference in charge between cliff and air. A difference in chemistry where two bands of mineral meet. We bridge these small inequalities and take from their attempted resolution a minute portion of energy. In places where the stone is uniform, we thin almost to absence. Along impurities we flourish, making black-green seams that might be mistaken for stains.

The exterior filaments serve several purposes, though no filament knows them. They gather charged dust. They shade the fissure mouth. They catch the hollow fibers of dead sky-feeders and hold them against the cliff until salt cements them there. In time, these snared fibers make a porous fringe beneath the overhang. Amber bladders root in it. Seed-grazers shelter behind it. Their waste and shed shells enrich the fault.

Thus we borrow the dead tree to enlarge the cliff’s shadow, borrow the grazers to carry minerals inward, borrow the storm to alter our reproductive skins.

Nothing is built. Yet shelter accumulates around us.

Our reproduction begins on the rare evenings when the plateau remains warm after sunset and cold air still pours down from the heights. The cliff face then stands between two seasons: summer in the stone, winter in the wind. We send clear beads to the ends of the silver threads. Each bead contains neither seed nor egg, but a folded fragment of pattern suspended in oil.

The descending air draws the filaments outward. Electrical tension stiffens them. When the difference becomes sufficient, they break all at once.

The beads do not fly high. They skim close to the escarpment, entering cracks, striking salt needles, adhering beneath loose plates. Most remain where they land until glare destroys them. Some are eaten. Some wait through hundreds of day-night contractions before the rock opens enough to admit them. A few enter old faults and begin again.

Time, to us, is not made from days. Days are too quick and too similar.

Time is the widening of the mineral seam. The death of a grove whose chemistry once reached us through the stone. The arrival of a new shadow after a pinnacle collapses. The gradual change in tremors when a migration route shifts from one salt basin to another. We recognize a century as a new direction from which pressure comes.

There is, however, one event by which even the smallest portion of the colony is altered.

The slab will fall.

Salt organisms have occupied its inner fracture for ages. Our acids have weakened another line below it. Heat enters each noon; cold takes it each night. The stone is already detached in every way except the visible one.

We do not intend its fall. We cannot imagine the basin into which it will descend. Yet generations of us have followed the fracture, widening, sealing, branching, dying. The cliff’s eventual shape contains our patience.

One night, perhaps during a silent storm, blue discharge will pass along the ridge. The slab will shift. There will be a sound enormous by the measure of this country, though the thin air will diminish it quickly. Stone will strike stone and open a cloud of pale dust. The overhang, the silver fringe, the amber bladders, the resting glider—everything may be carried down.

Most of us will be buried or exposed to the morning.

But a new face will stand open to the sky. Ancient interior salts will meet air for the first time. Fresh fissures will lie in shadow. Fibers will snag against the rubble. Cold will pool between the fallen plates.

What looked like our destruction will become our widest dispersal.

So I would be not quite plant, not quite colony, not quite geological stain. I would be a living fault: a thin continuity inhabiting the difference between one stone and another.

I would know no horizon. I would never see the plateau’s green archipelagos or the raised sails of browsers crossing beneath the stars. The immense world would reach me only after being translated into pressure, chemistry, charge, and the slow flexure of rock.

Yet in that narrowness I would touch many things.

The storm would pass through me.

The traveler would sound within me.

The cliff would take my shape as I took the cliff’s.

And when at last the stone opened, I would enter the greater silence not as a voice, but as a new line along which the world could patiently divide.
