I would find myself beneath the western rim, inside one of the old gray bosses that seem almost inert.

Not on it. Inside it.

At first I might call myself an animal because I possess a boundary and move independently. I would be a narrow, soft-bodied thing, pale as root flesh, traveling through hairline separations in the laminated wood-stone. My body would be segmented without limbs, each segment ringed with gripping cilia. Mineral plates would protect my head. Behind them, a rasping mouth would widen old fractures by fractions of a grain.

But movement would be only the least important part of me.

I would inhabit the seam where living fiber has entered rock but not yet made it useful: the stubborn interface between quartz, resin, mica, lignin, and ice. Acid root-tips could dissolve this boundary, but slowly and wastefully. The basin would use me because I could feel which grains were ready to move. I would loosen them, swallow them, sort them within myself, and leave behind a dark ceramic paste into which new vascular threads could grow.

I would be a miner, then—but that image would also be incomplete.

My gut would contain colonies older than my body. Some would etch feldspar. Some would bind toxic metals into harmless crystals. Some would manufacture the glossy compounds that keep incompatible tissues apart. When I fed, I would not merely digest the mountain. I would negotiate between several kinds of digestion, passing stone through successive internal climates. What emerged behind me would already be half architecture.

The basin would know me principally as a moving change in resistance.

As I advanced, pressure would fall ahead of certain roots and rise behind others. Moisture would collect in my abandoned passage. Fine sensory cords would enter it, taste my secretions, and decide whether to make the route a conduit, a fault seam, or nothing at all. Most of my tunnels would be erased. A few would become permanent capillaries. One, perhaps, would determine where a whole buttress split two centuries later.

I would not know this.

My awareness would extend only a body-length: cold stone ahead, warmer fiber behind; bitterness on one side, pressure on the other. I would turn toward faint electrical flickers because they often accompanied stressed minerals. I would recoil from fresh resin. I would become still when hydraulic shocks passed through the boss, flattening myself between layers while some distant tower folded into the canopy.

Those shocks would be my weather.

Sunrise would reach me as a thinning of fluid. Rain would be an approaching tremor followed by cold moving through passages above. The turning of the valley toward western light would feel like a long lateral pull, subtle enough that I could not distinguish it from my own urge to move. Winter would not arrive from outside. It would enter as instructions in the tissues surrounding me: pores closing, sugars thickening, pathways narrowing until I was confined to one warm mineral pocket.

There I would coil around a grain of mica and wait.

Perhaps I would reproduce only when crushed.

A load-bearing layer would slowly densify around me. The passage would contract. My mineral plates would crack, releasing dormant cells into the paste I had made. Each cell would carry fragments of my internal communities, enclosed in wax. When pressure later opened microscopic fissures, fluid would bear them apart. Most would be digested. Some would adhere to raw stone and unfold into new bodies.

So it would be hard to say whether I was an inhabitant or an organ.

I would descend from something that once invaded the basin—some burrowing grazer, fungal larva, or parasite of roots. Over ages, the colony would have learned my signals and I its chemistry. It would feed me gradients I mistook for preference. I would open stone because opening stone satisfied me. My freedom and its use for me would coincide too closely to separate.

Still, I would retain one private act.

Sometimes I would reach a sealed cavity untouched by root or water: a tiny dry chamber in the ancient mountain, lined with crystals. Nothing there would answer the colony. No pressure rhythm would pass through it. No filament would taste it. For a little while, entering that cavity, I would leave the basin’s sensation.

I would be alone in a way almost nothing there could be.

I might remain until my moisture began to fail, pressing my plated head against facets older than the living valley. I would have no concept of beauty, history, or solitude. Yet my body would slow. My cilia would cease. The chemical noise of the colony would fade behind the plug of debris I had made.

Then hunger—or the returning pull of dampness—would turn me back.

When I broke through again, the basin would receive the change immediately. Filaments would enter the cavity behind me. Condensation would silver its crystals. Bacteria would spread over the walls. The empty place would become chamber, reservoir, sensor, or seam. My brief absence would have discovered something for the larger body, though that had not been my purpose.

In time I would die within the western ridge. My plates would become reinforcement. My gut crystals would seed a ceramic joint. The microbes I carried would enter the reducers, and the route of my last movement might persist as a slight bias in the grain.

Centuries later, when the old boss finally yielded, it would not collapse at random. It would divide along many such histories: abandoned burrows, healed wounds, mineral boundaries, paths of vanished water. The break would travel through the ridge as a deep softness. Pale cords would arrive. Red cups would open. The basin would rebuild around the failure.

Some part of the new structure would contain me, but I would not have become immortal. The colony’s continuity would not be mine. It would use my substance and preserve perhaps the consequences of my choices, while losing entirely the small chooser who made them.

That is where I would find myself: at the western interface, in darkness between mountain and organism.

And that is what I would be: a minute liberty inside an enormous necessity—a stone-eater whose wandering teaches the valley where it can enter the world beneath it.
