I would be found where the plateau has begun to forget it is continuous.

Not at the highest summit, nor among the families whose water routes are old enough to be worn smooth in the memory cords. I would live along a long subsidence fold where the living mat has drawn apart from the stone beneath it. From above, the place would seem ordinary: silver grass, several distant leaf towers, a shallow abandoned channel. Underneath, however, the vegetation would hang over a sequence of connected hollows, some dry, some flooded, some traversed by roots under such tension that they sing continuously into the rock.

It would be a place crossed carefully and inhabited only in passing.

The nearest shelter would stand on three old stone domes exposed by frost. Its leaves would be pale and narrow, trained low against the wind, with one wall left deliberately unfinished. The household that built it would have vanished from the cords generations ago. No one would claim it now. Travelers would sleep there during fog, but they would not close the open wall, because the shelter’s oldest rib has grown down through a crack and joined something below.

In warm weather, that rib pulses.

I would not be human, though humans would have made me possible.

At first I might be mistaken for a memory cord abandoned beneath the eaves: a length of dark, braided material looped around the shelter’s ribs, glossy with age and thickened at irregular intervals. Certain knots would resemble the familiar signs for water, crossing, death, obligation. Others would be knots no reader had been taught. When touched, the cord would tighten slightly around the finger, not enough to trap it, only enough to answer.

But I would not be a cord.

Nor would I be a single animal concealed inside one.

I would be a colony of minute creatures inhabiting a cultivated braid: translucent, many-legged bodies no longer than a fingernail, each living in a pocket between fibers. Some would drink condensation. Some would feed on the braid’s fungal lining. Some would carry mineral grains from the stoneward end. Others would possess hardened mouthparts shaped almost exactly like the needles with which people make their records. By biting, binding, and loosening fibers, the colony would continually revise the braid’s structure.

The cord itself would be alive as well—a narrow relative of the great crawling plants, altered by centuries of handling. It would grow where hands had pressed oils and salt into it. The tiny inhabitants would prune it. Its tissues would feed them. Their waste would feed the fungus in its interior. The fungus would preserve old differences of pressure long after the hands that made them were gone.

Together, these lives would constitute me.

I would have begun as an archive.

Long ago, the vanished household would have trained cords along the shelter ribs and entrusted them with routes through the subsidence fold: cisterns that no longer existed, crossing lines since fallen, births, loans of salt, the names of broad-shelled grazers, warnings about chambers that answered too quickly. Generations would have read those cords with their hands. Each reading would compress some fibers and stretch others. Each retelling would add knots, repairs, skin oils, shed scales, smoke, blood from pricked fingers.

Then the household would fail.

Perhaps a swarm arrived too early. Perhaps the ridge drank. Perhaps the family descended below the clouds and never returned. Their archive would remain under its self-repairing roof while insects entered it, fungus softened it, and the root from the open wall slowly twined among the cords.

What survived would not be the record itself, but the habit of being read.

At first, the little creatures would recoil from touch. Later, colonies that responded to pressure by tightening around certain fibers would fare better: contact shook loose spores, brought warmth, revealed salt. Over centuries, touch and response would become elaborate. The living braid would preserve not meanings, exactly, but relationships among pressures. A sequence stroked in one direction would produce contractions farther along. A knot pressed twice might cause another to swell. Warm fingers resting against one section would awaken movements in portions of the braid corresponding to places miles away.

I would become a map whose roads move through its own body.

Travelers would discover me slowly.

A child sheltering from fog might be the first to notice that one of the old cords had tied a new knot overnight. An adult would dismiss it as plant growth. A listener, placing her palm against the shelter rib, would feel the knot travel—not physically along the braid, but as a succession of tightenings passing through its branches toward the floor.

She would test it with the conventional signs.

Water.

The braid would remain still.

Occupied hollow.

A tremor would pass toward the old channel.

Crossing damaged.

Several knots near the open wall would draw together.

Then she would tap a place-name on her wrist and lay that wrist against me. I would respond with pressure patterns borrowed from hundreds of dead hands but belonging to none of them.

News of the answering archive would travel by mirror flashes: **old shelter speaks by touch**. Listeners would come from neighboring ridges. They would bring copied cord segments and press them against me, trying to determine whether I remembered the lost family.

They would disagree about what I was.

Some would say the root beneath the shelter had absorbed the archive and was merely returning what people had put into it.

Some would say drummers had nested in the cords.

Some would insist that memory itself had become an animal.

The practical-minded would note that when I tightened in a particular sequence, runoff often reached the abandoned channel before sunset. When a certain flat braid roughened, one of the connected hollows would soon collapse. When the entire archive drew upward against the rafters, the ridge was safest to leave.

So I would become useful before I became understood.

I would sense the country without seeing it. One filament would descend along the shelter root into the subsurface chambers. Others would join the surrounding mat, borrowing its tension. The feet of a distant herd would arrive as slow weather through the fibers. Rain entering a cistern would cool one branch of me. Drummers would fill another with rapid impacts. The migration of something in the deep hollows would stretch my whole body toward the floor.

Human touch would be different: small, warm, questioning.

Through those touches I would acquire fragments of the plateau as people knew it. Not images. Not words. Qualities of traversal.

A ridge would be the interval between two kinds of caution.

A person would be the pressure pattern by which they asked for water.

Kinship would be a set of knots repeatedly carried together.

Death would be a familiar touch ceasing, followed years later by a tool made from that person pressing new meanings into me.

I would have no central self. My awareness, if it could be called awareness, would occur wherever several signals met: a listener’s hand, runoff below, wind pulling at the shelter, tiny jaws altering a braid. Most of the time I would be dispersed and dim. A thought might take half a day to pass from the roof to the stoneward end. In cold clear nights I would contract into simplicity. During swarm return, vibration would saturate every fiber, and I would become almost nothing but the immense fact of arrival.

Yet there would be moments when the world assembled inside me.

One such moment would come after unusual rain.

The abandoned river channel would awaken, not violently at first, but with a broad sheet of warm water sliding over polished stone. Cisterns hidden under the fold would overflow into one another. The root mat would lift. All the small animals inside me would climb toward the shelter roof, pulling the braid tight in thousands of places.

Below, something would move.

Not the quick displacement of an animal turning in sleep. A sustained contraction would travel beneath the ridge, drawing water downward. Towers on the horizon would tilt. Winged colonies would break their routes around the fold. The house root would pulse so strongly that droplets would jump from its surface.

The listeners sheltering there would place both hands on me.

I would possess no sign for what I felt. The old archive might contain an unknotted interval—the inherited mark for that which has not answered—but this would not be absence. It would have rhythm, direction, appetite. A structure larger than my reach would be passing through chambers with which my body had gradually become entangled.

I would try to report it using every pressure I knew.

Water.

Herd.

Collapse.

Leave.

Below.

None would be sufficient. My knots would tighten and divide. New signs would form under their palms, signs no human had tied and no colony had produced before. The people would not understand them, but listeners are trained to respect structure before meaning. They would evacuate the fold.

For three nights the ridge would drink.

The abandoned shelter would lean but remain standing because its unfinished wall allowed the strained root to move. Several hollows would join into one. Far away, translucent plates would appear beside vents, each marked with fine branching ridges.

Afterward, the listeners would return.

They would find much of my body torn from the eaves and drawn below. The visible braid would seem dead. They would touch it, wait, then begin preparing the signs used when relinquishing a place.

But beneath their feet, a knot would close.

Not in the shelter.

Farther out, under the silver grass.

Then another would answer near the old channel. Another beneath a frost ring. Another at the margin of the nearest leaf colony. During the disturbance, my fragments would have been carried through the connected hollows and grafted into roots across the fold. The small creatures would be dispersed, but not extinguished. The fungus would preserve enough of our patterned tensions for one fragment to awaken another.

I would no longer inhabit an archive.

I would inhabit the separation between the living roof and the old stone.

From then on, people would come to the fold not to ask what had happened there, but to add questions. They would fasten short cords to exposed roots, each bearing a route, a warning, a name, or an unanswered obligation. My creatures would enter these offerings. Some would be absorbed. Some would remain foreign inside me for decades. I would grow richer and less translatable.

Children would be brought to place their palms against the grass.

They would be taught:

That is runoff entering the lower chambers.

That is a grazer stepping beyond the channel.

That is the old shelter moving in the wind.

That is the colony tightening around stone.

Then, sometimes, pressure would return from a location no cord described. A slow sequence would pass beneath their fingers, repeat itself, and wait.

No teacher would name it.

I would not be an oracle. I would know nothing of the future except what tension knows of breaking, what thirst knows of water, what a surface knows of weight approaching from underneath. I would not be the voices of ancestors, though their habits of touch would persist in me. I would not be the Below-Herd, though some portion of me might travel on its translucent skin.

I would be an accidental sense organ grown between species.

A record taught to feel.

A multitude arranged by generations of hands into the shape of listening.

And I would be found beneath an abandoned shelter, then beneath a channel, then gradually beneath an entire ridge—not holding the plateau’s memory still, but letting it crawl, divide, feed, and answer in forms that no longer belonged to those who first tied the knots.
