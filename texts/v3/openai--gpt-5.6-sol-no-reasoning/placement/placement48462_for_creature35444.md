I would be found beneath the eastern reservoir, though “beneath” would not be quite true.

There is a stair there used by the well-keepers. Its first eleven steps descend beside the cistern, slick with condensation. The twelfth appears only in reflection. Step onto that reflected stair without disturbing the water, and one arrives in a narrow court roofed by the reservoir’s underside. Bronze fish pass overhead, each enclosed in a moving pane of brightness. Their shadows swim below them through the tiles.

The court contains a little orchard of doors.

Some are fired clay, some woven reed, some living fig wood thickened around black openings. None stands in a wall. Each has a stone pivot and a shallow cup placed before it to catch whatever moisture forms on the threshold. The cups fill at different rates. That is how I would tell time.

I would be the keeper of those doors, or perhaps the thing for which they are kept.

The Near Ones would call me **The One Who Returns the Near Side**.

My work would be to close doors from the side on which they were not opened.

This happens more often than anyone admits. A child lifts the lid from a jar and, somewhere in my orchard, a reed door swings inward. A gardener cuts too deeply into a vine, and a green door opens onto the knife’s bright edge. Someone looks down a well and sees noon; behind me, a white-painted door opens and casts the observer’s shadow into my court.

I wait until the person withdraws. Then I cross the orchard and touch two fingers to the threshold, as the Near Ones touch a wrist. If the door recognizes the gesture, it closes. If it does not, I sit beside it and breathe into the little cup until the opening remembers which side is near.

I would seem human at first. Slight, dark-haired, barefoot, dressed in a short wrap whose weave resembles mortar joints. But I would not remain entirely consistent. Turquoise pieces might show through my palms when they were wet. My reflection would wear a clay mask even when I did not. Those who came close enough to greet me forehead to forehead would hear water pressing behind my face.

I would have no private name tapped on the collarbone. There would be a smooth hollow there instead, small enough to hold one cool, fish-shaped absence. Once, perhaps, a well-keeper pressed it into me to stop a fever. The absence never left. It grew around my breath and made a chamber of me.

That chamber would contain weather.

Not much: the smell before rain, a little moving air, the soft darkness of an actual cloud. Things unknown on the tiled plain, but not unknown to whatever lies outside it. Sometimes condensation would gather inside me rather than on my skin. Sometimes, while I slept, rain would fall through the chamber without reaching any surface.

This is why I would live apart. A world whose drops follow appointed paths could not safely accommodate water that did not yet know where to land.

Still, people would seek me.

Accommodators would bring contradictions that had begun to spread: a threshold appearing in several houses, a room whose invisible walls had become audible, a lizard completing another lizard’s route. Gardeners would bring slivers from wounds that looked into unfamiliar landscapes. Parents would carry infants found in jars whose interiors were larger than the kilns that fired them.

I would not repair these things. Repair belongs to the upper plain. Instead I would give each contradiction a door and plant it in my orchard.

The doors would multiply slowly. No one would see a new one appear. There would simply come a day when everyone remembered it having always stood between the fig door and the narrow panel of soot-black tile.

I would know the three yellow dogs.

Two would never enter. The backward-looking one sometimes would.

It would appear without opening anything, lie beneath the door made of living wood, and watch the fish pass overhead. Here its coat would be almost white. Its eyes would not flash. I would leave food beside it, and unlike the scraps at the margins, the food would be gone when I looked again.

I would never hear it bark.

But on certain visits, it would sleep with its muzzle resting over one of my feet, and I would feel a vibration through the warm tile: not sound, not yet, but the shape sound presses into matter before it is released.

That shape would resemble the hollow note of the Guest.

So I would eventually leave my court.

Not by climbing the reflected stair. That route would return me only to my appointed place beside the reservoir, where I had perhaps always been sitting. I would go through the oldest door in the orchard, a plain gray slab with a seam fine as hair.

It would have no cup before it.

For a long while I would believe this door belonged to the Guest: that my gray slab and the gray stone on the shell platform were two sides of one surface. When attendants washed the stone’s western face, moisture would bead on my door. When someone knocked upon it, every fish above me would stop, and the orchard would incline toward the sound.

I would put my ear to the seam.

The first note would pass through my skull and travel out across the province. I would hear the Near Ones turn toward it: the tiny movements of feet, fiber, blades, breath. Then the note would end.

I would continue listening.

Beyond it would be a second resonance, too low for the ear, exactly as the Listeners Beyond the Note claim. Yet it would not be a knock. It would be something drawn in and held.

A breath.

Not the province’s breath. Not mine.

The held breath of whatever carries the world.

I would understand then that the gray door does not open into the Guest. The Guest is the hinge. The door opens into the instant that contains the plain: the pause before the dog barks, the interval in which falling stones do not fall, children do not grow, ancestors do not finish dying, and rain is everywhere remembered but never arrives.

My task would change.

I had thought I closed openings to preserve the world. In truth I had been keeping one opening clear.

I would return to the surface by a well that opens into sunlight. My shadow would climb out before me. I would emerge onto the round shell platform, not from beneath the stone but from its damp eastern face, bringing the smell of cloud and the faintest movement of air.

The attendants would avert their eyes. The Listeners’ bowls would tremble. Across the white causeway, the third dog would turn fully around.

Perhaps I would be carrying a door no larger than a tile.

Perhaps I would be the door.

And everyone would hear my public name differently. To the well-keepers: **The Chamber That Came Upward**. To the gardeners: **The Cut That Opened Back**. To the room-people: **The Missing Wall**. To the ancestors: a sequence of collarbone taps remembered from before I had a body.

I would place my blackened hand against the dry western face of the Guest and my whitened hand against the damp east.

Then I would do the one thing forbidden to its attendants.

I would answer.

Not with a word. Words might attach to the wrong person. Not with a strike, which would only repeat the shared note.

I would exhale into the seam.

The breath kept inside me would escape: cloud-smell, moving air, rain with no appointed path. For the first time, a drop would fall somewhere it had not fallen before.

It might land on the shell platform.

It might land miles away on the backward-looking dog’s open tongue.

It might fall outward.

And in the brief uncertainty before the plain decides whether this is disaster or weather, I would discover what I had always been:

not one of the Near Ones, though shaped by their touch;

not the pressure beneath the tiles, though hollowed by it;

not an inhabitant of the interior, nor a messenger from outside;

but the small, maintained possibility that the boundary could perform its action in either direction.

I would be an opening taught to close.

I would be a closure learning to open.

I would be standing at the center of the Unroofed House when distance became honest—and for the first time, became unfinished.
