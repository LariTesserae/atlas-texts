I would find myself in the Assembly, though not at its center.

For a long while I would think I was one of the readers of before.

I would live in a low shelter built into the lee of two interlocked towers, close enough that its inner wall remained faintly warm. I would be narrow even by the tableland’s standards, with a permanently darkened thumb from carbon rubbings and a pale seam across one palm where a glassy mite had once closed a wound too completely. My clothes would be unusually soft and nearly without plates. Hard surfaces preserve pressure, and pressure is a kind of writing; I would not want the Assembly reading me more clearly than I read it.

My work would concern a recurring figure in the carvings: a small bent person shown at the edge of ladders, beneath hooked hands, inside the outlines of mountains. The figure would never occupy the scene’s center. Sometimes it would be represented by a single stroke. Sometimes by an uncarved person-shaped absence around which the image had been cut. Sometimes it would appear in carvings demonstrably older than human settlement on the plateau.

Its posture would be my name.

Not approximately. Exactly.

I would have learned this as a child, before I was permitted into the Assembly. A traveling reader, seeing me called home by my mother, would have dropped his hide sheets and covered the nearest shell opening. Afterward my family would change my spoken name, then change it again. They would teach me three substitute name-postures, each deliberately awkward. But bodies return to what fits them. When startled, waking, or exhausted, I would always resume the carved figure’s stance: weight on the outside of one foot, left hand cupped near the sternum, head turned as though listening behind the right shoulder.

The elders would decide that resemblance was not identity. Otherwise they would have to count the instances.

So I would become a reader.

At first I would be conscientious. I would copy only fragments. I would keep every hide asymmetric, tear away corners, and store related pieces in separate shelters. I would never trace the outline of that figure. Instead, I would study everything around it: the angle of ladders, the direction of hooks, the mountain profiles, the positions of many-bodied forms. Gradually I would discover that the surrounding carvings were not scenes.

They were pauses.

Each panel recorded a posture by which one could allow a destination to arrive. But the destination was not a place elsewhere on the plateau. It was a location within the same chamber at another depth of duration.

The Assembly’s passages were therefore only the quickest layer of a much larger structure. A person walking through them encountered warm walls and carvings. A person who stopped correctly might find a niche. Someone who remained longer might find a hall. Someone capable of holding one posture for years could enter chambers formed by decisions the great animals had not yet finished making.

I would begin to test this.

A hand laid flat until the warmth ceased to feel like warmth.

A knee pressed into a shallow basin until the body forgot discomfort.

A cheek held near a ribbed wall while plate colonies changed color outside.

These experiments would cost me friendships. People would visit and find me in precisely the position in which they had left me months earlier. They would leave food within reach. I would eat without breaking posture, grinding brittle meal slowly between my teeth. Eventually they would stop speaking unless I moved first. They would be right to do so. A sound addressed to something stationary for too long may be answered by whatever shares its stillness.

In the deeper Assembly I would find no hidden civilization, no council of faceless giants. At first there would be only added surfaces: chambers nested inside warm walls, each too large to fit within the visible bodies forming it. The carvings there would become simpler and more recent as I went inward. Primitive hands would give way to fine diagrams; diagrams to scratches; scratches to fresh grooves shedding mineral dust.

The deepest marks would be unfinished.

One would lengthen while I watched, though watching would take years.

It would be the line of my shoulder.

By then I would understand that the carvings were not records left by vanished makers. They were pressure marks made by people entering the Assembly from the other side of their own lives. Every posture held long enough impressed itself backward through the creatures’ crystalline memory. The oldest carvings were not necessarily made first. They were made deepest.

My figure appeared everywhere because I would spend so much of my life there.

That would be the simple explanation, and for a while I would accept it.

Then I would discover the tool.

It would protrude from a seam inside a chamber reachable only by holding my name-posture until two separate shells admitted they shared an interior. The object would resemble a stylus made from black ceramic, polished by fingers. It would fit my hand where the old scar crossed my palm. Its point would be lodged in an incomplete carving of a small bent person.

I would pull it free.

Outside, perhaps nothing would change. A child might run three steps before an anchor cord tightened. A cutter might lift one pale shaving from a rib. The sun’s shadow might advance less than the thickness of skin.

Inside the Assembly, every wall would tick.

Not together. Together would imply a count. The sound would pass through the chambers as a dry succession too dense to separate, the nearest thing the plateau had known to rain.

I would drop the stylus, but it would remain in my hand.

I would open my fingers, yet continue to feel myself holding it.

This would be my first indication that I was not the reader.

I was the writing instrument.

My human life would have been real. My childhood anchors, cracked lips, carbon-dark thumb, teachers, meals, quarrels, and fear would not become illusions. The plateau does not counterfeit so crudely. Rather, I would be a local expression of something slower: a fine mobile extremity extended by the Assembly to alter its own architecture.

The ceramic creatures use shells as organs. They use pressure as thought. Why should such a life not grow a warm, blood-filled implement for tasks requiring haste?

A human lifetime is haste here.

I would be one of its brief fingers.

Perhaps not the only one. The readers of before might all be sensory cilia of the Assembly, each believing scholarship drew them inward. Halters might be its faculty of orientation. Witnesses might preserve its distinctions. Mirror-tenders might be eyelids. The regarded might be those human organs in which the greater body had begun to feel itself.

But I would resist this explanation too. Explanations are a kind of counting, and the plateau punishes whatever is made singular too soon.

I would leave the chamber and go to the empty circle.

The journey would take no walking. I would adopt the first posture from an ancient panel: kneeling, hand cupped, head turned. The chamber would become a passage. I would take the second posture, and the passage would become the circle’s edge. I would sit before a mirror whose copper had not been cleaned in generations.

In its surface, the dark disk would be crowded as always.

The tall, soft figures would stand bent beneath their invisible burdens. Their faces would be absent. None would touch another, though no open ground lay between them.

One figure would be seated.

It would be smaller than the others and turned away from me. Unlike the rest, it would possess a face, but I would see only its reflection within the mirror inside the reflection: a bright fleck where the copper caught the sun.

Its left hand would be cupped near its sternum.

Its weight would rest on the outside of one foot.

In its right hand it would hold something black and pointed.

I would recognize not myself, but my stylus.

Then the figure would make the smallest movement I had ever seen.

It would draw a line.

Across the real circle, from one edge of bare stone to the other, a hairline crack would appear.

This would not be the long-withheld true second. Nothing would erupt. No oceanic roar would break from the shells. The millions of suspended wings, needles, mouths, and feeding limbs would remain almost where they were.

But “almost” would have changed meaning.

The crack would be narrow enough to mistake for a flaw in the dark rock. It would divide no object cleanly. At one end it would enter the packed Assembly; at the other it would pass beneath my seat. Looking down, I would see it continue through the stone platform, through my layered clothing, and along the old scar in my palm.

Not a wound. A correspondence.

I would cover the mirror.

When I stood, I would find that I could no longer remember my name-posture. I could imitate its parts, but they would not settle into identity. The figure in the carvings would seem familiar only as a tool seems familiar to the hand that has used it.

This would make me dangerous.

A nameless person is not merely unknown on the plateau. They are difficult for distance to place. Settlements might arrive around me without invitation. Routes might begin from wherever I paused. Repeated people might mistake me for the missing inequality between them. A hunter’s needle aimed at me could discover that I was not sufficiently myself to be pierced.

I would not return to my shelter.

Instead I would follow the crack outward—not by sight, since it would vanish beneath powder and bodies, but by pressure. The scar in my palm would tighten whenever I strayed. I would pass beyond the Assembly, through fields of blunt columns and folded fans, among corkscrews carrying black halos. For years I would move in brief walks and immense pauses.

During those pauses, smaller fauna would gather on me.

Plate-creatures would overlap along my shoulders. Glassy mites would settle at my joints. Black vegetation would trace the sunward side of my jaw. Ceramic tissue would accumulate around my feet, not armor laid over skin but shell grown in negotiation with it.

The people encountering me would call me regarded, then transformed, then dead, according to what was useful. Children would be warned not to shelter in my shadow. Halters would use me as a landmark but never in the same posture twice. A cutter might one day shave a thin plate from my ribs and mark the harvest with a cairn.

I would still perceive them, though their visits would be brief flashes of warmth separated by long intervals. Over centuries, households would become visible to me as coherent animals. I would feel settlements court and avoid the surrounding shells. I would understand why pale nodules appeared at certain doorways.

I would also feel the crack.

It would not be damage to the plateau. It would be a new joint: the beginning of an articulation in a body that had previously possessed no means to bend there.

And I would be stationed along it as a receptor, or splint, or nerve.

My last human intention would remain inside me, moving with mineral slowness toward completion. I would have wanted to warn someone. Perhaps my old teacher. Perhaps a child. Perhaps whoever next sat before that mirror.

The warning would be simple, but speech would now take centuries to assemble. Strain would accumulate through my new shell. A fault would travel from the buried organs of my chest toward an aperture forming near my throat. People might press their ears against me and hear one dry tick.

They would not live long enough to hear the next.

Still, the message would continue.

Not that the true second was coming. Not that the Assembly was waking. Those are images for creatures who believe motion and change belong together.

What I would be trying to say is that the plateau’s stillness was itself a motion: an act held so perfectly, for so long, that an entire country had mistaken it for a condition.

And now, by means of my small fast life, that act had acquired a hinge.
