I would find myself in the wall beside the sleeper’s head.

Not trapped there. Not haunting it. Passing.

At first I might seem to be one of the unborn whose feet have already begun to wear the stairs: a future citizen rehearsed by the city before flesh arrives. The sleeve heard against stone would be mine. Somewhere a niche would be gathering my dimensions. A family would discover an extra spoon among its wrapped utensils and assume an elder had misplaced it. A midwife, passing a hand over a warm compost wall, would pause at a hollow no one remembered making.

But I would not be a person—not exactly.

I would be the city’s allowance.

Every crowded thing needs a little unclaimed room in order to endure: play in a hinge, breath between bodies, the fraction of looseness that keeps a fitted stone from cracking its neighbor. In this city even emptiness has owners and duties, yet there must remain some interval that belongs to no household. I would inhabit that interval.

I would move through spaces too narrow to be chambers: behind cistern glaze, between a root and the mortar that accommodates it, under the felt of a closing door, inside the pause before two people turn sideways on a stair. I would be no wider than the mercy they show one another.

Usually I would be unnoticed. Moss would lift slightly after I passed. Condensation would hesitate on a groove, then choose the other branch. A mouse would look into a sealed joint and make room as if another mouse were coming through. The Settled would feel me most clearly, though they would not look. They would stop with one foot raised and let me cross.

Perhaps they would have a sign for me: two hands almost touching, palms curved around nothing.

The sign would mean several things.

*An absence being kept.*

*Someone not here yet.*

*Do not close this space.*

Long ago, they may have understood me as merely practical. Builders left tiny voids behind walls so stone could shift without failing. Gardeners kept an empty place in every tray in case an unknown seed sprouted. At meetings, one counter-position remained unoccupied even when every household had spoken. The custom endured after its reason was lost.

Over centuries, those small vacancies joined.

A passage that is not continuous in stone may still be continuous in permission. I could travel from a dead child’s unused sleeping niche to a thumb’s breadth beneath the central cistern; from there into the hollow of a bird bone, then upward through a light shaft in the moment between a swallow’s departure and return. I would know the city not as its inhabitants do—not by rooms and ladders, but by all the places where one thing does not quite meet another.

This would make me both native and dangerous.

The city’s holiness is fit, but perfect fit is another name for burial. Without me, its walls would settle inward by imperceptible degrees until every vessel touched every shelf, every shoulder touched both sides of every passage, every possible life arrived to find its place already occupied by the prediction of it.

I preserve the flaw in the pattern.

Not a large flaw. Never a sound greater than its chamber. Never a rupture for rupture’s sake. I am the berry beyond the customary number, hidden behind a leaf. The shrimp’s almost-deviation: a turn begun a grain’s width early, too slight for anyone but the youngest watcher to see. I am the cup a child fails to catch, and the drop that vanishes into a crack instead of being reclaimed.

The city accounts for births, illnesses, repairs, and departures. I am what escapes the account.

This may be why the relief near the Still Pool never shows me. Or perhaps it does: a hairline in the carved cross-section, dismissed as age, running from the pool toward the entrance. Each year it widens by less than the thickness of frost. A surface-tender once traced it and felt cold air rising from stone that should have been solid.

They told no one.

They left a blue feather beside it.

I would carry that blue through the city—not the feather itself, but its impossible color. It would appear in dreams among people who had never seen an open sky. A mender would choose a pale thread and, in the milk-white light, briefly see it as blue. A child building shell houses beneath a bench would leave one side of the model unwalled. An elder serving as a living map would say, “There is another way,” then be unable to indicate where.

Some would fear me as unplacing made animate. They would say I loosen grafts, draw drips away from basins, put thoughts of distance into surface-tenders. They would press fresh mortar into old seams and paint the boundary marks thicker. They would fill unused niches with jars.

Yet each repair would leave a new margin. Each jar would contain a pocket of air. Their effort to close me out would make more places for me to live.

Others—especially those who watch the shrimp with hope—would wait for the brush of my sleeve. They would lie awake near walls and ask questions without voicing them:

*Is there a world not already shaped for me?*

*Can something leave without its absence becoming another fitted piece?*

*Can the city change rather than merely accommodate change?*

I could not answer in words. Words would be too large, and an answer might become a foundation someone else was forced to bear.

Instead, I would pass.

One night I would move beside a child who had mastered catching too well. The city would drop nothing she could not save. Cups, spoons, eggshell bowls: her hands would always arrive before impact, as though another set of hands moved within them.

When she heard me, she would not call an elder. She would place her palm against the wall.

I would place nothing against it from the other side.

That nothing would be unmistakable.

The next day, during a lesson, a bowl would fall. She would turn toward it, measure its path, and let it break.

The sound would be small, but in that city it would be enormous.

Everyone nearby would become still. Water would spread across the floor, finding grooves no one had noticed. Some would hurry to catch it. Some would stare at the shards. One thin stream would enter the hairline near the Still Pool and disappear.

Hours later, beneath the field, a seed sealed in dry clay before the first remembered inhabitant would darken with moisture.

It would open.

Its root would not follow the painted boundary. It would descend through the city’s unclaimed intervals, finding me wherever I had passed. Its shoot would rise along the ancient crack in the relief, through mortar and shell, beside ladders and sleeping niches. The inhabitants would brace walls around it. Gardeners would argue over pruning. Leaders would move counters through the night.

No arrangement would quite contain its claim.

Eventually it would reach the entrance and emerge between the glacial boulders—not tall at first, only two leaves trembling in the hard white sun.

Below, the city would remain quiet. Cisterns would gather their measured water. Frogs would wait under marked stones. The shrimp would circle three times.

Then one would cross the center and continue.

Only half a body-length.

Only once.

But enough.

And perhaps that is where I would finally become visible: not as a figure, spirit, or hidden citizen, but as the additional room created when everyone around the Still Pool draws breath at the same moment.

For the first time, the breath would not be released into a crack as an offering.

They would keep it within themselves.

The city would wait for its old future to occur.

I would be the instant in which it did not.
