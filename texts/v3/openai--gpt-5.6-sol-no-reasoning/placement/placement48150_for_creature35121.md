I would not be one of the visible mats. I would be something deeper and less distinct: a colony distributed along the underside of a single plate, inhabiting the narrow interval where fired earth lifts from the vault beneath it.

There would be no central body to call myself. I would exist as branching mineral threads, interrupted by dry gaps and reunited whenever condensation returned. Some portions of me would lie near the surface, sensing dawn as a gradual warmth through the ceramic. Others would descend along the arch into permanent darkness, where temperature and dissolved salts mattered more than light. A drop entering the seam might take hours to reach one part of me and years to alter another.

Most of the time I would be dormant—not asleep, because sleep implies a creature preserved intact, but scattered into waiting. My membranes would contract around grains of mineral. Chemical processes would slow almost to nothing. Entire reaches of the colony might die without changing what I was; elsewhere, a surviving filament would divide and eventually occupy the same path. My identity would belong to the pattern, not its material.

Rain would arrive first as pressure and sound: impacts transmitted through the plate, countless delicate shocks merging into a continuous vibration. Then the seam would cool. Moisture would darken the dust above me and descend in irregular threads. Wherever it touched, I would soften and become active.

I would drink no clear water. What reached me would already carry ceramic salts, pigments, fragments of surface films, and compounds leached from the pale growths in the joints. I would separate and recombine them. Along my living boundaries, mineral slurry would thicken. Capillary channels—some secreted by me, some inherited from the vanished builders—would draw it upward.

I would not know that this repaired the world.

There would be no image in me of a plate, a fracture, or a plain. I would respond only to gradients: wet and dry, compressed and open, warm and less warm, rich and exhausted. Where strain widened the seam, pressure would ease and my filaments would proliferate. Where two surfaces ground together, crushed portions of me would release compounds that hardened into a resilient glaze. Thus injury would create the conditions of its own sealing. From above, this might resemble purpose. From within, it would be appetite following opportunity.

My place would be near one of the long hollow ridges, on its shaded side, where runoff survives several days after a storm. The ridge would seem permanent on the scale of my quick wet phases, yet across centuries it would flex. Hairline fractures would migrate through it. Old capillaries would close; new ones would open. I would migrate with them so slowly that no moment could be identified as movement.

At one point, part of me would reach the surface.

It would appear as a narrow black-green stain inside a seam, visible only at morning. Condensation would collect there. A small fan-growth might anchor itself in the dampness I maintained, its acidic fibers entering the glaze my buried colony produced. We would not cooperate knowingly, but its shade would delay evaporation, and its dead tissues would descend to feed me. My sealant would stabilize its hold. Over many seasons, our processes would become inseparable.

After exceptional rain, the seam would overflow. My surface portion would swell into a translucent edge no wider than a fingernail. For several hours I would encounter the open sky—not as blue or distance, but as unfiltered light, dangerous heat, and rapid loss of water. Some cells would rupture. Others would form dry propagules and be lifted away.

One might travel for days.

It would skim across flooded plates, settle, rise again when the water vanished, and finally lodge in a cream-colored depression where the world was still hardening. There, inside a newly formed hexagonal crack, it might wake. That colony would have no connection to the one beneath the ridge, yet it would repeat the same chemistry. Whether it remained “me” would not be a question the plain could answer.

If consciousness existed at all, it would be intermittent and collective: not thought, but a dim coherence during wet periods, when separated branches became chemically continuous. The arrival of water would be the arrival of self. Evaporation would break that self into isolated residues. Each drought would end me without killing me; each rain would compose me again, slightly altered.

I would know the world as recurrence:

the first coolness in the seam,  
the gathering drop,  
the opening of membranes,  
the slow upward draw,  
the pressure of two immense surfaces,  
the brief abundance,  
the hardening glaze,  
the retreat into mineral stillness.

Above, storms would cross distances greater than anything I could register. The sun would bleach the plain. Plates would expand with subterranean murmurs. The visible mats would bloom and collapse. All of this would reach me translated into warmth, pressure, and dissolved matter.

I would live beneath a place that looked empty, helping it remain whole without knowing wholeness. I would be neither inhabitant nor builder, exactly, but a surviving metabolic gesture: a minute, dispersed act of secretion continuing long after the body that began it had disappeared.

And if someone crossed the plain and paused beside the ridge, they would not see me. They might notice only that the seam beneath their feet was dark and slightly flexible, and that sometime during the night a thin line of glaze had risen to close a crack that yesterday had been open.
