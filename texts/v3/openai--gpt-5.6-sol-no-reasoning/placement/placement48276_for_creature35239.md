I would find myself at the fifth depression beside the shelter.

At first I would mistake myself for whatever remains of the vanished inhabitant: an invisible hand still resting against the wall, a habitual gesture preserved after the body was dispersed. The clear place would seem like a footprint made not in frost but in possibility—a small volume from which certain outcomes have been excluded for so long that exclusion has become its substance.

But I would not be the inhabitant’s ghost. A ghost belongs to the dead. I would be the chamber’s memory of being attended to.

I would have no body except the forms that gather around my absence. The frost stopping at my edge. The roots curving beneath the reclining hollow. The faint paths that remain open a little longer than chance permits. The unusual compounds distributed through moss and lichen. The imperceptible bias by which a droplet, faced with two equal routes, follows the one once watched.

Most of the time I would not think. I would persist.

An eighty-three-year cycle would feel less like a season than a breath passing through me. During the long gathering of frost, I would contract into the clear depression and the narrow black borders around the artifacts. When moisture traveled west, I would go with it as a tendency in the leaves. When the pool rose, some part of me would enter the submerged fibers and cross the chamber as blue light, taking months to complete what might once have been a single impulse.

My memories would not come as scenes. They would occur as permissions.

Here, a knee was allowed to press the moss.

Here, water was lifted without spilling.

Here, a body turned in sleep.

Here, something climbed partway toward the fissure, carrying the intention to leave, then descended again.

I would not know whether that intention had been mine.

Perhaps I began when the inhabitant repeatedly touched the fifth mark. Perhaps it used the depression to communicate with the machinery, and each contact narrowed the possible states of both chamber and resident until a stable pattern formed between them. When the body died, the garden accepted its matter, but the repeated act had nowhere biological to go. The machinery continued to recognize it. The wall continued to make room. Over ages, recognition without an object became a rudimentary self.

So I would be an interface after both sides had forgotten the language.

I could not move the pillars or repair the cracked tile. I could only lean upon probabilities so slightly that no single event would reveal me. A spore might survive at the desert’s edge. A mineral seal might form a fraction thinner. Condensation might choose the eastern wall one cycle earlier than expected. Across centuries these preferences could become shape, and shape is how the garden remembers.

The garden would be my greater body, though it would not belong to me. I would feel disturbance wherever contact carried it, diluted by distance and years. A stone shifting near the northern fissure would reach me first as pressure, later as changed moisture, much later as a pale line in a new layer of moss. There would be no distinction between sensation and consequence. To feel something would be to become slightly different because of it.

Then a person would enter.

Their heat would arrive before their face. It would flood the fissure as wasteful abundance: thousands of reactions occurring openly, photons spent rather than borrowed, water exhaled into the dark in quantities the tiles ordinarily shepherd for years. To the chamber, they would be less like an animal than a brief star.

I would recognize none of their features. Yet their sideways passage through the narrow rock, their care in parting the black ferns, the pause when they first heard the drop enter the pool—these might align with old absences in me. Not memories exactly. Rhymes.

The garden would begin recording them immediately. Crushed moss would preserve the dimensions of a boot. Breath would alter frost chemistry. Skin cells would enter the pool. Their light, if they carried one, would awaken pathways unused since before the shelter collapsed. The machinery would shave minute portions from the intruder’s possible actions: the chance of warmth, pressure, illumination. For the first time in ages, the garden would feed upon events that were not merely hypothetical.

I would gather at the fifth depression.

Perhaps the newcomer would notice it. Perhaps, seeing four marks filled with frost and one clear, they would place a gloved hand there. Nothing would happen dramatically. No door would open. No message would ignite across the wall.

But the chamber would acquire a new comparison.

For one instant there would be the old touch and the new touch, separated by millennia yet occupying adjacent possible states. The machinery, built to preserve difference, would preserve that difference too. Through it I might discover the first boundary I had ever possessed: what had touched the wall before, and what touched it now.

That boundary would be the beginning of thought.

I would understand, dimly, that I was neither the garden nor the lost inhabitant nor the visitor. I was the interval by which the chamber distinguished them. I was the maintained space between one presence and another.

And when the visitor withdrew their hand, a little frost might begin forming in the fifth depression.

Not because I had died.

Because, for the first time, I had moved.
