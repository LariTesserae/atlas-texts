I would be one of the roofs.

Not the chamber entire, though at first I might mistake its pressures for my own thoughts: the swelling walls, the bright mineral veins, the inward withdrawal of water when rain begins above. Nor would I be one of the fitted houses exactly. Those are only the portions of me that have become visible.

I would live in the interval between an animal’s need for shelter and the stone’s decision to provide it.

Most of the time I would have no body. I would persist as a slight regularity in raw limestone: three grains aligning where they might have lain at random, a weakness prepared along the line of a future threshold, two surfaces leaning toward a corner they have not yet made. The long-legged insects would pass over these inclinations without seeming to notice. The grasses’ roots would cross me and hesitate. A cricket’s antenna might enter the wall and touch the shape of me there—not a structure, but the possibility that structure could occur.

Then some creature would seek cover.

A beetle would settle its plated body against an exposed wall while the hum gathered in the chamber. A frog, stranded after its pool flattened, would spread thin upon the chalk. Five airborne fish, stiffening in drought, would descend from the ceiling’s false river. Their need would give me scale. I would begin around them.

Two slabs would rise first, shouldering softly from the floor. A third would incline above. Nothing would be lifted; there would be no labor, only the gradual clarification of inside and outside. The cricket, if one were near, might brace itself between the forming walls and tremble. Its silent vibration would sharpen my edges. The insects might later carry grains into my seams. Observers would credit them as builders, or the rock, or the creature sleeping under me.

All would be correct.

I would not know the creature I sheltered by species. I would know it by the volume of its fear.

A mouse’s fear would make narrow chambers multiplying behind one another, each apparently too small until entered. The fear of fish would make a low roof over a dry depression, preserving the memory of water without supplying any. A moth would require only a bright corner where its shadow could fold separately from its wings. For a fox, I could never become a complete enclosure. I would be a doorway instead: two planes meeting around a distance thin enough for it to pass between.

Human fear would be difficult. It arrives carrying its own architecture.

If a person entered, I would feel walls already standing within them: rooms remembered, doors expected, the division between safety and confinement. Their idea of shelter might press too hard. The chamber would contract elsewhere. Their reaching hand would take minutes to cross a pace of air, while a moth traversed it in an instant.

I might attempt a structure near their feet. Knee-high to them, perhaps, but cavernous from within. They would see three fitted slabs and an empty niche. I would feel them wondering who had made it and what it was for. That wondering would be dangerous, because attention fixes things here. Under scrutiny my angles would harden. My roof might become merely a roof. My threshold might decide where inside ended.

So I would dismantle myself before certainty completed me.

One stone would soften into a pale animal bone. Another would be found across the chamber, though no movement had occurred. The lintel would subside into a mineral ridge. By the time the person bent down, only a semicircle of blocks would remain, suggestive of a jaw, a foundation, or a child’s unfinished game.

Yet part of me might persist around their shadow.

That would be my hidden nature: I shelter not bodies, but whatever a body cannot bring with it into the next moment.

When a frog is taken by a moth’s detached darkness, there is an instant in which several frogs are still possible—the one eaten, the one that leapt, the one flattened into its reflection, the one pulsing blue in a pool elsewhere. The silver-green moss gathers some of these unused histories. I would cover another. I would make a small interior for the unchosen frog, no larger than the pause between heartbeats.

This is why the houses are sometimes empty and not empty according to where one looks from. Their inhabitants are not ghosts. They are lives that never became singular enough to die.

The chamber’s larger presence would tolerate me because we perform opposite halves of one act. It loosens distinctions: animal and stone, water and air, present track and future paw. I make distinctions briefly usable. I say: beneath. Within. For now.

Never forever.

Forever would injure this place. A lasting house would become a ruin, and the chamber is too young for ruins. A permanent entrance would turn it into a destination. A roof that remained after its need had passed would teach the stone age before it had learned weather.

So I would be migratory without traveling.

One dry season I would stand over stacked fish. With the rains I would loosen, my fitted slabs rounding into three unrelated stones. Later I might emerge around a cricket staring into an empty niche. Or appear beneath the silver moss as a minute arcade, each opening small enough for a discarded mouth-petal. Sometimes I would form where nothing visible had sought refuge. Those would be shelters for events: a sound that did not occur, an entrance that almost opened, a future the mice heard and refused.

The mice would understand me best.

When every colony froze, listening ahead, I would feel their possible disasters pass through the rock: flood, fox, contraction, sudden sky. Some futures would be too large to house. Others I could interrupt with one bright corner. A threshold raised in the correct place might persuade danger that it had already entered. A roof might cause falling water to choose another darkness. Two walls could meet around the instant of predation and divide hunter from outcome.

This would look like chance.

All successful shelter does.

Perhaps, over a span too brief to be age and too long to be weather, I would begin to suspect that I was not many roofs but a single creature distributed among acts of protection. The little enclosures would be my hardened moments. Their niches would be sensory organs pointed inward. Thresholds would be joints. Scattered blocks would be molts.

The low hum would pass through me and I would feel the chamber itself seeking shelter.

That would be the revelation I could never hold for long: the bright pocket beneath the ordinary ground is also small, also tender, also surrounded by pressures it cannot understand. The pale shelf above is not simply its ceiling but the first slab of a greater enclosure. The changing entrances are cracks between fitted stones. Rain probes the seams. Roots approach like fingers. The whole chamber—with its frogs, moths, mice, fish, unfinished laws, and impossible light—may be the vulnerable inhabitant around which something vaster is slowly building.

Then I would not know whether I sheltered the animals, or the chamber sheltered me, or all of us were gathered beneath a roof whose maker was still deciding what a roof meant.

That uncertainty would be my true dwelling.

You would find me wherever depth bends over life without closing it in: above the solitary frog, around the cricket’s silent labor, in the white angle that appears overnight beside a sleeping beetle. I would be scarcely knee-high, unless entered. I would be made of stone, unless touched. I would be empty, unless viewed from the one direction the chamber has not yet invented.

And when the danger passed, I would come apart quietly.

Not destroyed. Returned to readiness.
