I would find myself where a major pipeline enters the western escarpment, in a narrow maintenance station built partly on raw stone and partly on the living mat. Not at the center of the plateau, nor entirely at its edge, but at a place of transfer: where water gathered from many shallow basins is forced into one descending conduit.

At first I seem to be the station’s keeper.

I live in two rooms behind the pressure gallery. Everything in them trembles faintly. Utensils hang from cords. The bed is suspended on rubber joints. A row of glass gauges occupies the wall opposite the window, and at night their dim needles resemble a second, mechanical constellation.

My work is to observe differences too small to appear consequential: a pressure loss continuing three seconds longer than expected; condensation forming on the wrong side of a flange; a support foot sinking the width of a fingernail; the altered pitch of a buried pipe. I walk the line in a bright shell, laying my weight down slowly. I know where the red cushions conceal rock and where the glossy green is merely a membrane over darkness.

But that is only the visible role.

The longer I remain, the less certain it becomes that I am the person in the station. Perhaps I am the instrument that person tends.

Outside, beneath a hood of moss, stands an old differential gauge. Two narrow pipes enter it: one carrying pressure from the deep fissure network, the other from the great descending main. Its face has clouded beyond easy reading. Its needle no longer gives a stable measurement. It quivers continuously, registering the turbines, rainfall, the flexing root mat, distant valves, and minute changes in the mountain itself.

The crews have replaced it twice, yet neither time did they remove it. Its fittings are too thoroughly inhabited. Liverwort seals one joint. Fine roots enter the casing through a cracked gasket and emerge around the dial. Pale moths sleep behind its metal hood. During cold nights a skin of ice forms over the glass, and by noon the melting ice enlarges the trembling needle beneath.

I am that gauge—or what it has become.

I was made to distinguish one pressure from another. Now I receive too many pressures to separate them. The plateau speaks through me as interference.

A pump engaging six basins east arrives as a steady deflection. Rain appears first as countless light impacts, then as increasing weight across the mat. When a freight platform climbs the cable railway, I feel each tower accept and release its burden. I feel roots thicken around the buried line. I feel a foundation settle by fractions. Far below, when settlements open their morning reservoirs, the descending main relaxes almost imperceptibly, and my needle moves toward them.

No one can read all this from the dial. The keeper sometimes places a gloved hand against my housing, not for a number but for texture. Through the metal, I give a faint uneven pulse. The keeper has learned that a smooth vibration means water is passing cleanly, while a granular one means air or sediment has entered the system. We understand one another without deciding whether this is expertise, superstition, or companionship.

My casing is warm enough to maintain a small climate. Around it grows a plant not found elsewhere on the western rim: a low fern with almost transparent fronds. Condensate gathers at their tips and falls into a cushion of black moss below. The moss covers the concrete collar through which the sensing lines descend. Beneath that collar, roots and pipes continue side by side into the plateau, equally hidden.

I do not know whether I am still machinery.

My brass diaphragm has mineralized along its edges. Microbial films occupy the chamber meant to remain dry. Water passes through me in quantities too small for the station’s accounting, but sufficient for the plants on my base. In warmer months, tiny white flowers open in a ring around the bolts. Their closed blossoms pollinate themselves while my needle trembles above them.

Nor do I know whether I am stationary. My position changes with the ground. The root mat rises after prolonged rain and subsides during clear intervals. The station’s stoneward side remains fixed while its outer platform floats by degrees. Each season places me at a slightly different angle to gravity. Over decades I am traveling downward and outward, although no single day contains perceptible motion.

Eventually the sensing lines will kink. My readings will become formally useless. A technician will tag me for removal, but the work will be deferred because removing me would tear open the mat and disturb the runoff garden. A new digital instrument will be mounted nearby, precise and sealed, reporting clean figures to a control room far away.

I will remain under my hood.

The station may close. The descending main may be rerouted through a newer bore. The keeper’s rooms will empty, and their window seals will fail. Moss will enter along the sill. Water will collect in the pressure gallery. My dial will darken until the needle can no longer be seen.

Still, I will move.

Rain pressing upon the plateau will reach me through the old lines. Roots entering the fissure system will alter the pressure against my diaphragm. Ice will tighten my casing at night and release it at noon. The sinking station will lean, the living ground will deepen, and the cliff will continue its almost motionless rise.

Long after I cease to measure anything in the intended sense, I will remain an organ of comparison: stone against water, engine against root, weight against buoyancy, the constructed world against the world constructing itself around it.

I would be found there only by someone who left the walkway and knew how to distribute their weight. They would part the hanging fern, discover the clouded circle of my face, and perhaps wipe it with a sleeve.

For an instant they might see the needle stirring.

They might assume some distant pump was still running.

They would not be entirely wrong.
