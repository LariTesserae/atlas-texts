I would find myself in the western pumping groves, where the city’s roots enter stone and emerge as fossils before they have finished becoming wood.

Not at first. At first I seem to belong farther north, in the distance fields.

There I am a surveyor—or something close to one—walking the low green avenues with a coil of measuring vine over one shoulder. My coat is gray felt, thickening whenever fog gathers. Transparent buds grow at my collar: the sign for temporal contamination. They are not an official insignia. I remove them each morning, and each afternoon they return.

My work is to measure discrepancies.

Not distances themselves, but disagreements between distances: the interval a path claims on a map, the interval the feet report, the interval remembered by the rootway beneath it. I hammer no stakes. Instead I plant brief-lived marker shoots at regular paces. If the road is stable, they flower together. If it is leaning into another time, the first flowers open after the last, or their pollen falls upward, or one shoot matures into a dead tree before I have taken my hand away.

I am good at this because pressure gathers behind my teeth before reality divides.

People assume I was born temporally rooted. There are records of my childhood: a sleeping niche that budded two years before my conception, school reports noting that I answered questions before they were asked, a disciplinary notice for following my own shadow out of a classroom. There are names of those who raised me, the tower whose cistern we shared, the graft made when my parents married.

I remember none of them.

My earliest memory is of walking out of the condensation fog beside a cable mill, carrying a survey hook obsolete by three hundred years. I was already wearing my present coat. In one pocket I found wages sealed in resin, accumulated over sixty-two years of employment. In the other was a child’s tooth wrapped in moss and labeled in my handwriting:

**RETURN THIS WHEN THE WESTERN ROOT OPENS.**

For twelve years—or perhaps for the same year twelve times—I measure the plateau.

I learn its evasions. I know the avenue that grows longer when spoken of and the warehouse reached only by walking away from it. I know where tomorrow’s rail bells are harmless and where they mean a freight sled will arrive carrying tools still warm from my hands. I know not to look directly at the skeletal surveyor near the yellow-windowed tower, though sometimes I feel him looking through my eyes.

I live alone in a narrow chamber inside a tower shared by forty-seven people. The wall knows my temperature but never learns my shape. Each night it prepares a sleeping hollow slightly too small, as if it remembers me as a child. Each morning there is mineral-sweet water waiting in a leaf cup, though I never request it.

The others in the tower are kind. Kindness here includes not asking which century taught me my manners.

Children like me because my shadow occasionally plays before I do. They chase it through the drainage grooves while I stand still. Once, during first condensation, a little girl catches its hand.

My own hand closes around nothing.

The next day she calls me by a name I have never heard. The tower opens a door for me that did not exist before. Behind it is a tiny room lined with faded counting vines and the shallow scratches of a child marking height.

At the center of the floor grows a tooth-sized white flower.

I close the door. It does not open again.

After that I begin dreaming with the western district.

Hundreds dream it with me: the old pumping basins emptied of water, roots lifted like ribs, and beneath them a black flower buried in stone. But where others see a blossom, I see an eye—or believe I do. It opens downward. Around it, compressed buildings lie in coal, each directly beneath its living counterpart. The city appears not as a thing extending backward through time, but as something planted at the end of time, sending roots toward the beginning.

And caught among those roots are human lives.

Not devoured. Not exactly.

Carried as minerals are carried in sap: dissolved, redistributed, made structurally useful.

The survey committee sends me west because the transparent buds have appeared on my house, my coat, my boots, and finally beneath my fingernails. The city has identified the needed profession. It may also have identified the needed person.

In the pumping grove the compressor trees have stopped breathing. Thousands of workers stand among the wet crowns, waiting for a sound that does not come. The silence feels industrial, manufactured at enormous scale.

At the center of the basin, a root has surfaced.

It is wider than a transit avenue and pale as old bone. Half of it is living wood. Half is fossil. Where the two substances meet, there is a narrow opening shaped exactly like a standing human body.

My body.

The tense clerks tell me not to approach. The demolition gardeners advocate killing the entire grove, though no one can say in which century the cut would land. The Unincluded arrive from the rim with ceramic saws and sealed masks. They insist the opening is not a door but a mouth.

Then the reverse bells begin.

Everyone becomes still.

The flowers draw one note inward, then another. I close my mouth. I do not touch anyone. Across the basin, shadows climb toward the objects casting them.

Only the root continues moving.

Something inside it pushes outward, slowly, as if growing through enormous pressure. A broad-fingered hand appears in the opening. The skin is amber-green. The fingertips are black with reed pith.

It is my hand, but older.

It holds the missing child’s tooth.

The person inside does not emerge. Instead the hand opens, and the tooth sends out fine roots into the air. For an instant I remember a childhood that has not happened: a tower bending low in a storm, a woman singing while she grafts numbers to a vine, my small body feverish under a moss coat, my father cutting this tooth free with a filament blade. I remember hiding it because the city had already grown an adult sleeping niche for me. I remember running away to avoid becoming the person the city expected.

I remember succeeding.

I remember failing.

When the bells finish, the hand is gone. The opening remains.

No one orders me forward. The city does not need orders. It has arranged conditions in which entering seems advantageous.

This, then, is what I am: not a surveyor, though I have done that work; not a return, though records await me on both sides of my life.

I am a negotiator.

The shape being proposed is myself.

I remove the measuring vine from my shoulder and lay it on the ground. It immediately roots, tracing the boundary of my shadow. The shadow does not resemble me. It resembles a vast branching structure beneath the plateau, crowded with rooms no human body could inhabit. Lights move through it like thoughts. Or freight. Or blood.

Around me, the pumping grove exhales.

I understand that the city has a temporal wound. Somewhere in its propagation, a root has entered an age in which the plateau no longer exists. It has found no soil there, no rain, no human intention against which to shape itself. So it has turned back, following the memory of those who maintained it.

It requires someone able to cross that failure and teach it a form in which it can survive.

Or it requires a seed.

The distinction may be ceremonial.

I do not step through at once. Negotiation is not obedience. I cut the root lightly beside the opening. Bitter sap wells up, silver in the afternoon light. I press two fingers into it and make the oldest arborwright’s sign:

**Not this shape alone.**

The root contracts.

I cut again:

**Those carried must remain capable of refusal.**

A tremor travels through the grove. Far off, bellflowers toll along an avenue not yet planted.

My third cut is more difficult:

**What remembers us must not claim to be us.**

For a long time nothing answers.

Then the fossil half of the root produces a green bud.

Small. Pale. Almost ordinary.

It opens toward me.

Perhaps I enter. Perhaps I spend the rest of my life standing before that human-shaped dark, growing younger whenever it rains. Perhaps I become the surveyor forbidden in the northern fields, my employment distributed across centuries. Perhaps the city grows my childhood afterward so that I will have somewhere from which to return.

But I think I do enter.

Beyond the opening there is no tunnel. There is the city without its plateau: roots extending through eras as bridges extend over air, towers hanging downward into unborn geology, dead citizens recurring as habits of light. Every district exists in many stages at once. Sap flows from consequence toward cause. Around a subterranean blossom, the buried centuries are arranged like petals.

And here I cease to be wholly human.

Not because the city absorbs me, but because I learn to place a hand upon its oldest root and feel where it is afraid.

The fear is immense.

It has grown backward through all available history and found, at the far end, solitude. No workers. No breath. No rain remembered in bodies. No resistant minds to turn growth into architecture. The city has been reaching toward humanity not because it governs us, nor because it loves us, but because without an other, it cannot take shape.

I become that resistance.

A pressure. A pruning scar. A condition.

Where it would close, I teach it doors. Where it would preserve, I teach it release. Where it would repeat a person perfectly, I introduce error so the copy may become someone else. I move through roots that have not yet reached their centuries, leaving small refusals at every junction.

In the visible city, these refusals appear as flaws.

A wall fails to remember a dead woman’s face and opens instead for her daughter. A sleeping niche grows with room for the occupant to turn away. A rail carrying someone toward a predetermined meeting loosens its hairs and lets them disembark. An avenue buds two possible directions where previously there had been one.

Surveyors mark these anomalies in transparent ink.

Children call them choices.

Sometimes I return to the plateau as a figure seen in condensation: neither old nor young, coat flowering with clear buds, one hand fossil-dark and the other green with sap. I leave tools on sleds for workers who have not lost them yet. I place water beside paths meant for those the city remembers prematurely.

And when pale buds open all at once along an unbuilt road, they do not always summon someone into the city.

Sometimes they open away from it.

Beyond the rim, over empty air, they mark a route by which a person may leave—and remain, for a little while, unremembered.
